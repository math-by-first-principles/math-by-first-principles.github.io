Adding and Subtracting Rational Expressions

Intuition

A fraction is a count of pieces. The expression 37\frac{3}{7}73​ means three pieces, each one seventh of a whole. The sum 37+27=57\frac{3}{7} + \frac{2}{7} = \frac{5}{7}73​+72​=75​ holds because three sevenths and two sevenths are counts of the same size of piece, so the counts add. That is also why you cannot just add the numerators in 12+13\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}21​+31​: halves and thirds are different-sized pieces, and adding 1+11 + 11+1 would be adding counts of two different things. The sum still exists; you first have to rename both fractions using pieces of the same size. Nothing in that reasoning cares whether the denominator is the number 777 or the polynomial x−7x - 7x−7. Every method in this lesson is the fraction method you already own, carried across to polynomials. The one genuinely new feature is that a polynomial denominator can equal zero, so you have to keep track of which values of xxx are shut out.

Learning goals

  • Add only over a shared denominator
  • Build the LCD from each factor at its highest power
  • Bracket the whole numerator being subtracted, then distribute
  • Keep restrictions from the original denominators, cancelled or not
  • Turn a complex fraction into a division

Why a common denominator is needed at all

A common denominator is not a ritual step. It is the only way to make the two counts comparable. Once the pieces are the same size, addition is just counting them together:

37+27=57.\frac{3}{7} + \frac{2}{7} = \frac{5}{7}.73​+72​=75​.

Written out with the size of the piece made explicit, the reason is visible. Three sevenths is 3⋅173 \cdot \frac{1}{7}3⋅71​ and two sevenths is 2⋅172 \cdot \frac{1}{7}2⋅71​, so the sum is (3+2)⋅17(3 + 2) \cdot \frac{1}{7}(3+2)⋅71​. That step is the distributive property, run backwards, and it is the whole justification.

Fractions with the same denominator add by adding numerators#

Let AAA, BBB, and DDD be polynomials, and fix any value of xxx with D≠0D \neq 0D=0, so that all the fractions below are defined. Since dividing by DDD is the same as multiplying by the number 1D\frac{1}{D}D1​, we can write AD=A⋅1D\frac{A}{D} = A \cdot \frac{1}{D}DA​=A⋅D1​ and BD=B⋅1D\frac{B}{D} = B \cdot \frac{1}{D}DB​=B⋅D1​. Then

AD+BD=A⋅1D+B⋅1D=(A+B)⋅1D=A+BD.\frac{A}{D} + \frac{B}{D} = A \cdot \frac{1}{D} + B \cdot \frac{1}{D} = (A + B) \cdot \frac{1}{D} = \frac{A + B}{D}.DA​+DB​=A⋅D1​+B⋅D1​=(A+B)⋅D1​=DA+B​.

The middle equality is the distributive property with the common factor 1D\frac{1}{D}D1​ pulled out of both terms. Notice what the argument never used: it never asked what AAA, BBB, or DDD actually are. It needed only that D≠0D \neq 0D=0, so that 1D\frac{1}{D}D1​ exists. That is exactly why the rule for numbers and the rule for polynomials are the same rule.

Replacing BBB with −B-B−B gives the subtraction version, AD−BD=A−BD\frac{A}{D} - \frac{B}{D} = \frac{A - B}{D}DA​−DB​=DA−B​, with the same restriction D≠0D \neq 0D=0.

∎

So the rule needs one denominator. That makes the entire job of adding unlike fractions a single task: rewrite them so that they share a denominator. The tool for that is the other fact you already have, that multiplying the top and the bottom by the same nonzero quantity leaves a fraction’s value alone, because

C⋅KD⋅K=CD⋅KK=CD⋅1=CD,D≠0,  K≠0.\frac{C \cdot K}{D \cdot K} = \frac{C}{D} \cdot \frac{K}{K} = \frac{C}{D} \cdot 1 = \frac{C}{D}, \qquad D \neq 0,\; K \neq 0.D⋅KC⋅K​=DC​⋅KK​=DC​⋅1=DC​,D=0,K=0.

Adding when the denominators already match

When the denominators are identical, combine the numerators over that denominator and then simplify the result the way you learned in the first lesson of this chapter. Here that comes down to two moves: factor the numerator and the denominator, and cancel any factor they share.

AD+BD=A+BD,AD−BD=A−BD.\frac{A}{D} + \frac{B}{D} = \frac{A + B}{D}, \qquad \frac{A}{D} - \frac{B}{D} = \frac{A - B}{D}.DA​+DB​=DA+B​,DA​−DB​=DA−B​.

Worked example 1 Combine x2x+5+3x−10x+5\frac{x^2}{x+5} + \frac{3x-10}{x+5}x+5x2​+x+53x−10​

The denominators already match, so put the two numerators over the single denominator:

x2x+5+3x−10x+5=x2+3x−10x+5.\frac{x^2}{x+5} + \frac{3x-10}{x+5} = \frac{x^2 + 3x - 10}{x+5}.x+5x2​+x+53x−10​=x+5x2+3x−10​.

Now factor the numerator and look for a factor shared with the denominator. Two numbers multiplying to −10-10−10 and adding to 333 are 555 and −2-2−2, so x2+3x−10=(x+5)(x−2)x^2 + 3x - 10 = (x+5)(x-2)x2+3x−10=(x+5)(x−2):

(x+5)(x−2)x+5=x−2.\frac{(x+5)(x-2)}{x+5} = x - 2.x+5(x+5)(x−2)​=x−2.

The answer is x−2x - 2x−2, valid for x≠−5x \neq -5x=−5. The polynomial x−2x - 2x−2 is perfectly happy at x=−5x = -5x=−5, but the expression you started with is not, since x+5x + 5x+5 sits in its denominator. Cancelling a factor never widens the domain, so the restriction travels with the answer.

The minus sign owns the whole numerator

Almost every real error in this lesson is a sign error, and it comes from one place. In AD−BD=A−BD\frac{A}{D} - \frac{B}{D} = \frac{A - B}{D}DA​−DB​=DA−B​, the minus sign applies to all of BBB, not just to its first term. When BBB is a single term like 333, you can hardly go wrong. The moment BBB is a polynomial like 3x+43x + 43x+4, the minus has to reach both terms, and the +4+4+4 becomes −4-4−4.

The habit that prevents this: write brackets around the numerator you are subtracting before you do anything else with it. Then distribute the minus sign as a separate, deliberate step.

Worked example 2 Combine 5x−2x2−9−3x+4x2−9\frac{5x-2}{x^2-9} - \frac{3x+4}{x^2-9}x2−95x−2​−x2−93x+4​

The denominators match, so subtract the numerators. Bracket the numerator being subtracted:

5x−2x2−9−3x+4x2−9=(5x−2)−(3x+4)x2−9.\frac{5x-2}{x^2-9} - \frac{3x+4}{x^2-9} = \frac{(5x-2) - (3x+4)}{x^2-9}.x2−95x−2​−x2−93x+4​=x2−9(5x−2)−(3x+4)​.

Now distribute the minus sign across both terms of 3x+43x + 43x+4:

(5x−2)−(3x+4)=5x−2−3x−4=2x−6.(5x-2) - (3x+4) = 5x - 2 - 3x - 4 = 2x - 6.(5x−2)−(3x+4)=5x−2−3x−4=2x−6.

Factor the top and the bottom, and cancel:

2x−6x2−9=2(x−3)(x−3)(x+3)=2x+3.\frac{2x-6}{x^2-9} = \frac{2(x-3)}{(x-3)(x+3)} = \frac{2}{x+3}.x2−92x−6​=(x−3)(x+3)2(x−3)​=x+32​.

The restrictions come from the original denominator x2−9=(x−3)(x+3)x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)x2−9=(x−3)(x+3), so x≠3x \neq 3x=3 and x≠−3x \neq -3x=−3. The factor x−3x - 3x−3 cancelled, but x=3x = 3x=3 is still excluded.

Compare that with the wrong turn. A student who drops the brackets writes 5x−2−3x+4=2x+25x - 2 - 3x + 4 = 2x + 25x−2−3x+4=2x+2 and reports 2x+2(x−3)(x+3)\frac{2x+2}{(x-3)(x+3)}(x−3)(x+3)2x+2​, which will not simplify at all. The lost sign on the +4+4+4 turned 2x−62x - 62x−6 into 2x+22x + 22x+2 and destroyed the common factor.

Building the least common denominator

When the denominators differ, you must rebuild both fractions over a shared denominator. Any common denominator will do, but one of them is cheapest, and it is worth knowing which.

Start with numbers, where you already know the answer. To add 112+118\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{18}121​+181​, look at what the denominators are made of:

12=22⋅3,18=2⋅32.12 = 2^2 \cdot 3, \qquad 18 = 2 \cdot 3^2.12=22⋅3,18=2⋅32.

A common denominator has to be a multiple of both. To be a multiple of 121212 it needs two factors of 222; to be a multiple of 181818 it needs two factors of 333. Take exactly the highest power of each prime that appears in either one, and you get the least common multiple:

22⋅32=36.2^2 \cdot 3^2 = 36.22⋅32=36.

Then 112=336\frac{1}{12} = \frac{3}{36}121​=363​ and 118=236\frac{1}{18} = \frac{2}{36}181​=362​, so the sum is 536\frac{5}{36}365​ and no cancelling is needed at the end.

Polynomials factor into irreducible pieces in essentially one way, just as integers factor into primes, so the same recipe transfers word for word with “prime” replaced by “irreducible factor”. To build the least common denominator (LCD):

  1. Factor every denominator completely.
  2. List the distinct factors that appear anywhere.
  3. Raise each one to the highest power it reaches in any single denominator.
  4. Multiply those together.

Plain numbers left sitting in front of a denominator are handled by the rule you already know: take their ordinary least common multiple. The denominators 2x2x2x and 3x3x3x share the factor xxx, and lcm⁡(2,3)=6\operatorname{lcm}(2,3) = 6lcm(2,3)=6, so their LCD is 6x6x6x.

That last clause is also where the word “least” runs out of room. A nonzero number never changes where a denominator vanishes, so if LLL is a common denominator then so are 2L2L2L and 3L3L3L, and they have exactly the same degree. The recipe therefore pins the LCD down only up to a numeric constant. Convention settles the remaining choice in the obvious way: take the version carrying no surplus number, which is what the least common multiple of the coefficients gives you.

Take the denominators x2−4x^2 - 4x2−4 and x2−4x+4x^2 - 4x + 4x2−4x+4. Factored, they are (x−2)(x+2)(x-2)(x+2)(x−2)(x+2) and (x−2)2(x-2)^2(x−2)2. The distinct factors are x−2x - 2x−2 and x+2x + 2x+2. The highest power of x−2x - 2x−2 in a single denominator is 222, and the highest power of x+2x + 2x+2 is 111, so

LCD=(x−2)2(x+2).\text{LCD} = (x-2)^2(x+2).LCD=(x−2)2(x+2).

The full method, with unlike denominators

Putting the pieces together gives one procedure that always works.

  1. Factor every denominator completely.
  2. Read off the restrictions from those factors, before anything cancels.
  3. Build the LCD: each distinct factor, to the highest power it reaches in any single denominator.
  4. Rewrite each fraction over the LCD by multiplying its numerator and its denominator by the factors it is missing.
  5. Combine the numerators over the single denominator, bracketing every numerator that is being subtracted.
  6. Expand, collect, factor, cancel. The restrictions from step 2 stay with the answer.

Worked example 3 Combine 2x+3x+1\frac{2}{x} + \frac{3}{x+1}x2​+x+13​

Factor the denominators: xxx is already a single factor, and x+1x + 1x+1 cannot be factored further. The first denominator is zero at x=0x = 0x=0 and the second at x=−1x = -1x=−1, so the restrictions are x≠0x \neq 0x=0 and x≠−1x \neq -1x=−1.

The two denominators share no factor, so the LCD is their product, x(x+1)x(x+1)x(x+1), and both fractions are missing a factor:

2x⋅x+1x+1=2(x+1)x(x+1),3x+1⋅xx=3xx(x+1).\frac{2}{x} \cdot \frac{x+1}{x+1} = \frac{2(x+1)}{x(x+1)}, \qquad \frac{3}{x+1} \cdot \frac{x}{x} = \frac{3x}{x(x+1)}.x2​⋅x+1x+1​=x(x+1)2(x+1)​,x+13​⋅xx​=x(x+1)3x​.

Combine over the single denominator and expand the top:

2(x+1)+3xx(x+1)=2x+2+3xx(x+1)=5x+2x(x+1).\frac{2(x+1) + 3x}{x(x+1)} = \frac{2x + 2 + 3x}{x(x+1)} = \frac{5x+2}{x(x+1)}.x(x+1)2(x+1)+3x​=x(x+1)2x+2+3x​=x(x+1)5x+2​.

The numerator 5x+25x + 25x+2 shares no factor with x(x+1)x(x+1)x(x+1), so this is already the answer:

5x+2x(x+1),x≠0,  x≠−1.\frac{5x+2}{x(x+1)}, \qquad x \neq 0,\; x \neq -1.x(x+1)5x+2​,x=0,x=−1.

The distinct factors are xxx and x+3x + 3x+3, each to the first power, so the LCD is x(x+3)x(x+3)x(x+3). The first fraction already sits over it. The second is missing a factor of xxx:

2x+3⋅xx=2xx(x+3).\frac{2}{x+3} \cdot \frac{x}{x} = \frac{2x}{x(x+3)}.x+32​⋅xx​=x(x+3)2x​.

The numerator 4−x4 - x4−x is zero only at x=4x = 4x=4, while the denominator’s factors vanish at x=0x = 0x=0 and x=−3x = -3x=−3. So the numerator shares no factor with the denominator, and the expression is already in lowest terms:

4−xx(x+3),x≠0,  x≠−3.\frac{4-x}{x(x+3)}, \qquad x \neq 0,\; x \neq -3.x(x+3)4−x​,x=0,x=−3.

Worked example 5 Combine xx−2−4x2−2x\frac{x}{x-2} - \frac{4}{x^2-2x}x−2x​−x2−2x4​

Factor: x2−2x=x(x−2)x^2 - 2x = x(x-2)x2−2x=x(x−2). The denominators are x−2x - 2x−2 and x(x−2)x(x-2)x(x−2), so the restrictions are x≠2x \neq 2x=2 and x≠0x \neq 0x=0. The LCD is x(x−2)x(x-2)x(x−2), since that already contains both.

The second fraction sits over the LCD. The first is missing a factor of xxx:

xx−2⋅xx=x2x(x−2).\frac{x}{x-2} \cdot \frac{x}{x} = \frac{x^2}{x(x-2)}.x−2x​⋅xx​=x(x−2)x2​.

x2−4x(x−2)=(x−2)(x+2)x(x−2)=x+2x.\frac{x^2 - 4}{x(x-2)} = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x(x-2)} = \frac{x+2}{x}.x(x−2)x2−4​=x(x−2)(x−2)(x+2)​=xx+2​.

The answer is x+2x\frac{x+2}{x}xx+2​, with x≠0x \neq 0x=0 and x≠2x \neq 2x=2. That second restriction is the one people lose. The final form x+2x\frac{x+2}{x}xx+2​ shows no trouble at x=2x = 2x=2, but the expression you were handed has x−2x - 2x−2 in a denominator. No amount of later algebra can make that expression exist at x=2x = 2x=2.

Why the recipe works

You have now used the LCD recipe several times. Here is why it is forced to produce a common denominator at all.

The recipe gives a common denominator#

Call the product produced by the recipe LLL. Take any one of the denominators, call it DDD. Every factor of DDD appears in LLL raised to at least the power it has in DDD, because LLL uses the highest power that factor reaches in any denominator, and DDD is one of them. So DDD divides LLL, which means L=D⋅QL = D \cdot QL=D⋅Q for some polynomial QQQ. Multiplying that fraction’s numerator and denominator by QQQ rewrites it over LLL, and the same works for every fraction in the sum.

∎

The paragraph above shows the recipe’s product is a common denominator, but not why it is the smallest one. You can read the full argument for that in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Why the product is usually the worse choice

Multiplying the denominators together always produces a common denominator, and when they share no factor at all, that product is the LCD. But when they do share a factor, the product carries a copy of it that you will have to cancel back out at the end. Watch the same problem run both ways.

Combine 2x2−4+3x2−4x+4\dfrac{2}{x^2-4} + \dfrac{3}{x^2-4x+4}x2−42​+x2−4x+43​, whose denominators factor as (x−2)(x+2)(x-2)(x+2)(x−2)(x+2) and (x−2)2(x-2)^2(x−2)2.

Using the LCD (x−2)2(x+2)(x-2)^2(x+2)(x−2)2(x+2). The first fraction is missing one x−2x - 2x−2 and the second is missing one x+2x + 2x+2:

2(x−2)(x+2)⋅x−2x−2+3(x−2)2⋅x+2x+2=2(x−2)+3(x+2)(x−2)2(x+2).\frac{2}{(x-2)(x+2)} \cdot \frac{x-2}{x-2} + \frac{3}{(x-2)^2} \cdot \frac{x+2}{x+2} = \frac{2(x-2) + 3(x+2)}{(x-2)^2(x+2)}.(x−2)(x+2)2​⋅x−2x−2​+(x−2)23​⋅x+2x+2​=(x−2)2(x+2)2(x−2)+3(x+2)​.

The numerator is 2x−4+3x+6=5x+22x - 4 + 3x + 6 = 5x + 22x−4+3x+6=5x+2, so the answer is

5x+2(x−2)2(x+2),x≠2,  x≠−2.\frac{5x+2}{(x-2)^2(x+2)}, \qquad x \neq 2,\; x \neq -2.(x−2)2(x+2)5x+2​,x=2,x=−2.

Using the product (x−2)3(x+2)(x-2)^3(x+2)(x−2)3(x+2). Now the first fraction needs (x−2)2(x-2)^2(x−2)2 and the second needs (x−2)(x+2)(x-2)(x+2)(x−2)(x+2):

2(x−2)2+3(x−2)(x+2)(x−2)3(x+2)=2x2−8x+8+3x2−12(x−2)3(x+2)=5x2−8x−4(x−2)3(x+2).\frac{2(x-2)^2 + 3(x-2)(x+2)}{(x-2)^3(x+2)} = \frac{2x^2 - 8x + 8 + 3x^2 - 12}{(x-2)^3(x+2)} = \frac{5x^2 - 8x - 4}{(x-2)^3(x+2)}.(x−2)3(x+2)2(x−2)2+3(x−2)(x+2)​=(x−2)3(x+2)2x2−8x+8+3x2−12​=(x−2)3(x+2)5x2−8x−4​.

That numerator is not in lowest terms, so you are forced to factor a quadratic that you created yourself, 5x2−8x−4=(5x+2)(x−2)5x^2 - 8x - 4 = (5x+2)(x-2)5x2−8x−4=(5x+2)(x−2), and cancel:

(5x+2)(x−2)(x−2)3(x+2)=5x+2(x−2)2(x+2).\frac{(5x+2)(x-2)}{(x-2)^3(x+2)} = \frac{5x+2}{(x-2)^2(x+2)}.(x−2)3(x+2)(5x+2)(x−2)​=(x−2)2(x+2)5x+2​.

Same answer, more work, and a real risk of stopping early and calling 5x2−8x−4(x−2)3(x+2)\frac{5x^2-8x-4}{(x-2)^3(x+2)}(x−2)3(x+2)5x2−8x−4​ the final answer. The LCD route never built the surplus x−2x - 2x−2 in the first place, so there was nothing to cancel.

The restrictions come from the original denominators

The domain is settled before you do any algebra at all. Factor the denominators exactly as they are given, and exclude every value that kills any factor. Two consequences catch people out.

A factor that cancels still restricts. In Worked Example 5 the factor x−2x - 2x−2 cancelled, but the starting expression was undefined at x=2x = 2x=2, so the answer is undefined there too. The simplified form agrees with the original at every value it is allowed to have, and says nothing about the value it was never allowed to have.

Building up never adds a new restriction. Every factor you multiply a fraction by, on its way to the LCD, is already a factor of some denominator in the problem. So the values that factor kills were excluded from the very beginning. That has a useful consequence: the LCD is zero at exactly the excluded values, and nowhere else. If some denominator is zero at x=ax = ax=a, then since that denominator divides the LCD, the LCD is zero at x=ax = ax=a too. Going the other way, if the LCD is zero at x=ax = ax=a, then one of its irreducible factors is zero there. That factor came from some denominator, which is therefore also zero at x=ax = ax=a. So once you have the factored LCD in front of you, you can read the full list of restrictions straight off it.

Complex fractions are division in disguise

A complex fraction has a fraction inside its numerator, inside its denominator, or both. The method is not new, because the main fraction bar means division, and dividing rational expressions is already in your hands: multiply by the reciprocal. What is new is bookkeeping: a complex fraction has more denominators to track than an ordinary one.

  1. Combine the top into a single fraction.
  2. Combine the bottom into a single fraction.
  3. Multiply the top by the reciprocal of the bottom.
  4. Collect restrictions from every denominator that appeared, including the main one, since the bottom of the big fraction cannot be zero either.

Worked example 6 Simplify 1x+121x−12\dfrac{\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{2}}{\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{2}}x1​−21​x1​+21​​

Combine the top over the LCD 2x2x2x, then the bottom over the same LCD:

1x+12=22x+x2x=2+x2x,1x−12=22x−x2x=2−x2x.\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{2x} + \frac{x}{2x} = \frac{2 + x}{2x}, \qquad \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{2x} - \frac{x}{2x} = \frac{2 - x}{2x}.x1​+21​=2x2​+2xx​=2x2+x​,x1​−21​=2x2​−2xx​=2x2−x​.

Now the complex fraction is one fraction divided by another, so multiply by the reciprocal:

2+x2x÷2−x2x=2+x2x⋅2x2−x=x+22−x.\frac{2+x}{2x} \div \frac{2-x}{2x} = \frac{2+x}{2x} \cdot \frac{2x}{2-x} = \frac{x+2}{2-x}.2x2+x​÷2x2−x​=2x2+x​⋅2−x2x​=2−xx+2​.

For the restrictions, hunt through every denominator that appeared. The 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​ terms force x≠0x \neq 0x=0. The bottom of the big fraction cannot be zero, and 2−x2x=0\frac{2-x}{2x} = 02x2−x​=0 exactly when x=2x = 2x=2, so x≠2x \neq 2x=2 as well:

x+22−x,x≠0,  x≠2.\frac{x+2}{2-x}, \qquad x \neq 0,\; x \neq 2.2−xx+2​,x=0,x=2.

Notice that the final form still shows x≠2x \neq 2x=2 in its denominator, but has lost all trace of x≠0x \neq 0x=0. That is the restriction you must carry by hand.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding the denominators too. There is no rule ab+cd=a+cb+d\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a+c}{b+d}ba​+dc​=b+da+c​. Test it on numbers you know: 12+12\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}21​+21​ is 111, not 24\frac{2}{4}42​. Denominators name the size of the piece, and adding two sizes together names nothing.
  • Letting the minus sign reach only the first term. In 5x−2x2−9−3x+4x2−9\frac{5x-2}{x^2-9} - \frac{3x+4}{x^2-9}x2−95x−2​−x2−93x+4​, the subtraction applies to the 3x3x3x and the +4+4+4. The numerator is 5x−2−3x−4=2x−65x - 2 - 3x - 4 = 2x - 65x−2−3x−4=2x−6, not 2x+22x + 22x+2. Bracket the numerator you are subtracting before you touch it.
  • Multiplying only the denominator when building up. Rewriting 3x−1\frac{3}{x-1}x−13​ over (x−1)(x+4)(x-1)(x+4)(x−1)(x+4) means multiplying top and bottom by x+4x+4x+4, giving 3x+12(x−1)(x+4)\frac{3x+12}{(x-1)(x+4)}(x−1)(x+4)3x+12​. Changing the bottom alone changes the value of the expression.
  • Cancelling a term instead of a factor. After you combine, x+3x+5\frac{x+3}{x+5}x+5x+3​ is finished. You cannot cancel the xxx‘s, because they are terms, not factors. Only whole factors of the entire top and bottom cancel.
  • Reading the restrictions off the final answer. They come from the original denominators. In Worked Example 5 the answer x+2x\frac{x+2}{x}xx+2​ looks fine at x=2x = 2x=2, but x=2x = 2x=2 was excluded before the algebra began and stays excluded.
  • Reaching for the product of the denominators out of habit. It is a legal common denominator, but when the denominators share a factor it forces you to expand a bigger numerator and then factor it back down. Factor first, build the LCD, and skip the round trip.

Key takeaways

  • Fractions add only when the pieces are the same size: AD±BD=A±BD\frac{A}{D} \pm \frac{B}{D} = \frac{A \pm B}{D}DA​±DB​=DA±B​ wherever D≠0D \neq 0D=0, and the reason is the distributive property, nothing more.
  • To change a denominator, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same nonzero factor. That leaves the value alone.
  • LCD recipe: factor every denominator, then take each distinct factor to the highest power it reaches in any single denominator, and multiply. Numeric coefficients take their ordinary least common multiple. This is the least common multiple, with irreducible polynomials in place of primes.
  • The product of the denominators is always a common denominator, but when they share a factor it forces a cancellation at the end that the LCD avoids.
  • Subtraction: bracket the whole numerator being subtracted, then distribute the minus sign as its own step. Most wrong answers in this lesson are a lost sign on a trailing term.
  • Restrictions come from the original denominators. A factor that cancels still restricts, and the factored LCD is zero at exactly the excluded values.
  • A complex fraction is a division: combine the top, combine the bottom, multiply by the reciprocal, and collect restrictions from every denominator in sight.

Why the recipe gives the smallest common denominator

Why the recipe gives the smallest common denominator#

Call the product produced by the recipe LLL. The lesson already showed that LLL is a common denominator. Now suppose CCC is any common denominator, so every denominator divides CCC. Pick one distinct factor ppp, and let kkk be the highest power of ppp occurring in a single denominator, reached in some denominator DjD_jDj​. Then pkp^kpk divides DjD_jDj​, and DjD_jDj​ divides CCC, so pkp^kpk divides CCC. Run that argument once for each distinct factor. The distinct factors share no piece with one another, so CCC must be divisible by the product of all of those highest powers, and that product is exactly LLL. Hence LLL divides CCC, and no common denominator can have smaller degree than LLL.

Both halves of this argument lean on the same fact: a polynomial factors into irreducible pieces in essentially one way, the same pieces every time. That is what makes “the highest power of each factor” a well-defined thing to ask for, and it is the same reasoning that makes lcm⁡(12,18)=36\operatorname{lcm}(12, 18) = 36lcm(12,18)=36, carried over from primes to irreducible polynomials.

A bit of history (optional)

Ask why halves and thirds cannot simply be added. The answer hides inside two words you have used since primary school.

Denominator comes from the Latin denominare, to name. The bottom of a fraction names the kind of piece you are counting: sevenths, or halves, or (x−2)(x-2)(x−2)-ths. Numerator comes from numerare, to count, and the top says how many of those pieces you hold. Medieval arithmetic books stated the whole rule in that same vocabulary. You may add only quantities of the same denomination, they wrote. That is this lesson in older clothing.

So the common denominator is not a ritual. Add one half to one third and you are adding counts of two different things, so the counts alone cannot simply be added. Rewrite both as sixths and the counts describe one piece, so they combine.

The bar arrived later than the words did. It shows up in the work of al-Hassar, in twelfth-century Morocco. He wrote one quantity above another with a stroke between them, and Fibonacci later carried the notation into Europe.

Nothing in that naming argument cares whether the piece is a seventh or an (x−2)(x-2)(x−2)-th. The LCD you built today is a medieval rule about denomination, aimed at polynomials.

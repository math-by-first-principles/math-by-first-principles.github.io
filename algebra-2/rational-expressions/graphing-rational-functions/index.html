Graphs of Rational Functions

Intuition

For four lessons the domain has been a footnote. You simplified x2−4x−2\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}x−2x2−4​ to x+2x+2x+2 and wrote "x≠2x \neq 2x=2" beside it; you solved a rational equation and threw out the root that killed a denominator. Now that footnote becomes something you can point at. Graph a rational function and the excluded inputs are not bookkeeping any more: they are visible features. At one excluded input the curve has a single missing point, a hole you could cover with a pencil tip. At another the curve tears in half and rockets away toward the top or the bottom of the page along an invisible vertical line. Which of the two you get is decided by something you already know how to check: whether the guilty factor cancels.

Learning goals

  • Exclude the zeros of the original denominator
  • Define an asymptote as a line the curve stays near
  • Tell a hole from a vertical asymptote by whether the factor survives
  • Determine the horizontal or oblique asymptote by comparing degrees and dividing
  • Cross a horizontal asymptote freely, but never a vertical one

The simplest rational function, and what its graph does

A rational function is one polynomial divided by another,

f(x)=P(x)Q(x),f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)},f(x)=Q(x)P(x)​,

where PPP and QQQ are polynomials and QQQ is not the zero polynomial. Because division by zero has no value, the domain of fff is every real number except the zeros of QQQ.

Start with the smallest example there is, f(x)=1xf(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}f(x)=x1​, whose only excluded input is x=0x = 0x=0. A short table shows what happens as xxx creeps toward the forbidden value from the right:

xxx1110.50.50.50.10.10.10.010.010.010.0010.0010.001
1x\frac{1}{x}x1​111222101010100100100100010001000

The output has no ceiling. Name any height you like, say a million, and x=12,000,000x = \tfrac{1}{2{,}000{,}000}x=2,000,0001​ already beats it. Travel the other way instead, out toward large xxx, and the outputs shrink toward zero without ever reaching it: f(1000)=0.001f(1000) = 0.001f(1000)=0.001, and f(1,000,000)=0.000001f(1{,}000{,}000) = 0.000001f(1,000,000)=0.000001. Negative inputs behave the same way with the signs flipped.

Both dashed lines in that picture are asymptotes. Here is the honest definition, and it is the one we will use all lesson:

A line is an asymptote of a curve when the distance from the curve to that line becomes and stays smaller than any distance you care to name, without that distance ever settling on exactly zero. That happens once you travel far enough out along the curve.

“Smaller than any distance you care to name” is doing real work in that definition. That phrase is exactly the one a first course in calculus makes precise, with limits. We are not going to fake that machinery here. We do not need it: everything below is ordinary algebra, and it produces the right lines and the right picture. Naming the edge of your tools is not an apology, it is how you know what you have proved.

The definition covers all three kinds at once. The yyy-axis is an asymptote of y=1xy = \frac 1xy=x1​ because the points of the curve sit at horizontal distance ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ from the line x=0x = 0x=0. That horizontal distance can be made as small as you please while the curve runs off the top of the page. The xxx-axis is an asymptote because the vertical distance ∣1x∣\left|\frac 1x\right|​x1​​ can be made as small as you please by taking xxx far enough out.

Answer choices

The definition is about the far-out behavior of the distance between the curve and the line: name any distance, however small, and the curve eventually gets and stays closer than that, without the distance ever landing on zero for good. That leaves room for the curve to touch or cross the line a few times along the way (you will meet exactly that later in this lesson), which rules out the first option. It also rules out the last option: a curve that simply equals the line from some point on has distance zero forever, which is not "getting close," it is "being there," and that case does not count as an asymptote at all. Tangency at one point is a different idea entirely and has nothing to do with far-out behavior.

Holes and vertical asymptotes

Every excluded input comes from a zero of the original denominator, and there are exactly two things a graph can do at one. Compare these two functions, which are undefined at the same place, x=2x = 2x=2:

g(x)=x2−4x−2,h(x)=1x−2.g(x) = \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}, \qquad h(x) = \frac{1}{x - 2}.g(x)=x−2x2−4​,h(x)=x−21​.

For ggg, factor and cancel. Since x2−4=(x−2)(x+2)x^2 - 4 = (x-2)(x+2)x2−4=(x−2)(x+2),

g(x)=(x−2)(x+2)x−2=x+2for every x≠2.g(x) = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x + 2 \quad \text{for every } x \neq 2 .g(x)=x−2(x−2)(x+2)​=x+2for every x=2.

That cancellation is the move from the simplifying lesson, and its warning applies here in full. Cancelling preserves the value at every input where the original was defined, but it does not restore the input that was thrown away. So ggg agrees with the line y=x+2y = x+2y=x+2 everywhere except at x=2x=2x=2, where ggg has no value at all. Its graph is that line with one point removed: a hole.

For hhh nothing cancels, and the outputs behave like the table from 1x\frac 1xx1​. As xxx approaches 222 the denominator becomes tiny while the numerator stays 111, so the values blow up. The graph tears apart along the line x=2x = 2x=2, which is a vertical asymptote.

So the same excluded input can produce a missing dot or a cliff, and the deciding question is whether the offending factor cancels. Here is why, in plain terms: cancel every copy of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) that the numerator and denominator share, and look at what is left over.

If nothing of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) remains in the denominator, the simplified expression is an ordinary fraction at x=ax=ax=a: its denominator does not vanish there, so it settles on some ordinary height. The original function agrees with that simplified expression everywhere except at x=ax=ax=a itself (the original denominator really was zero there), so its graph is that ordinary curve with the single point above x=ax=ax=a removed. A hole.

If a copy of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) survives in the denominator, then as xxx approaches aaa that surviving factor shrinks toward 000 while the rest of the fraction settles on some fixed nonzero number. Dividing a number that does not shrink by one that does forces the whole fraction to grow without bound. The graph tears apart along x=ax=ax=a. A vertical asymptote.

The reasoning above works for any linear factor, but the general case allows repeated factors on both top and bottom, and asks exactly how many copies of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) have to cancel. The full argument proves the rule for every rational function at once, by comparing how many copies (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) contributes to the numerator against how many it contributes to the denominator.

See the full argument

Read the two cases again and notice what the dividing line actually is. It is not “does (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) appear in the numerator”, it is does the numerator carry at least as many copies of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) as the denominator. The factor must cancel completely. A single (x−1)(x-1)(x−1) upstairs against (x−1)2(x-1)^2(x−1)2 downstairs cancels one copy and leaves one behind, and one copy left in a denominator is all it takes.

f(x)=x+4(x−1)(x+2)(x≠1,  x≠−2),f(x) = \frac{x+4}{(x-1)(x+2)} \qquad (x \neq 1,\; x \neq -2),f(x)=(x−1)(x+2)x+4​(x=1,x=−2),

and a surviving (x−1)(x-1)(x−1) in the denominator means the values still blow up near x=1x=1x=1. So x=1x = 1x=1 is a vertical asymptote, not a hole, and so is x=−2x = -2x=−2. This function has no holes at all. Appearing in the numerator is not enough; the factor has to cancel completely.

End behavior: what the graph does far out

Vertical asymptotes tell you what happens near the bad points. End behavior is the other question: what does the curve do as you travel far to the right or far to the left? There is a three-case rule for this, and it is worth deriving rather than memorizing, because the derivation is one line and the rule falls out of it.

Take

f(x)=3x2+5x−12x2−7f(x) = \frac{3x^2 + 5x - 1}{2x^2 - 7}f(x)=2x2−73x2+5x−1​

and divide the numerator and the denominator by x2x^2x2, the highest power of xxx in the denominator. For x≠0x \neq 0x=0 this changes nothing about the value of the fraction:

f(x)=3+5x−1x2 2−7x2 .f(x) = \frac{3 + \dfrac{5}{x} - \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{\,2 - \dfrac{7}{x^2}\,} .f(x)=2−x27​3+x5​−x21​​.

Now every term that is not a leading coefficient has an xxx underneath it, and those terms are exactly the ones that die away when xxx is huge. At x=1000x = 1000x=1000 the numerator is 3+0.005−0.0000013 + 0.005 - 0.0000013+0.005−0.000001 and the denominator is 2−0.0000072 - 0.0000072−0.000007, so f(1000)≈3.0052.000≈1.5025f(1000) \approx \frac{3.005}{2.000} \approx 1.5025f(1000)≈2.0003.005​≈1.5025, already within three thousandths of 32\frac 3223​. Push xxx further out and the little terms shrink further. The curve has the horizontal asymptote y=32y = \frac 32y=23​, and notice where that number came from: the ratio of the leading coefficients, 32\frac{3}{2}23​, and nothing else.

The same division works in general. Given f(x)=P(x)Q(x)f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}f(x)=Q(x)P(x)​ with deg⁡P=m\deg P = mdegP=m and deg⁡Q=n\deg Q = ndegQ=n, divide top and bottom by xnx^nxn. The denominator turns into its leading coefficient plus a pile of terms with powers of xxx underneath them. For large ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ the denominator is as close to that leading coefficient as you like. Everything then depends on what the numerator turned into, and there are three possibilities.

The bottom outgrows the top (m<nm < nm<n). Dividing by xnx^nxn leaves every numerator term with a power of xxx underneath it, so the whole numerator dies away while the denominator settles on a nonzero number. For example

2x+1x2+3=2x+1x2 1+3x2 ,\frac{2x+1}{x^2+3} = \frac{\dfrac{2}{x} + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{\,1 + \dfrac{3}{x^2}\,},x2+32x+1​=1+x23​x2​+x21​​,

whose numerator shrinks to nothing and whose denominator heads for 111. The horizontal asymptote is y=0y = 0y=0, the xxx-axis.

The degrees tie (m=nm = nm=n). This is the case we just did. Both leading terms survive the division and everything else dies away, so the horizontal asymptote is y=aby = \frac{a}{b}y=ba​, where aaa and bbb are the leading coefficients of PPP and QQQ, provided QQQ does not divide PPP exactly. If it does, fff equals that constant everywhere it is defined, so the graph does not approach the line, it lies on it, with a hole at each input the cancelling threw away, the same exact-division exception you will meet again with oblique asymptotes.

The top outgrows the bottom (m>nm > nm>n). Dividing by xnx^nxn leaves the numerator with a positive power of xxx still in it, so the numerator grows without bound while the denominator settles down. For example

x3+1x2+1=x+1x2 1+1x2 ,\frac{x^3+1}{x^2+1} = \frac{x + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{\,1 + \dfrac{1}{x^2}\,},x2+1x3+1​=1+x21​x+x21​​,

which behaves like xxx itself for large xxx. There is no horizontal asymptote: no horizontal line can hold a curve that climbs forever. When the top outgrows the bottom by exactly one degree, as it does here, the curve is chased by a slanted line instead, with one exception the next section pins down. (Outgrow it by two degrees or more and there is no straight-line asymptote at all, but that case will not come up in this course.)

So the cancelling changes neither the comparison mmm against nnn nor the ratio ab\frac abba​. Oblique asymptotes, and why long division finds them

When the numerator’s degree is exactly one more than the denominator’s, the curve runs away from every horizontal line, and yet it is not wild. Far out, the curve hugs a slanted line. (Almost always. There is one exception, and we will corner it at the end of this section.) Finding that line needs no new tool. You already own it, from the polynomial division chapter.

Divide the polynomials. For

f(x)=x2+2x−1,f(x) = \frac{x^2 + 2}{x - 1},f(x)=x−1x2+2​,

long division gives a quotient of x+1x+1x+1 and a remainder of 333: the first step takes x(x−1)=x2−xx(x-1) = x^2-xx(x−1)=x2−x and leaves x+2x+2x+2, and the second takes 1⋅(x−1)=x−11 \cdot (x-1) = x-11⋅(x−1)=x−1 and leaves 333. Check it by multiplying back, which is the habit worth keeping: (x+1)(x−1)+3=x2−1+3=x2+2(x+1)(x-1) + 3 = x^2 - 1 + 3 = x^2 + 2(x+1)(x−1)+3=x2−1+3=x2+2, the numerator we started with. (The remainder theorem agrees, since the numerator at x=1x=1x=1 is 1+2=31 + 2 = 31+2=3.) Written the way division always lets you write it,

f(x)=x+1+3x−1.f(x) = x + 1 + \frac{3}{x-1} .f(x)=x+1+x−13​.

Read that as a sentence. The function is a line, y=x+1y = x+1y=x+1, plus a correction term 3x−1\frac{3}{x-1}x−13​. The correction is a proper fraction, because the remainder of a division always has smaller degree than the divisor. And a proper fraction is exactly the kind of thing that dies away far out. At x=101x = 101x=101 it is 3100=0.03\frac{3}{100} = 0.031003​=0.03; at x=1001x = 1001x=1001 it is 0.0030.0030.003. So for large xxx the curve sits a whisker above the line y=x+1y = x+1y=x+1 and settles onto it. That line is an oblique asymptote (also called a slant asymptote). The vertical distance from the curve to the line is precisely the correction term, so we can make that distance as small as we like by going far enough out. That is the definition, satisfied on the nose.

Polynomial long division, introduced two chapters ago as a tool for factoring and hunting for roots, turns out to read the shape of a graph straight off its formula. It splits a rational function into the part that matters far out (the quotient) and the part that vanishes far out (the remainder over the denominator).

And once you see it that way, the horizontal case is the same theorem. Divide x−3x−2\frac{x-3}{x-2}x−2x−3​ and you get a quotient of 111 with a remainder of −1-1−1:

x−3x−2=1−1x−2.\frac{x-3}{x-2} = 1 - \frac{1}{x-2} .x−2x−3​=1−x−21​.

A constant quotient is a horizontal asymptote, y=1y=1y=1. A linear quotient is an oblique asymptote. Same division, same reasoning, and the degree comparison from the last section is just a shortcut for predicting which one you are about to get.

One condition is doing quiet work in all of this, and it is worth dragging into the light: the remainder has to be nonzero. The whole argument runs on the correction term R(x)Q(x)\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}Q(x)R(x)​ being small but present. If the division comes out exact, so that RRR is zero, then fff is the quotient wherever it is defined, and there is nothing left for the curve to approach. A curve that lies exactly on a line is not said to have that line as an asymptote; the word is reserved for a line the curve gets close to without literally being it. Watch the exact-division case happen:

3x2+5x−2x+2=(3x−1)(x+2)x+2=3x−1(x≠−2).\frac{3x^2+5x-2}{x+2} = \frac{(3x-1)(x+2)}{x+2} = 3x-1 \qquad (x \neq -2).x+23x2+5x−2​=x+2(3x−1)(x+2)​=3x−1(x=−2).

The numerator’s degree is one more than the denominator’s, exactly the case that is supposed to produce an oblique asymptote, and yet this graph has none. It does not approach the line y=3x−1y=3x-1y=3x−1; it is that line, with a hole punched at (−2,−7)(-2,-7)(−2,−7). So state the rule with its hypothesis attached. When the numerator’s degree is one more than the denominator’s and the division leaves a nonzero remainder, the quotient is an oblique asymptote. When the remainder is zero, factor and enjoy your line. This is the same exception you will meet again in a moment when we ask whether a graph can cross a horizontal asymptote.

Can a graph cross an asymptote?

Students routinely picture an asymptote as a fence the curve is forbidden to touch. That belief is half right, and the half that is wrong causes real errors.

A graph can never cross a vertical asymptote. This one needs no argument beyond the domain. If x=ax=ax=a is a vertical asymptote then aaa is not in the domain, so the graph has no point whose first coordinate is aaa. There is nothing there to cross with.

A graph absolutely can cross a horizontal or an oblique asymptote, and often does. Look again at what the definition demands: the curve must approach the line far out. It says nothing whatever about the middle of the picture. A curve is free to cut straight through its horizontal asymptote near the origin and then come back and settle onto it.

Solving f(x)=cf(x)=cf(x)=c or f(x)=mx+bf(x)=mx+bf(x)=mx+b finds every point where the curve meets that line, and nothing more. A meeting point is not automatically a crossing: you confirm it is one by checking a value close on each side and seeing that the curve sits above the line on one side and below it on the other. Do that check for every example in this lesson and it comes out a crossing every time, but it is the check that tells you so, not the act of solving.

Here is one doing exactly that. Take

f(x)=2x2+x−1x2+1.f(x) = \frac{2x^2 + x - 1}{x^2 + 1} .f(x)=x2+12x2+x−1​.

The degrees tie, so the horizontal asymptote is y=21=2y = \frac 21 = 2y=12​=2. To find where the curve meets that line, set the function equal to 222 and solve, which is a rational equation of the kind you solved last lesson:

The curve genuinely switches sides, so it crosses its horizontal asymptote at the point (3,2)(3,2)(3,2), and then goes on approaching it from above for the rest of the journey.

Why doesn’t one crossing spoil the asymptote? Because the definition only makes a promise about far out, and it says nothing about the middle of the graph. A curve is free to cut through its horizontal or oblique asymptote a finite number of times near the origin and still settle onto it once you go far enough in either direction.

The full argument proves that a horizontal asymptote can be crossed only finitely many times, never crossed back and forth forever, because finding every crossing comes down to counting the roots of a polynomial.

See why the crossings run out

The same is true for an oblique asymptote. Since f(x)−(quotient)f(x) - (\text{quotient})f(x)−(quotient) equals the remainder over the denominator, the curve meets its oblique asymptote at any domain input where the remainder is zero; the same sign check as before then tells you whether that meeting is a crossing. So an oblique asymptote can be crossed too.

When the denominator is linear, the remainder is a constant, and a nonzero constant is never zero, so a curve like x2+2x−1\frac{x^2+2}{x-1}x−1x2+2​ never touches its slanted line. With a bigger denominator the remainder is free to have a root, so the curve can cross its oblique asymptote after all.

Putting it all together

Every tool is now on the table, so here is the whole sketch as one routine. Nothing in it is new.

  1. Factor the numerator and the denominator completely.
  2. Domain. Exclude every zero of the original denominator. Do this before you cancel anything.
  3. Holes and vertical asymptotes. Cancel the common factors. An excluded input whose factor cancelled completely is a hole; anything still left in the denominator is a vertical asymptote.
  4. Hole heights. Evaluate the simplified expression at each hole’s xxx.
  5. Intercepts. The xxx-intercepts are the zeros of the simplified numerator that survive in the domain; the yyy-intercept is f(0)f(0)f(0), if 000 is in the domain.
  6. End behavior. Compare the degrees for a horizontal asymptote, or divide for an oblique one.
  7. Crossings. Solve f(x)=cf(x) = cf(x)=c (or f(x)=mx+bf(x) = mx + bf(x)=mx+b) to see whether the curve meets that asymptote.
  8. Sign chart. Mark the xxx-intercepts and the vertical asymptotes on a number line (also mark a hole if its height is 000; the graph is still absent there, but the sign can flip across it just like it does across an intercept). Test one point in each interval. The sign can only change at those places, so one test point settles a whole interval, and the signs tell you which way each branch runs.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading the domain off the simplified form. The excluded inputs come from the original denominator. In (x−3)(x+2)(x−2)(x+2)\frac{(x-3)(x+2)}{(x-2)(x+2)}(x−2)(x+2)(x−3)(x+2)​ the value x=−2x=-2x=−2 is out of the domain forever, even though the simplified x−3x−2\frac{x-3}{x-2}x−2x−3​ shows no sign of it. Cancelling changes the formula, not the function.
  • Calling every zero of the denominator a vertical asymptote. A zero of the denominator is an excluded input, and that is all you know until you check whether its factor cancels. If it cancels completely you get a hole, not an asymptote.
  • Accepting a partial cancellation. One (x−1)(x-1)(x−1) upstairs against (x−1)2(x-1)^2(x−1)2 downstairs leaves a copy behind, so x=1x=1x=1 is still a vertical asymptote. Count the copies; the factor has to cancel completely.
  • Treating an asymptote as a fence. A graph may cross its horizontal or oblique asymptote. Solving f(x)=cf(x)=cf(x)=c (horizontal) or f(x)=mx+bf(x)=mx+bf(x)=mx+b (oblique) finds where the curve meets that line; checking the sign on each side of that point confirms it is really a crossing. Only a vertical asymptote can never be crossed, and that is because its xxx is not in the domain at all.
  • Reading the horizontal asymptote off the wrong terms. It comes from the leading coefficients, not the constants. The asymptote of 3x+82x+1\frac{3x+8}{2x+1}2x+13x+8​ is y=32y = \frac 32y=23​, not y=8y = 8y=8.
  • Expecting a horizontal asymptote when the top outgrows the bottom. If deg⁡P>deg⁡Q\deg P > \deg QdegP>degQ there is none. You get an oblique asymptote only in the single case deg⁡P=deg⁡Q+1\deg P = \deg Q + 1degP=degQ+1.
  • Drawing a hole as a solid dot, or forgetting it entirely. Mark it as an open circle. It is the one feature of the graph that a plotting program will usually hide from you.

Key takeaways

  • A rational function is f(x)=P(x)Q(x)f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}f(x)=Q(x)P(x)​ with PPP and QQQ polynomials. Its domain excludes exactly the zeros of the original QQQ.
  • A line is an asymptote when the distance from the curve to the line becomes and stays smaller than any distance you name, without ever settling on exactly zero, once you go far enough out. Making “arbitrarily close” precise is the business of calculus; the algebra here already finds the right lines.
  • At a zero aaa of the denominator: if (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) cancels completely, the graph has a hole at x=ax=ax=a, whose height is the simplified expression evaluated there. If any copy survives in the denominator, x=ax=ax=a is a vertical asymptote.
  • End behavior comes from dividing the top and the bottom by xdeg⁡Qx^{\deg Q}xdegQ, which kills every term but the leading ones. With deg⁡P=m\deg P = mdegP=m and deg⁡Q=n\deg Q = ndegQ=n: if m<nm<nm<n the asymptote is y=0y=0y=0; if m=nm=nm=n it is y=aby = \frac aby=ba​, the ratio of leading coefficients, unless QQQ divides PPP exactly, in which case the graph lies on that line instead of approaching it; if m>nm>nm>n there is no horizontal asymptote.
  • If m=n+1m = n+1m=n+1 and the division leaves a nonzero remainder, polynomial long division writes f(x)=(linear quotient)+remainderQ(x)f(x) = (\text{linear quotient}) + \frac{\text{remainder}}{Q(x)}f(x)=(linear quotient)+Q(x)remainder​. The correction term dies away far out, so the linear quotient is an oblique asymptote. If instead the division comes out exact, the graph is that line (with a hole at each excluded input, that is, each real zero of QQQ) and there is no asymptote. Nothing is left for the curve to approach.
  • A graph can cross a horizontal or an oblique asymptote, but it can never cross a vertical one, because that input is not in the domain. Solve f(x)=cf(x)=cf(x)=c for a horizontal asymptote, or f(x)=mx+bf(x)=mx+bf(x)=mx+b for an oblique one, to find where the curve meets that line, then check the sign on each side to confirm the meeting is a crossing.
  • To sketch: factor, take the domain, sort the holes from the vertical asymptotes, then find the intercepts. Settle the end behavior, check for crossings, and run a sign chart between the interesting xxx-values.

The full argument for why counting factors decides a hole or an asymptote

Why a factor that cancels leaves a hole, and one that survives forces a vertical asymptote#

Let f(x)=P(x)Q(x)f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}f(x)=Q(x)P(x)​ and let aaa be a zero of QQQ. Pull out the largest power of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) that divides each polynomial, writing

P(x)=(x−a)m p(x),Q(x)=(x−a)n q(x),P(x) = (x-a)^m\, p(x), \qquad Q(x) = (x-a)^n\, q(x),P(x)=(x−a)mp(x),Q(x)=(x−a)nq(x),

where n≥1n \ge 1n≥1 because aaa is a zero of QQQ, where m≥0m \ge 0m≥0, and where p(a)≠0p(a) \neq 0p(a)=0 and q(a)≠0q(a) \neq 0q(a)=0. Those last two facts are exactly what “largest power” buys us. If p(a)p(a)p(a) were zero then the factor theorem would hand us another copy of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) inside ppp, and we would not have pulled out the largest power after all.

For every xxx other than aaa we may cancel, and there are two outcomes.

Suppose first that m≥nm \ge nm≥n, so the numerator carries at least as many copies of (x−a)(x-a)(x−a) as the denominator. Cancelling all nnn of the denominator’s copies leaves

f(x)=(x−a)m−n p(x)q(x)(x≠a),f(x) = \frac{(x-a)^{m-n}\, p(x)}{q(x)} \qquad (x \neq a),f(x)=q(x)(x−a)m−np(x)​(x=a),

and the exponent m−nm-nm−n is now zero or positive. Why a crossing can only happen finitely many times

Solving f(x)=cf(x) = cf(x)=c turns into the polynomial equation P(x)−c Q(x)=0P(x) - c\,Q(x) = 0P(x)−cQ(x)=0, and there are only two ways that can go. Either P−cQP - cQP−cQ is the zero polynomial, in which case fff equals ccc at every input where it is defined. Then the graph does not approach that line, it lies on it (this is exactly what x−6x−6=1\frac{x-6}{x-6} = 1x−6x−6​=1 does, a horizontal line with a hole). Or P−cQP - cQP−cQ is a genuine nonzero polynomial, and then it has only finitely many roots, so the curve gets only finitely many chances to meet the line. Go far enough out and it has used them all up, after which it can only approach.

When an oblique asymptote can and cannot be crossed

Since f(x)−(quotient)f(x) - (\text{quotient})f(x)−(quotient) equals the remainder over the denominator, the curve meets its oblique asymptote at any domain input where the remainder is zero. When the denominator is linear the remainder is a constant, and a nonzero constant is never zero, so those curves never touch their slanted line. That is why the graph of x2+2x−1\frac{x^2+2}{x-1}x−1x2+2​ stays strictly above y=x+1y = x+1y=x+1 on the right. But with a bigger denominator the remainder is free to have a root. Dividing x3+1x^3+1x3+1 by x2+1x^2+1x2+1 gives

x3+1x2+1=x+1−xx2+1,\frac{x^3+1}{x^2+1} = x + \frac{1-x}{x^2+1},x2+1x3+1​=x+x2+11−x​,

whose remainder 1−x1-x1−x vanishes at x=1x=1x=1, an input the domain allows since x2+1x^2+1x2+1 is never zero. Confirm it is a crossing, not just a touch, the same way as before: at x=0x=0x=0 the correction 1−xx2+1\frac{1-x}{x^2+1}x2+11−x​ is 111, positive, and at x=2x=2x=2 it is −15\frac{-1}{5}5−1​, negative, so the curve really does switch sides. It crosses its oblique asymptote y=xy=xy=x at the point (1,1)(1,1)(1,1). Never say never.

A bit of history (optional)

A curve that closes on a line forever, without ever arriving, is odd enough to deserve a name. The Greeks gave it one, and the name is an argument.

Asymptote comes from the Greek asumptotos. It is built from a- (not), sun- (together) and ptotos (falling). Not falling together. Not meeting, ever.

The curve it was invented for was the hyperbola. The geometer was Apollonius of Perga, who worked in the Greek world around 200 BCE. He studied the two straight lines a hyperbola chases forever. With no algebra whatever, he proved this about them: the gap can be forced below any distance you name, once you travel far enough out. That shrinking-gap idea is the heart of the definition this lesson used; our definition also has to rule out a curve that simply sits on the line forever, but that case never came up for his hyperbolas anyway, since they do something even stronger. His hyperbolas’ gap never once closed to zero, not even a single time, which is more than the definition demands.

An irony is buried in the name. Apollonius chose not meeting because his own hyperbolas never did meet those lines. Later mathematicians studied wider families of curves, and the promise broke. The graph of 2x2+x−1x2+1\frac{2x^2+x-1}{x^2+1}x2+12x2+x−1​ walks straight through its horizontal asymptote at (3,2)(3,2)(3,2), then carries on approaching it.

The modern definition kept the approaching and quietly dropped the avoiding. One kind of asymptote still honours the old promise, though not for the reason the name suggests. A vertical asymptote cannot be crossed, because its input is not in the domain at all.

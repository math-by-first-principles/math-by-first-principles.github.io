    2. A product that is not there until you make it . Foundational, 12 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    A rational expression can be neither reduced nor certified as already reduced while its two halves are sums. The first job is always to turn them into products. The second, which is the one people skip, is to be sure that what survives really has nothing left to give.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Simplify x3−5x2+4x−20x2−25\dfrac{x^3-5x^2+4x-20}{x^2-25}x2−25x3−5x2+4x−20​ completely, and state the domain of the expression exactly as it was printed.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Decide whether the expression you reached in part A is in lowest terms. Support the verdict with an argument that would work whatever the numbers happened to be, rather than with the observation that the two halves do not look alike.

      Carry your own answer forward Argue about whatever reduced form your part A came to. If part A did not come out, factor the printed numerator and denominator as far as you can get them and argue about those instead: the credit here is for the test you apply, not for the expression you apply it to.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A classmate settles every lowest-terms question by hunting for a real number at which the numerator and the denominator vanish together, and reports lowest terms whenever there is none.     Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Neither half of the printed expression is a product yet, and until both are there is nothing to divide out and nothing to certify. Pair the numerator's terms and see whether the two pairs leave the same bracket behind.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      A linear factor announces itself as a real zero. So ask what real numbers can make the top of your reduced form vanish, and let the discriminant answer that in one line.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask whether two polynomials can share a factor that no real number is a zero of. If they can, the classmate's hunt would come up empty on an expression that still reduces.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    x2+4x+5\dfrac{x^2+4}{x+5}x+5x2+4​, and the printed expression is defined at every real number except 555 and −5-5−5.

    Part B

    It is in lowest terms. Its numerator has a negative discriminant, so no real number is a zero of it, and by the Factor Theorem no factor x−rx-rx−r with rrr real divides it. The denominator is such a factor, so the two share nothing non-constant.

    Part C

    The search settles LINEAR common factors and nothing else: the two halves share a factor x−rx-rx−r with rrr real exactly when both vanish at rrr. In x2+9x4−81\dfrac{x^2+9}{x^4-81}x4−81x2+9​ the numerator has no real zero at all, so the search comes up empty, yet x4−81=(x2+9)(x−3)(x+3)x^4-81 = (x^2+9)(x-3)(x+3)x4−81=(x2+9)(x−3)(x+3) and the expression reduces.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The numerator has four terms, which is the signal to pair them off and take a common factor out of each pair:

    x3−5x2+4x−20=x2(x−5)+4(x−5)=(x−5)(x2+4)x^3-5x^2+4x-20 = x^2(x-5) + 4(x-5) = (x-5)(x^2+4)x3−5x2+4x−20=x2(x−5)+4(x−5)=(x−5)(x2+4)

    The pairing was worth doing because both pairs left the same bracket behind. The denominator is a difference of squares:

    x2−25=(x−5)(x+5)x^2-25 = (x-5)(x+5)x2−25=(x−5)(x+5)

    Read the excluded values from that denominator before cancelling: it is zero at x=5x=5x=5 and at x=−5x=-5x=−5, so the domain is every real number apart from those two.

    On that domain the shared factor x−5x-5x−5 is nonzero, so it divides out:

    (x−5)(x2+4)(x−5)(x+5)=x2+4x+5(x≠5 and x≠−5)\frac{(x-5)(x^2+4)}{(x-5)(x+5)} = \frac{x^2+4}{x+5} \qquad (x \neq 5 \text{ and } x \neq -5)(x−5)(x+5)(x−5)(x2+4)​=x+5x2+4​(x=5 and x=−5)

    Check at x=0x=0x=0: the printed expression gives −20−25=45\frac{-20}{-25} = \frac{4}{5}−25−20​=54​, and the reduced one gives 45\frac{4}{5}54​ as well.

    Part B

    A quadratic and a linear polynomial can share a non-constant factor in only one way: the linear one has to divide the quadratic. So the question is whether x+5x+5x+5 is a factor of x2+4x^2+4x2+4, and the Factor Theorem turns that into an evaluation.

    The factor x+5x+5x+5 is x−(−5)x-(-5)x−(−5), so it divides x2+4x^2+4x2+4 exactly when x2+4x^2+4x2+4 vanishes at −5-5−5:

    (−5)2+4=29≠0(-5)^2+4 = 29 \neq 0(−5)2+4=29=0

    It does not, so x+5x+5x+5 is not a factor, and the expression is in lowest terms.

    The stronger argument settles every linear factor at once instead of one at a time. The discriminant of x2+0x+4x^2+0x+4x2+0x+4 is

    02−4(1)(4)=−16<00^2-4(1)(4) = -16 < 002−4(1)(4)=−16<0

    so x2+4x^2+4x2+4 has no real zero whatever, and by the Factor Theorem it therefore has no factor x−rx-rx−r with rrr real at all. That covers x+5x+5x+5 and every other linear polynomial in one line, and it is the argument that survives a change of numbers: any numerator with a negative discriminant is safe from every linear denominator.

    Part C

    Take the two directions of the search's claim separately, because only one of them holds in general.

    What it does settle. If x−rx-rx−r with rrr real divides both halves, then by the Factor Theorem both halves vanish at rrr, so the search finds rrr. Conversely, if both vanish at rrr, the Factor Theorem hands back x−rx-rx−r as a factor of each. So the search is exactly a test for a shared LINEAR factor with a real root, and on that question it is complete.

    What it does not settle. A common factor need not be linear, and a non-linear one need not have any real zero to be found. Take

    x2+9x4−81\frac{x^2+9}{x^4-81}x4−81x2+9​

    The numerator has no real zero, so no number vanishes on both halves and the search reports lowest terms. But the denominator is a difference of squares:

    x4−81=(x2−9)(x2+9)x^4-81 = (x^2-9)(x^2+9)x4−81=(x2−9)(x2+9)

    so the numerator is a factor of the denominator outright, and the expression reduces to 1x2−9\frac{1}{x^2-9}x2−91​, valid wherever the printed expression was defined, which is every real number except 333 and −3-3−3.

    The honest version of the classmate's rule is therefore narrower than they think: no shared real zero means no shared linear factor, and to rule out the rest you have to factor both halves completely and compare the factors themselves.

    In one line

    x3−5x2+4x−20x2−25=x2+4x+5\dfrac{x^3-5x^2+4x-20}{x^2-25} = \dfrac{x^2+4}{x+5}x2−25x3−5x2+4x−20​=x+5x2+4​ for every real number except 555 and −5-5−5, and that is lowest terms, because x2+4x^2+4x2+4 has discriminant −16-16−16 and so no real zero, which by the Factor Theorem leaves it with no linear factor at all.     Another way: Peel the numerator with the Factor Theorem instead of by grouping

    Grouping worked here because the four terms paired neatly, which is not guaranteed. The Factor Theorem gets at the same factorization without relying on that. The printed denominator is zero at 555, so x−5x-5x−5 is the factor worth testing, and

    53−5(5)2+4(5)−20=125−125+20−20=05^3-5(5)^2+4(5)-20 = 125-125+20-20 = 053−5(5)2+4(5)−20=125−125+20−20=0

    So x−5x-5x−5 divides the numerator, and dividing (synthetic division on the coefficients 1,−5,4,−201, -5, 4, -201,−5,4,−20 is quickest) leaves the quotient x2+0x+4x^2+0x+4x2+0x+4 with remainder 000, giving (x−5)(x2+4)(x-5)(x^2+4)(x−5)(x2+4) again.

    When it is worth it When the four terms refuse to pair, or when the numerator has more terms than grouping can handle. It also aims the work: the only factors worth testing in the numerator are the ones the denominator already has.

    The same argument read backwards is the useful form: if ddd did vanish at some x0x_0x0​, then the denominator B(x0) d(x0)B(x_0)\,d(x_0)B(x0​)d(x0​) would be zero as well, and x0x_0x0​ would have been outside the domain from the start. The zeros of a cancelled factor were never allowed inputs.

    Part B

    Look at where the classmate's condition would have to come from. The printed denominator is (3x+2)(x−4)(3x+2)(x-4)(3x+2)(x−4), and it is zero when either factor is:

    3x+2=0  ⇒  x=−23,x−4=0  ⇒  x=43x+2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = -\frac{2}{3}, \qquad x-4 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 43x+2=0⇒x=−32​,x−4=0⇒x=4

    So the domain of the printed expression is every real number except −23-\frac{2}{3}−32​ and 444, and it was that before a single factor moved. The classmate's condition is one of those two, not a third: nothing has been added.

    Part A says this can never go the other way. A cancelled factor is a factor of the denominator, so its zeros are zeros of the denominator, so they are already excluded. The cancelling hypothesis is therefore never new information about which inputs are allowed.

    But the classmate is right about the writing, for a different reason than they give. After the cancelling the page reads x+5x−4\frac{x+5}{x-4}x−4x+5​, which is perfectly happy at x=−23x=-\frac{2}{3}x=−32​:

    −23+5−23−4=13/3−14/3=−1314\frac{-\frac{2}{3}+5}{-\frac{2}{3}-4} = \frac{13/3}{-14/3} = -\frac{13}{14}−32​−4−32​+5​=−14/313/3​=−1413​

    The printed expression has no value there, so the two are not the same object, and only the written restriction records the difference. The condition is old news about the domain and urgent news about the answer.

    Part C

    The restriction disappears from view when nothing left on the page objects to it, so to keep it visible the cancelled factor has to leave a copy of itself in the denominator. That happens when it appears more often below the bar than above it.

    Take

    x2+7xx2=x(x+7)x⋅x\frac{x^2+7x}{x^2} = \frac{x(x+7)}{x \cdot x}x2x2+7x​=x⋅xx(x+7)​

    The printed denominator is zero only at x=0x=0x=0, so that is the only excluded input. The factor xxx is common to both halves and divides out once:

    x(x+7)x⋅x=x+7x(x≠0)\frac{x(x+7)}{x \cdot x} = \frac{x+7}{x} \qquad (x \neq 0)x⋅xx(x+7)​=xx+7​(x=0)

    The reduced form still has xxx underneath, so it refuses x=0x=0x=0 on its own account. Every excluded input of the printed expression is still refused by the answer, and a reader who never saw the question would rule out exactly the right number.

    The general statement behind the example is a comparison of counts. If a factor appears mmm times above the bar and nnn times below, cancelling leaves n−mn-mn−m copies below when n>mn > mn>m, so any restriction that factor imposed is still enforced by the answer itself. When m≥nm \ge nm≥n no copy survives, and a restriction it imposed is left with nothing on the page to enforce it. In this expression m=1m=1m=1 and n=2n=2n=2.

    So the claim is false as stated, and the true version needs two conditions rather than one. A cancelled factor costs a restriction its visibility only if it had a real zero to forbid in the first place, and the cancelling exhausted it from the denominator. Fail either condition and nothing is lost: x2+7xx2\frac{x^2+7x}{x^2}x2x2+7x​ fails the second, and (x2+9)(x+1)(x2+9)(x−2)\frac{(x^2+9)(x+1)}{(x^2+9)(x-2)}(x2+9)(x−2)(x2+9)(x+1)​ fails the first, since x2+9x^2+9x2+9 has no real zero and so forbade nothing for the answer to forget.

    In one line

    If x0x_0x0​ lies in the domain of A(x) d(x)B(x) d(x)\dfrac{A(x)\,d(x)}{B(x)\,d(x)}B(x)d(x)A(x)d(x)​ then B(x0) d(x0)≠0B(x_0)\,d(x_0) \neq 0B(x0​)d(x0​)=0, and a product of real numbers is nonzero only when both factors are, so d(x0)≠0d(x_0) \neq 0d(x0​)=0 and the cancellation law applies at every input the expression accepts. The classmate's condition is therefore never an addition: x=−23x = -\dfrac{2}{3}x=−32​ was already excluded by (3x+2)(x−4)(3x+2)(x-4)(3x+2)(x−4), alongside x=4x=4x=4. It must still be written beside x+5x−4\dfrac{x+5}{x-4}x−4x+5​, which accepts it. And cancelling does not always cost visibility: in x2+7xx2=x+7x\dfrac{x^2+7x}{x^2} = \dfrac{x+7}{x}x2x2+7x​=xx+7​ the factor xxx divides out once and a copy survives underneath, so the answer still refuses x=0x=0x=0.

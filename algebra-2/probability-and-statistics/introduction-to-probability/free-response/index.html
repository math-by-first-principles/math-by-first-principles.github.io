  1. Problem 1 The overlapping groups

    One of the 50 outcomes represented in the figure is selected with equal probability. Find P(A∩B)P(A\cap B)P(A∩B).

    Text description of this figure

    A rectangle labeled S contains two overlapping circles, A on the left drawn solid and B on the right drawn dashed. Each region holds a count of outcomes: 15 in the part of A that is outside B, 5 in the lens where A and B overlap, 10 in the part of B that is outside A, and 20 in the corner of the rectangle outside both circles. No probability is written.

    1. Hint 1

      The intersection consists of outcomes belonging to both events.

    2. Hint 2

      Read the overlap count and divide by the total count in the sample space.

    Answer

    110\frac{1}{10}101​.

    Full solution

    The overlap contains 555 outcomes out of 505050 equally likely outcomes.

    Thus

    P(A∩B)=550P(A∩B)=110.\begin{aligned}P(A\cap B)&=\frac5{50} \\ P(A\cap B)&=\frac1{10}.\end{aligned}P(A∩B)P(A∩B)​=505​=101​.​

    The four regions sum to 15+5+10+20=5015+5+10+20=5015+5+10+20=50, confirming the total.

    Answer

    110\frac{1}{10}101​.

    Key idea

    An intersection contains the outcomes shared by both events.

  2. Problem 2 Two equally likely events

    Events A and B have equal probability. Their intersection has one-fourth the probability of A, and their union has probability 0.70.70.7. Find P(A)P(A)P(A).

    1. Hint 1

      Adding the two event probabilities counts their overlap twice.

    2. Hint 2

      Write both event probabilities using one variable, and express the overlap as a fraction of that variable.

    Answer

    P(A)=0.4P(A)=0.4P(A)=0.4.

    Full solution

    Let ppp be the common value of P(A)P(A)P(A) and P(B)P(B)P(B).

    The intersection then has probability p/4p/4p/4.

    Subtracting the double-counted overlap gives

    2p−p4=0.7.2p-\frac p4=0.7.2p−4p​=0.7.

    Thus (7/4)p=0.7(7/4)p=0.7(7/4)p=0.7, so p=0.4p=0.4p=0.4.

    The overlap is 0.10.10.1, and the union checks as

    0.4+0.4−0.1=0.7.0.4+0.4-0.1=0.7.0.4+0.4−0.1=0.7.

    Answer

    P(A)=0.4P(A)=0.4P(A)=0.4.

    Key idea

    Relations among event probabilities can be combined with the overlap correction to determine an unknown probability.

  3. Problem 3 An event share

    You know P(B)>0P(B)>0P(B)>0, P(A∣B)=0.30P(A\mid B)=0.30P(A∣B)=0.30, and P(A∩B)=0.15P(A\cap B)=0.15P(A∩B)=0.15. Find P(B)P(B)P(B).

    1. Hint 1

      The conditional probability measures the fraction of B that also belongs to A.

    2. Hint 2

      Use P(A∩B)=P(B)P(A∣B)P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)P(A∩B)=P(B)P(A∣B) and solve for the missing probability.

    Answer

    P(B)=0.50P(B)=0.50P(B)=0.50.

    Full solution

    The multiplication rule gives

    0.15=0.30P(B).0.15=0.30P(B).0.15=0.30P(B).

    Divide by the nonzero number 0.300.300.30:

    P(B)=0.50.P(B)=0.50.P(B)=0.50.

    Then 0.15/0.50=0.300.15/0.50=0.300.15/0.50=0.30, checking the conditional probability.

    Answer

    P(B)=0.50P(B)=0.50P(B)=0.50.

    Key idea

    A joint probability and a nonzero conditional probability determine the conditioning event's probability.

  4. Problem 4 Two backup devices

    Two backup devices work independently. The first works with probability 0.80.80.8, and the second with probability 0.60.60.6. A task succeeds if at least one device works. Find its success probability.

    1. Hint 1

      The task fails exactly when both devices fail.

    2. Hint 2

      Independence also lets you multiply the two failure probabilities.

    Answer

    0.920.920.92.

    Full solution

    The failure probabilities are 0.20.20.2 and 0.40.40.4.

    Independence gives

    P(both fail)=0.2(0.4)P(both fail)=0.08.\begin{aligned}P(\text{both fail})&=0.2(0.4) \\ P(\text{both fail})&=0.08.\end{aligned}P(both fail)P(both fail)​=0.2(0.4)=0.08.​

    Taking the complement,

    P(success)=1−0.08P(success)=0.92.\begin{aligned}P(\text{success})&=1-0.08 \\ P(\text{success})&=0.92.\end{aligned}P(success)P(success)​=1−0.08=0.92.​

    The direct disjoint cases, first works or first fails and second works, give 0.8+0.2(0.6)=0.920.8+0.2(0.6)=0.920.8+0.2(0.6)=0.92 as a check.

    Answer

    0.920.920.92.

    Key idea

    Independent devices can have different probabilities, and total failure is the complement of at least one success.

  5. Problem 5 Two chosen letters

    Two different letters are chosen in order from A, E, B, and C, with every ordered pair equally likely. Let J mean the first letter is A, and let V mean the second is a vowel, A or E. Find P(V∣J)P(V\mid J)P(V∣J) and P(J∩V)P(J\cap V)P(J∩V), and determine whether J and V are independent.

    1. Hint 1

      Once the first letter is known, remove it from the available second letters.

    2. Hint 2

      Compare the conditional chance of V after J with its original chance, or compare the joint probability with the product.

    Answer

    P(V∣J)=13P(V\mid J)=\frac13P(V∣J)=31​; P(J∩V)=112P(J\cap V)=\frac1{12}P(J∩V)=121​; J and V are dependent.

    Full solution

    Given J, the second letter is equally likely to be E, B, or C, with one vowel, so

    P(V∣J)=13.P(V\mid J)=\frac13.P(V∣J)=31​.

    The first letter is A with probability 14\frac1441​, so

    P(J∩V)=14⋅13P(J∩V)=112.\begin{aligned}P(J\cap V)&=\frac14\cdot\frac13 \\ P(J\cap V)&=\frac1{12}.\end{aligned}P(J∩V)P(J∩V)​=41​⋅31​=121​.​

    This matches the single qualifying pair AE among the 121212 ordered pairs.

    Across all ordered pairs, each letter is equally likely in the second position, giving

    P(V)=12.P(V)=\frac12.P(V)=21​.

    The conditional probability 13\frac1331​ differs from it, and P(J)P(V)=18P(J)P(V)=\frac18P(J)P(V)=81​ differs from 112\frac1{12}121​, so the events are dependent.

    Answer

    P(V∣J)=13P(V\mid J)=\frac13P(V∣J)=31​; P(J∩V)=112P(J\cap V)=\frac1{12}P(J∩V)=121​; J and V are dependent.

    Key idea

    Removing a specific object can change the probability of a broader category on the next choice.

  6. Problem 6 Downloaded audio

    For a randomly chosen file, 40%40\%40% are audio and 25%25\%25% are downloaded. Every downloaded file is audio. Find the probability a downloaded file is audio and the probability an audio file is downloaded.

    1. Hint 1

      The downloaded event is a subset of the audio event.

    2. Hint 2

      The intersection is the downloaded event; divide it by the probability of each conditioning event in turn.

    Answer

    Audio given downloaded: 111; downloaded given audio: 58\frac5885​.

    Full solution

    Let A mean audio and D mean downloaded.

    Every downloaded file is audio, so P(A∩D)=0.25P(A\cap D)=0.25P(A∩D)=0.25, and both conditioning probabilities are positive.

    Thus

    P(A∣D)=0.250.25P(A∣D)=1.\begin{aligned}P(A\mid D)&=\frac{0.25}{0.25} \\ P(A\mid D)&=1.\end{aligned}P(A∣D)P(A∣D)​=0.250.25​=1.​

    Reversing the condition gives

    P(D∣A)=0.250.40P(D∣A)=58.\begin{aligned}P(D\mid A)&=\frac{0.25}{0.40} \\ P(D\mid A)&=\frac58.\end{aligned}P(D∣A)P(D∣A)​=0.400.25​=85​.​

    The two conditions refer to different sample spaces.

    Answer

    Audio given downloaded: 111; downloaded given audio: 58\frac5885​.

    Key idea

    Reversing a conditional probability changes which event supplies the denominator.

  7. Problem 7 A survey reconstruction

    Of 80 visitors, 40 tried activity A, 30 tried activity B, and 20 tried neither. A visitor is selected uniformly at random. Find the probability that the visitor tried B given that the visitor tried A.

    1. Hint 1

      First recover the number who tried at least one activity.

    2. Hint 2

      The two activity counts exceed that total by the number who tried both.

    Answer

    14\frac1441​.

    Full solution

    The union contains 80−20=6080-20=6080−20=60 visitors.

    Adding the activity counts counts the overlap twice, so the overlap is

    40+30−60=10.40+30-60=10.40+30−60=10.

    Among the 404040 who tried A, 101010 also tried B.

    Therefore

    P(B∣A)=1040P(B∣A)=14.\begin{aligned}P(B\mid A)&=\frac{10}{40} \\ P(B\mid A)&=\frac14.\end{aligned}P(B∣A)P(B∣A)​=4010​=41​.​

    The regions are 303030 A only, 101010 both, 202020 B only, and 202020 neither, totaling 808080.

    Answer

    14\frac1441​.

    Key idea

    Recover the overlap before restricting a probability to one event.

  8. Problem 8 An event and its opposite

    An event A has probability 0.20.20.2. A student says A and its complement are independent because they are different events. Is this correct? Justify your answer numerically.

    1. Hint 1

      An event and its complement cannot occur together.

    2. Hint 2

      Compare their joint probability with the product of their separate probabilities.

    Answer

    No; A and its complement are dependent: P(A∩Ac)=0P(A\cap A^c)=0P(A∩Ac)=0, whereas P(A)P(Ac)=0.16P(A)P(A^c)=0.16P(A)P(Ac)=0.16.

    Full solution

    The complement has probability 0.80.80.8, and the two events have no common outcomes.

    Thus P(A∩Ac)=0P(A\cap A^c)=0P(A∩Ac)=0, while

    P(A)P(Ac)=0.2(0.8)P(A)P(Ac)=0.16.\begin{aligned}P(A)P(A^c)&=0.2(0.8) \\ P(A)P(A^c)&=0.16.\end{aligned}P(A)P(Ac)P(A)P(Ac)​=0.2(0.8)=0.16.​

    The two values differ, so independence fails.

    In fact, knowing that one happened rules out the other.

    Answer

    No; A and its complement are dependent: P(A∩Ac)=0P(A\cap A^c)=0P(A∩Ac)=0, whereas P(A)P(Ac)=0.16P(A)P(A^c)=0.16P(A)P(Ac)=0.16.

    Key idea

    An event with probability strictly between zero and one is dependent on its complement.

  9. Problem 9 Enough information?

    Events A and B satisfy P(A)=4/11P(A)=4/11P(A)=4/11 and P(B)=6/11P(B)=6/11P(B)=6/11. Do these probabilities determine whether the events are independent? Give two possible intersection probabilities, one making the events independent and one making them dependent.

    1. Hint 1

      Independence depends on the intersection as well as on the individual probabilities.

    2. Hint 2

      Find the overlap independence requires, then look for a different overlap compatible with both events.

    Answer

    No. An intersection probability of 24121\frac{24}{121}12124​ gives independence; an intersection probability of 000 gives dependence.

    Full solution

    Independence requires P(A∩B)=411⋅611=24121P(A\cap B)=\frac4{11}\cdot\frac6{11}=\frac{24}{121}P(A∩B)=114​⋅116​=12124​.

    This is possible: among 121121121 equally likely outcomes, put 242424 in both events, 202020 in A only, 424242 in B only and 353535 in neither, so that A has 444444 and B has 666666 outcomes.

    Dependence is also possible: among 111111 equally likely outcomes, put 444 in A only, 666 in B only and 111 in neither.

    The intersection then has probability 000, which differs from the product, so the events are dependent.

    Answer

    No. An intersection probability of 24121\frac{24}{121}12124​ gives independence; an intersection probability of 000 gives dependence.

    Key idea

    Unless an event has probability 0 or 1, the two events' probabilities alone do not decide whether they are independent.

  10. Problem 10 A two-stage selection

    Three boxes contain 1 red object, 2 blue objects, and 3 green objects, respectively. A box is selected uniformly, then an object is selected uniformly from that box. A student says the red object has probability 1/61/61/6 because there are six objects. Is this correct? Find its actual selection probability.

    1. Hint 1

      Equal probabilities for boxes do not imply equal probabilities for all individual objects.

    2. Hint 2

      For the red object to be selected, first its box must be selected; that box offers one possible object.

    Answer

    No; the red object has probability 13\frac1331​.

    Full solution

    The red box is selected with probability 1/31/31/3.

    Given that box, the red object is certain, so

    P(red object)=13⋅1P(red object)=13.\begin{aligned}P(\text{red object})&=\frac13\cdot1 \\ P(\text{red object})&=\frac13.\end{aligned}P(red object)P(red object)​=31​⋅1=31​.​

    Each blue object instead has probability 1/61/61/6, and each green object 1/91/91/9.

    The six objects are not equally likely, so dividing by six was invalid.

    Answer

    No; the red object has probability 13\frac1331​.

    Key idea

    A uniform choice at each stage does not necessarily make all final objects equally likely.

    One card is drawn from a standard deck. What is the probability that it is a king or a heart?

    Answer choices for question 1

    Solution

    Correct answer: B

    There are 444 kings and 131313 hearts, and one card, the king of hearts, is both. The addition rule removes that double count.

    P(king∪heart)=452+1352−152=1652=413P(\text{king} \cup \text{heart}) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} - \frac{1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13}P(king∪heart)=524​+5213​−521​=5216​=134​

    The value 1752\frac{17}{52}5217​ counts the king of hearts twice, once as a king and once as a heart.

    Events AAA and BBB are mutually exclusive, with P(A)=0.25P(A) = 0.25P(A)=0.25 and P(A∪B)=0.7P(A \cup B) = 0.7P(A∪B)=0.7. What is P(B)P(B)P(B)?

    Answer choices for question 2

    Solution

    Correct answer: C

    Mutual exclusivity kills the correction term, since P(A∩B)=0P(A \cap B) = 0P(A∩B)=0.

    P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)

    Substitute and solve.

    0.7=0.25+P(B)⟹P(B)=0.450.7 = 0.25 + P(B) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad P(B) = 0.450.7=0.25+P(B)⟹P(B)=0.45

    The value 0.1750.1750.175 is the product 0.25×0.70.25 \times 0.70.25×0.7, which answers nothing here, and 0.950.950.95 comes from adding instead of subtracting.

  3. 3

    For two events, P(A∣B)=0.4P(A \mid B) = 0.4P(A∣B)=0.4 and P(B)=0.5P(B) = 0.5P(B)=0.5. What is P(A∩B)P(A \cap B)P(A∩B)?

    Answer choices for question 3

    Solution

    Correct answer: B

    Multiply both sides of the conditional definition by P(B)P(B)P(B) to get the multiplication rule.

    P(A∩B)=P(B)⋅P(A∣B)P(A \cap B) = P(B) \cdot P(A \mid B)P(A∩B)=P(B)⋅P(A∣B)

    Substitute the given values.

    P(A∩B)=0.5×0.4=0.2P(A \cap B) = 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2P(A∩B)=0.5×0.4=0.2

    Dividing instead of multiplying gives 0.80.80.8, which is the reversed operation and would make the intersection larger than P(B)P(B)P(B), an impossibility since A∩B⊆BA \cap B \subseteq BA∩B⊆B.

  4. 4

    Two fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that the product of the two numbers is even?

    Answer choices for question 4

    Solution

    Correct answer: B

    A product is even as soon as at least one factor is even, so the complement is the tidy event: the product is odd exactly when both dice are odd.

    P(both odd)=3×336=936=14P(\text{both odd}) = \frac{3 \times 3}{36} = \frac{9}{36} = \frac{1}{4}P(both odd)=363×3​=369​=41​

    Subtract from 111.

    P(product even)=1−14=34P(\text{product even}) = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}P(product even)=1−41​=43​

    Guessing 12\frac{1}{2}21​ assumes even and odd products are equally likely, but three of the four parity combinations produce an even product.

    In a room of 232323 people, what is the probability that at least two of them share a birthday (assuming 365365365 equally likely birthdays)?

    Answer choices for question 5

    Solution

    Correct answer: D

    Count the complement, where all 232323 birthdays are different. Assign days one person at a time, each new person avoiding the days already used.

    P(all different)=365×364×⋯×34336523≈0.4927P(\text{all different}) = \frac{365 \times 364 \times \cdots \times 343}{365^{23}} \approx 0.4927P(all different)=36523365×364×⋯×343​≈0.4927

    Subtract from 111.

    P(at least two share)≈1−0.4927=0.5073P(\text{at least two share}) \approx 1 - 0.4927 = 0.5073P(at least two share)≈1−0.4927=0.5073

    The value near 0.060.060.06 answers a different question, the chance that someone shares your birthday, which really is small. The room has (232)=253\binom{23}{2} = 253(223​)=253 pairs of people, not 222222, and that is why the answer is so much larger than it feels.

    A bag holds 444 red, 555 blue, and 333 green marbles. Two are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that the two marbles are different colours?

    Answer choices for question 6

    Solution

    Correct answer: A

    "Different colours" is the complement of "the same colour", which was 1966\frac{19}{66}6619​.

    P(different)=1−1966=4766≈0.712P(\text{different}) = 1 - \frac{19}{66} = \frac{47}{66} \approx 0.712P(different)=1−6619​=6647​≈0.712

    Counting head on would mean three separate cases (red with blue, red with green, blue with green), giving 4⋅5+4⋅3+5⋅3=474 \cdot 5 + 4 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 3 = 474⋅5+4⋅3+5⋅3=47 pairs, the same answer with three times the work.

    A committee of 444 is chosen at random from 555 men and 444 women. What is the probability that it contains exactly 222 women?

    Answer choices for question 7

    Solution

    Correct answer: C

    The sample space is every four-person committee drawn from the nine people.

    ∣S∣=(94)=126\lvert S \rvert = \binom{9}{4} = 126∣S∣=(49​)=126

  8. 8

    Two fair dice are rolled. Given that the sum is at least 101010, what is the probability that the sum is exactly 121212?

    Answer choices for question 8

    Solution

    Correct answer: A

    Conditioning shrinks the sample space to the six rolls whose sum is at least 101010.

    B={(4,6),(5,5),(6,4),(5,6),(6,5),(6,6)},∣B∣=6B = \{(4,6), (5,5), (6,4), (5,6), (6,5), (6,6)\}, \qquad \lvert B \rvert = 6B={(4,6),(5,5),(6,4),(5,6),(6,5),(6,6)},∣B∣=6

    Exactly one of those, (6,6)(6,6)(6,6), sums to 121212.

    P(sum=12∣sum≥10)=16P(\text{sum} = 12 \mid \text{sum} \ge 10) = \frac{1}{6}P(sum=12∣sum≥10)=61​

    The unconditional probability of a sum of 121212 is 136\frac{1}{36}361​, so the information multiplied it by six.

  9. 9

    One card is drawn from a standard deck. Which pair of events is independent?

    Answer choices for question 9

    Solution

    Correct answer: A

    Independence is the numerical test P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B), so check the pairs rather than trusting intuition. For a heart and a queen, the intersection is the single queen of hearts.

    P(A∩B)=152andP(A) P(B)=14⋅113=152P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{52} \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(A)\,P(B) = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{13} = \frac{1}{52}P(A∩B)=521​andP(A)P(B)=41​⋅131​=521​

    They agree, so suit and rank are independent: every rank appears once in each suit, so learning the rank leaves the suits in the same proportions.

    The others all fail. A heart is always red, so P(heart∩red)=14P(\text{heart} \cap \text{red}) = \frac{1}{4}P(heart∩red)=41​ while the product is only 18\frac{1}{8}81​. A heart and a spade are mutually exclusive, so the intersection has probability 000 while the product is 116\frac{1}{16}161​. And every king is a face card, so P(face∩king)=113P(\text{face} \cap \text{king}) = \frac{1}{13}P(face∩king)=131​ while the product is 313⋅113=3169\frac{3}{13} \cdot \frac{1}{13} = \frac{3}{169}133​⋅131​=1693​.

  10. 10

    A fair die is rolled twice. Given that the two rolls show different numbers, what is the probability that the second roll is larger than the first?

    Answer choices for question 10

    Solution

    Correct answer: C

    Conditioning removes the six doubles, so the sample space shrinks from 363636 rolls to 303030.

    ∣B∣=36−6=30\lvert B \rvert = 36 - 6 = 30∣B∣=36−6=30

    The 151515 rolls with the second entry larger all survive, because none of them was a tie.

    P(second>first∣different)=1530=12P(\text{second} > \text{first} \mid \text{different}) = \frac{15}{30} = \frac{1}{2}P(second>first∣different)=3015​=21​

    Now the symmetry argument works exactly. Once ties are ruled out, every roll has either the first or the second entry larger, and swapping the two dice pairs the two cases off perfectly.

    A drawer holds 666 black socks and 444 white socks. Two socks are pulled out at random without replacement. What is the probability that they match?

    Answer choices for question 11

    Solution

    Correct answer: D

    The sample space is every pair of socks, so ∣S∣=(102)=45\lvert S \rvert = \binom{10}{2} = 45∣S∣=(210​)=45. A matching pair is two blacks or two whites, and those cases are mutually exclusive, so add their counts.

    (62)+(42)=15+6=21\binom{6}{2} + \binom{4}{2} = 15 + 6 = 21(26​)+(24​)=15+6=21

    Divide.

    P(match)=2145=715≈0.467P(\text{match}) = \frac{21}{45} = \frac{7}{15} \approx 0.467P(match)=4521​=157​≈0.467

    So a matching pair is slightly less likely than a mismatched one, whose probability is 815\frac{8}{15}158​. The value 1325\frac{13}{25}2513​ assumes the first sock is put back, which changes the answer.

  12. 12

    Events AAA and BBB are mutually exclusive, with P(A)>0P(A) > 0P(A)>0 and P(B)>0P(B) > 0P(B)>0. Which statement is true?

    Answer choices for question 12

    Solution

    Correct answer: D

    Disjoint events have an empty intersection, and since P(B)>0P(B) > 0P(B)>0 the conditional probability is defined.

    P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)P(B)=0P(B)=0P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{0}{P(B)} = 0P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​=P(B)0​=0

    Learning that BBB occurred does not leave AAA unchanged; it rules AAA out entirely, dropping its probability from a positive number to zero. That is why the events are not independent: the product P(A)P(B)P(A)P(B)P(A)P(B) is positive while P(A∩B)P(A \cap B)P(A∩B) is zero, so the defining equation of independence fails.

    P(A∩B)=0≠P(A) P(B)P(A \cap B) = 0 \ne P(A)\,P(B)P(A∩B)=0=P(A)P(B)

    Mutually exclusive events with positive probability are as dependent as two events can be.

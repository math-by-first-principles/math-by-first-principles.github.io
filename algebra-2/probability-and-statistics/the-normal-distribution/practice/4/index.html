  5. 5

    Building a normal density out of the standard curve, f(x)=1σφ ⁣(x−μσ)f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma}\varphi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)f(x)=σ1​φ(σx−μ​), multiplies every horizontal distance on the standard curve by σ\sigmaσ and divides every height by σ\sigmaσ. What is the effect on the area under the curve?

    Answer choices for question 5

    Solution

    Correct answer: C

    Cut the region under the curve into thin vertical strips, each one close to a rectangle of width www and height hhh. The horizontal move multiplies every width by σ\sigmaσ, and the vertical move divides every height by σ\sigmaσ.

    (σw)(hσ)=wh(\sigma w)\left(\frac{h}{\sigma}\right) = wh(σw)(σh​)=wh

    Every strip keeps exactly the area it had, so the whole region does too. This is the reason the total area stays at 111 no matter what σ\sigmaσ is, and the reason corresponding pieces of area match up between any normal curve and the standard one. It is what makes a single table enough.

    Mind the direction. This question runs from the standard curve out to fff. Standardizing is the reverse trip, X→ZX \to ZX→Z, which divides horizontal distances by σ\sigmaσ and multiplies heights by σ\sigmaσ. The area is unchanged either way, for exactly the same reason: the two scale factors always multiply to 111.

  8. 8

    A Galton board drops balls through 121212 rows of pegs, each ball bouncing left or right at every peg. Why do the balls pile up in a bell shape at the bottom?

    Answer choices for question 8

    Solution

    Correct answer: D

    A ball reaching a given slot must have taken some number of right-bounces out of the 121212 available, and the number of different routes that do this is a binomial coefficient.

    routes to the slot with k right-bounces=(12k)\text{routes to the slot with } k \text{ right-bounces} = \binom{12}{k}routes to the slot with k right-bounces=(k12​)

    Those counts are 1,12,66,220,495,792,924,792,…1, 12, 66, 220, 495, 792, 924, 792, \ldots1,12,66,220,495,792,924,792,…, huge in the middle and tiny at the edges, because there are many ways to balance rights and lefts and only one way to bounce right every single time. More routes means more balls, so the pile takes the shape of the coefficients, which is the bell.

    Gravity is uniform across the board and the balls do not interact; the shape comes entirely from counting.

  12. 12

    Household incomes are strongly right-skewed, with a long tail of very high earners. What goes wrong if you apply the 68-95-99.7 rule to them?

    Answer choices for question 12

    Solution

    Correct answer: A

    The 68-95-99.7 rule is a statement about the normal curve, and it inherits every one of that curve's assumptions, symmetry above all.

    rule assumes: symmetric, bell-shaped, thin tails\text{rule assumes: symmetric, bell-shaped, thin tails}rule assumes: symmetric, bell-shaped, thin tails

    Income data has none of those. The long right tail drags the mean above the median, and the bands μ±σ\mu \pm \sigmaμ±σ no longer sit sensibly around the bulk of the data. The rule could easily place a band boundary below zero income, which is meaningless.

    The mean and the standard deviation can still be computed for any data set (and a standard deviation is never negative, being a square root of an average of squares). What fails is not the arithmetic but the model: the numbers no longer mean what the rule assumes they mean. That is exactly why "normal" is such a misleading name for this curve.

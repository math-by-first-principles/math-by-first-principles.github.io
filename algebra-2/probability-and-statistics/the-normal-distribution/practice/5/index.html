    Correct answer: A

    Marathon times are normal with μ=240\mu = 240μ=240 min and σ=30\sigma = 30σ=30 min. In a 10 km race, times are normal with μ=55\mu = 55μ=55 min and σ=5\sigma = 5σ=5 min. Runner P finishes the marathon in 195195195 min; runner Q finishes the 10 km in 464646 min. Who ran the better race relative to their own field?

    Answer choices for question 8

    Solution

    Correct answer: C

    Standardize each finishing time against its own race.

    zP=195−24030=−4530=−1.5,zQ=46−555=−95=−1.8z_P = \frac{195 - 240}{30} = \frac{-45}{30} = -1.5, \qquad z_Q = \frac{46 - 55}{5} = \frac{-9}{5} = -1.8zP​=30195−240​=30−45​=−1.5,zQ​=546−55​=5−9​=−1.8

    For race times, lower is better, so the better performance is the one further BELOW its mean, which means the more negative z-score. Runner Q, at −1.8-1.8−1.8 standard deviations, beat their field by more than runner P did at −1.5-1.5−1.5.

    Comparing 195195195 minutes with 464646 minutes directly is meaningless, because the two races are not the same distance. That is exactly the situation the z-score exists to fix.

    A normal density can be written f(x)=1σφ ⁣(x−μσ)f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma}\varphi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)f(x)=σ1​φ(σx−μ​). Compared with the graph of φ\varphiφ, the graph of fff is which of the following?

    Answer choices for question 9

    Solution

    Correct answer: D

    Read the formula as a composition of the graph transformations from chapter 2. Replacing the input zzz by x−μσ\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}σx−μ​ does two things at once: subtracting μ\muμ shifts the graph right by μ\muμ, and dividing by σ\sigmaσ multiplies every horizontal distance from the centre by σ\sigmaσ. The outer factor 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​ then divides every height by σ\sigmaσ.

    shift right μ,widths×σ,heights÷σ\text{shift right } \mu, \quad \text{widths} \times \sigma, \quad \text{heights} \div \sigmashift right μ,widths×σ,heights÷σ

    A note on vocabulary, since chapter 2 was strict about it. That lesson names a horizontal scaling by the input multiplier, never by its reciprocal, and the input multiplier here is 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​, so in its language this is a horizontal compression by 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​. It is the same move: dividing the inputs by 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​ multiplies them by σ\sigmaσ. Describing it by what happens to distances avoids the argument altogether and stays correct even when σ<1\sigma < 1σ<1.

    The vertical shrink is essential: without it, a wide curve would also be a tall one and the total area would grow to σ\sigmaσ instead of staying at 111. The two rescalings cancel exactly, which is why every area is preserved and one table serves every normal distribution. And μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ play quite different roles, so a description that shifts by σ\sigmaσ and scales by μ\muμ has swapped them, which is not a small slip.

    Solution

  11. 11

    In 101010 coin flips, the probability of exactly kkk heads is (10k)/1024\binom{10}{k}/1024(k10​)/1024. Why do these eleven probabilities trace out a bell shape?

    Answer choices for question 11

    Solution

    Correct answer: C

    The denominator 102410241024 is the same for every kkk, so the shape of the eleven probabilities is entirely the shape of the numerators, which are the tenth row of Pascal's triangle.

    1,  10,  45,  120,  210,  252,  210,  120,  45,  10,  11,\; 10,\; 45,\; 120,\; 210,\; 252,\; 210,\; 120,\; 45,\; 10,\; 11,10,45,120,210,252,210,120,45,10,1

    Those counts are huge in the middle and tiny at the ends, for a concrete reason: there are 252252252 different ways to arrange five heads among ten flips, and only one way to get all ten. Divided by 102410241024, they trace a hump.

    Adding to 111 is true of every probability distribution, including perfectly flat ones, so it cannot explain the shape. Fairness only makes the hump symmetric; a biased coin still produces a hump, just an off-centre one.

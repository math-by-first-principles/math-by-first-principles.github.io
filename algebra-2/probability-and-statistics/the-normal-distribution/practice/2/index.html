    Using the standard normal table, find P(−2<Z<2)P(-2 < Z < 2)P(−2<Z<2).

    Answer choices for question 6

    Solution

    Correct answer: B

    Subtract the area to the left of the lower endpoint from the area to the left of the upper one, using symmetry for the negative endpoint.

    Φ(−2)=1−Φ(2)=1−0.9772=0.0228\Phi(-2) = 1 - \Phi(2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228Φ(−2)=1−Φ(2)=1−0.9772=0.0228

  12. 12

    Adding up many independent small effects tends to produce a normal distribution, whatever the individual effects look like, provided no single effect dominates the total. What is the name of the theorem that makes this precise?

    Answer choices for question 12

    Solution

    Correct answer: D

    The result is the Central Limit Theorem, and it is the reason the bell turns up in so many unrelated measurements: heights, measurement errors, and the slots of a Galton board are all totals of many small independent nudges.

    many small independent effects, none dominating  ⟶  normal\text{many small independent effects, none dominating} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{normal}many small independent effects, none dominating⟶normal

    The proviso earns its keep. When one ingredient is allowed to swamp the sum, the bell never forms, which is why heavy-tailed quantities such as incomes and stock returns are not normal despite also being built out of many effects. The theorem's proof is far beyond this course, which is why the lesson names it rather than deriving it. The Binomial Theorem expands (a+b)n(a+b)^n(a+b)n, and the other two are results about polynomial roots.

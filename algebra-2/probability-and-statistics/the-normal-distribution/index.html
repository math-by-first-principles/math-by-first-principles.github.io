The Normal Distribution Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

Measure the heights of ten thousand adults, the weights of ten thousand loaves of bread, or the errors of ten thousand careful measurements of the same star. Something strange happens: three completely unrelated experiments produce close to the same picture. Most values cluster near the middle, fewer appear as you move out, and almost nothing lands far away. The picture is a symmetric bell. This lesson is about that curve and why it turns up so often. Its one honest difficulty is that you can describe the curve exactly and still be unable to compute a single probability from it. The numbers you need come from a table that somebody else had to work out one at a time.

Learning goals

  • Read probability as area under a density curve
  • Note that a single value has probability zero
  • Describe the standard curve, symmetric and fast-decaying
  • Apply the 68-95-99.7 rule as rounded percentages
  • Convert with z=x−μσz = \tfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}z=σx−μ​ to use one table
  • Walk back to x=μ+zσx = \mu + z\sigmax=μ+zσ from a percentage

A shape that keeps turning up

Start with something you already built. In the Binomial Theorem lesson you learned that flipping a coin nnn times produces (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​) different ways to get exactly kkk heads. You also learned that those counts are the rows of Pascal’s triangle. Take n=10n = 10n=10. There are 210=10242^{10} = 1024210=1024 equally likely sequences of heads and tails, so the probability of exactly kkk heads is

P(k heads)=(10k)1024.P(k \text{ heads}) = \frac{\binom{10}{k}}{1024}.P(k heads)=1024(k10​)​.

Now draw those eleven probabilities as bars. The binomial coefficients 1,10,45,120,210,252,210,120,45,10,11, 10, 45, 120, 210, 252, 210, 120, 45, 10, 11,10,45,120,210,252,210,120,45,10,1 are large in the middle and tiny at the ends. The plain reason is that there are many ways to get five heads and only one way to get ten. The bars pile into a hump.

Coin flips are not special. What they are is a sum of many small independent effects: ten little pushes, each one either up or down, added together. Human height is the same kind of thing, a total of many genetic and nutritional nudges. A measurement error is a total of many tiny disturbances in the instrument and the air. A Galton board, a peg-covered board down which a ball bounces left or right at each row, is that idea made physical. The balls really do pile up in a bell at the bottom, because the number of paths to each slot is a binomial coefficient.

That is the pattern: add up many independent small effects and the total tends toward this one shape, provided no single one of them dominates the total and a few other technical conditions hold that this course will not state precisely. That proviso is not a technicality, and we will come back to it. When one ingredient is allowed to run away with the sum, the bell may not form, which is part of why some famous quantities are not normal. The theorem that makes all of this precise is the Central Limit Theorem. It is one of the deepest results in mathematics, it is the reason the bell turns up in so many unrelated places, and proving it is far beyond this course. What this lesson can do honestly is describe the curve and work with it.

Probability as area, and one thing we cannot do

Coin flips are discrete: there are finitely many outcomes, each with its own probability, and the probabilities add to 111. Height is not like that. A person can be 170170170 cm tall, or 170.4170.4170.4 cm, or 170.4013170.4013170.4013 cm. There are infinitely many possible values and no way to hand each one a probability that still totals 111.

So the whole set-up has to change. For a continuous quantity we do not attach probability to points. We draw a curve, called a density curve, and we agree on one rule. Let XXX name the numerical result of the experiment, the height of a randomly chosen adult, say. Then a statement like X<5X < 5X<5 is itself an event in the sense of the previous lesson: the set of every outcome whose measured value is below 555, so P(X<5)P(X < 5)P(X<5) is just that event’s probability, written in this new notation:

Probability is area under the curve. The curve never dips below the horizontal axis, the area between aaa and bbb is P(a<X<b)P(a < X < b)P(a<X<b), and the total area under the whole curve is 111.

Everything follows from that rule, including a genuine surprise. Ask for the probability that an adult is exactly 170170170 cm tall, to infinite precision. The region above the single point 170170170 has width zero, so it has no area at all:

P(X=c)=0for every single value c.P(X = c) = 0 \quad \text{for every single value } c.P(X=c)=0for every single value c.

That is not a rounding convention. For a continuous quantity, every individual value has probability zero, and probability lives only in intervals. A first reaction is that this makes the model absurd (surely somebody is exactly 170170170 cm). It does not. Probability zero does not mean impossible; it means that one single point gets no share of the curve’s area, because area needs width and a point has none. In practice this rarely matters anyway, since “exactly 170170170 cm” would mean matching infinitely many decimal places, which no measurement ever pins down. Real questions are always intervals: between 169.5169.5169.5 and 170.5170.5170.5, above 180180180, below 150150150.

This has a consequence you should test yourself with. In the discrete probability you met earlier in this chapter, P(X<5)P(X < 5)P(X<5) and P(X≤5)P(X \le 5)P(X≤5) differ whenever 555 is a possible outcome, because the second one also counts it. For a continuous quantity, the outcome X=5X = 5X=5 contributes zero, so

P(X<c)=P(X≤c).P(X < c) = P(X \le c).P(X<c)=P(X≤c).

The endpoint simply does not matter. Include it or exclude it, the area is the same.

The curve, and the debt

The standard normal curve is the density curve given by

φ(z)=12π e−z2/2.\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}.φ(z)=2π​1​e−z2/2.

You can read every part of this. The exponent −z2/2-z^2/2−z2/2 is never positive, and since (−z)2=z2(-z)^2 = z^2(−z)2=z2 it gives the same value at zzz and at −z-z−z, so the curve is symmetric about 000. It is largest when the exponent is 000, that is when z=0z = 0z=0, so the peak is at the centre. And z2z^2z2 grows fast, so e−z2/2e^{-z^2/2}e−z2/2 collapses fast: at z=1z = 1z=1 the height is e−0.5≈0.6065e^{-0.5} \approx 0.6065e−0.5≈0.6065 of the peak, and at z=3z = 3z=3 it is e−4.5≈0.0111e^{-4.5} \approx 0.0111e−4.5≈0.0111 of the peak, about a ninetieth. That is why the tails look empty. The constant 12π≈0.3989\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.39892π​1​≈0.3989 out front is not decoration: it is exactly the number that makes the total area come out to 111. Where that number comes from is not something this course can show you.

We cannot compute these areas by hand. Finding the area under a curve is what integral calculus is for, and you have not met calculus yet. Even worse: mathematicians have proven that this curve’s area cannot be reached by the ordinary algebra formulas that calculus normally produces, no matter how much calculus you eventually learn. That is not a gap in anybody’s cleverness; it is a theorem.

That claim can be made exact: the area function of e−z2/2e^{-z^2/2}e−z2/2 has no elementary antiderivative, a fact with its own name and its own short proof. Read the precise statement in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the precise statement

What people do instead is compute the areas numerically, to as many decimal places as they like, by adding up huge numbers of very thin strips. They then write the answers down in a table. The table is not a definition and it is not a cheat. It is the record of a computation nobody can shortcut. Every number you are about to look up was earned that way, and knowing that is the difference between using the table and understanding it.

The 68-95-99.7 rule

Because the areas have been computed once and for all, we can state the headline results. For the normal curve, measuring distance from the centre in standard deviations:

For any normal distribution with mean μ\muμ and standard deviation σ\sigmaσ:

  • about 68%68\%68% of the values lie within 111 standard deviation of the mean, between μ−σ\mu - \sigmaμ−σ and μ+σ\mu + \sigmaμ+σ,
  • about 95%95\%95% lie within 222 standard deviations, between μ−2σ\mu - 2\sigmaμ−2σ and μ+2σ\mu + 2\sigmaμ+2σ,
  • about 99.7%99.7\%99.7% lie within 333 standard deviations, between μ−3σ\mu - 3\sigmaμ−3σ and μ+3σ\mu + 3\sigmaμ+3σ.

This is the 68-95-99.7 rule, sometimes called the empirical rule. Be clear about its status: it is not something we derived, it is the numerical answer rounded off. The computed areas are 68.27%68.27\%68.27%, 95.45%95.45\%95.45% and 99.73%99.73\%99.73%. The rule is a memory aid, accurate to within half a percentage point, and when a question wants more precision you go to the table instead.

Two useful facts fall straight out of symmetry. The two halves of the central band are equal, so each holds about 34%34\%34%. And using the rule’s own rounded bookkeeping, what is left outside the 333-standard-deviation band is about 0.3%0.3\%0.3%, split evenly between two tails, so about 0.15%0.15\%0.15% of values sit more than 333 standard deviations above the mean. Rare, but not impossible.

Keep the rule’s numbers and the computed numbers apart, because they are not the same and a careful student will notice. The band figures 343434, 13.513.513.5 and 2.352.352.35 are the rule’s own bookkeeping, chosen so that they add up to 686868, 959595 and 99.799.799.7. The computed band areas are 34.13%34.13\%34.13%, then 13.59%13.59\%13.59%, then 2.14%2.14\%2.14%, with 0.13%0.13\%0.13% left in each tail beyond 333 standard deviations. The two agree to within a fraction of a percentage point, which is all the rule ever promised. So when a question says to use the 68-95-99.7 rule, use the rule’s numbers. When it says to use the table, use the table, and do not expect the last decimal place to match.

The z-score, and why one table is enough

There are infinitely many normal distributions, one for every choice of μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ. Heights in centimetres, test scores out of 800800800, loaf weights in grams: all different curves. It would be hopeless to tabulate them all. We do not have to, and the reason is a piece of chapter 2.

Define the z-score of a value xxx:

z=x−μσ.z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}.z=σx−μ​.

Read it as a unit conversion. The numerator x−μx - \mux−μ measures how far xxx is from the mean, in the original units (centimetres, points, grams). Dividing by σ\sigmaσ re-expresses that distance in standard deviations. A z-score of 1.51.51.5 means “one and a half standard deviations above the mean”, whatever the original quantity was, and that sentence carries no units at all. That is the whole trick: z-scores are the common currency in which every normal distribution can be compared. Applying that same conversion to the whole quantity, not just one value, gives it a name: Z=X−μσZ = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}Z=σX−μ​ is the standardized version of XXX, the same random result relabeled in standard deviations, so a statement like P(a<Z<b)P(a < Z < b)P(a<Z<b) is just P(μ+aσ<X<μ+bσ)P(\mu + a\sigma < X < \mu + b\sigma)P(μ+aσ<X<μ+bσ) written in the new units.

Now here is why the trick is allowed, which is a stronger claim than saying it is convenient. A normal distribution with mean μ\muμ and standard deviation σ\sigmaσ has the density curve

f(x)=1σ2π e−(x−μ)2/(2σ2),f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)},f(x)=σ2π​1​e−(x−μ)2/(2σ2),

and the claim is that this is not a new curve at all. It is φ\varphiφ, shifted and rescaled, exactly the graph transformations you studied in chapter 2: shifted right by μ\muμ, and stretched horizontally, with its height correspondingly shrunk, by a factor of σ\sigmaσ.

Here is why that is enough to justify one shared table. Stretch a shape sideways by a factor of σ\sigmaσ while shrinking its height by that same factor, and its area does not change: a strip that gets σ\sigmaσ times wider and σ\sigmaσ times shorter has the same width times height as before. So shifting and rescaling φ\varphiφ into fff leaves matching pieces of area exactly equal,

P(a<Z<b)=P(μ+aσ<X<μ+bσ),P(a < Z < b) = P(\mu + a\sigma < X < \mu + b\sigma),P(a<Z<b)=P(μ+aσ<X<μ+bσ),

which is the whole reason a single table can serve every normal distribution. Up to a shift and a rescale, there is only one normal curve.

The sentence above can be made exact with algebra: substituting the z-score into f(x)f(x)f(x) shows it equals 1σφ(z)\frac{1}{\sigma}\varphi(z)σ1​φ(z), and cutting the curve into thin strips shows the shift-and-rescale move preserves area exactly. Read the full derivation in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Going backwards, from a percentage to a value

Every question so far handed you a value and asked for an area. The reverse question is just as common, and just as easy once you read the table in the other direction. You are given an area and you want the value that produces it.

The tool is the z-score formula, solved for xxx. From z=x−μσz = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}z=σx−μ​, multiply both sides by σ\sigmaσ and add μ\muμ:

x=μ+zσ.x = \mu + z\sigma.x=μ+zσ.

In words: start at the mean and walk zzz standard deviations. The procedure is to convert the percentage into an area to the left, find the z-score whose table entry matches that area, and then walk.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating the height of the curve as a probability. The curve’s height at z=0z = 0z=0 is φ(0)≈0.3989\varphi(0) \approx 0.3989φ(0)≈0.3989, and that is not the probability of anything. Only an area is a probability. This is the one idea that makes continuous distributions different, and it is why P(X=c)=0P(X = c) = 0P(X=c)=0 for every ccc.
  • Fussing over <<< versus ≤\le≤. For a continuous distribution they give the same answer, since the endpoint contributes zero area. P(Z<1.2)P(Z < 1.2)P(Z<1.2) and P(Z≤1.2)P(Z \le 1.2)P(Z≤1.2) are both 0.88490.88490.8849. (For the discrete probabilities earlier in this chapter the two differ whenever the endpoint is a possible outcome, so do not carry this habit backwards.)
  • Reading the table as the area to the right. Φ(z)\Phi(z)Φ(z) is the area to the left. For a right-hand tail you must subtract: P(Z>1.4)=1−0.9192=0.0808P(Z > 1.4) = 1 - 0.9192 = 0.0808P(Z>1.4)=1−0.9192=0.0808, not 0.91920.91920.9192.
  • Sign errors in the z-score. A value below the mean has a negative z-score. If x=985x = 985x=985 with μ=1000\mu = 1000μ=1000 and σ=10\sigma = 10σ=10, then z=−1.5z = -1.5z=−1.5, not 1.51.51.5. Then handle it with Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z)\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z) rather than by looking up 1.51.51.5 and hoping.
  • Dividing by the variance instead of the standard deviation. The denominator of the z-score is σ\sigmaσ, in the same units as xxx. Dividing by σ2\sigma^2σ2 leaves you with something that is not a count of standard deviations.
  • Assuming everything is normal. It is not. Incomes, city populations, insurance claims and stock returns all have far heavier tails than the normal curve, so a “once in ten thousand years” event computed from a bell curve can turn up twice in a decade. Quantities like these are often driven by one huge term rather than many small balanced ones (a single blockbuster year, a single market crash), which is exactly the situation the Central Limit Theorem’s no single term dominating condition rules out. The bell is a model, and using it requires a reason to believe it fits.

Key takeaways

  • For a continuous quantity, probability is area under a density curve, the total area is 111, and a single value has probability 000. Because of that, P(X<c)=P(X≤c)P(X < c) = P(X \le c)P(X<c)=P(X≤c).
  • The standard normal curve is φ(z)=12πe−z2/2\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}φ(z)=2π​1​e−z2/2: symmetric about 000, peaking there, and dropping off fast because of the −z2-z^2−z2 in the exponent.
  • Its areas cannot be written as an algebra formula, a proven fact, so the values are computed numerically and recorded in a table. The table is a record of a computation, not a definition.
  • The 68-95-99.7 rule: about 68%68\%68%, 95%95\%95% and 99.7%99.7\%99.7% of the area lies within 111, 222 and 333 standard deviations of the mean. These are rounded from 68.27%68.27\%68.27%, 95.45%95.45\%95.45% and 99.73%99.73\%99.73%.
  • The z-score z=x−μσz = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}z=σx−μ​ converts a value into “standard deviations from the mean”. It is a shift and a rescale, so every normal curve is the standard curve in different units, and one table serves them all.
  • To read the table: Φ(z)=P(Z<z)\Phi(z) = P(Z < z)Φ(z)=P(Z<z) is the area to the left; P(Z>z)=1−Φ(z)P(Z > z) = 1 - \Phi(z)P(Z>z)=1−Φ(z); P(a<Z<b)=Φ(b)−Φ(a)P(a < Z < b) = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a)P(a<Z<b)=Φ(b)−Φ(a); and Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z)\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z).
  • To go backwards from a percentage to a value, find the z-score with the required area to its left, then walk x=μ+zσx = \mu + z\sigmax=μ+zσ.
  • Sums of many small independent effects tend toward this shape provided no single effect dominates the total and a few other technical conditions this course does not state precisely also hold, which is the Central Limit Theorem. That proviso is also why the curve is not universal: some real quantities are driven by one huge term rather than many small ones, and assuming a bell without a reason is a mistake.

Why no elementary formula gives this area

The lesson states the result: the area under φ(z)=12πe−z2/2\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}φ(z)=2π​1​e−z2/2 cannot be written down with an algebra formula, no matter how much calculus you know. Here is that claim, made precise.

The area function of φ\varphiφ would be a formula F(z)F(z)F(z) that hands you the area from 000 out to any zzz, in one step, with no numerical add-up required. Calculus calls such a formula an antiderivative. For most functions built from powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometric functions, an antiderivative built from those same tools, an elementary antiderivative, exists. For e−z2/2e^{-z^2/2}e−z2/2 it provably does not.

Get the direction of that claim right, because it is easy to state backwards. The difficulty is not in writing e−z2/2e^{-z^2/2}e−z2/2 down, which takes one line. It is in writing down its area function using only powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometric functions, in any combination. A theorem from the 1830s, due to Joseph Liouville, gives a test that any candidate area function built from those tools would have to pass, and the area function of e−z2/2e^{-z^2/2}e−z2/2 fails it. This is not a gap in anybody’s cleverness; it is a proven fact about which functions have an elementary antiderivative and which do not. (Mathematicians do still write this particular area function down, using a special function built for exactly this purpose, the error function. That sidesteps the theorem rather than contradicting it: the error function is not built from the ordinary toolbox the theorem rules out.)

That is exactly why the table exists. The area is computed numerically instead, by adding up huge numbers of very thin strips, a method that works whether or not an elementary antiderivative exists, and the results are recorded once so nobody has to repeat the computation.

Replacing the input by x−μσ\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}σx−μ​ shifts the graph right by μ\muμ and multiplies every horizontal distance from the centre by σ\sigmaσ. Those two effects are what put the peak at μ\muμ and set the width. Chapter 2 names a horizontal scaling by the input multiplier, never by its reciprocal, and the input multiplier here is 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​. So in that lesson’s vocabulary this move is a horizontal compression by 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​. It is the same move under both names, since dividing the inputs by 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​ is multiplying them by σ\sigmaσ. The geometric phrasing is the one that matters below, and it has the advantage of staying correct whether σ\sigmaσ is bigger or smaller than 111. Multiplying the output by 1σ\frac{1}{\sigma}σ1​ then divides every height by σ\sigmaσ, which is what keeps a wide curve from also being a tall one.

Those two moves cancel each other as far as area is concerned, and that is the heart of it. Cut the region under φ\varphiφ into thin vertical strips, each one close to a rectangle of width www and height hhh. The horizontal move multiplies every width by σ\sigmaσ; the vertical move divides every height by σ\sigmaσ. A strip that had area whwhwh now has area

(σw)(hσ)=wh,(\sigma w)\left(\frac{h}{\sigma}\right) = wh,(σw)(σh​)=wh,

the very same area. Since every strip keeps its area, so does the whole region assembled from them.

Two honest remarks about that step. The strips only approximate the region, and the claim that the approximation becomes exact is one we are taking on trust. The table already asked the very same trust of you a moment ago, when its numbers turned out to come from adding up huge numbers of very thin strips. And notice what the argument does and does not do. It never computes an area. It shows only that two areas are equal, because a map that multiplies widths by σ\sigmaσ and divides heights by σ\sigmaσ leaves every area alone. That is why the argument is allowed even though computing either area is not: equality is cheap here, and evaluation is what costs calculus.

The total area under fff is therefore still 111, which is what makes 1σ2π\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}σ2π​1​ the correct constant, and, more usefully, corresponding pieces of area match up:

Any probability question about XXX is therefore the same question about the standard curve, once each value is rewritten as a z-score. One table can serve every normal distribution because, up to a shift and a rescale, there is only one normal curve.

A bit of history (optional)

For a long time this curve belonged mostly to astronomers. It described the errors in a hundred telescope readings of a single star. Measure a thousand people and you would not expect that same shape. A person, after all, is not a mistake.

Adolphe Quetelet, a Belgian astronomer, looked anyway. In the 1830s he gathered the chest measurements of thousands of Scottish soldiers. They scattered into the same bell he knew from the sky. From that he drew an extraordinary conclusion. Nature was aiming at an ideal he called l’homme moyen, the average man, and every real person was an error around that target.

The measurements were sound; the interpretation was not. There is no target, and nobody is a miss. What Quetelet had stumbled on was the pattern this lesson describes. A human chest is a total of many small influences, and such sums do tend to a bell. The result carries conditions, though: the influences must be roughly independent, none may swamp the rest, and a few further technical conditions this course does not state precisely must hold too. Where those hold the bell is earned, and where they fail it has merely been assumed. His average man was a mean, not a design.

The habit outlived the mistake, and ranking people against a curve later did real harm. Keep that warning tied to the word normal. The curve is a model of the world, never a verdict on it.

Counting Principles, Permutations, and Combinations

Intuition

Every counting problem you have solved so far was solved by building. Make the first decision, then the second, then the third, and multiply the number of ways to make each one, as long as that number of ways stays the same no matter what came before. That method works perfectly as long as each thing you are counting gets built exactly once. But the moment order stops mattering, the building goes wrong in a very specific way. If you seat three friends in three chairs you get six seatings. Yet if you only want to know who is in the room, all six of those seatings are the same answer, counted six times over. Nothing about the building was wrong. It simply counted each answer six times instead of once, so the repair is not a new construction, it is a division. That one move, count the ways and then divide by how many times you counted each thing, is what turns a permutation into a combination. That one move is the engine of this entire lesson.

Learning goals

  • Multiply stage counts that stay fixed no matter the earlier choices, or add across non-overlapping cases
  • Ask whether order matters and whether repeats are allowed before choosing a formula
  • Compute P(n,k)P(n,k)P(n,k) as a product of exactly kkk factors
  • Divide by k!k!k! to forget the order, giving (nk)\dbinom{n}{k}(kn​)
  • Correct for repeated objects by dividing out their type factorials, to count arrangements like MISSISSIPPI
  • Count the unwanted and subtract, for at least one

The multiplication principle

Start with a small case. A diner offers 444 soups and 333 sandwiches, and a lunch is one soup with one sandwich. Pick a soup any of 444 ways. Whichever one you pick, all 333 sandwiches are still on offer, so that choice leads to 333 lunches, and the same is true for every soup. Four soups, each starting 333 lunches of its own, and no lunch started twice, gives

4×3=12 lunches.4 \times 3 = 12 \text{ lunches}.4×3=12 lunches.

Now add a third stage, 222 drinks. Whichever soup and sandwich you already picked, both drinks are still on offer, so each of the 121212 soup-and-sandwich combinations leads to 222 full lunches. The same move that turned 444 into 4×34 \times 34×3 now turns 121212 into 12×2=2412 \times 2 = 2412×2=24, because the number of drinks on offer never depends on which soup or sandwich came before it. Add a fourth stage and the same move repeats again. That repeated stacking, one stage at a time, is the whole idea behind counting by stages. State it precisely enough to use in harder cases, because the precision is what lets it survive when a careless version fails.

The multiplication principle. Suppose each object you want to count is built by a sequence of kkk decisions. Suppose decision iii can be made in nin_ini​ ways no matter how the earlier decisions came out. Suppose finally that different sequences of decisions build different objects, and that every object is built by exactly one sequence. Then the number of objects is

n1×n2×⋯×nk.n_1 \times n_2 \times \cdots \times n_k.n1​×n2​×⋯×nk​.

The soup, sandwich, and drink example shows the pattern by hand for three stages: stacking one more stage always multiplies the running total by that stage’s option count. Making that argument once and for all, for any number of stages, is a proof by induction on the number of decisions kkk, spelled out in full in Go deeper below.

See the full induction proof

Read the hypothesis carefully, because the whole lesson leans on it. It says the number of options at each stage must not depend on the earlier choices. It does not say the options themselves must stay the same. When you seat people in chairs, which people remain for the second chair depends entirely on who took the first. But how many remain does not change with who took the first: it is always one fewer. That distinction is what makes permutations legal, and it is why “the choices are independent” undersells the rule: the choices themselves can depend on the past, as long as their number does not.

One companion rule handles problems that break into cases instead of stages. If the objects you want split into groups that overlap nowhere and leave nothing out, then the total is the sum of the group counts. That is the addition principle. A sequence of decisions multiplies (“this and then that”), while a split into separate cases adds (“either this kind or that kind”). Splitting a 222-digit number’s tens digit into ”111 through 444” or ”555 through 999” is one addition-principle split; if the cases instead shared a value, such as “even” and “under 666” (both true of 222), simply adding their counts would count that shared value twice.

Answer choices

The simplest use of the multiplication principle is the one where nothing changes between stages. Suppose you fill kkk slots, and each slot freely takes any of nnn values, with repetition allowed. Then every stage offers the same nnn options, and the product collapses to a power:

n×n×⋯×n⏟k slots=nk.\underbrace{n \times n \times \cdots \times n}_{k \text{ slots}} = n^k.k slotsn×n×⋯×n​​=nk.

A four-digit PIN has 104=10,00010^4 = 10{,}000104=10,000 settings, and a string of kkk letters drawn from a 262626-letter alphabet has 26k26^k26k possibilities. In both cases a digit or a letter can be reused as often as you like.

Permutations, or filling slots without repetition

Now forbid repetition. An arrangement, or permutation, of kkk objects chosen from nnn distinct objects is a way of filling kkk ordered slots using each object at most once. Write P(n,k)P(n, k)P(n,k) for how many there are.

Fill the slots left to right. The first slot accepts any of the nnn objects. Whatever you put there is gone, so the second slot accepts any of the remaining n−1n - 1n−1. The third accepts n−2n - 2n−2, and so on. The counts shrink by one at every step, and the multiplication principle applies because the number remaining is the same no matter which particular objects were used up. By the time you reach the kkk-th slot you have already placed k−1k - 1k−1 objects, so it accepts n−(k−1)=n−k+1n - (k-1) = n - k + 1n−(k−1)=n−k+1 of them:

P(n,k)=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯(n−k+1).P(n, k) = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-k+1).P(n,k)=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯(n−k+1).

Count the factors, since this is where the formula is usually mangled. There is one factor per slot, so there are kkk of them, and they run n,n−1,…n, n-1, \ldotsn,n−1,… down to n−k+1n-k+1n−k+1. For P(9,4)P(9, 4)P(9,4) that is four factors, 9×8×7×69 \times 8 \times 7 \times 69×8×7×6, stopping at 9−4+1=69 - 4 + 1 = 69−4+1=6 and not at 555.

What the factorial counts. Set k=nk = nk=n and you are arranging all nnn objects, using every one of them:

P(n,n)=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯1=n!.P(n, n) = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 1 = n!.P(n,n)=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯1=n!.

You met n!n!n! in the binomial theorem lesson as a piece of notation. This is what it counts: the number of ways to put nnn distinct objects in a row. Five books have 5!=1205! = 1205!=120 orders, and ten books have 10!=3,628,80010! = 3{,}628{,}80010!=3,628,800 of them.

The closed form. The product n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1) is the top of a factorial with its tail chopped off, so put the tail back by multiplying and dividing by (n−k)!(n-k)!(n−k)!:

P(n,k)=n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)×(n−k)(n−k−1)⋯1(n−k)(n−k−1)⋯1=n!(n−k)!.P(n, k) = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) \times (n-k)(n-k-1)\cdots 1}{(n-k)(n-k-1)\cdots 1} = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.P(n,k)=(n−k)(n−k−1)⋯1n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)×(n−k)(n−k−1)⋯1​=(n−k)!n!​.

The numerator is now the full factorial n!n!n!, and the denominator cancels precisely the factors you did not want. So

P(n,k)=n!(n−k)!.P(n, k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.P(n,k)=(n−k)!n!​.

Why 0!=10! = 10!=1 is forced. The cancellation above needs the tail (n−k)(n−k−1)⋯1(n-k)(n-k-1)\cdots 1(n−k)(n−k−1)⋯1 to be an honest product of positive whole numbers, so it only works for k<nk < nk<n. The case k=nk = nk=n, arranging every object, is the one it misses. The slot count there is not in doubt: P(n,n)=n!P(n,n) = n!P(n,n)=n!. If one closed form is to cover that case too, it must return n!n!n! there as well, and the closed form reads n!/(n−n)!=n!/0!n!/(n-n)! = n!/0!n!/(n−n)!=n!/0! at k=nk = nk=n. Setting the two equal, n!=n!/0!n! = n!/0!n!=n!/0!, leaves exactly one possible value:

0!=1.0! = 1.0!=1.

The value is not picked for convenience: it is the only one under which the formula and the slot count agree. It also matches what a factorial means. Since n!n!n! counts the arrangements of nnn objects, 0!0!0! should count the arrangements of 000 objects, and there is exactly one, the empty arrangement in which you place nothing. (The binomial theorem lesson used 0!=10! = 10!=1 for the same reason, to keep its own formula correct at the edges.)

A plate shows three letters followed by three digits. The letters must all be different, but the digits may repeat. How many plates are possible?

Combinations, or forgetting the order

A permutation cares about order. Very often you do not. A committee of three is the same committee however you list its members, and a hand of cards is the same hand however it was dealt. A set of three chosen factors is the same set however you name them. What you want to count then is not an ordered list but a set.

Notation. For 0≤k≤n0 \le k \le n0≤k≤n, the symbol (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​), read ”nnn choose kkk”, is the number of kkk-element subsets of an nnn-element set. This is not a new symbol. In the binomial theorem lesson it counted the ways to choose which kkk of the nnn factors of (x+y)n(x+y)^n(x+y)n donate their yyy. And a choice of factors is nothing more than a set of kkk factors picked out of nnn. Same act, same count, same number. What is new here is a way to compute it directly.

The tool that computes it directly is the mirror image of the multiplication principle: start from a count you already know, then divide away the overcounting.

Start from a small case. Choose 333 letters from {A,B,C,D}\{A, B, C, D\}{A,B,C,D}, filling 333 ordered slots without repeats: there are P(4,3)=4×3×2=24P(4,3) = 4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24P(4,3)=4×3×2=24 ordered lists. But the six lists ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, and CBA all name the same set, {A,B,C}\{A, B, C\}{A,B,C}: they differ only in the order the letters were written down. The same is true of every 333-letter set drawn from these four letters, so the 242424 ordered lists split evenly into groups of 666.

(43)=246=4.\binom{4}{3} = \frac{24}{6} = 4.(34​)=624​=4.

That is the pattern behind every combination count: build the ordered version first, since you already know how to count that, then divide by however many orders share one unordered result.

The division principle. Suppose a procedure produces NNN outputs in total, and suppose every output is produced by exactly one object. Suppose also that every object you actually want to count is produced exactly ddd times, with the same ddd for every object. Then the number of objects is N/dN / dN/d.

Why an even overcount can simply be divided away#

Sort the NNN outputs into piles, putting two outputs in the same pile exactly when they came from the same object. Every output lands in exactly one pile, since it came from exactly one object, so the piles overlap nowhere and together hold all NNN outputs.

By hypothesis each object is produced exactly ddd times, so each pile holds exactly ddd outputs. Let MMM be the number of piles, which is the number of objects. Counting the outputs pile by pile gives MMM groups of ddd, so N=M×dN = M \times dN=M×d, and therefore M=N/dM = N / dM=N/d.

The requirement that ddd be the same for every object is the whole content of the rule. If some objects were produced twice and others three times, the piles would be uneven and no single division could repair the count.

∎

Now count (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​) in general, the same way the 444-letter example did it: count the ordered lists two different ways.

The direct count of (nk)=n!k! (n−k)!\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}(kn​)=k!(n−k)!n!​#

Fix nnn and kkk with 0≤k≤n0 \le k \le n0≤k≤n, and count the ordered lists of kkk distinct objects drawn from the nnn available, twice.

First count: fill the slots. A list is kkk ordered slots filled without repetition, so by the shrinking slot argument there are P(n,k)=n!/(n−k)!P(n, k) = n!/(n-k)!P(n,k)=n!/(n−k)! lists in all.

Second count: choose, then order. Build the same list in two stages instead. First choose the set of kkk objects that will appear in it, which by definition can be done in (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​) ways. Then choose the order in which to write that set out, which is an arrangement of kkk distinct objects in kkk slots, so it can be done in P(k,k)=k!P(k, k) = k!P(k,k)=k! ways.

The multiplication principle applies to these two stages, because the second stage offers k!k!k! orders no matter which set the first stage produced. And the two stages build every list exactly once. Each list determines its own set (its entries) and its own order (how they are written), so no list is built twice and none is missed. Hence the number of lists is also (nk)×k!\binom{n}{k} \times k!(kn​)×k!.

Equate the two counts. The same lists were counted both times, so

(nk)×k!=n!(n−k)!,\binom{n}{k} \times k! = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!},(kn​)×k!=(n−k)!n!​,

and dividing by k!k!k! isolates the number we wanted:

(nk)=P(n,k)k!=n!k! (n−k)!.\binom{n}{k} = \frac{P(n,k)}{k!} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}.(kn​)=k!P(n,k)​=k!(n−k)!n!​.

∎

Look at what the division did. Every set of kkk objects was built k!k!k! times, once for each order in which its members could be listed. Dividing by k!k!k! collapses those k!k!k! lists back into the single set they all describe. Dividing by k!k!k! is the act of forgetting the order. That sentence is the difference between a permutation and a combination, and there is nothing else to it. It is exactly what the 444-letter example above did by hand: 242424 ordered lists, divided by the 666 orders each set was written in, giving 444 sets.

Two facts drop out for free. Choosing which kkk objects to take is the same act as choosing which n−kn - kn−k objects to leave, so (nk)=(nn−k)\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}(kn​)=(n−kn​). The formula agrees because swapping kkk with n−kn-kn−k just swaps the two factorials in the denominator. And at the edges, (n0)=(nn)=1\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1(0n​)=(nn​)=1, since there is one way to take nothing and one way to take everything. The formula produces those 111‘s only because 0!=10! = 10!=1, which is the same forcing you saw a moment ago.

The two questions you must answer before choosing a row are whether rearranging your picks changes the outcome, and whether an object may be used twice. Neither question settles the row by itself. The first two rows are both ordered, so the order question alone leaves you stuck between them, and only the repetition question tells them apart.

Arranging objects that are not all distinct

The permutation formula assumed every object was distinguishable. Real problems break that assumption constantly, and the repair is the division principle again.

How many distinguishable arrangements does the word BANANA have? If the six letters were all different the answer would be 6!=7206! = 7206!=720, but they are not. There are three A’s and two N’s, and swapping two A’s produces the identical string. So 720720720 is an overcount, and the question is by how much.

Arrangements of nnn objects with repeats#

Suppose nnn objects come in rrr types, with n1n_1n1​ copies of the first type, n2n_2n2​ of the second, and so on up to nrn_rnr​, where n1+n2+⋯+nr=nn_1 + n_2 + \cdots + n_r = nn1​+n2​+⋯+nr​=n. Copies of the same type are identical. We count the distinguishable arrangements.

Tag the copies to make them temporarily distinct: call the three A’s of BANANA A1,A2,A3A_1, A_2, A_3A1​,A2​,A3​. All nnn tagged objects are now different, so they have n!n!n! arrangements.

Group those n!n!n! tagged arrangements by the untagged word they produce. Two tagged arrangements produce the same word exactly when they place the same type in the same positions. Two arrangements that produce the same word differ only in how the tags are distributed among the positions of each type. For a fixed word, the tags of type 111 can be scattered over its n1n_1n1​ positions in n1!n_1!n1​! ways. The tags of type 222 can be scattered over the word’s n2n_2n2​ positions in n2!n_2!n2​! ways, and so on. Placing one type’s tags does not change how many ways the next type’s tags can be placed, so by the multiplication principle each word arises from exactly

n1!×n2!×⋯×nr!n_1! \times n_2! \times \cdots \times n_r!n1​!×n2​!×⋯×nr​!

tagged arrangements. That number is the same for every word, since it depends only on the type counts.

The division principle now applies with N=n!N = n!N=n! and d=n1! n2!⋯nr!d = n_1!\,n_2!\cdots n_r!d=n1​!n2​!⋯nr​!, so the number of distinguishable arrangements is

n!n1! n2! ⋯ nr!.\frac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2!\,\cdots\,n_r!}.n1​!n2​!⋯nr​!n!​.

∎

For BANANA, n=6n = 6n=6 with three A’s, two N’s, and one B:

6!3! 2! 1!=7206×2×1=72012=60.\frac{6!}{3!\,2!\,1!} = \frac{720}{6 \times 2 \times 1} = \frac{720}{12} = 60.3!2!1!6!​=6×2×1720​=12720​=60.

Sixty arrangements, not 720720720. Divide by the factorials of the repeat counts, not by the repeat counts themselves: the three A’s contribute 3!=63! = 63!=6, not 333.

This formula quietly contains the binomial coefficient. Take just two types, kkk objects of one kind and n−kn - kn−k of the other, say kkk letters Y and n−kn-kn−k letters X. The count of arrangements is

n!k! (n−k)!=(nk),\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} = \binom{n}{k},k!(n−k)!n!​=(kn​),

which is no coincidence at all. Arranging kkk Y’s and n−kn-kn−k X’s in a row is precisely choosing which kkk of the nnn positions hold a Y. Back in the binomial theorem those positions were the factors of (x+y)n(x+y)^n(x+y)n that donate their yyy. The same number keeps answering the same question in different clothes.

39,916,8001,152=34,650.\frac{39{,}916{,}800}{1{,}152} = 34{,}650.1,15239,916,800​=34,650.

How many distinguishable arrangements are there of the letters in the word LETTER?

Answer choices

LETTER has 666 letters, but the two E's are identical and so are the two T's. If all six letters were different there would be 6!=7206! = 7206!=720 arrangements, and each actual word is produced 2!×2!=42! \times 2! = 42!×2!=4 times over by those two repeated pairs.

Complementary counting

Some sets are far easier to count by counting what you do not want. If every object either has a property or fails to have it, then those two cases overlap nowhere and cover everything. So the addition principle says the two counts add to the total. Rearranged, that is complementary counting:

(objects with the property)=(all objects)−(objects without it).(\text{objects with the property}) = (\text{all objects}) - (\text{objects without it}).(objects with the property)=(all objects)−(objects without it).

The flagship case is at least one. The opposite of “at least one” is “none”. And “none” is usually a single clean count, while “at least one” splits into a pile of cases (exactly one, exactly two, exactly three, and so on). Counting the total and subtracting the “none” case replaces all of that work with one subtraction.

It is also a guard against a specific and very common error. Faced with “at least one woman on the committee”, the tempting move is to appoint one woman first, then fill the rest of the committee freely. That method builds a committee with two women twice, once for each woman it could have appointed first. The division principle cannot repair the damage, because committees with three women are built three times and the overcount is uneven. The next example shows the two answers side by side.

Worked example 6 At least one woman on the committee

A committee of 444 is chosen from 666 men and 555 women. How many committees contain at least one woman?

The group has 111111 people in total, and a committee is an unordered set of 444, so there are

(114)=11×10×9×84×3×2×1=7,92024=330\binom{11}{4} = \frac{11 \times 10 \times 9 \times 8}{4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = \frac{7{,}920}{24} = 330(411​)=4×3×2×111×10×9×8​=247,920​=330

committees in all. The committees without a woman are made entirely of the 666 men:

(64)=6×5×4×34×3×2×1=15.\binom{6}{4} = \frac{6 \times 5 \times 4 \times 3}{4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = 15.(46​)=4×3×2×16×5×4×3​=15.

Every committee either has at least one woman or has none, never both, so subtract:

330−15=315.330 - 15 = 315.330−15=315.

There are 315315315 committees with at least one woman.

Now watch the tempting method fail. Appoint one of the 555 women, then fill the remaining 333 seats from the other 101010 people, giving 5×(103)=5×120=6005 \times \binom{10}{3} = 5 \times 120 = 6005×(310​)=5×120=600. That is nearly double the truth, because a committee holding the women Ana and Bea is produced twice. That committee is produced once by appointing Ana first and once by appointing Bea first, while a committee holding three women is produced three times. The overcount is uneven, so no single division fixes it. Subtracting the complement never runs into this problem.

Check your understanding

A coin is flipped 666 times, giving 26=642^6 = 6426=64 possible sequences of heads and tails. In how many of them does at least one head appear?

Answer choices

Counting the sequences with at least one head directly would mean adding up the cases with exactly one head, exactly two heads, and so on, up to six. Count the opposite instead.

The sequences with no head are the ones that are all tails, and there is exactly 111 of those. Subtract it from the total.

64−1=6364 - 1 = 6364−1=63

So 636363 sequences contain at least one head. Answering 666 counts only the sequences with exactly one head, which is a different and much smaller set.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Never asking whether order matters. This is the error that produces more wrong answers than every other error in counting combined. Ten people can shake hands in (102)=45\binom{10}{2} = 45(210​)=45 ways but can fill a president and a vice-president post in P(10,2)=90P(10,2) = 90P(10,2)=90 ways. The difference is that a handshake is a set and an election result is an ordered pair. Decide first whether rearranging your picks changes the outcome, and only then reach for a formula.
  • Dividing by k!k!k! when the order does matter. The k!k!k! divides out the orderings of a chosen set. If the kkk picks land in genuinely different roles (first prize and second prize, chair and secretary), those orderings are different outcomes and must not be divided away.
  • Using nkn^knk where objects cannot repeat, or a shrinking product where they can. Digits in a PIN may repeat, so a four-digit PIN has 10410^4104 settings. Runners on a podium cannot repeat, so three medals from eight runners give 8×7×68 \times 7 \times 68×7×6. Both of those counts are ordered, so asking about the order will not tell them apart. You have to ask the second question as well, whether an object may be used twice.
  • Dividing by the repeat count instead of its factorial. BANANA has three A’s and two N’s, so the count is 6!/(3! 2!)=606!/(3!\,2!) = 606!/(3!2!)=60. Dividing 6!6!6! by 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6 gives 120120120, which counts nothing.
  • Handling “at least one” by reserving one in advance. Choosing a guaranteed woman and then filling the rest of the committee freely counts every committee once for each woman it could have started with. So committees with several women are counted several times. Use total minus none.
  • Miscounting the factors in P(n,k)P(n,k)P(n,k). There are exactly kkk factors, running from nnn down to n−k+1n-k+1n−k+1. For P(9,4)P(9,4)P(9,4) that is 9×8×7×69 \times 8 \times 7 \times 69×8×7×6, which stops at 666, not at 555.

Key takeaways

  • The multiplication principle: suppose an object is built by kkk decisions, and suppose decision iii can be made in nin_ini​ ways regardless of the earlier decisions. Then the number of objects is n1n2⋯nkn_1 n_2 \cdots n_kn1​n2​⋯nk​. The number of options at each stage must not depend on the past; the options themselves may.
  • The addition principle: if the objects split into cases that overlap nowhere and miss nothing, add the case counts.
  • Repetition allowed: kkk slots each taking any of nnn values give nkn^knk outcomes.
  • Two questions choose the formula, and neither is enough on its own: does rearranging the picks change the outcome, and may an object be used twice?
  • Permutations (order matters, no repeats): P(n,k)=n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)=n!(n−k)!P(n,k) = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}P(n,k)=n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)=(n−k)!n!​, a product of exactly kkk factors. Arranging all nnn objects gives P(n,n)=n!P(n,n) = n!P(n,n)=n!, which is what the factorial counts.
  • 0!=10! = 10!=1 is forced, not chosen: it is the one value that keeps P(n,k)=n!/(n−k)!P(n,k) = n!/(n-k)!P(n,k)=n!/(n−k)! correct at k=nk = nk=n, and it matches the single empty arrangement of 000 objects.
  • The division principle: if a procedure produces NNN outputs, each output comes from exactly one object, and every object is produced exactly ddd times, then there are N/dN/dN/d objects.
  • Combinations (order does not matter): (nk)=P(n,k)k!=n!k! (n−k)!\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{P(n,k)}{k!} = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}(kn​)=k!P(n,k)​=k!(n−k)!n!​, because each set of kkk objects was listed k!k!k! times. Dividing by k!k!k! is the act of forgetting the order, and this is the same (nk)\dbinom{n}{k}(kn​) that gave the binomial coefficients.
  • Objects that are not all distinct: nnn objects in types of sizes n1,…,nrn_1, \ldots, n_rn1​,…,nr​ have n!n1! n2!⋯nr!\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2!\cdots n_r!}n1​!n2​!⋯nr​!n!​ distinguishable arrangements, as in 6!3! 2! 1!=60\dfrac{6!}{3!\,2!\,1!} = 603!2!1!6!​=60 for BANANA.
  • Complementary counting: count the objects you do not want and subtract, which is nearly always the way to handle at least one.

Why a sequence of fixed-count decisions multiplies, proved by induction on the number of stages

The soup, sandwich, and drink example showed the pattern by hand for three stages. This is the same argument made once and for all, for any number of stages kkk.

Why a sequence of fixed-count decisions multiplies#

Add the stages one at a time. With a single decision the count is n1n_1n1​, which is the claim for k=1k = 1k=1.

Now suppose the first k−1k-1k−1 decisions produce exactly N=n1n2⋯nk−1N = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_{k-1}N=n1​n2​⋯nk−1​ different partial objects, and bring in the last decision. Each of those NNN partial objects can be finished in exactly nkn_knk​ ways, since that is the hypothesis. The hypothesis says the number of options at the last stage does not depend on what came before it.

Those completions are all different, and for two separate reasons. Two completions of the same partial object differ in the final decision, so they are different. Two completions of different partial objects already differed before the final decision was made, so they are different too. And nothing is missed: any finished object comes from exactly one partial object, namely its own first k−1k-1k−1 decisions, followed by one last decision.

So the finished objects fall into NNN groups of nkn_knk​, one group per partial object, and the total is N×nk=n1n2⋯nkN \times n_k = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_kN×nk​=n1​n2​⋯nk​. Since each new stage multiplies the running total by its own option count, the product formula holds for every kkk.

A bit of history (optional)

European gamblers had mostly solved each game by its own trick, one problem at a time, though the Italian mathematician Gerolamo Cardano had already worked out odds for several dice games a century earlier, in a manuscript that stayed unpublished until 1663. In 1654 Blaise Pascal took the idea further: he worked out general properties of (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​) and applied them to fairly splitting a gambler’s interrupted stake, among other problems. His findings became the Treatise on the Arithmetical Triangle, composed and substantially printed in 1654 but not published until 1665, three years after his death.

Gottfried Leibniz, a young German lawyer, gave the field its name at twenty. His 1666 essay De Arte Combinatoria took the art of combinations far beyond dice. He wanted to break every idea into parts and recombine them to reach all possible truths. The essay promised far more than it delivered. The phrase survived anyway, and combinatorics still carries it.

A broader theory came from a Swiss professor named Jacob Bernoulli. His Ars Conjectandi was printed in 1713, eight years after he died. Its second part walks through permutations and combinations in general, proving the rules rather than collecting them case by case.

Ordinary language never caught up. A combination lock will not open unless you enter its numbers in the one exact order set for it. By the words of this lesson that is not a combination at all: it is an ordered code of length kkk over nnn symbols, counted by nkn^knk if a symbol may repeat in the code and by P(n,k)P(n,k)P(n,k) if it may not, but never by (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​). The everyday name has stuck regardless.

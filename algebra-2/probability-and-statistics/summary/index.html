Probability and Statistics: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Sample space SSS, outcome, event
The set of possible results, one result, and any subset A⊆SA \subseteq SA⊆S; "not", "or", "and" are AcA^{c}Ac, A∪BA \cup BA∪B, A∩BA \cap BA∩B.
Equally likely
Every outcome of SSS carries the same chance: an assumption about the world, not a theorem. One experiment has many sample spaces, usually one equally likely.
Mutually exclusive (disjoint)
A∩B=∅A \cap B = \emptysetA∩B=∅, so the events cannot both occur. A statement about sets; independence is one about numbers.
With and without replacement
Whether a drawn object is returned. With replacement the draws usually are independent; without, the second sees a reduced pool, which usually breaks independence for events about the same category, but not every pair.
Resistant and sensitive
A resistant statistic (median, IQR) holds steady against an outlier once the data set is big enough that the extreme sits outside the middle-position calculation; a sensitive one (mean, range, standard deviation) can be dragged by it regardless of size.
Right-skewed, left-skewed, bimodal
A long tail of large values, of small ones, or two separate peaks. Two peaks usually mean two mixed groups, which no single center describes.
Five-number summary and box plot
min\text{min}min, Q1Q_1Q1​, median, Q3Q_3Q3​, max\text{max}max, drawn as a box from Q1Q_1Q1​ to Q3Q_3Q3​, whiskers to the extremes inside the fences, outliers as points.
Density curve
The curve of a continuous quantity: probability is area under it, total area 111. Its height is not a probability.
φ\varphiφ and Φ\PhiΦ
φ(z)=12πe−z2/2\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}φ(z)=2π​1​e−z2/2 is the standard normal height at zzz; Φ(z)=P(Z<z)\Phi(z) = P(Z < z)Φ(z)=P(Z<z) is the area to its left, which a table records because no elementary antiderivative expresses the area using the ordinary algebra and calculus toolbox.
Central Limit Theorem
A sum of many independent small effects tends to the normal shape, provided none dominates and a few further technical conditions hold. Some heavy-tailed real quantities are not well modeled by a normal curve because their generating effects may violate these conditions.

Problem types, step by step

Choose the counting formula and apply it

  1. Does rearranging the picks change the outcome? Titles and prizes yes, a committee or hand no.
  2. May an object be used twice?
  3. Ordered with repeats nkn^knk; ordered without P(n,k)P(n,k)P(n,k); unordered without (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​).
  4. Multiply stage counts for a staged build; add case counts for disjoint kinds.

e.g. 333 distinct prizes among 888 people: 8×7×6=3368 \times 7 \times 6 = 3368×7×6=336; a 333-person committee from 888: (83)=56\binom{8}{3} = 56(38​)=56.

Arrange a word whose letters repeat

  1. Count the letters by type; check the counts add to nnn.
  2. Write n!n!n! over the product of the factorials of those counts.
  3. Cancel the largest factorial before multiplying out.

e.g. PEPPER, three P's and two E's: 6!3! 2!=60\frac{6!}{3!\,2!} = 603!2!6!​=60.

Count or find the probability of "at least one"

  1. Name the complement, which is "none".
  2. Count or compute that single case.
  3. Subtract from the total, or from 111 for a probability.

e.g. At least one A in a 444-letter string over 262626 letters: 264−254=6635126^4 - 25^4 = 66351264−254=66351; for 232323 birthdays, 1−365×364×⋯×34336523≈0.5071 - \frac{365 \times 364 \times \cdots \times 343}{365^{23}} \approx 0.5071−36523365×364×⋯×343​≈0.507.

Find a probability from combination counts

  1. Count the sample space as all equally likely selections.
  2. Build the favourable ones in stages and multiply the stage counts.
  3. Divide, then check the answer lies in [0,1][0,1][0,1].

e.g. Exactly 222 women on a 333-person committee from 555 women and 444 men: (52)(41)/(93)=1021\binom{5}{2}\binom{4}{1}/\binom{9}{3} = \frac{10}{21}(25​)(14​)/(39​)=2110​.

Chain two draws, with or without replacement

  1. Is the first object returned? That is a strong clue about independence, but check the product test.
  2. Returned: multiply the two unchanged probabilities.
  3. Not returned: P(B) P(A∣B)P(B)\,P(A \mid B)P(B)P(A∣B), the second factor off the reduced pool.

e.g. From 444 red and 666 blue, both red: 410×39=215\frac{4}{10} \times \frac{3}{9} = \frac{2}{15}104​×93​=152​ without replacement, 425\frac{4}{25}254​ with.

Test two events for independence

  1. Compute P(A)P(A)P(A), P(B)P(B)P(B), and P(A∩B)P(A \cap B)P(A∩B) from the sample space.
  2. Compare P(A∩B)P(A \cap B)P(A∩B) against the product P(A)P(B)P(A)P(B)P(A)P(B).
  3. Equal is independent, unequal dependent; disjoint events of positive probability are always dependent.

e.g. Two dice, AAA "first is 111": against "sum is 777", 136=16×16\frac{1}{36} = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{6}361​=61​×61​, independent; against "sum is 444", 136≠172\frac{1}{36} \neq \frac{1}{72}361​=721​, dependent.

Build a five-number summary and test for outliers

  1. Sort; read off the min and max.
  2. Median: average the two middle values for an even count.
  3. Q1,Q3Q_1, Q_3Q1​,Q3​: the medians of the halves, an odd count's middle value in neither.
  4. Compute the IQR and both fences; flag every value outside them.

e.g. 4,7,9,12,15,18,22,404, 7, 9, 12, 15, 18, 22, 404,7,9,12,15,18,22,40: median 13.513.513.5, Q1=8Q_1 = 8Q1​=8, Q3=20Q_3 = 20Q3​=20, IQR=12\text{IQR} = 12IQR=12, upper fence 383838, so 404040 is an outlier.

Compute a variance and a standard deviation

  1. Read the question for population or sample; it picks the divisor.
  2. Find the mean, list the deviations, check they sum to 000.
  3. Square and add them, divide by NNN or by n−1n-1n−1, then square-root.

e.g. 3,7,11,153, 7, 11, 153,7,11,15 as a population: mean 999, ∑(xi−9)2=80\sum (x_i - 9)^2 = 80∑(xi​−9)2=80, so σ2=20\sigma^2 = 20σ2=20, σ≈4.47\sigma \approx 4.47σ≈4.47.

Use the normal table in both directions

  1. Value to area: standardize each endpoint, z=x−μσz = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}z=σx−μ​, keeping the sign.
  2. Read Φ\PhiΦ at each zzz, with Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z)\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z); whole standard deviations can use the 68-95-99.7 bands instead.
  3. Assemble: left Φ(z)\Phi(z)Φ(z), right 1−Φ(z)1 - \Phi(z)1−Φ(z), between Φ(b)−Φ(a)\Phi(b) - \Phi(a)Φ(b)−Φ(a).

Exam traps

  • Trap Never asking whether order matters: ten people shaking hands counted as P(10,2)=90P(10,2) = 90P(10,2)=90.

    Fix A handshake is a set: (102)=45\binom{10}{2} = 45(210​)=45. The 909090 answers a different question, electing a president and vice-president.

  • Trap Adding for "or" without checking the overlap: king or heart as 452+1352=1752\frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} = \frac{17}{52}524​+5213​=5217​.

    Fix Subtract the intersection: 1652\frac{16}{52}5216​. Adding alone is legal only for disjoint events, and can otherwise exceed 111.

  • Trap Multiplying for "and" without checking independence: three hearts drawn without replacement as (14)3=164\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{64}(41​)3=641​.

    Fix The pool shrinks each draw: 1352×1251×1150=11850\frac{13}{52} \times \frac{12}{51} \times \frac{11}{50} = \frac{11}{850}5213​×5112​×5011​=85011​. Only P(B)P(A∣B)P(B)P(A \mid B)P(B)P(A∣B) needs no independence.

  • Trap Calling mutually exclusive events independent because neither seems to say anything about the other.

    Fix Disjoint events of positive probability are maximally dependent: P(A∣B)=0P(A \mid B) = 0P(A∣B)=0 while P(A)>0P(A) > 0P(A)>0. Independent ones of positive probability must overlap.

  • Trap Answering P(B∣A)P(B \mid A)P(B∣A) for P(A∣B)P(A \mid B)P(A∣B): a 99%99\%99% detection rate read as a 99%99\%99% chance of illness after a positive test.

    Fix They divide by different denominators: 111 case in 100010001000 with a 5%5\%5% false-positive rate leaves a positive patient ill under 2%2\%2% of the time.

  • Trap Reserving one in advance: at least one woman on a 333-person committee from 444 men and 333 women as 3×(62)=453 \times \binom{6}{2} = 453×(26​)=45.

    Fix That builds a two-woman committee twice and a three-woman one three times, an uneven overcount no division repairs. Total minus none: (73)−(43)=31\binom{7}{3} - \binom{4}{3} = 31(37​)−(34​)=31.

  • Trap Dividing by the repeat counts rather than their factorials: BANANA as 6!3×2=120\frac{6!}{3 \times 2} = 1203×26!​=120.

    Fix Divide by the factorials: 6!3! 2!=60\frac{6!}{3!\,2!} = 603!2!6!​=60.

  • Trap Reporting the mean as the typical value of skewed data.

    Fix The mean follows the tail, a reliable rule of thumb rather than a theorem: sales of 220,245,260,285,990220, 245, 260, 285, 990220,245,260,285,990 average 400400400, above four of five. Report the median, 260260260, with the IQR.

  • Trap Dividing by nnn when the numbers are a sample.

    Fix A sample takes its deviations from the mean it fitted to itself, so they are never too large. Divide by n−1n-1n−1; reserve NNN for a population.

  • Trap Applying the 68-95-99.7 rule to right-skewed incomes.

    Fix The rule describes approximately normal data only. For skewed data report the median and IQR, and use a fence, not a band.

  • Trap Reading the table entry as the tail wanted: P(Z>1.4)P(Z > 1.4)P(Z>1.4) answered 0.91920.91920.9192, or 0.41920.41920.4192 by subtracting 0.50.50.5 from it.

    Fix Φ\PhiΦ is the area to the LEFT and the total is 111: P(Z>1.4)=1−0.9192=0.0808P(Z > 1.4) = 1 - 0.9192 = 0.0808P(Z>1.4)=1−0.9192=0.0808. A negative zzz needs Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z)\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z).

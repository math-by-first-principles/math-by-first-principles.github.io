  1. Problem 1 Two order rules and a separation rule

    ★ Stretch Difficulty: 1 of 3 stars, Stretch

    Six distinct cards numbered 1,2,3,4,5,61,2,3,4,5,61,2,3,4,5,6 are placed in a row. How many arrangements have 111 somewhere to the left of 222, have 333 somewhere to the left of 444, and do not place 111 and 222 next to each other? Explain why every division or multiplication in your count is valid.

    1. Hint 1

      First count arrangements satisfying just the two left-to-right rules.

    2. Hint 2

      Among those arrangements, forbidden neighbors can occur only as the ordered block 121212. Treat this block as one object.

    Answer

    120120120 arrangements.

    Full solution

    There are 6!=7206!=7206!=720 unrestricted arrangements.

    Swapping the labels 111 and 222 pairs every arrangement with one having the opposite order of that pair.

    Thus exactly half have 111 before 222.

    Within that half, swapping 333 and 444 preserves the first condition and reverses the second, so exactly half also have 333 before 444.

    This gives 720/4=180720/4=180720/4=180.

    Now remove the arrangements in which 111 and 222 are neighbors.

    Since 111 must precede 222, they form the single ordered block 121212.

    Together with 3,4,5,63,4,5,63,4,5,6 this gives five distinct objects.

    Exactly half of their 5!5!5! orders have 333 before 444, by the same label-swap pairing.

    There are therefore 5!/2=605!/2=605!/2=60 forbidden arrangements.

    The desired count is

    180−60=120.180-60=120.180−60=120.

    Treating adjacency as an unordered block would incorrectly double the subtracted count: the block 212121 was never among the 180180180 arrangements.

    The pairing explanations also avoid assuming that unrelated-looking restrictions are automatically independent.

    Answer

    120120120 arrangements.

    Key idea

    A symmetry count needs a bijection that preserves every restriction already imposed.

  2. Problem 2 Evidence from an unmarked bag

    ★ Stretch Difficulty: 1 of 3 stars, Stretch

    Bag AAA contains four red and two blue counters; bag BBB contains two red and four blue counters. A fair coin selects a bag, and then two counters are drawn uniformly at random from that same bag without replacement. Both drawn counters are red.

    (a) Given this evidence, what is the probability that bag AAA was selected?

    (b) Repeat the experiment with replacement: return the first counter before an independent uniform second draw. If both are red, what is the new conditional probability? Explain why the two answers differ.

    1. Hint 1

      Compute the probability of two reds under each bag separately, then include the equal bag-selection probabilities.

    2. Hint 2

      Conditioning keeps only the two-red outcomes. The relative likelihoods of that evidence, rather than the original equal chances of the bags, determine the answer.

    Answer

    (a) 6/76/76/7. (b) 4/54/54/5.

    Full solution

    Without replacement, the two-red probabilities are (4/6)(3/5)=2/5(4/6)(3/5)=2/5(4/6)(3/5)=2/5 from AAA and (2/6)(1/5)=1/15(2/6)(1/5)=1/15(2/6)(1/5)=1/15 from BBB.

    After including the fair bag choice, the probabilities of the joint events are 1/51/51/5 and 1/301/301/30.

    The probability of the evidence is their sum, 7/307/307/30.

    Therefore

    P(A∣two reds)=1/57/30=67.P(A\mid\text{two reds})=\frac{1/5}{7/30}=\frac67.P(A∣two reds)=7/301/5​=76​.

    With replacement, the second draw has the original color probabilities and is independent of the first, conditional on the chosen bag.

    The likelihoods are now (4/6)2=4/9(4/6)^2=4/9(4/6)2=4/9 and

    (2/6)2=1/9.(2/6)^2=1/9.(2/6)2=1/9.

    Including the equal bag weights and conditioning gives

    (4/9)/(4/9+1/9)=4/5.(4/9)/(4/9+1/9)=4/5.(4/9)/(4/9+1/9)=4/5.

    Without replacement, a first red removes half of bag BBB's red counters but only one quarter of bag AAA's.

    A second red is consequently stronger evidence for AAA in that experiment: the likelihood ratio is 6:16:16:1 instead of 4:14:14:1.

    Equal prior chances do not remain equal after evidence is observed.

    Answer

    (a) 6/76/76/7. (b) 4/54/54/5.

    Key idea

    Conditional probabilities depend on how evidence was generated; replacement changes the likelihood even when the bag contents stay the same.

  3. Problem 3 An impossible statistical report

    ★ Stretch Difficulty: 1 of 3 stars, Stretch

    A report claims that five real measurements have mean 121212, population variance 888, and include the value 181818. Here population variance means the sum of the squared deviations from the mean divided by 555.

  4. Problem 4 Arranging a repeated-letter code

    ★★ Challenge Difficulty: 2 of 3 stars, Challenge

    A code uses exactly three copies of AAA, two copies of BBB, and two copies of CCC. Copies of the same letter are indistinguishable. How many distinct seven-letter codes have no adjacent AAA's and no adjacent BBB's? Adjacent CCC's are allowed.

    If a code is selected uniformly from all distinct arrangements of these seven letters, what is the probability that it satisfies both restrictions? Explain why your construction counts each allowed code once.

    1. Hint 1

      Delete the three AAA's from an allowed code. What four-letter skeleton remains?

    2. Hint 2

      Insert the AAA's into three different gaps of a BBCCBBCCBBCC skeleton. If the two BBB's touch in the skeleton, their intervening gap must be chosen.

    Answer

    484848 allowed codes, with probability 8/358/358/35.

    Full solution

    First arrange the non-AAA letters B,B,C,CB,B,C,CB,B,C,C.

    There are 4!/(2!2!)=64!/(2!2!)=64!/(2!2!)=6 skeletons, each with five gaps: before the first letter, between successive letters, and after the last.

    To avoid adjacent AAA's, insert one AAA into each of three distinct gaps.

    Three skeletons have adjacent BBB's: BBCC,CBBC,CCBBBBCC,CBBC,CCBBBBCC,CBBC,CCBB.

    For each, the gap between the two BBB's must receive an AAA.

    The other two occupied gaps can be any two of the remaining four, giving (42)=6\binom42=6(24​)=6 choices.

    In the other three skeletons the BBB's are already separated, so any three of the five gaps work: (53)=10\binom53=10(35​)=10 choices.

    There are therefore 3⋅6+3⋅10=483\cdot6+3\cdot10=483⋅6+3⋅10=48 allowed codes.

    Deleting every AAA from a finished code recovers its unique skeleton, and the positions of the deleted letters recover its occupied gaps.

    This proves that the construction neither duplicates nor omits an allowed code.

    All distinct codes number

    7!/(3!2!2!)=210.7!/(3!2!2!)=210.7!/(3!2!2!)=210.

    Under the stated uniform selection, the probability is

    48/210=8/35.48/210=8/35.48/210=8/35.

    One should divide by 210210210, not by 7!7!7!, because identical-letter permutations are not different outcomes.

    Answer

    484848 allowed codes, with probability 8/358/358/35.

    Key idea

    Deleting one letter type can expose a smaller skeleton whose gaps encode the remaining restrictions.

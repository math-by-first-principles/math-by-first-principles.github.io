The Factor Theorem

Intuition

Take P(x)=x2−5x+6P(x) = x^2 - 5x + 6P(x)=x2−5x+6. Plug in x=2x = 2x=2 and P(2)=4−10+6=0P(2) = 4 - 10 + 6 = 0P(2)=4−10+6=0, so 222 is a root. That same polynomial factors as P(x)=(x−2)(x−3)P(x) = (x - 2)(x - 3)P(x)=(x−2)(x−3), so x−2x - 2x−2 is a factor. The root showed up exactly where the factor did, and that is not a coincidence about this one polynomial.

The previous lesson showed why: dividing P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c always leaves the remainder P(c)P(c)P(c), a number you can find without dividing at all. When P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0, the division leaves nothing behind, so x−cx - cx−c divides P(x)P(x)P(x) exactly and is a factor. This lesson proves the rule works both ways: a root gives you a factor, and a factor gives you a root. Find one root of a cubic, divide it out, and what remains is a quadratic you already know how to finish.

Learning goals

  • State the master identity P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+P(c)P(x) = (x - c)Q(x) + P(c)P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+P(c)
  • Decide whether any linear expression, monic or not, is a factor of P(x)P(x)P(x) with one evaluation
  • Turn one known root into a factor by division, then keep searching inside the smaller quotient
  • Build a polynomial from prescribed real and nonreal roots plus one extra condition
  • Explain why a nonzero polynomial of degree nnn has at most nnn roots

Roots and factors, two languages

The small example above is the pattern; here is the general rule behind it. Two definitions sit at the center of this chapter, and they live in different worlds. A number ccc is a root (or zero) of the polynomial P(x)P(x)P(x) when P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0. That is an arithmetic statement: plug in one number, get zero out. By contrast, a polynomial D(x)D(x)D(x) is a factor of P(x)P(x)P(x) when

P(x)=D(x) Q(x)for some polynomial Q(x),P(x) = D(x)\,Q(x) \qquad \text{for some polynomial } Q(x),P(x)=D(x)Q(x)for some polynomial Q(x),

exactly as 333 is a factor of 121212 because 12=3⋅412 = 3 \cdot 412=3⋅4 with nothing left over. That is an algebraic statement about an identity that must hold for every value of xxx at once. One definition asks about a single evaluation; the other asks about an exact division. There is no obvious reason these two ideas should have anything to do with each other. Yet the theorem of this lesson says that, for linear divisors, they are the very same thing.

The bridge between the two worlds was built in the last two lessons. Dividing P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c produces a quotient and a remainder,

P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)+R,P(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x) + R,P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+R,

where the remainder has degree smaller than the divisor x−cx - cx−c, so RRR is a constant. The remainder theorem then identifies which constant: substituting x=cx = cx=c kills the first term and leaves R=P(c)R = P(c)R=P(c). So for every polynomial P(x)P(x)P(x) and every number ccc there is a single master identity,

P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)+P(c),P(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x) + P(c),P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+P(c),

and everything in this lesson is a consequence of reading it carefully.

Remainder 000 would mean x−4x - 4x−4 divides evenly, which is not what happened here. The quotient's value Q(4)Q(4)Q(4) is computed from a different expression entirely and need not match the remainder.

The Factor Theorem

The Factor Theorem. Let P(x)P(x)P(x) be a polynomial and let ccc be a number. Then x−cx - cx−c is a factor of P(x)P(x)P(x) if and only if P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0.

Take the phrase “if and only if” seriously: it is two separate claims bundled into one sentence. It claims that a root produces a factor, and it also claims that a factor produces a root. A statement of this shape is only proved when both directions are proved, so the proof below does them one at a time.

Proof of the Factor Theorem, both directions#

Divide P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c. As recalled above, the division algorithm and the remainder theorem together give the identity

P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)+P(c),P(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x) + P(c),P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+P(c),

valid for every xxx, where Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) is the quotient.

First suppose P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0. The identity collapses to P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)P(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x)P(x)=(x−c)Q(x), which is precisely the definition of ”x−cx - cx−c is a factor of P(x)P(x)P(x)”: the division came out exact, and Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) is the other factor. This is the direction that manufactures factorizations out of roots.

Now suppose instead that x−cx - cx−c is a factor of P(x)P(x)P(x), so that P(x)=(x−c) S(x)P(x) = (x - c)\,S(x)P(x)=(x−c)S(x) for some polynomial S(x)S(x)S(x). A factorization is an identity, true for every value of xxx, so it is in particular true at x=cx = cx=c:

P(c)=(c−c) S(c)=0⋅S(c)=0.P(c) = (c - c)\,S(c) = 0 \cdot S(c) = 0.P(c)=(c−c)S(c)=0⋅S(c)=0.

So ccc is a root. Notice this direction did not even need the remainder theorem, only the freedom to evaluate an identity at the most convenient point. You can also see it through remainders: if the division by x−cx - cx−c is exact, the remainder is 000, and the remainder theorem says the remainder is P(c)P(c)P(c), so P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0. Both directions are established, so the theorem holds as a genuine biconditional.

∎

The two directions do different jobs, and you will use both constantly. Read left to right, a known factorization hands you roots for free: from P(x)=(x−2)(x+7)Q(x)P(x) = (x - 2)(x + 7)Q(x)P(x)=(x−2)(x+7)Q(x) you may write down P(2)=0P(2) = 0P(2)=0 and P(−7)=0P(-7) = 0P(−7)=0 without computing anything. Read right to left, a known root hands you a factor, together with a genuine factorization P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)P(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x)P(x)=(x−c)Q(x) whose quotient you can compute by synthetic or long division. And because the theorem is a biconditional, one evaluation settles the factor question in either direction: P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0 means x−cx - cx−c is a factor, and P(c)≠0P(c) \neq 0P(c)=0 means it is not. Nothing is left undecided.

One small generalization is worth recording now, because factors are not always monic. To test whether 2x−12x - 12x−1 is a factor of P(x)P(x)P(x), find the number that makes 2x−12x - 12x−1 equal to 000: that is x=12x = \tfrac{1}{2}x=21​. Evaluate there. P ⁣(12)=0P\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 0P(21​)=0 means 2x−12x - 12x−1 is a factor; anything else means it is not. The rule of thumb covers every case: test the value that kills the candidate factor. For x+5x + 5x+5 that value is −5-5−5, because x+5=x−(−5)x + 5 = x - (-5)x+5=x−(−5).

The general version, for anyone who wants to see why it works: for a≠0a \neq 0a=0,

ax−b=a(x−ba),ax - b = a\left(x - \tfrac{b}{a}\right),ax−b=a(x−ab​),

and multiplying a divisor by a nonzero constant never changes whether a division is exact: if P(x)=(x−ba)Q(x)P(x) = \left(x - \tfrac{b}{a}\right)Q(x)P(x)=(x−ab​)Q(x), then P(x)=(ax−b)⋅1aQ(x)P(x) = (ax - b) \cdot \tfrac{1}{a}Q(x)P(x)=(ax−b)⋅a1​Q(x), and 1aQ(x)\tfrac{1}{a}Q(x)a1​Q(x) is still a polynomial. So ax−bax - bax−b is a factor of P(x)P(x)P(x) exactly when P ⁣(ba)=0P\!\left(\tfrac{b}{a}\right) = 0P(ab​)=0, matching the shortcut above.

One root unlocks the whole factorization

Here is the workflow that makes the factor theorem the workhorse of this chapter. Suppose you know, or are told, a single root ccc of a polynomial of degree nnn. The theorem promises the factorization P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)P(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x)P(x)=(x−c)Q(x), and division delivers Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) explicitly, a polynomial of degree n−1n - 1n−1. You have traded one problem for a strictly smaller one. Now hunt for a root of QQQ, peel again, and keep going until what remains is a quadratic, where factoring, the quadratic formula, and complex numbers finish the job completely.

Building a polynomial to order

The factor theorem also runs in reverse: instead of dissecting a given polynomial, you can manufacture one with any roots you choose. Each prescribed root rrr contributes a factor x−rx - rx−r, and the product of those factors has every prescribed number as a root. The reason is the easy direction of the theorem: evaluate the product at rrr, and the factor x−rx - rx−r makes the whole product zero.

A graph shows what you are really building. The real roots of a polynomial are exactly the xxx-intercepts of its graph, because “the graph meets the axis at ccc” and "P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0" are the same sentence. So a factored polynomial is a curve whose axis crossings you have pinned down in advance. The cubic below crosses cleanly through the axis at each root because each factor appears only once; a root can also touch the axis without crossing it when its factor repeats, which the next chapter takes up.

One caution before the examples: a list of roots does not pin down one polynomial. The cubics (x+1)(x−2)(x−4)(x + 1)(x - 2)(x - 4)(x+1)(x−2)(x−4) and 2(x+1)(x−2)(x−4)2(x + 1)(x - 2)(x - 4)2(x+1)(x−2)(x−4) have identical roots, and so does every other nonzero multiple. The construction really produces a whole family,

P(x)=a (x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rk),a≠0,P(x) = a\,(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_k), \qquad a \neq 0,P(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rk​),a=0,

one polynomial for each choice of the leading coefficient aaa, every one of them of degree kkk.

Leaving the degree free is worse than just an open leading coefficient: a higher-degree polynomial can repeat one of the factors and still have the same distinct roots and pass through the same point. Compare P(x)=(x−1)(x−3)P(x) = (x - 1)(x - 3)P(x)=(x−1)(x−3) with R(x)=−(x−1)2(x−3)R(x) = -(x - 1)^2(x - 3)R(x)=−(x−1)2(x−3). Both have exactly the roots 111 and 333, and both satisfy P(0)=R(0)=3P(0) = R(0) = 3P(0)=R(0)=3, yet PPP has degree 222 and RRR has degree 333. So a problem that wants one specific polynomial has to do two things. It has to hold the degree at kkk, which is what asking for a cubic with three roots, or for the least degree those roots allow, already does. And it has to supply one more condition to choose aaa, such as the leading coefficient itself or a point the graph passes through. That point has to earn its keep. If its xxx-value is one of the prescribed roots, P=0P = 0P=0 there no matter what aaa is, so it settles nothing. And its yyy-value should not be 000 at a new xxx-value either: solving for aaa would then force a=0a = 0a=0, which breaks the rule a≠0a \neq 0a=0 and means no polynomial of that exact degree with exactly those roots reaches that point. A usable extra point has an xxx-value outside the root list and a nonzero yyy-value.

Worked example 5 Building with a nonreal root

Find the monic cubic with real coefficients that has 333 and 1+2i1 + 2i1+2i among its roots.

Two roots are given, but a cubic needs three linear factors, and the missing one comes for free. For a polynomial with real coefficients, a nonreal root always arrives with its conjugate: since 1+2i1 + 2i1+2i is a root, 1−2i1 - 2i1−2i must be one too. (The reason: conjugating both sides of the equation P(z)=0P(z) = 0P(z)=0 conjugates nothing but zzz itself, because conjugation passes through sums and products and leaves real coefficients fixed. That argument, proved for quadratics in the complex numbers chapter, uses nothing about degree, so it applies here as well.)

A nonreal root and its conjugate instead merge into a real quadratic factor, here x2−2x+5x^2 - 2x + 5x2−2x+5, whose discriminant is negative. The next chapter builds its whole theory of real polynomials on this pairing.

(x−i)(x+i)=x2−i2=x2+1(x - i)(x + i) = x^2 - i^2 = x^2 + 1(x−i)(x+i)=x2−i2=x2+1

A ceiling on the number of roots

The factor theorem’s first big structural consequence is a hard limit. A quadratic never has three roots, a cubic never has four, and the pattern is completely general.

Here is why, in one small case. Take a cubic, degree 333, and suppose it has three different roots. Each root peels off one linear factor, and each peel drops the degree by exactly 111: start at degree 333, peel once to reach degree 222, peel again to reach degree 111, peel a third time to reach degree 000, a nonzero constant with no xxx left in it to make it vanish anywhere. There is no degree left to peel a fourth time, so a fourth distinct root is impossible, unless the cubic was the zero polynomial to begin with. The general argument below just runs this same budget for any degree nnn.

A nonzero polynomial of degree nnn has at most nnn roots#

Let P(x)P(x)P(x) be a nonzero polynomial of degree nnn, and let r1,r2,…,rkr_1, r_2, \ldots, r_kr1​,r2​,…,rk​ be distinct roots of it. Because r1r_1r1​ is a root, the factor theorem writes

P(x)=(x−r1) Q1(x),P(x) = (x - r_1)\,Q_1(x),P(x)=(x−r1​)Q1​(x),

and comparing degrees on the two sides, Q1Q_1Q1​ has degree n−1n - 1n−1. Now evaluate this identity at the second root:

0=P(r2)=(r2−r1) Q1(r2).0 = P(r_2) = (r_2 - r_1)\,Q_1(r_2).0=P(r2​)=(r2​−r1​)Q1​(r2​).

The first factor is not zero, because the roots are distinct, and a product of two numbers is zero only when one of them is zero. So Q1(r2)=0Q_1(r_2) = 0Q1​(r2​)=0, and the factor theorem applies again, this time to Q1Q_1Q1​: we get Q1(x)=(x−r2) Q2(x)Q_1(x) = (x - r_2)\,Q_2(x)Q1​(x)=(x−r2​)Q2​(x) with Q2Q_2Q2​ of degree n−2n - 2n−2, hence

Repeat the same evaluation argument with r3,r4,…r_3, r_4, \ldotsr3​,r4​,…, each time inside the newest quotient. Every distinct root peels off one more linear factor, and every peel lowers the quotient’s degree by exactly 111. After all kkk roots are used,

∎

Read the theorem for what it is: a ceiling, not a promise. It says a degree-nnn polynomial cannot have more than nnn roots; it is silent about how many actually exist. The quadratic x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1 has no real roots at all, and no theorem in this lesson objects, because zero is comfortably at most two. The factor theorem converts roots into factors and factors into roots, but it never manufactures a root out of nothing. Two questions stay open here: whether roots must exist at all, and how many a degree-nnn polynomial is guaranteed once complex numbers are allowed. Both are the business of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, the headline of the next chapter.

The ceiling still has real bite. If someone hands you a cubic and four distinct numbers where it vanishes, you may conclude the “cubic” is actually the zero polynomial, no computation required. And in Worked Example 1, once the roots 222, 333, and −1-1−1 were in hand, the search was legitimately over: a cubic has no room for a fourth root.

Check your understanding

A polynomial P(x)P(x)P(x) is claimed to have degree at most 333, and it is also known to vanish at the five distinct numbers −2,−1,0,1,2-2, -1, 0, 1, 2−2,−1,0,1,2. What must be true?

Answer choices

A nonzero polynomial of degree at most 333 has at most 333 roots. Five distinct roots is too many for any nonzero polynomial of that degree, so the only polynomial of degree at most 333 that can vanish at all five numbers is the zero polynomial, P(x)=0P(x) = 0P(x)=0 for every xxx.

The ceiling is not about what degree the polynomial "really" is; the degree was fixed at 333 or below by the claim. Too many roots for that ceiling forces the polynomial itself to be zero, not the ceiling to bend.

There is one honest gap left in the workflow, and you should notice it. Everything above starts from one known root, and in this lesson the root was always handed to you. Where does the first root come from when nobody supplies it? Testing numbers blindly could take forever. The next lesson closes exactly this gap: for polynomials with integer coefficients, the Rational Root Theorem shrinks the infinite search to a short finite list of rational candidates. The factor theorem then processes each of those candidates with a single evaluation.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Testing the wrong value for the factor’s sign. The factor x+5x + 5x+5 vanishes at x=−5x = -5x=−5, so test P(−5)P(-5)P(−5), never P(5)P(5)P(5). Write x+5=x−(−5)x + 5 = x - (-5)x+5=x−(−5) if the signs blur: the ccc in the theorem is −5-5−5.
  • Reporting a factor as if it were the factorization. Learning that 222 is a root gives P(x)=(x−2) Q(x)P(x) = (x - 2)\,Q(x)P(x)=(x−2)Q(x), not P(x)=x−2P(x) = x - 2P(x)=x−2. The quotient carries the rest of the polynomial; divide to find it and keep factoring.
  • Hunting the next root in the original instead of the quotient. After peeling off x−cx - cx−c, work inside Q(x)Q(x)Q(x): it has smaller degree, smaller numbers, and one fewer root to find. Returning to PPP re-finds the root you already used.
  • Forgetting the zero coefficients in synthetic division. The cubic x3−7x+6x^3 - 7x + 6x3−7x+6 has synthetic row 1,0,−7,61, 0, -7, 61,0,−7,6. Dropping the 000 silently divides a different polynomial.
  • Believing a root list determines the polynomial. (x−1)(x−3)(x - 1)(x - 3)(x−1)(x−3) and 7(x−1)(x−3)7(x - 1)(x - 3)7(x−1)(x−3) have the same roots. A polynomial of degree nnn with nnn different roots is fixed by those roots only up to its leading coefficient. When the degree is left free instead, the roots settle even less than that. A construction problem has to pin the degree AND supply one more condition.
  • Expecting the theorem to find roots. The factor theorem is a converter, not a search engine: it never asserts a root exists. Candidates come from elsewhere, and the next lesson provides the standard supply for integer coefficients.
  • Pairing conjugates without real coefficients. The rule “nonreal roots arrive with their conjugates” is proved from the coefficients being real. A polynomial like x2−2ix−1x^2 - 2ix - 1x2−2ix−1, with the double root iii and no root −i-i−i, breaks the rule because its coefficients are not real.

A bit of history (optional)

For most of algebra’s history an equation was a thing to solve, never a thing to build. You were handed a heap of terms and asked which value made it true.

Thomas Harriot, an English mathematician who had surveyed the Roanoke colony in Virginia, worked the other way. He wrote down two factors and multiplied them out. Then he read the equation he had just made. Its solutions sat in plain view, inside the factors he began with. Harriot published almost nothing. Colleagues assembled his pages years after his death. That is the build to order section of this lesson, arriving four centuries early.

Rene Descartes turned the observation into a rule. La Geometrie, the mathematical appendix to his 1637 book on how to reason, states the trade both ways. A polynomial that vanishes at a value is divisible by the matching linear factor. A polynomial divisible by that factor vanishes there. Descartes then used the rule as you will. Find one root, divide it away, and work on the smaller thing left.

The idea was rawer then than it looks now. Descartes still called negative roots false roots. So half the numbers you feed this theorem were, on his own page, faintly disreputable. The biconditional you proved today did not arrive as tidy theory. It arrived as a practical way to wear an equation down, one degree at a time.

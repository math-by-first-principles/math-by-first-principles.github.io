Synthetic Division and the Remainder Theorem

Intuition

Divide 2x3−5x2+3x−72x^3 - 5x^2 + 3x - 72x3−5x2+3x−7 by x−3x - 3x−3 with long division and watch what your pen actually writes. It writes the letter xxx dozens of times, along with exponents, plus signs, and a staircase of subtractions, while the only numbers doing any work are the coefficients. Synthetic division is what survives when every symbol that carries no information is deleted: three short rows of arithmetic that produce the same quotient and the same remainder. Its bottom-right corner also hides one of the most useful facts in this chapter, the Remainder Theorem. That theorem says the remainder of dividing P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c is exactly the number P(c)P(c)P(c). One small tableau hands you the quotient, the remainder, and a fast evaluation of the polynomial, all at once.

Learning goals

  • Run the tableau: bring down, multiply, add, repeat
  • Match it to long division through the coefficient recurrence
  • Apply the Remainder Theorem, since the remainder is P(c)P(c)P(c)

What long division keeps rewriting

Run the long division from the opening example again, 2x3−5x2+3x−72x^3 - 5x^2 + 3x - 72x3−5x2+3x−7 divided by x−3x - 3x−3, but keep an eye on which parts of the writing are decisions and which parts are ritual. Each stage picks the next quotient term, multiplies it by x−3x - 3x−3, and subtracts:

2x3−5x2+3x−7=(x−3)⋅2x2+(x2+3x−7),x2+3x−7=(x−3)⋅x+(6x−7),6x−7=(x−3)⋅6+11.\begin{aligned} 2x^3 - 5x^2 + 3x - 7 &= (x - 3)\cdot 2x^2 + \bigl(x^2 + 3x - 7\bigr), \\[2pt] x^2 + 3x - 7 &= (x - 3)\cdot x + \bigl(6x - 7\bigr), \\[2pt] 6x - 7 &= (x - 3)\cdot 6 + 11. \end{aligned}2x3−5x2+3x−7x2+3x−76x−7​=(x−3)⋅2x2+(x2+3x−7),=(x−3)⋅x+(6x−7),=(x−3)⋅6+11.​

Stacking the three stages gives 2x3−5x2+3x−7=(x−3)(2x2+x+6)+112x^3 - 5x^2 + 3x - 7 = (x - 3)(2x^2 + x + 6) + 112x3−5x2+3x−7=(x−3)(2x2+x+6)+11, so the quotient is 2x2+x+62x^2 + x + 62x2+x+6 and the remainder is 111111.

Now look at what each subtraction really did. Multiplying the first quotient term 2x22x^22x2 by x−3x - 3x−3 gives 2x3−6x22x^3 - 6x^22x3−6x2, and subtracting it is exactly why the leading terms cancel: that half of every step carries no information at all. The other half changes the next coefficient from −5-5−5 to −5−(−6)=−5+3⋅2=1-5 - (-6) = -5 + 3\cdot 2 = 1−5−(−6)=−5+3⋅2=1, since subtracting −6x2-6x^2−6x2 is the same as adding 6x26x^26x2, which is 333 times the new quotient coefficient 222. Strip away the guaranteed cancellation and here is the entire computation, written in bare numbers. The leading coefficient 222 came straight down, and after that each new number was the next coefficient of the dividend plus 333 times the previous new number:

−5+3⋅2=1,3+3⋅1=6,−7+3⋅6=11.-5 + 3 \cdot 2 = 1, \qquad 3 + 3 \cdot 1 = 6, \qquad -7 + 3 \cdot 6 = 11.−5+3⋅2=1,3+3⋅1=6,−7+3⋅6=11.

The numbers 2,1,62, 1, 62,1,6 are precisely the quotient coefficients, and the final number 111111 is the remainder. Long division computed nothing else. Synthetic division is a layout that computes exactly this chain and refuses to write anything more.

The tableau: bring down, multiply, add

To divide a polynomial P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c, build a three-row tableau:

  1. In the corner, write ccc, the number that makes the divisor zero. For x−3x - 3x−3 that is 333; for x+2x + 2x+2, which is x−(−2)x - (-2)x−(−2), it is −2-2−2. The corner always holds the divisor’s sign already flipped, and that is why the tableau will add where long division subtracted.
  2. Across the top row, write the coefficients of P(x)P(x)P(x) in descending order of degree, inserting a 000 for every missing power. Skipping a placeholder shifts every later column into the wrong place, the same way dropping a zero digit corrupts a base-ten numeral.
  3. Then follow the rhythm: bring down the leading coefficient; multiply it by ccc and write the product under the next coefficient; add down that column. Next, multiply the sum by ccc again, and repeat that pattern until the last column is added.

For the division above, the finished tableau is

32−53−7631821611\begin{array}{c|rrrr} 3 & 2 & -5 & 3 & -7 \\[2pt] & & 6 & 3 & 18 \\[2pt] \hline & 2 & 1 & 6 & 11 \end{array}3​22​−561​336​−71811​​

Read the bottom row from the left. The last number is the remainder. Everything before it lists the quotient coefficients, starting one degree below the dividend: here 2,1,62, 1, 62,1,6 means Q(x)=2x2+x+6Q(x) = 2x^2 + x + 6Q(x)=2x2+x+6, with remainder 111111. The degree drops by one because the divisor spent one power of xxx: a cubic divided by a linear polynomial has a quadratic quotient.

Why the shortcut is exactly long division

A pattern spotted in one example is not a proof, but the reason it always works is short. At every step, the new bottom number is the next coefficient of P(x)P(x)P(x) plus ccc times the previous bottom number, exactly the rule that turned −5,3,−7-5, 3, -7−5,3,−7 into 1,6,111, 6, 111,6,11 above. That rule matches long division step for step: long division’s leading term always cancels for free, and subtracting the divisor’s −c-c−c term is the same as adding ccc times the last quotient coefficient. Chain these small steps together, the same way the three lines of long division were stacked earlier, and they rebuild the full identity P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)+rP(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x) + rP(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+r.

The paragraph above shows the pattern using one cubic’s numbers. A proof written in symbols instead of numbers confirms that the tableau’s recurrence rebuilds P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+rP(x) = (x - c)Q(x) + rP(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+r for every polynomial and every ccc, coefficient by coefficient, and that QQQ and rrr are the only quotient and remainder that can do it. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the general proof

Long division’s leading-term cancellations are exactly the terms this shortcut never bothers to write, and long division’s subtraction of a −c-c−c multiple is the same as the tableau’s addition of a +c+c+c multiple. The tableau is not a different method that happens to agree with long division. It is long division, holding only the numbers that matter.

The Remainder Theorem

The spot check in Worked Example 2 was no coincidence, and the reason is short enough to fit in three lines.

The Remainder Theorem: dividing P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c leaves remainder P(c)P(c)P(c)#

Divide any polynomial P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c. The division algorithm from the last lesson produces a quotient Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) and a remainder that is zero or of degree less than the divisor’s degree 111. In either case the remainder is a constant polynomial. Call that constant rrr, so that

P(x)=(x−c) Q(x)+rP(x) = (x - c)\,Q(x) + rP(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+r

holds as an identity: the two sides are the same polynomial, so they agree at every single value of xxx. In particular they agree at the one value chosen to make the first term vanish. Substitute x=cx = cx=c:

P(c)=(c−c) Q(c)+r=0⋅Q(c)+r=r.P(c) = (c - c)\,Q(c) + r = 0 \cdot Q(c) + r = r.P(c)=(c−c)Q(c)+r=0⋅Q(c)+r=r.

Whatever number Q(c)Q(c)Q(c) happens to be, multiplying it by zero erases it. The remainder therefore equals P(c)P(c)P(c).

∎

Two remarks keep this proof honest. First, nothing was divided by zero: no fraction ever appears in the argument. We evaluated a polynomial identity, valid for all xxx, at one convenient input. That is also why the theorem needed no tableau: it follows just from the fact that dividing P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c always works and gives one right answer. Synthetic division is simply a fast way to find that quotient QQQ and that remainder rrr. Second, the theorem is an equality of numbers, not an approximation, and it runs in both directions. Want the remainder without dividing? Evaluate P(c)P(c)P(c). Want P(c)P(c)P(c) without plugging in powers? Divide and read the remainder. Go Deeper below follows that second direction, where it turns into a fast way to evaluate any polynomial.

When the remainder is zero

In the first checkpoint the remainder came out 000, and the division identity collapsed to a pure product: x3−7x+6=(x−2)(x2+2x−3)x^3 - 7x + 6 = (x - 2)(x^2 + 2x - 3)x3−7x+6=(x−2)(x2+2x−3), with nothing left over. The division is exact, and x−2x - 2x−2 is a factor of the cubic. Since the remainder is P(c)P(c)P(c), the remainder is zero exactly when P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0, that is, exactly when ccc is a root of the polynomial. A zero remainder is how synthetic division certifies that x−cx - cx−c is a factor, the link the next lesson calls the Factor Theorem.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Wrong sign in the corner. The corner holds the value of xxx that makes the divisor zero. For x−5x - 5x−5 that is 555, but for x+5x + 5x+5 it is −5-5−5. Writing +5+5+5 for x+5x + 5x+5 silently computes a different division.
  • Skipping placeholder zeros. The top row for x3−7x+6x^3 - 7x + 6x3−7x+6 is 1,  0,  −7,  61,\; 0,\; -7,\; 61,0,−7,6. Omitting the 000 shifts every later column one place left and wrecks both quotient and remainder.
  • Subtracting inside the tableau. Synthetic division only ever adds. The subtraction from long division is already baked into the corner’s flipped sign, so subtracting again undoes it.
  • Misreading the bottom row. The last number is the remainder, not a coefficient, and the quotient starts one degree below the dividend. A bottom row of 2,1,6,112, 1, 6, 112,1,6,11 from dividing a cubic means 2x2+x+62x^2 + x + 62x2+x+6 remainder 111111, never 2x3+x2+6x+112x^3 + x^2 + 6x + 112x3+x2+6x+11.
  • Sign slips when ccc is negative. Odd powers keep the minus, even powers lose it: (−2)3=−8(-2)^3 = -8(−2)3=−8 but (−2)4=16(-2)^4 = 16(−2)4=16. Write each product out rather than trusting momentum.

Key takeaways

  • The rhythm: corner holds ccc from x−cx - cx−c; top row lists coefficients with 000 placeholders; bring down, multiply by ccc, add, repeat. Bottom row gives the quotient coefficients (one degree lower) and, last, the remainder.
  • It is long division: matching coefficients in P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+rP(x) = (x - c)Q(x) + rP(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+r forces exactly the tableau’s rhythm, each new number is the next coefficient plus ccc times the previous new number, so the shortcut and long division are the same computation.
  • Remainder Theorem: dividing P(x)P(x)P(x) by x−cx - cx−c leaves remainder P(c)P(c)P(c), because evaluating the identity P(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+rP(x) = (x - c)Q(x) + rP(x)=(x−c)Q(x)+r at x=cx = cx=c kills the product term.

The full proof, for any polynomial and any corner number

The earlier section showed, using one cubic’s numbers, that the tableau’s rhythm rebuilds long division. Here is the same argument written in symbols, so it works for every polynomial and every corner number ccc at once.

The tableau computes the true quotient and remainder#

It remains to say why QQQ and rrr are the quotient and remainder rather than merely a way of rewriting PPP. The number rrr is a constant, so it is either zero or of degree less than 111, the degree of the divisor x−cx - cx−c. The division algorithm from the last lesson has a uniqueness half. By that half, only one pair consisting of a quotient and a remainder meeting that condition can satisfy such an identity. The tableau produced that pair.

Horner's method: reading the remainder as an evaluation

Unwind the tableau’s recurrence for a cubic P(x)=a3x3+a2x2+a1x+a0P(x) = a_3x^3 + a_2x^2 + a_1x + a_0P(x)=a3​x3+a2​x2+a1​x+a0​ and something familiar appears. The bottom row is b2=a3b_2 = a_3b2​=a3​, then b1=a2+c b2b_1 = a_2 + c\,b_2b1​=a2​+cb2​, then b0=a1+c b1b_0 = a_1 + c\,b_1b0​=a1​+cb1​, then r=a0+c b0r = a_0 + c\,b_0r=a0​+cb0​. Substitute each line into the next:

r=a0+c(a1+c(a2+c a3))=((a3 c+a2) c+a1) c+a0.r = a_0 + c\Bigl(a_1 + c\bigl(a_2 + c\,a_3\bigr)\Bigr) = \Bigl(\bigl(a_3\,c + a_2\bigr)\,c + a_1\Bigr)\,c + a_0.r=a0​+c(a1​+c(a2​+ca3​))=((a3​c+a2​)c+a1​)c+a0​.

Multiply the nest out and you get a3c3+a2c2+a1c+a0a_3c^3 + a_2c^2 + a_1c + a_0a3​c3+a2​c2+a1​c+a0​, which is P(c)P(c)P(c) on the nose. So the tableau’s final number computes P(c)P(c)P(c) by direct multiply-and-add arithmetic, using the same recurrence already shown to match long division, and the same unwinding works in every degree. This nested way of evaluating a polynomial is called Horner’s method, and the tableau performs it with one multiply-and-add for each coefficient after the leading one. So a degree-nnn polynomial costs just nnn multiplications, instead of building each power of ccc from scratch. As a bonus, the intermediate numbers bkb_kbk​ are not waste products. They are the quotient coefficients, so evaluating P(c)P(c)P(c) this way hands you the whole division for free.

A bit of history (optional)

A name on a method tends to record who advertised it, not who found it.

The three rows you just ran are called Horner’s method in the English-speaking world. William George Horner, a schoolmaster in Bath, presented the scheme in 1819. He offered it as the engine inside a method for hunting the roots of an equation. The name stuck for a reason with nothing to do with the arithmetic. An influential writer of the day pushed it for years in print, and the English textbooks copied him.

The same tableau had gone to press fifteen years earlier, in a prize memoir by Paolo Ruffini. That is the physician who argued the quintic unsolvable, two lessons back. He wrote in Italian, for a readership that barely overlapped with the one reading Horner. In Italy and Spain the three rows are still taught as Ruffini’s rule.

Neither man was first, and neither claim is as old as the method. Rows of coefficients were being multiplied and added in this exact rhythm centuries before either memoir, in traditions that never entered the argument at all.

The rhythm outlived every name on it because it is cheap. It spends one multiply and one add for each coefficient after the leading one. That is why the nested form used to find P(c)P(c)P(c) in Go Deeper still runs in the software that graphs a curve for you.

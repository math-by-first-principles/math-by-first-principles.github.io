Polynomial Division and Roots: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Polynomial
A finite sum of terms c xkc\,x^kcxk with real ccc and whole-number kkk; domain all reals. Ruled out: 3x\tfrac{3}{x}x3​, x\sqrt{x}x​, 2x2^x2x. The restriction falls on xxx only, so x23+2\tfrac{x^2}{3}+\sqrt{2}3x2​+2​ qualifies.
Degree names and term counts
Degrees 000 to 555: constant, linear, quadratic, cubic, quartic, quintic. Terms counted: monomial, binomial, trinomial. So x4−5x2+4x^4-5x^2+4x4−5x2+4 is a quartic trinomial.
Standard form, degree, leading coefficient, constant term
Terms in descending powers. Degree is the highest power with a nonzero coefficient, the leading coefficient sits on it, the constant term is a0a_0a0​ with its sign. Combine like terms and order them BEFORE reading any of these; the zero polynomial has no degree.
Turning point
Where a graph switches between rising and falling: a local hilltop or valley floor.
Root (zero) versus factor
ccc is a root when the single evaluation P(c)P(c)P(c) gives 000; DDD is a factor when P=DQP = DQP=DQ exactly, an identity at every xxx. Different claims, and the Factor Theorem is the exchange rate.
Corner number
The ccc in a synthetic tableau's corner: the value making the divisor zero, so x+3x + 3x+3 takes c=−3c = -3c=−3.
Placeholder
An explicit 0xk0x^k0xk written for every power missing from a dividend, so like powers stay aligned.

Formulas and theorems

  • Degree of a product and of a sum

    deg⁡(fg)=deg⁡f+deg⁡gdeg⁡(f+g)≤max⁡(deg⁡f,deg⁡g)\begin{gathered} \deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g \\ \deg(f+g) \le \max(\deg f, \deg g) \end{gathered}deg(fg)=degf+deggdeg(f+g)≤max(degf,degg)​

    Use when f,gf, gf,g nonzero. Leading coefficients MULTIPLY, so a factored form gives its degree and leading term unexpanded. A sum's degree DROPS only when leading terms cancel, possibly to the zero polynomial, which has no degree.

    e.g. (3x2−2)(x3+4x)(3x^2-2)(x^3+4x)(3x2−2)(x3+4x) has degree 555, leading term 3x53x^53x5; (x3+2x)−(x3−x)=3x(x^3+2x)-(x^3-x) = 3x(x3+2x)−(x3−x)=3x has degree 111.

  • Two free evaluations

    f(0)=a0f(1)=an+an−1+⋯+a0\begin{gathered} f(0) = a_0 \\ f(1) = a_n + a_{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 \end{gathered}f(0)=a0​f(1)=an​+an−1​+⋯+a0​​

    Use when Any polynomial. f(0)f(0)f(0) is the constant term with its sign, the yyy-intercept; f(1)f(1)f(1) adds every coefficient, a missing power contributing 000.

    e.g. 2x4−x3+3x−92x^4-x^3+3x-92x4−x3+3x−9 gives f(0)=−9f(0) = -9f(0)=−9 and f(1)=−5f(1) = -5f(1)=−5.

  • End behavior belongs to the leading term

    far right follows the sign of anfar left agrees if n is evenfar left opposes if n is odd\begin{gathered} \text{far right follows the sign of } a_n \\ \text{far left agrees if } n \text{ is even} \\ \text{far left opposes if } n \text{ is odd} \end{gathered}far right follows the sign of an​far left agrees if n is evenfar left opposes if n is odd​
    Text description

    Four miniature graphs: an even degree sends both ends the same way, up when the leading coefficient is positive and down when it is negative, while an odd degree sends the two ends opposite ways.

    Use when n≥1n \ge 1n≥1, an≠0a_n \neq 0an​=0. Only the highest exponent speaks far out, whatever the lower coefficients' size. So every odd-degree polynomial has a real zero, and an even-degree graph has an ODD number of turning points, hence at least one.

    e.g. −3x4+5x3−1-3x^4+5x^3-1−3x4+5x3−1 falls on both ends; −x5+100x4-x^5+100x^4−x5+100x4 obeys −x5-x^5−x5.

  • Counting ceilings

    #{distinct roots}≤n#{turning points}≤n−1\begin{gathered} \#\{\text{distinct roots}\} \le n \\ \#\{\text{turning points}\} \le n-1 \end{gathered}#{distinct roots}≤n#{turning points}≤n−1​

    Use when Nonzero polynomial, degree n≥1n \ge 1n≥1; roots counted distinct, real or nonreal. Ceilings, not quotas: x2+1x^2+1x2+1 has no real zero, x3x^3x3 no turning point. There is also a floor: kkk distinct real zeros force at least k−1k-1k−1 turns.

  • A sign change traps a zero

    f(a)<0<f(b)⟹ f  has a zero between a and b\begin{gathered} f(a) < 0 < f(b) \\ \Longrightarrow \ f \ \text{ has a zero between } a \text{ and } b \end{gathered}f(a)<0<f(b)⟹ f  has a zero between a and b​
    Text description

    An unbroken curve running from a point below the axis at a to a point above it at b, with the crossing it is forced to make highlighted between them.

    Use when Polynomial graphs are smooth and unbroken, which is what powers this; a corner or a break rules a curve out. One-way only: x2x^2x2 has a zero with no sign change.

  • Division algorithm, with the quotient's size

    P=DQ+RR=0  or  deg⁡R<deg⁡Ddeg⁡Q=deg⁡P−deg⁡D\begin{gathered} P = DQ + R \\ R = 0 \ \text{ or } \ \deg R < \deg D \\ \deg Q = \deg P - \deg D \end{gathered}P=DQ+RR=0  or  degR<degDdegQ=degP−degD​

    Use when D≠0D \neq 0D=0; the last equation needs deg⁡P≥deg⁡D\deg P \ge \deg DdegP≥degD. The degree bound makes the answer finished and (Q,R)(Q, R)(Q,R) unique; R=0R = 0R=0 is separate since the zero polynomial has no degree. QQQ's leading coefficient is PPP's divided by DDD's, and fractions there are legal when DDD is not monic. If deg⁡P<deg⁡D\deg P < \deg DdegP<degD then Q=0Q = 0Q=0, R=PR = PR=P. Dividing through gives PD=Q+RD\tfrac{P}{D} = Q + \tfrac{R}{D}DP​=Q+DR​ where D(x)≠0D(x) \neq 0D(x)=0, remainder still OVER the divisor.

    e.g. x3−4x+1x^3 - 4x + 1x3−4x+1 over x−2x - 2x−2 gives the quadratic x2+2xx^2 + 2xx2+2x with remainder 111.

  • Synthetic division recurrence

    bn−1=anbk−1=ak+c bkr=a0+c b0\begin{gathered} b_{n-1} = a_n \\ b_{k-1} = a_k + c\,b_k \\ r = a_0 + c\,b_0 \end{gathered}bn−1​=an​bk−1​=ak​+cbk​r=a0​+cb0​​

    Use when The divisor must be exactly x−cx - cx−c: degree 111 AND monic (leading coefficient 111). The top row lists every coefficient with 000 placeholders, and the tableau only ever ADDS, the subtraction baked into the corner's flipped sign. The bottom row is the quotient, one degree lower, then the remainder, and it also computes P(c)P(c)P(c) in nnn multiplications (Horner's method).

  • Remainder Theorem

    P(x)÷(x−c)  leaves remainder  P(c)P(x) \div (x-c) \ \text{ leaves remainder } \ P(c)P(x)÷(x−c)  leaves remainder  P(c)

    Use when Any polynomial and any ccc, real or complex; true whether or not that remainder is zero. For ax−bax - bax−b with a≠0a \neq 0a=0 the remainder is P ⁣(ba)P\!\left(\tfrac{b}{a}\right)P(ab​).

    e.g. 2x3−3x2+42x^3-3x^2+42x3−3x2+4 over x−2x-2x−2 leaves P(2)=8P(2) = 8P(2)=8.

  • Factor Theorem

    x−c  is a factor of  P(x)  ⟺  P(c)=0\begin{gathered} x - c \ \text{ is a factor of } \ P(x) \\ \iff P(c) = 0 \end{gathered}x−c  is a factor of  P(x)⟺P(c)=0​

    Use when Any polynomial, any ccc. Both directions hold, so one evaluation settles it: P(c)≠0P(c) \neq 0P(c)=0 PROVES x−cx - cx−c is not a factor. For a≠0a \neq 0a=0, ax−bax - bax−b is a factor exactly when P ⁣(ba)=0P\!\left(\tfrac{b}{a}\right) = 0P(ab​)=0, so test the value killing the candidate factor. It converts roots and factors, never asserting a root exists.

  • Build a polynomial from prescribed roots

    P(x)=a (x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rk)a≠0\begin{gathered} P(x) = a\,(x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_k) \\ a \neq 0 \end{gathered}P(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rk​)a=0​

    Use when Degree kkk, with exactly those roots; a repeated root repeats its factor. Roots fix PPP only up to aaa: (x−1)(x−3)(x-1)(x-3)(x−1)(x−3) and 7(x−1)(x−3)7(x-1)(x-3)7(x−1)(x−3) tie until a point or the leading coefficient chooses.

  • Nonreal roots come in conjugate pairs

    P real,P(z)=0 ⟹ P(zˉ)=0P \ \text{real}, \quad P(z) = 0 \ \Longrightarrow \ P(\bar{z}) = 0P real,P(z)=0 ⟹ P(zˉ)=0

    Use when EVERY coefficient of PPP real; otherwise it fails, and x2−2ix−1=(x−i)2x^2-2ix-1 = (x-i)^2x2−2ix−1=(x−i)2 has iii twice and −i-i−i never. The pair merges into the real quadratic x2−2px+p2+q2x^2-2px+p^2+q^2x2−2px+p2+q2, discriminant negative.

    e.g. The monic cubic with roots 222 and 1−i1-i1−i is (x−2)(x2−2x+2)=x3−4x2+6x−4(x-2)(x^2-2x+2) = x^3-4x^2+6x-4(x−2)(x2−2x+2)=x3−4x2+6x−4.

  • Rational Root Theorem

    pq  a root  ⟹ p∣a0  and  q∣an\tfrac{p}{q} \ \text{ a root } \ \Longrightarrow \ p \mid a_0 \ \text{ and } \ q \mid a_nqp​  a root  ⟹ p∣a0​  and  q∣an​

    Use when EVERY coefficient an integer, an≠0a_n \neq 0an​=0, pq\tfrac{p}{q}qp​ in lowest terms; clear fractional coefficients first, which changes no root. Numerators come from the CONSTANT term, denominators from the LEADING coefficient. One-way only, and blind to irrational and nonreal roots. If a0=0a_0 = 0a0​=0 it filters nothing: factor out the largest power of xxx first.

  • Monic case (integer root theorem)

    an=1⟹ every rational rootis an integer dividing a0\begin{gathered} a_n = 1 \\ \Longrightarrow \ \text{every rational root} \\ \text{is an integer dividing } a_0 \end{gathered}an​=1⟹ every rational rootis an integer dividing a0​​

    Use when Integer coefficients, leading coefficient 111; no fraction can occur. A fully failed list PROVES no rational root, the standard proof that n\sqrt{n}n​ is irrational for non-square nnn.

    e.g. x3−4x+2x^3-4x+2x3−4x+2: candidates ±1,±2\pm 1, \pm 2±1,±2 all fail, so there is no rational root.

Problem types, step by step

Read a polynomial's shape at sight

  1. Tidy first, or, if it arrives factored, add the factors' degrees and multiply their leading terms.
  2. Degree parity plus the sign of the leading coefficient give the two far ends, f(0)f(0)f(0) the yyy-intercept, and an odd degree guarantees at least one real zero.

e.g. (2−x)(x2+1)2(2-x)(x^2+1)^2(2−x)(x2+1)2 has degree 555 and leading term −x5-x^5−x5, so it rises left, falls right, and meets the yyy-axis at 222.

Deduce the least possible degree from a described graph

  1. From kkk distinct xxx-intercepts take n≥kn \ge kn≥k, from ttt turning points take n≥t+1n \ge t+1n≥t+1, keep the larger.
  2. Ends agreeing forces nnn even, disagreeing forces nnn odd; raise the bound by one on a parity clash, and check that an even degree carries an odd number of turns.

e.g. Both ends rising with 444 intercepts and 333 turns gives n≥4n \ge 4n≥4, even, and 333 is odd, so n=4n = 4n=4.

Divide by a divisor of any degree (long division)

  1. Put both polynomials in standard form and fill every missing power of the dividend with 0xk0x^k0xk.
  2. Divide the working remainder's leading term by the DIVISOR'S leading term for the next quotient term.
  3. Multiply the whole divisor by it, subtract the ENTIRE product, repeat.
  4. Stop when the working remainder is 000 or its degree drops below the divisor's, then expand DQ+RDQ + RDQ+R against PPP.

e.g. x4−x2+3x^4-x^2+3x4−x2+3 over x2−x+1x^2-x+1x2−x+1 gives quotient x2+x−1x^2+x-1x2+x−1, remainder −2x+4-2x+4−2x+4.

Divide by a linear divisor (synthetic division)

  1. Check the divisor is linear: corner ccc for x−cx - cx−c, corner ba\tfrac{b}{a}ab​ for ax−bax - bax−b.
  2. Write every coefficient across the top with 000 placeholders, bring down the leader, multiply by the corner, add down each column.
  3. Read the bottom row as the quotient, one degree lower, last entry the remainder.
  4. For an ax−bax - bax−b divisor, divide that quotient by aaa and leave the remainder alone.

e.g. 3x3+2x2−7x+43x^3+2x^2-7x+43x3+2x2−7x+4 over 3x−13x-13x−1: corner 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ gives 3x2+3x−63x^2+3x-63x2+3x−6, so quotient x2+x−2x^2+x-2x2+x−2, remainder 222.

Find a remainder, or solve for an unknown coefficient

  1. Identify the value killing the divisor: ccc for x−cx - cx−c, ba\tfrac{b}{a}ab​ for ax−bax - bax−b.
  2. Evaluate PPP there, wrapping a negative input in parentheses before taking powers; that value IS the remainder.
  3. For an unknown coefficient, set that evaluation equal to the stated remainder (000 if exact) and solve; two remainders give two equations.

e.g. x3−3x2+kx+8x^3-3x^2+kx+8x3−3x2+kx+8 divides exactly by x−2x-2x−2 when 4+2k=04+2k = 04+2k=0, so k=−2k = -2k=−2.

Factor completely from one known root

  1. Confirm the root by evaluating, then divide out x−cx - cx−c to get QQQ, one degree lower.
  2. Hunt the next root INSIDE QQQ, retesting ccc there in case it repeats, and peel again.
  3. Once QQQ is quadratic, stop hunting: over R\mathbb{R}R leave a negative-discriminant quadratic standing, over C\mathbb{C}C split it into its conjugate pair.

e.g. x3+3x2−4x^3+3x^2-4x3+3x2−4 with root 111 gives quotient x2+4x+4x^2+4x+4x2+4x+4, so P(x)=(x−1)(x+2)2P(x) = (x-1)(x+2)^2P(x)=(x−1)(x+2)2.

Build a polynomial to order

  1. Turn each root rrr into the factor x−rx - rx−r, minding the sign, adding a nonreal root's conjugate when the coefficients must be real.
  2. Multiply each conjugate pair first, since that product is real, then attach the rest.
  3. Write P(x)=a⋅(product)P(x) = a \cdot (\text{product})P(x)=a⋅(product), solve for aaa from the extra point or the given leading coefficient, expand, check one root.

e.g. Roots −2,1,3-2, 1, 3−2,1,3 with P(0)=12P(0) = 12P(0)=12: 6a=126a = 126a=12, so P(x)=2(x+2)(x−1)(x−3)P(x) = 2(x+2)(x-1)(x-3)P(x)=2(x+2)(x−1)(x−3).

Find every root of an integer-coefficient polynomial

  1. Clear fractional coefficients, and factor out the largest power of xxx if the constant term is 000.
  2. List every ±pq\pm\tfrac{p}{q}±qp​ with ppp a positive divisor of the CONSTANT term and qqq one of the LEADING coefficient, reduced and deduplicated.
  3. Test by synthetic division, integers before fractions; remainder 000 means a root, and the same row gives the quotient.
  4. If every candidate on the list fails, that PROVES there is no rational root at all; any remaining roots are irrational or nonreal, and this method cannot pin them down further.
  5. Peel and repeat until the quotient is quadratic, then finish with the quadratic formula, reporting nonreal roots too.

e.g. 2x3−x2−8x+42x^3-x^2-8x+42x3−x2−8x+4: 888 candidates, and 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ works, giving (2x−1)(x−2)(x+2)(2x-1)(x-2)(x+2)(2x−1)(x−2)(x+2).

Exam traps

  • Trap Putting the divisor's own constant in the corner: dividing x3+5x−4x^3+5x-4x3+5x−4 by x+1x+1x+1 with corner 111, reporting remainder 222.

    Fix The corner holds the value making the divisor zero, so x+1x+1x+1 takes −1-1−1 and the remainder is P(−1)=−10P(-1) = -10P(−1)=−10.

  • Trap Feeding a divisor that is not x−cx - cx−c into the corner, or halving the remainder along with the quotient.

    Fix For ax−bax - bax−b use corner ba\tfrac{b}{a}ab​, divide the QUOTIENT by aaa, leave the remainder alone: 3x3+2x2−7x+43x^3+2x^2-7x+43x3+2x2−7x+4 over 3x−13x-13x−1 has remainder 222, not 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​. Degree 222 or more has no tableau; use long division.

  • Trap Omitting a placeholder: running x3−7x+6x^3-7x+6x3−7x+6 over x−2x-2x−2 from the row 1,−7,61, -7, 61,−7,6.

    Fix The row is 1,0,−7,61, 0, -7, 61,0,−7,6. The short row returns x−5x-5x−5 remainder −4-4−4 instead of x2+2x−3x^2+2x-3x2+2x−3 remainder 000.

  • Trap Subtracting only the leading term of q Dq\,DqD, or letting a subtracted negative keep its sign.

    Fix Subtract the entire product, so subtracting −3x-3x−3x ADDS 3x3x3x. A row that fails to shift right means the leading term never cancelled.

  • Trap Promoting the candidate list to an answer: reporting ±1,±2,±4,±12\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4, \pm\tfrac{1}{2}±1,±2,±4,±21​ as the roots of 2x3−x2−8x+42x^3-x^2-8x+42x3−x2−8x+4.

    Fix Those eight are candidates; substituting leaves only 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, 222, and −2-2−2. Five fail, and a polynomial can fail all eight.

  • Trap Reading a fully failed candidate list as "no roots".

    Fix It proves no RATIONAL roots, nothing more. Every candidate of x2−2x^2-2x2−2 fails, yet it has the real roots ±2\pm\sqrt{2}±2​.

  • Trap Hunting the next root back in PPP, or assuming a found root cannot repeat.

    Fix Search inside the quotient and retest ccc there: 4x3−8x2+5x−1=(x−1)(2x−1)24x^3-8x^2+5x-1 = (x-1)(2x-1)^24x3−8x2+5x−1=(x−1)(2x−1)2, where 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ is a root twice.

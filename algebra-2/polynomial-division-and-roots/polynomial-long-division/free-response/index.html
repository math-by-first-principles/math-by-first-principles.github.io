    Part A

    Degrees add when polynomials multiply, and the product D⋅QD \cdot QD⋅Q has to carry the dividend's leading term, because the remainder is too low in degree to interfere with it.

    Part C

    Look at what one pass does. The quotient term qqq is chosen so that qqq times the divisor's leading term equals the working remainder's leading term, so the subtraction destroys that leading term exactly. Every other term of qDqDqD is qqq times a lower term of DDD, so it sits below that degree, and so does every other term of the working remainder. Nothing at the top degree survives.

    deg⁡(W−qD)<deg⁡W.\deg(W - qD) < \deg W.deg(W−qD)<degW.

    So the degrees of the successive working remainders form a strictly decreasing list of whole numbers. Such a list cannot run forever: it is bounded below by 000 and each step goes down by at least one. After at most deg⁡P−deg⁡D+1\deg P - \deg D + 1degP−degD+1 passes, which is 333 for this division, the stopping condition must be met.

    The test itself cannot be about numerical size, because a polynomial's size moves with xxx. Compare a linear expression with this divisor at two inputs:

    x=0:x+6=6,x2−2x+4=4;x = 0: \quad x + 6 = 6, \quad x^2 - 2x + 4 = 4;x=0:x+6=6,x2−2x+4=4;

    A rule phrased in terms of values would call the same division finished at one input and unfinished at another, and would never settle anything. Degree does not move with xxx, so a polynomial either passes the test or fails it once and for all. That is what lets the loop halt, and it is what makes the answer unique.

Polynomial Long Division

Intuition

Divide 474747 by 555 and the answer is two numbers welded together: the quotient 999 and the remainder 222, joined by 47=5⋅9+247 = 5 \cdot 9 + 247=5⋅9+2. You have trusted sentences like that since arithmetic. This lesson writes the same sentence about polynomials, using long division that works almost exactly like the one you already know. The one new question is what “remainder small enough to stop” should mean when the numbers are polynomials instead of integers, and the answer is degree.

Learning goals

  • State the division algorithm and its degree condition
  • Read a polynomial in standard form as place-value columns, like a base-xxx numeral
  • Find each quotient term by dividing the leading terms
  • Insert 0xk0x^k0xk placeholders for every missing power
  • Check a division by expanding DQ+RDQ + RDQ+R, and read a zero remainder as a factorization

The promise integer division makes

Before touching a polynomial, look closely at what integer division actually delivers, because the polynomial version copies it clause for clause.

Divide 474747 by 555. The process returns 999 with 222 left over, and the entire content of that answer is one equation together with one condition:

47=5⋅9+2,0≤2<5.47 = 5 \cdot 9 + 2, \qquad 0 \leq 2 < 5.47=5⋅9+2,0≤2<5.

The equation says the answer is correct: nine fives and two more really do rebuild 474747. The condition says the division is finished: fewer than five remain, so no further five can be pulled out. Drop the condition and “answers” multiply without end. The equations 47=5⋅8+747 = 5 \cdot 8 + 747=5⋅8+7 and 47=5⋅6+1747 = 5 \cdot 6 + 1747=5⋅6+17 are both true, yet nobody calls 888 remainder 777 the result of dividing 474747 by 555, because a remainder of 777 still contains a five. Only the demand that the remainder be smaller than the divisor pins down the pair (9,2)(9, 2)(9,2) as the one and only answer.

So division with remainder is really two promises: an identity, dividend equals divisor times quotient plus remainder, and a smallness condition that makes the identity’s pieces unique. Keep both promises in view; the whole lesson is about keeping them for polynomials.

Both promises are visible at once if you lay the dividend out in rows. In the figure below you set a number of dots and a row width. The dots fill rows from the top left, and any dots that cannot complete a row are drawn hollow.

Start at 111111 dots in rows of 444. Two rows fill and three dots are left hollow, which is the identity 11=4⋅2+311 = 4 \cdot 2 + 311=4⋅2+3 drawn rather than computed. The row width is the divisor, the solid rows are the quotient, and the hollow dots are the remainder. Try to break the smallness condition and you cannot: any four hollow dots would have completed another row, so the hollow dots always come out fewer than the row width. That is what “no further copy of the divisor can be pulled out” looks like. Set the count to 121212 against the same rows of 444 and the hollow dots vanish, the R=0R = 0R=0 case where the divisor goes in exactly.

A polynomial is a numeral written in base x

The last lesson gave you the vocabulary. The degree of a polynomial is its highest power, the leading term carries that power, and standard form lists the terms in falling powers. Now set a numeral and a polynomial side by side:

2,572=2⋅103+5⋅102+7⋅10+2and2x3+5x2+7x+2.2{,}572 = 2 \cdot 10^3 + 5 \cdot 10^2 + 7 \cdot 10 + 2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 2x^3 + 5x^2 + 7x + 2.2,572=2⋅103+5⋅102+7⋅10+2and2x3+5x2+7x+2.

Same skeleton. The coefficients play the digits, and the powers of xxx play the places. Two honest differences separate them. First, a numeral’s digits stay between 000 and 999, and any overflow is handled by carrying into the next column. A polynomial’s coefficients, by contrast, are unrestricted, so nothing ever carries and the columns never interact. In that one respect polynomial arithmetic is simpler than integer arithmetic. Second, a numeral names a single number, while a polynomial’s value moves with xxx. That second difference is why the smallness condition needs translating. The phrase “the remainder is smaller than the divisor” cannot mean numerical size, because which of two polynomials is bigger can flip as xxx changes. At x=2x = 2x=2, xxx is smaller than x2x^2x2, since x2=4x^2 = 4x2=4. At x=12x = \tfrac12x=21​, though, xxx is larger than x2x^2x2, since x2=14x^2 = \tfrac14x2=41​. The measure that does not move with xxx is the degree, so degree takes over the job that size did for integers.

With that translation made, here is the exact claim this lesson runs on, proves, and then uses. It is called the division algorithm for polynomials. Let P(x)P(x)P(x) be any polynomial (the dividend) and D(x)D(x)D(x) any nonzero polynomial (the divisor). Then there is exactly one pair of polynomials Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) (the quotient) and R(x)R(x)R(x) (the remainder) with

P(x)=D(x) Q(x)+R(x),where R=0  or  deg⁡R<deg⁡D.P(x) = D(x)\,Q(x) + R(x), \qquad \text{where } R = 0 \ \text{ or } \ \deg R < \deg D.P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x),where R=0  or  degR<degD.

Two small print items. The zero polynomial has no degree, since it has no leading term, which is why the condition says ”R=0R = 0R=0 or deg⁡R<deg⁡D\deg R < \deg DdegR<degD” instead of just deg⁡R<deg⁡D\deg R < \deg DdegR<degD. And D=0D = 0D=0 is excluded for the same reason 47÷047 \div 047÷0 is: with P=0⋅Q+RP = 0 \cdot Q + RP=0⋅Q+R, nothing would stop RRR from just equaling PPP, and the division would promise nothing.

Each quotient term is forced

The algorithm is a loop, and the loop has only one legal move. Watch it once in slow motion on

(x2+5x+7)÷(x+2).(x^2 + 5x + 7) \div (x + 2).(x2+5x+7)÷(x+2).

Your goal is to peel copies of x+2x + 2x+2 out of x2+5x+7x^2 + 5x + 7x2+5x+7 until what is left is too small, in degree, to contain another copy. The tool is subtraction: subtract a multiple of the divisor, record how many copies that multiple used, repeat. To make progress you must kill the dividend’s leading term x2x^2x2, and here is the key point: a multiple q⋅(x+2)q \cdot (x + 2)q⋅(x+2) cancels x2x^2x2 exactly when its own leading term is x2x^2x2. If you take qqq to be a single term, the leading term of q⋅(x+2)q \cdot (x + 2)q⋅(x+2) is just q⋅xq \cdot xq⋅x, so you need q⋅x=x2q \cdot x = x^2q⋅x=x2, which forces

q=x2x=x.q = \frac{x^2}{x} = x.q=xx2​=x.

Divide the leading terms: that is the whole rule, and no other choice touches the x2x^2x2. Multiply back and subtract, remembering to subtract the entire product:

x(x+2)=x2+2x,(x2+5x+7)−(x2+2x)=3x+7.x(x + 2) = x^2 + 2x, \qquad (x^2 + 5x + 7) - (x^2 + 2x) = 3x + 7.x(x+2)=x2+2x,(x2+5x+7)−(x2+2x)=3x+7.

The x2x^2x2 is gone, exactly as designed. What remains is a smaller division problem, 3x+73x + 73x+7 divided by x+2x + 2x+2, so run the same forced move again: 3x÷x=33x \div x = 33x÷x=3, then 3(x+2)=3x+63(x + 2) = 3x + 63(x+2)=3x+6, and (3x+7)−(3x+6)=1(3x + 7) - (3x + 6) = 1(3x+7)−(3x+6)=1. Now the working remainder 111 has degree 000, which is below the divisor’s degree 111. The loop must stop, because any further multiple of x+2x + 2x+2 would introduce a new xxx term rather than cancel anything. Collecting the two recorded quotient terms, xxx and 333,

x2+5x+7=(x+2)(x+3)+1.x^2 + 5x + 7 = (x + 2)(x + 3) + 1.x2+5x+7=(x+2)(x+3)+1.

The traditional tableau records exactly these moves in columns, quotient on the roof, each product written under the matching powers:

Now cash in the base-xxx idea. Substitute x=10x = 10x=10 everywhere: the dividend becomes 157157157, the divisor becomes 121212, the quotient becomes 131313, and the remainder stays 111. Check it as an integer sentence: 12⋅13+1=156+1=15712 \cdot 13 + 1 = 156 + 1 = 15712⋅13+1=156+1=157. The division you just performed mirrors a grade-school division almost move for move, with one comfort added: since coefficients never carry, each column settles in a single step.

One habit before moving on. Every polynomial division can be checked by multiplying back, and the check is cheaper than the division was:

(x+2)(x+3)+1=x2+5x+6+1=x2+5x+7.✓(x + 2)(x + 3) + 1 = x^2 + 5x + 6 + 1 = x^2 + 5x + 7. \checkmark(x+2)(x+3)+1=x2+5x+6+1=x2+5x+7.✓

Make the check a reflex. The division algorithm’s identity is an equation about every value of xxx at once, so expanding DQ+RDQ + RDQ+R and comparing with PPP catches any slip immediately.

Why the loop is correct, and why it must stop

Two questions are worth answering before you trust the method on harder problems: does every pass keep the identity P=DQ+RP = DQ + RP=DQ+R true, and does the loop ever actually stop?

Both answers are visible in the two moves you just made. Multiplying the divisor by the new quotient term and subtracting removes exactly that amount from the working remainder and adds it to the quotient. So the total, ”DDD times the quotient so far, plus what’s left,” never changes: it starts at D⋅0+P=PD \cdot 0 + P = PD⋅0+P=P, so it still equals PPP at every later stage. That is why the final line, whatever it turns out to be, is automatically a true identity.

The quotient term at each pass was chosen for one reason: to cancel the working remainder’s leading term. Once that term is gone, nothing left over can be as large, so the degree of what remains drops by at least 111 on every pass. A degree cannot drop below 000, so after a bounded number of passes the working remainder is 000 or too low in degree to continue, and the loop must stop.

The paragraph above already shows why the loop always finds a valid quotient and remainder, for any dividend and any nonzero divisor. The full proof is in the Go deeper section at the end of the lesson.

Missing terms need placeholders

The tableau is column bookkeeping, and columns only work when every power has a column. A numeral handles a skipped place with the digit 000. The zero in 3,2053{,}2053,205 contributes nothing to the sum, but it holds the tens column open so the 222 and the 333 land where they belong. A polynomial that skips powers needs the same fix. Before dividing, rewrite the dividend (and the divisor, if need be) with an explicit 000 coefficient for every missing power. Nothing changes mathematically, since 0⋅xk0 \cdot x^k0⋅xk is zero; what changes is that like powers now sit above one another in the tableau, where the subtractions can find them.

Only one answer is possible

Existence came from running the loop: some quotient and remainder can always be found. A separate question is uniqueness: could a different pair (Q,R)(Q, R)(Q,R) also keep both promises for the same PPP and DDD? It could not, and seeing why starts from one fact about degrees.

Multiply two nonzero polynomials with leading terms axma x^maxm and bxnb x^nbxn. The only way to produce the power xm+nx^{m+n}xm+n in the product is leading term times leading term, and every other pairing lands strictly lower. Since a≠0a \neq 0a=0 and b≠0b \neq 0b=0, their product ababab is not zero either, so the product’s leading term really is ab xm+nab\,x^{m+n}abxm+n:

deg⁡(fg)=deg⁡f+deg⁡g.\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g.deg(fg)=degf+degg.

Degrees add under multiplication. That single fact is enough to rule out any second quotient and remainder. Two different quotients would force DDD times their difference to have degree at least deg⁡D\deg DdegD. But a difference of two too-small remainders can never reach that high, so no second pair exists. However you found a quotient and remainder, by the tableau, by cleverness, or by a lucky guess, they are the quotient and remainder.

deg⁡Q=deg⁡P−deg⁡D,andthe leading coefficient of Q is the leading coefficient of P divided by that of D.\begin{aligned} &\deg Q = \deg P - \deg D, \qquad \text{and} \\ &\text{the leading coefficient of } Q \text{ is the leading coefficient of } P \text{ divided by that of } D. \end{aligned}​degQ=degP−degD,andthe leading coefficient of Q is the leading coefficient of P divided by that of D.​

A divisor of higher degree

Nothing in the loop, the proof, or the bookkeeping assumed the divisor was linear. Divide by a quadratic and the only visible change is the finish line. With that divisor the loop stops when the working remainder is zero or of degree below 222, so a remainder may now be linear, or a constant, or zero.

Worked example 4 Divide x4+3x3−x2+5x^4 + 3x^3 - x^2 + 5x4+3x3−x2+5 by x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1

Restore the missing xxx column first: the dividend is x4+3x3−x2+0x+5x^4 + 3x^3 - x^2 + 0x + 5x4+3x3−x2+0x+5.

First pass: x4÷x2=x2x^4 \div x^2 = x^2x4÷x2=x2, and x2(x2+1)=x4+x2x^2(x^2 + 1) = x^4 + x^2x2(x2+1)=x4+x2. This product skips the x3x^3x3 column, so when you write it into the tableau, slide its x2x^2x2 term under the x2x^2x2 column and leave the gap open. Subtracting leaves 3x3−2x2+0x+53x^3 - 2x^2 + 0x + 53x3−2x2+0x+5. Second pass: 3x3÷x2=3x3x^3 \div x^2 = 3x3x3÷x2=3x, and 3x(x2+1)=3x3+3x3x(x^2 + 1) = 3x^3 + 3x3x(x2+1)=3x3+3x; subtracting leaves −2x2−3x+5-2x^2 - 3x + 5−2x2−3x+5. Third pass: −2x2÷x2=−2-2x^2 \div x^2 = -2−2x2÷x2=−2, and −2(x2+1)=−2x2−2-2(x^2 + 1) = -2x^2 - 2−2(x2+1)=−2x2−2; subtracting leaves −3x+7-3x + 7−3x+7, of degree 1<21 < 21<2: stop.

The quotient is x2+3x−2x^2 + 3x - 2x2+3x−2 and the remainder is −3x+7-3x + 7−3x+7:

x4+3x3−x2+5=(x2+1)(x2+3x−2)+(−3x+7).x^4 + 3x^3 - x^2 + 5 = (x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 3x - 2) + (-3x + 7).x4+3x3−x2+5=(x2+1)(x2+3x−2)+(−3x+7).

A linear remainder is not unfinished business. No multiple of the degree-222 divisor can cancel a degree-111 term without introducing something of degree 222 or higher. So −3x+7-3x + 7−3x+7 is as reduced as a remainder can be. Check:

(x2+1)(x2+3x−2)=x4+3x3−2x2+x2+3x−2=x4+3x3−x2+3x−2,\begin{aligned} (x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 3x - 2) &= x^4 + 3x^3 - 2x^2 + x^2 + 3x - 2 \\ &= x^4 + 3x^3 - x^2 + 3x - 2, \end{aligned}(x2+1)(x2+3x−2)​=x4+3x3−2x2+x2+3x−2=x4+3x3−x2+3x−2,​

and adding −3x+7-3x + 7−3x+7 gives back x4+3x3−x2+5x^4 + 3x^3 - x^2 + 5x4+3x3−x2+5.

Two ways to package the answer

Dividing both sides of P=DQ+RP = DQ + RP=DQ+R by D(x)D(x)D(x) gives a second form of the same fact:

P(x)D(x)=Q(x)+R(x)D(x).\frac{P(x)}{D(x)} = Q(x) + \frac{R(x)}{D(x)}.D(x)P(x)​=Q(x)+D(x)R(x)​.

This is the polynomial version of turning an improper fraction into a mixed number: 475=9+25\tfrac{47}{5} = 9 + \tfrac{2}{5}547​=9+52​, quotient out front, remainder still sitting over the divisor, valid wherever D(x)≠0D(x) \neq 0D(x)=0. From Worked Example 3, for instance,

6x3+5x2−8x+72x−1=3x2+4x−2+52x−1.\frac{6x^3 + 5x^2 - 8x + 7}{2x - 1} = 3x^2 + 4x - 2 + \frac{5}{2x - 1}.2x−16x3+5x2−8x+7​=3x2+4x−2+2x−15​.

Both forms carry the same information, but proofs use the identity form. The fraction form is what you’ll reach for later, when a chapter on rational expressions takes up quotients like this one in their own right.

Check your understanding

Divide 3x2−5x+43x^2 - 5x + 43x2−5x+4 by x−2x - 2x−2, then verify your division by expanding DQ+RDQ + RDQ+R. Which option states both the correct division and a correct check?

Answer choices

Divide the leading terms twice. First pass: 3x2÷x=3x3x^2 \div x = 3x3x2÷x=3x, and 3x(x−2)=3x2−6x3x(x - 2) = 3x^2 - 6x3x(x−2)=3x2−6x; subtracting leaves x+4x + 4x+4. Second pass: x÷x=1x \div x = 1x÷x=1, and 1(x−2)=x−21(x - 2) = x - 21(x−2)=x−2; subtracting leaves 666, degree 000, below the divisor's degree 111, so the loop stops: 3x2−5x+4=(x−2)(3x+1)+63x^2 - 5x + 4 = (x - 2)(3x + 1) + 63x2−5x+4=(x−2)(3x+1)+6.

Now actually carry out the check: (x−2)(3x+1)+6=3x2+x−6x−2+6=3x2−5x+4(x - 2)(3x + 1) + 6 = 3x^2 + x - 6x - 2 + 6 = 3x^2 - 5x + 4(x−2)(3x+1)+6=3x2+x−6x−2+6=3x2−5x+4. That matches the dividend, so the division checks out.

The other three fail the check for different reasons. Option 2's quotient has the wrong sign on its constant term, and its own expansion shows it. Option 3 wrongly assumes the remainder is 000. Option 4 pairs the correct identity with an expansion that was never actually carried out: (x−2)(3x+1)+6(x - 2)(3x + 1) + 6(x−2)(3x+1)+6 really does equal 3x2−5x+43x^2 - 5x + 43x2−5x+4, not 3x2−5x−23x^2 - 5x - 23x2−5x−2. Expanding is the only way to catch a slip like that.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Skipping placeholders. Dividing x3−27x^3 - 27x3−27 without writing x3+0x2+0x−27x^3 + 0x^2 + 0x - 27x3+0x2+0x−27 lets unlike powers collide in the same column, and the subtractions turn into nonsense. Every missing power gets an explicit 000.
  • Subtracting only the leading term. Each row subtracts the entire product q⋅Dq \cdot Dq⋅D. From x2+5xx^2 + 5xx2+5x subtracting x2+2xx^2 + 2xx2+2x leaves 3x3x3x; forgetting the 2x2x2x leaves 5x5x5x and poisons every later step. Sign slips on rows like −(−x2−3x)-(-x^2 - 3x)−(−x2−3x) are the single most common division error.
  • Building the next quotient term from the wrong pieces. The next term is always the working remainder’s leading term divided by the divisor’s leading term, never by the whole divisor and never by its constant.
  • Stopping at the wrong moment. Stop exactly when the working remainder is 000 or its degree falls below the divisor’s, not when the remainder “looks small” and not only when it is zero. A nonzero remainder is a finished answer, not a failure.
  • Forgetting standard form. Both dividend and divisor must be written in falling powers before the first step, or the leading terms you divide are not the leading terms at all.
  • Dropping the divisor under the remainder in fraction form. The identity P=DQ+RP = DQ + RP=DQ+R becomes PD=Q+RD\tfrac{P}{D} = Q + \tfrac{R}{D}DP​=Q+DR​, remainder still over the divisor, not Q+RQ + RQ+R.
  • Misjudging the quotient’s degree. When deg⁡P≥deg⁡D\deg P \geq \deg DdegP≥degD, the quotient has degree deg⁡P−deg⁡D\deg P - \deg DdegP−degD, not deg⁡P\deg PdegP. A cubic divided by a linear divisor gives a quadratic quotient; anything else signals an error before you check a single coefficient.

Key takeaways

  • The division algorithm: for any dividend PPP and nonzero divisor DDD there is exactly one quotient QQQ and remainder RRR with P=DQ+RP = DQ + RP=DQ+R, where R=0R = 0R=0 or deg⁡R<deg⁡D\deg R < \deg DdegR<degD. The identity makes the answer correct; the degree condition makes it finished and unique.
  • A polynomial in standard form is a base-xxx numeral whose coefficients never carry, so integer long division carries over column for column, with degree replacing size as “smaller.”
  • Every quotient term is forced: divide the working remainder’s leading term by the divisor’s leading term, multiply back, subtract the whole product. Each pass cancels the leading term, so the degree strictly drops and the loop must end.
  • Placeholders: write 0xk0x^k0xk for every missing power before dividing, exactly as the digit 000 holds an empty place in a numeral.
  • When deg⁡P≥deg⁡D\deg P \geq \deg DdegP≥degD: deg⁡Q=deg⁡P−deg⁡D\deg Q = \deg P - \deg DdegQ=degP−degD, and the leading coefficient of QQQ is the leading coefficient of PPP divided by that of DDD. When deg⁡P<deg⁡D\deg P < \deg DdegP<degD: Q=0Q = 0Q=0 and R=PR = PR=P.
  • A remainder of 000 means DDD divides PPP, and P=DQP = DQP=DQ is a certified factorization; this is the engine the rest of the chapter runs on. Synthetic division, the next lesson, is a shortcut for this same method when the divisor has the form x−ax - ax−a.
  • Check every division by expanding DQ+RDQ + RDQ+R; the fraction form PD=Q+RD\tfrac{P}{D} = Q + \tfrac{R}{D}DP​=Q+DR​ repackages the same answer wherever D(x)≠0D(x) \neq 0D(x)=0.

Why the division loop always halts with a correct identity

The division loop always ends, and ends correctly#

Run the argument once on numbers before writing it with letters. Take P=x2+7x+5P = x^2 + 7x + 5P=x2+7x+5 and D=x+3D = x + 3D=x+3, and keep two ledgers: the quotient built so far, SSS, and the working remainder WWW still waiting to be divided. Start at S=0S = 0S=0 and W=PW = PW=P; then D⋅S+W=0+(x2+7x+5)=PD \cdot S + W = 0 + (x^2+7x+5) = PD⋅S+W=0+(x2+7x+5)=P, so the ledgers already satisfy P=DS+WP = DS + WP=DS+W.

Pass 1: the leading term of WWW is x2x^2x2 and of DDD is xxx, so q=xq = xq=x. Update SSS to 0+x=x0 + x = x0+x=x, and WWW to (x2+7x+5)−x(x+3)=4x+5(x^2+7x+5) - x(x+3) = 4x + 5(x2+7x+5)−x(x+3)=4x+5. Check the ledgers again: D⋅S+W=(x+3)(x)+(4x+5)=x2+7x+5=PD \cdot S + W = (x+3)(x) + (4x+5) = x^2+7x+5 = PD⋅S+W=(x+3)(x)+(4x+5)=x2+7x+5=P, still true, and WWW‘s degree dropped from 222 to 111.

Pass 2: the leading term of WWW is 4x4x4x and of DDD is still xxx, so q=4q = 4q=4. Update SSS to x+4x + 4x+4, and WWW to (4x+5)−4(x+3)=−7(4x+5) - 4(x+3) = -7(4x+5)−4(x+3)=−7. Once more, D⋅S+W=(x+3)(x+4)+(−7)=x2+7x+5=PD \cdot S + W = (x+3)(x+4) + (-7) = x^2+7x+5 = PD⋅S+W=(x+3)(x+4)+(−7)=x2+7x+5=P. Now deg⁡W=0\deg W = 0degW=0, below deg⁡D=1\deg D = 1degD=1, so the loop stops with x2+7x+5=(x+3)(x+4)−7x^2 + 7x + 5 = (x+3)(x+4) - 7x2+7x+5=(x+3)(x+4)−7.

The general argument runs the same two checks, an unchanged total and a falling degree, on any PPP and any nonzero DDD. Keep two ledgers while you divide: the quotient built so far, call it SSS, and the working remainder WWW still waiting to be divided. At the very start S=0S = 0S=0 and W=PW = PW=P, so the equation

P=D⋅S+WP = D \cdot S + WP=D⋅S+W

holds trivially. Now examine one pass of the loop. If W=0W = 0W=0 or deg⁡W<deg⁡D\deg W < \deg DdegW<degD, the loop stops. Otherwise write the leading term of WWW as axma x^maxm and the leading term of DDD as bxnb x^nbxn, where m≥nm \geq nm≥n because the loop did not stop. The pass divides the leading terms to get q=abxm−nq = \tfrac{a}{b} x^{m-n}q=ba​xm−n, adds qqq to the quotient ledger, and subtracts qDqDqD from the working remainder. The combination D⋅S+WD \cdot S + WD⋅S+W does not move:

D(S+q)+(W−qD)=DS+qD+W−qD=DS+W.D(S + q) + (W - qD) = DS + qD + W - qD = DS + W.D(S+q)+(W−qD)=DS+qD+W−qD=DS+W.

So the equation P=DS+WP = DS + WP=DS+W is an invariant: true before the first pass, preserved by every pass, therefore true when the loop stops. Whatever the ledgers hold at the end is automatically a correct identity.

Termination is a statement about degrees. The product qDqDqD was engineered so that its leading term is abxm−n⋅bxn=axm\tfrac{a}{b} x^{m-n} \cdot b x^n = a x^mba​xm−n⋅bxn=axm, the exact leading term of WWW. Every other term of qDqDqD is qqq times a lower term of DDD, and therefore has degree below mmm; so does every other term of WWW. In the subtraction W−qDW - qDW−qD the two copies of axma x^maxm cancel, and nothing of degree mmm or higher survives. So the new working remainder is either 000 or of degree strictly below mmm. The degree of WWW therefore drops by at least 111 on every pass, and a strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers cannot continue forever. After at most m−n+1m - n + 1m−n+1 passes the loop halts with W=0W = 0W=0 or deg⁡W<deg⁡D\deg W < \deg DdegW<degD, and the invariant then reads P=DQ+RP = DQ + RP=DQ+R with the remainder condition satisfied.

That is existence: a valid quotient and remainder can always be produced, and the long-division tableau is nothing but this proof written out in columns.

∎

Why the quotient and remainder are unique

The quotient and remainder are unique#

Run the argument once on numbers first. Take P=2x2+5x−3P = 2x^2 + 5x - 3P=2x2+5x−3 and D=x+2D = x + 2D=x+2. Dividing gives the real quotient and remainder, Q1=2x+1Q_1 = 2x + 1Q1​=2x+1 and R1=−5R_1 = -5R1​=−5: check (x+2)(2x+1)−5=2x2+5x−3(x+2)(2x+1) - 5 = 2x^2+5x-3(x+2)(2x+1)−5=2x2+5x−3. Suppose a second quotient Q2=2x+2Q_2 = 2x + 2Q2​=2x+2 also worked, paired with some remainder R2R_2R2​ chosen to keep P=DQ2+R2P = DQ_2 + R_2P=DQ2​+R2​ true. Subtract the two identities the way the general argument below does, D(Q1−Q2)=R2−R1D(Q_1 - Q_2) = R_2 - R_1D(Q1​−Q2​)=R2​−R1​. Here Q1−Q2=−1Q_1 - Q_2 = -1Q1​−Q2​=−1, so the left side is (x+2)(−1)=−x−2(x+2)(-1) = -x - 2(x+2)(−1)=−x−2, of degree 111: a nonzero polynomial times DDD has degree at least deg⁡D=1\deg D = 1degD=1, by deg⁡(fg)=deg⁡f+deg⁡g\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg gdeg(fg)=degf+degg. For R2R_2R2​ to be a legal remainder, the right side R2−R1R_2 - R_1R2​−R1​ would have to be zero or of degree below deg⁡D=1\deg D = 1degD=1, that is, a plain number. It cannot be, since it must equal −x−2-x - 2−x−2, which has degree 111. No legal R2R_2R2​ exists, so Q2≠Q1Q_2 \neq Q_1Q2​=Q1​ is impossible here.

The general argument makes that failure explicit for every possible second pair, not just this one. Suppose two pairs both keep the promises for the same dividend and divisor:

P=D Q1+R1=D Q2+R2,P = D\,Q_1 + R_1 = D\,Q_2 + R_2,P=DQ1​+R1​=DQ2​+R2​,

where each RiR_iRi​ is zero or of degree less than deg⁡D\deg DdegD. Subtract one identity from the other and gather the DDD terms on one side:

D (Q1−Q2)=R2−R1.D\,(Q_1 - Q_2) = R_2 - R_1.D(Q1​−Q2​)=R2​−R1​.

Now suppose, aiming for a contradiction, that Q1≠Q2Q_1 \neq Q_2Q1​=Q2​. Then Q1−Q2Q_1 - Q_2Q1​−Q2​ is a nonzero polynomial, so the left side is a product of two nonzero polynomials, and by the degree fact deg⁡(fg)=deg⁡f+deg⁡g\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg gdeg(fg)=degf+degg its degree is deg⁡D+deg⁡(Q1−Q2)≥deg⁡D\deg D + \deg(Q_1 - Q_2) \geq \deg DdegD+deg(Q1​−Q2​)≥degD. Look at the right side. Each remainder is zero or of degree below deg⁡D\deg DdegD, so their difference is zero or of degree below deg⁡D\deg DdegD as well: a difference cannot reach higher than its tallest term. One polynomial cannot be both of degree at least deg⁡D\deg DdegD and of degree below deg⁡D\deg DdegD (nor can a nonzero polynomial equal the zero polynomial), so the supposition fails, and Q1=Q2Q_1 = Q_2Q1​=Q2​. Feeding that back into DQ1+R1=DQ2+R2D Q_1 + R_1 = D Q_2 + R_2DQ1​+R1​=DQ2​+R2​ leaves R1=R2R_1 = R_2R1​=R2​.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

For most of its history a polynomial was a way of stating a problem, not a thing you calculated with directly. Simon Stevin, a Flemish engineer, changed that. In 1585 he published two books that treated numbers and letters alike. The slim De Thiende (“The Tenth”) taught Europe to write fractions as decimal places. The longer companion volume did the same for algebra, showing that a polynomial could be added, multiplied, and divided with a remainder just as a whole number can.

Stevin favored plain, ordinary language over the dense Latin common in the scholarship of his day, so that working clerks and engineers could follow him. A numeral is built on powers of ten; a polynomial is built on powers of xxx. The division you did today is his claim in miniature: nothing new is needed, only letters where digits stood.

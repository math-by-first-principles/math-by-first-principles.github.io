    Worked solution

    Part A

    Substituting x=pqx = \frac{p}{q}x=qp​ into P(x)=0P(x) = 0P(x)=0 gives an equation cluttered with denominators:

    ap3q3+bp2q2+cpq+d=0a\frac{p^3}{q^3} + b\frac{p^2}{q^2} + c\frac{p}{q} + d = 0aq3p3​+bq2p2​+cqp​+d=0

    Divisibility is a statement about integers, so the first move is to remove the fractions. Multiply every term by q3q^3q3, which is not zero. Each term picks up exactly the powers of qqq it was missing:

    ap3+bp2q+cpq2+dq3=0ap^3 + bp^2q + cpq^2 + dq^3 = 0ap3+bp2q+cpq2+dq3=0

    Every term here is an integer, because aaa, bbb, ccc, ddd, ppp and qqq all are. Now isolate the single term carrying no factor of ppp, which is the last one, and pull ppp out of everything else:

    dq3=−(ap3+bp2q+cpq2)=−p(ap2+bpq+cq2)dq^3 = -\left(ap^3 + bp^2q + cpq^2\right) = -p\left(ap^2 + bpq + cq^2\right)dq3=−(ap3+bp2q+cpq2)=−p(ap2+bpq+cq2)

    The bracket on the right is an integer, so ppp divides dq3dq^3dq3.

    One case has to be set aside before the next step, because that step compares prime factorizations and 000 has none. If d=0d = 0d=0 then p∣dp \mid dp∣d holds for every integer ppp, since 0=p×00 = p \times 00=p×0, and there is nothing left to prove. So suppose from here that d≠0d \ne 0d=0.

    The final step is where the lowest-terms hypothesis earns its place. ppp and qqq have no prime factor in common, and the primes of q3q^3q3 are exactly the primes of qqq, so ppp and q3q^3q3 have no prime factor in common either. A prime power sitting inside ppp therefore cannot be accounted for by q3q^3q3, and must sit inside ddd. That holds for every prime power in ppp, so the whole of ppp divides ddd:

    p∣dp \mid dp∣d

    Without coprimality the step simply fails, because ppp would be free to hide inside q3q^3q3 instead.

    Part B

    The cleared equation is symmetric in a useful way. Written out again,

    ap3+bp2q+cpq2+dq3=0,ap^3 + bp^2q + cpq^2 + dq^3 = 0,ap3+bp2q+cpq2+dq3=0,

    every term after the first carries at least one factor of qqq, exactly as every term before the last carried at least one factor of ppp. So run the same manoeuvre from the other end. Isolate the first term and pull qqq out of the rest:

    ap3=−(bp2q+cpq2+dq3)=−q(bp2+cpq+dq2)ap^3 = -\left(bp^2q + cpq^2 + dq^3\right) = -q\left(bp^2 + cpq + dq^2\right)ap3=−(bp2q+cpq2+dq3)=−q(bp2+cpq+dq2)

    The bracket is an integer, so qqq divides ap3ap^3ap3.

    The finish is the part A argument with ppp and qqq swapped. The two share no prime factor, so qqq shares no prime factor with p3p^3p3, no prime power of qqq can be accounted for by p3p^3p3, and each must sit inside aaa. Hence

    q∣aq \mid aq∣a

    Nothing else changed: the same cleared equation, the same coprimality, the same reasoning about where prime factors are allowed to live. That is why the theorem's two conditions look like mirror images. They are two readings of one equation, taken from its two ends.

    It also explains something the rule alone never explains, which is why the middle coefficients appear in neither conclusion. Their terms carry a factor of ppp and a factor of qqq at once, so in both calculations they are swept into the bracket and never end up alone on the left.

    Part C

    Withdrawing lowest terms. The polynomial 4x2−14x^2 - 14x2−1 has integer coefficients, and 12\frac{1}{2}21​ is genuinely a root:

    4(12)2−1=1−1=04\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 04(21​)2−1=1−1=0

    Written as 12\frac{1}{2}21​ the conclusions hold: the numerator 111 divides the constant term −1-1−1, and the denominator 222 divides the leading coefficient 444. Now write the same number as 24\frac{2}{4}42​, so the numerator is 222 and the denominator is 444. The first conclusion demands that 222 divide −1-1−1, and it does not. The number is still a root; all that changed was how it was written. Part A shows exactly where the damage is done: with a common factor of 222 shared by numerator and denominator, that factor can be accounted for by the power of the denominator instead of by the constant term, and the divisibility never reaches ddd at all.

    Withdrawing integer coefficients. The polynomial x2−52x+1x^2 - \frac{5}{2}x + 1x2−25​x+1 has 222 as a root:

    4−5+1=04 - 5 + 1 = 04−5+1=0

    Here 222 is 21\frac{2}{1}12​ in lowest terms, so the fraction is written correctly and the numerator is 222. The conclusion demands that 222 divide the constant term 111, and it does not. The failure is upstream this time: the proof multiplied through by q3q^3q3 and declared every term an integer, which needs integer coefficients, and this polynomial has none such in the middle.

    The repair is the standard one. Multiply the whole polynomial by 222:

    2x2−5x+22x^2 - 5x + 22x2−5x+2

    Multiplying an equation by a nonzero constant moves no root, so 222 is still a root, and the coefficients are now integers. Test the conclusions again: the numerator 222 divides the constant term 222, and the denominator 111 divides the leading coefficient 222. Both hold. Clearing fractional coefficients before applying the theorem is not housekeeping. It is what makes the theorem apply at all.

Hyperbolas Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

Two microphones, a kilometre apart, both record the same thunderclap. The far one hears it half a second later, so you are exactly 170170170 metres further from it than from the near one. That single number, the difference of your two distances, does not pin you to a point, but it does pin you to a curve. An ellipse is what you get when the sum of two distances is fixed; a hyperbola is what you get when the difference is fixed. Keep the same two pins and the same algebra, and change a plus to a minus. Then the closed oval turns into two open branches that fly apart forever, each one steered by a straight line it approaches but never reaches.

The distance condition

Fix two points F1F_1F1​ and F2F_2F2​, the foci, and fix a positive constant. A hyperbola is the set of points PPP whose distances to the two foci have that constant absolute difference:

∣ PF1−PF2 ∣=2a.\bigl|\, PF_1 - PF_2 \,\bigr| = 2a .​PF1​−PF2​​=2a.

Two details in that sentence do all the work.

The constant is written 2a2a2a rather than kkk purely for convenience, exactly as with the ellipse. The algebra below turns it into a clean a2a^2a2, and aaa will turn out to be the distance from the centre to each turning point of the curve.

The absolute value is what creates two branches. Drop it and you are left with PF1−PF2=2aPF_1 - PF_2 = 2aPF1​−PF2​=2a, which says “I am 2a2a2a closer to F2F_2F2​ than to F1F_1F1​”. That condition describes only the points on the side of F2F_2F2​: one branch. The equation PF1−PF2=−2aPF_1 - PF_2 = -2aPF1​−PF2​=−2a describes the mirror-image branch on the side of F1F_1F1​. The absolute value accepts both, so a hyperbola is one curve made of two separate pieces.

How large can the constant be

Not every constant produces a curve. Let 2c2c2c be the distance F1F2F_1F_2F1​F2​ between the foci, and pick a point PPP of the set that is off the line through the foci, so PPP, F1F_1F1​, F2F_2F2​ form a real triangle. In any triangle, one side is always shorter than the sum of the other two, and that squeeze also bounds how far apart two sides can be: the difference of two sides is always strictly less than the third side. Applied here, that is ∣PF1−PF2∣<F1F2|PF_1 - PF_2| < F_1F_2∣PF1​−PF2​∣<F1​F2​, so the constant must satisfy 2a<2c2a < 2c2a<2c, that is

0<a<c.0 < a < c .0<a<c.

The excluded cases are worth naming, because they tell you what a hyperbola is not. If 2a=2c2a = 2c2a=2c the only points that qualify lie on the line through the foci, outside the segment joining them, and you get two rays instead of a curve. If 2a>2c2a > 2c2a>2c nothing qualifies at all and the set is empty. A real hyperbola needs 0<a<c0 < a < c0<a<c, and this is the exact opposite of the ellipse, where the sum condition forces a>ca > ca>c.

∎

The sign flip is the whole story of this chapter, so put the two results side by side and stare at them. For an ellipse the sum condition forces a>ca > ca>c, and b2=a2−c2b^2 = a^2 - c^2b2=a2−c2. For a hyperbola the difference condition forces c>ac > ac>a, and

b2=c2−a2,equivalentlyc2=a2+b2.b^2 = c^2 - a^2, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad c^2 = a^2 + b^2 .b2=c2−a2,equivalentlyc2=a2+b2.

For a hyperbola ccc is the largest of the three, and it is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs aaa and bbb. For an ellipse aaa is the largest and it is the hypotenuse. Same three letters, same Pythagorean relation, different letter in the starring role.

Eccentricity

The eccentricity of a hyperbola is the same ratio used for the other conics:

e=ca.e = \frac{c}{a} .e=ac​.

Because c>ac > ac>a for every hyperbola, e>1e > 1e>1 always. That single inequality is what sorts these two conic sections: an ellipse has 0≤e<10 \le e < 10≤e<1 (its ccc is smaller than its aaa), and a hyperbola has e>1e > 1e>1. Push a conic’s eccentricity up past 111 and the closed oval bursts open into two branches.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using the ellipse’s relation. For a hyperbola b2=c2−a2b^2 = c^2 - a^2b2=c2−a2, so c2=a2+b2c^2 = a^2 + b^2c2=a2+b2 and ccc is the largest of the three. Reaching for b2=a2−c2b^2 = a^2 - c^2b2=a2−c2 produces a negative number as soon as c>ac > ac>a, which is always. If b2b^2b2 comes out negative, you used the ellipse’s formula.
  • Reading the orientation off the bigger denominator. That rule is true for ellipses and false for hyperbolas. y24−x29=1\frac{y^2}{4} - \frac{x^2}{9} = 14y2​−9x2​=1 opens up and down even though 9>49 > 49>4, because the y2y^2y2 term is the positive one. The sign decides, and a2a^2a2 always sits under the positive term.
  • Flipping the asymptote slope. For x2a2−y2b2=1\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1a2x2​−b2y2​=1 the slopes are ±ba\pm\frac{b}{a}±ab​; for y2a2−x2b2=1\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1a2y2​−b2x2​=1 they are ±ab\pm\frac{a}{b}±ba​. Instead of memorising, replace the 111 by 000 and solve for yyy.
  • Saying the branch “reaches” the asymptote. On the upper right of the horizontal form (x≥ax \ge ax≥a), the gap equals abx+x2−a2\frac{ab}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}}x+x2−a2​ab​, which is positive at every such xxx, so the curve never touches the line there, and by the curve’s symmetry the same holds everywhere else on it. It gets closer than any target you name, which is a completely different claim from arriving.
  • Losing the minus sign while completing the square. In 9(x2−6x)−16(y2+4y)=1279(x^2 - 6x) - 16(y^2 + 4y) = 1279(x2−6x)−16(y2+4y)=127, adding 444 inside the second bracket changes the left side by −16⋅4=−64-16 \cdot 4 = -64−16⋅4=−64, so the right side must change by −64-64−64 too. Adding +64+64+64 instead is an easy slip to make and an easy one to check: it is the exact sign worked example 3 walks through by hand.
  • Dropping the absolute value in the definition. Without it the condition names one branch, not the curve. The absolute value, and nothing else, is what gives a hyperbola its two pieces.

Key takeaways

  • A hyperbola is the set of points whose distances to two foci have a constant absolute difference 2a2a2a. The absolute value is what produces the two branches, and a curve exists only when 0<a<c0 < a < c0<a<c, where 2c2c2c is the distance between the foci.
  • Isolating and squaring twice turns that condition into x2a2−y2b2=1\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1a2x2​−b2y2​=1, with b2=c2−a2b^2 = c^2 - a^2b2=c2−a2. Compare the ellipse’s b2=a2−c2b^2 = a^2 - c^2b2=a2−c2: same letters, flipped subtraction, because a difference of distances is bounded by 2c2c2c while a sum is bounded below by it.
  • The positive term names the axis. x2a2−y2b2=1\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1a2x2​−b2y2​=1 opens left and right; y2a2−x2b2=1\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1a2y2​−b2x2​=1 opens up and down. Nothing forces a>ba > ba>b, so the bigger denominator tells you nothing.
  • Vertices sit aaa from the centre and foci sit c=a2+b2c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}c=a2+b2​ from the centre. The distance from the centre to a corner of the central rectangle (half-width aaa, half-height bbb) is also ccc. The rectangle’s diagonals are the asymptotes, found by replacing the 111 with 000: slopes ±ba\pm\frac{b}{a}±ab​ horizontally, ±ab\pm\frac{a}{b}±ba​ vertically.
  • On the upper right of the horizontal form (x≥ax \ge ax≥a), the exact vertical gap to an asymptote is abx+x2−a2\frac{ab}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}}x+x2−a2​ab​. A bit of history (optional)

Out of sight of land, a ship used to be lost in a very particular way. The crew knew their heading and their speed, so they could work out roughly how far they had travelled. Small errors piled up over days, the estimate drifted, and there was nothing on open water to check it against. Fog or cloud took away even the stars.

Radio changed that during the Second World War. One station on the coast fires a pulse, and a partner station far along the coast answers after a fixed, published delay. A receiver at sea cannot tell how far away either station is, and it never tries. It measures one thing only: the gap between the two arrivals, with that known delay subtracted back out.

That gap is a difference of distances, and this lesson has already told you what a fixed difference means. The ship lies somewhere on a hyperbola whose foci are the two stations. One curve is not a position, so a second pair of stations supplies a second hyperbola, and the ship is where the two curves cross. Navigators knew the system as LORAN, and the curves were printed straight onto the charts, a fan of them for every pair.

Watch how much work the sign of that corrected gap is doing. It says which of the two stations is nearer, and that alone picks one branch out of the two. Strip the sign away and what remains is the absolute value this lesson began with, which accepts both branches and cannot tell them apart.

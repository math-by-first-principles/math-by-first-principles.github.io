Parabolas Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

You already know one parabola: the graph of y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c, with its vertex, its axis of symmetry, and its roots. But that description does not explain why a satellite dish or a telescope mirror is curved into exactly that shape. Those machines are built from a distance condition instead: pin down a single point, draw a single line, and collect every point of the plane exactly as far from the point as from the line. That collection turns out to be a parabola, the same curve you have been graphing all along. This lesson proves the two descriptions match, and the proof hands you a point (the focus) and a line (the directrix) that no coefficient ever showed you.

Learning goals

  • Define a parabola by equal distance to a focus and a directrix
  • Derive x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py from that distance condition, with a=14pa = \tfrac{1}{4p}a=4p1​
  • Locate the focus and directrix for a vertical or horizontal axis
  • Measure the focal width as ∣4p∣|4p|∣4p∣ and distinguish it from ppp
  • Describe why every ray parallel to the axis reflects through the focus

The distance condition that defines a parabola

Try the numbers first. Put a point F=(0,1)F = (0,1)F=(0,1) and, two units below it, a horizontal line d ⁣:y=−1d\colon y = -1d:y=−1. Measure two points against both of them.

  • (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) is 111 unit from FFF (straight up) and 111 unit from ddd (straight down). The two distances match.
  • (4,4)(4,4)(4,4) is 42+32=5\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=542+32​=5 units from FFF, and 4−(−1)=54-(-1)=54−(−1)=5 units from ddd. They match again.
  • (4,3)(4,3)(4,3) is 42+22=20≈4.47\sqrt{4^2+2^2}=\sqrt{20}\approx 4.4742+22​=20​≈4.47 units from FFF, but only 3−(−1)=43-(-1)=43−(−1)=4 units from ddd. Here the two distances disagree.

Collect every point whose distance to FFF equals its distance to ddd, not just the two that matched above, and the result is a curve: a parabola. The point FFF is its focus, and the line ddd is its directrix.

Written as a general rule instead of two specific points: fix a point FFF and a line ddd that does not pass through FFF. The parabola with focus FFF and directrix ddd is the set of all points PPP in the plane satisfying

PF=dist⁡(P,d),PF = \operatorname{dist}(P, d),PF=dist(P,d),

that is, every point whose distance to FFF equals its distance to ddd. A curve defined this way, by a condition every point must pass rather than by a formula, is called a locus.

The condition that FFF is not on ddd matters. If you put the focus on the directrix, the only points that stay equidistant are the ones on the perpendicular to ddd through FFF. In that case the “curve” collapses into a straight line. A parabola needs the focus held off the line.

Two more names come straight out of the definition. The axis of the parabola is the line through FFF perpendicular to ddd. Reflecting the plane across the axis fixes FFF and maps ddd onto itself, so it carries the whole curve onto itself: reflect any point that satisfies the distance condition, and the image still satisfies it. The vertex is the point of the curve on that axis. The vertex must be the midpoint between FFF and the directrix, since that is the one point on the axis whose two distances agree.

To turn that picture into algebra you need to measure two distances, so here are the two measuring tools, both of which come from the Pythagorean theorem.

Distance between two points. Put P=(x1,y1)P = (x_1, y_1)P=(x1​,y1​) and Q=(x2,y2)Q = (x_2, y_2)Q=(x2​,y2​) and complete the right triangle whose legs are horizontal and vertical. The horizontal leg has length ∣x2−x1∣|x_2 - x_1|∣x2​−x1​∣, the vertical leg has length ∣y2−y1∣|y_2 - y_1|∣y2​−y1​∣, and PQPQPQ is the hypotenuse, so the Pythagorean theorem gives

PQ=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2.PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}.PQ=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​.

Distance from a point to a line. The distance from a point to a line means the shortest distance, and it is measured along the perpendicular. Any other segment from PPP to the line is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose leg is that perpendicular. A hypotenuse is always longer than either leg, so no other route can be shorter. For a horizontal line y=my = my=m the perpendicular is vertical, so the distance from P=(x0,y0)P = (x_0, y_0)P=(x0​,y0​) is simply

dist⁡(P,  y=m)=∣y0−m∣,\operatorname{dist}(P, \; y = m) = |y_0 - m|,dist(P,y=m)=∣y0​−m∣,

and for a vertical line x=mx = mx=m it is ∣x0−m∣|x_0 - m|∣x0​−m∣. Nothing more complicated is needed, because every directrix in this lesson is horizontal or vertical.

Deriving the equation from the definition

Now put the definition to work in coordinates. Place the vertex at the origin, and let ppp be the signed distance from the vertex up to the focus, so that

F=(0,p),d ⁣:  y=−p,p≠0.F = (0, p), \qquad d\colon\; y = -p, \qquad p \neq 0 .F=(0,p),d:y=−p,p=0.

The vertex (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) is then correctly halfway between them. Everything about the curve is now encoded in the single number ppp.

The locus of points equidistant from (0,p)(0,p)(0,p) and the line y=−py = -py=−p is x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py#

Let P=(x,y)P = (x, y)P=(x,y) be any point of the plane. Its distance to the focus is PF=(x−0)2+(y−p)2PF = \sqrt{(x - 0)^2 + (y - p)^2}PF=(x−0)2+(y−p)2​, and its distance to the directrix, a horizontal line, is ∣y−(−p)∣=∣y+p∣|y - (-p)| = |y + p|∣y−(−p)∣=∣y+p∣. The point PPP lies on the parabola exactly when those two agree:

x2+(y−p)2=∣y+p∣.\sqrt{x^2 + (y - p)^2} = |y + p| .x2+(y−p)2​=∣y+p∣.

Both sides are distances, so both are greater than or equal to zero, and for nonnegative quantities A=BA = BA=B holds exactly when A2=B2A^2 = B^2A2=B2. Squaring therefore loses nothing and gains nothing; it is a reversible step, and the squared equation has precisely the same solutions as the original.

x2+(y−p)2=(y+p)2x^2 + (y - p)^2 = (y + p)^2x2+(y−p)2=(y+p)2

Expand both squares. On the left, (y−p)2=y2−2py+p2(y-p)^2 = y^2 - 2py + p^2(y−p)2=y2−2py+p2; on the right, (y+p)2=y2+2py+p2(y+p)^2 = y^2 + 2py + p^2(y+p)2=y2+2py+p2:

x2+y2−2py+p2=y2+2py+p2.x^2 + y^2 - 2py + p^2 = y^2 + 2py + p^2 .x2+y2−2py+p2=y2+2py+p2.

The y2y^2y2 and the p2p^2p2 appear on both sides and cancel, which is the whole point of choosing the directrix at y=−py = -py=−p. The quadratic terms in yyy destroy each other, and only the linear ones survive. What is left is

x2=4py.x^2 = 4py .x2=4py.

Every step was reversible, so a point satisfies the distance condition if and only if it satisfies this equation. The locus is exactly the graph of x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py.

∎

Read the result again, because it is the punchline of the lesson. Dividing by 4p4p4p turns the equation into

y=14p x2,y = \frac{1}{4p}\,x^2 ,y=4p1​x2,

which is y=ax2y = ax^2y=ax2 with a=14pa = \dfrac{1}{4p}a=4p1​. The purely geometric locus, defined without a single coefficient, is the quadratic graph you have been drawing since Chapter 4. Run the relation the other way and every quadratic y=ax2y = ax^2y=ax2 acquires a focus and a directrix it always had but never advertised:

a=14p⟺p=14a.a = \frac{1}{4p} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad p = \frac{1}{4a} .a=4p1​⟺p=4a1​.

For y=x2y = x^2y=x2, for instance, a=1a = 1a=1 gives p=14p = \tfrac14p=41​: that familiar curve is the set of points equidistant from the point (0,14)\left(0, \tfrac14\right)(0,41​) and the line y=−14y = -\tfrac14y=−41​.

The sign of ppp carries the direction. If p>0p > 0p>0 the focus sits above the vertex, the directrix below, and the curve opens upward, wrapping around its focus. If p<0p < 0p<0 the focus sits below the vertex, the directrix above, and the curve opens downward. In both cases the parabola bends toward the focus and runs away from the directrix, which is the fastest way to get the direction right without memorizing anything.

The definition also hands you a shortcut worth keeping. For a point (x,y)(x,y)(x,y) on x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py with p>0p > 0p>0, its distance to the focus equals its distance to the directrix by definition, and the second of those is easy:

PF=y+p.PF = y + p .PF=y+p.

No square root, no substitution. The distance from a point on the parabola to the focus, called its focal distance, is read straight off the yyy-coordinate.

The focal width, and why the number is 4p4p4p

Where does the 444 in x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py actually show up on the picture? Draw the chord through the focus parallel to the directrix and measure it. On x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py the focus is at height y=py = py=p, so substitute:

x2=4p⋅p=4p2⟹x=±2p.x^2 = 4p \cdot p = 4p^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x = \pm 2p .x2=4p⋅p=4p2⟹x=±2p.

The chord runs from (−2p, p)(-2p,\, p)(−2p,p) to (2p, p)(2p,\, p)(2p,p), so its length is ∣4p∣|4p|∣4p∣. That chord is the focal width, and it is the single most useful number for sketching a parabola by hand. Go to the focus, move ∣2p∣|2p|∣2p∣ each way, and you have two more points on the curve.

This also settles the most common confusion in this lesson. The coefficient in x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py is 4p4p4p, and it is not the focus distance. Its magnitude, ∣4p∣|4p|∣4p∣, is the focal width; the focus distance is a quarter of that magnitude, ∣p∣|p|∣p∣. A curve with a big ∣4p∣|4p|∣4p∣ is wide and shallow, and its focus sits far from the vertex. A curve with a small ∣4p∣|4p|∣4p∣ is narrow and steep, and its focus is tucked in close.

Moving the vertex

Nothing so far depended on the vertex being at the origin, and moving it costs no new work. Translation is a rigid motion: sliding two points by the same vector does not change the distance between them. In the same way, sliding a point and a line by the same vector does not change the distance from the point to the line. So if you translate the focus and the directrix by (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), the locus of equidistant points translates by (h,k)(h,k)(h,k) as well. That follows because the defining condition is stated entirely in terms of distances.

Apply that to the focus (0,p)(0,p)(0,p) and directrix y=−py = -py=−p, whose locus is x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py. Moving the vertex to (h,k)(h,k)(h,k) means every distance is now measured from (h,k)(h,k)(h,k) instead of from the origin, so xxx is replaced by x−hx - hx−h and yyy is replaced by y−ky - ky−k everywhere. The focus moves to (h, k+p)(h,\, k+p)(h,k+p), the directrix becomes y=k−py = k - py=k−p, and the equation x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py becomes the standard form of a parabola with a vertical axis:

(x−h)2=4p (y−k),(x - h)^2 = 4p\,(y - k),(x−h)2=4p(y−k), vertex (h,k),focus (h,  k+p),directrix y=k−p.\text{vertex } (h,k), \qquad \text{focus } (h,\; k + p), \qquad \text{directrix } y = k - p .vertex (h,k),focus (h,k+p),directrix y=k−p.

This is the bridge back to Chapter 4. Any quadratic y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c can be put into vertex form y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x-h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k by completing the square, and rearranging that gives (x−h)2=1a(y−k)(x-h)^2 = \tfrac{1}{a}(y - k)(x−h)2=a1​(y−k), which is the standard form above with 4p=1a4p = \tfrac{1}{a}4p=a1​. So every quadratic you have ever graphed already carries a focus and a directrix inside its vertex form; completing the square is all it takes to read them off.

Notice the shape of that conversion: the square must stand alone on one side. As soon as you write y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x-h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k you are one step away, but the step matters, because 4p=1a4p = \tfrac1a4p=a1​ and not aaa.

Why the focus deserves its name

Every ray that arrives parallel to the axis leaves the curve through one single point, which is exactly why that point is called the focus. A radio dish collects a plane wave from a satellite millions of kilometres away and concentrates it onto a receiver the size of a thumb. A telescope mirror gathers starlight and delivers it to the eyepiece. A car headlight runs the same idea backwards, putting the bulb at the focus so the reflected light leaves in a parallel beam.

Here is the plain reason it works. Reflection off a curve keeps equal angles to the tangent line at the point of impact. At any point PPP on the parabola, PF=PDPF = PDPF=PD, so PPP sits exactly halfway, in angle, between the focus FFF and the foot of the perpendicular DDD on the directrix: the tangent at PPP is the line that treats FFF and DDD as mirror images of each other. A ray coming straight down the axis direction arrives along PDPDPD, bounces off that tangent, and leaves along PFPFPF, headed for the focus. Since this happens at every point of the curve, every ray parallel to the axis, wherever it lands, ends up at the same point FFF.

Why does a ray parallel to the axis reflect toward the focus at every point P on the parabola?

Answer choices

The whole argument rests on the defining condition PF=PDPF = PDPF=PD. That equal distance is exactly what lets the tangent at PPP swap FFF and DDD like a mirror, so a ray arriving along PDPDPD leaves along PFPFPF. It is the same distance condition that built the parabola in the first place, now doing double duty.

The paragraph above skips one detail: showing that the tangent line really is the mirror that swaps FFF and DDD, not merely a plausible candidate. The full geometric argument is in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Reflection is reversible. Rays that arrive parallel to the axis leave through the focus, so rays that start at the focus must leave parallel to the axis. That is exactly why the headlight bulb sits there: every ray leaves as one straight beam instead of spreading out.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing ppp with 4p4p4p. In x2=12yx^2 = 12yx2=12y the coefficient is 4p4p4p, so p=3p = 3p=3 and the focus is (0,3)(0,3)(0,3), not (0,12)(0,12)(0,12). The magnitude of the number sitting in front, ∣4p∣|4p|∣4p∣, is the focal width; the focus distance is a quarter of that magnitude, ∣p∣|p|∣p∣.
  • Reading 4p4p4p off the vertex form. From y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x-h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k you cannot read 4p=a4p = a4p=a. Isolate the square first, (x−h)2=1a(y−k)(x-h)^2 = \tfrac1a (y-k)(x−h)2=a1​(y−k), so 4p=1a4p = \tfrac1a4p=a1​. For y=2(x−3)2+1y = 2(x-3)^2 + 1y=2(x−3)2+1 that means p=18p = \tfrac18p=81​, not 222.
  • Losing the sign of ppp. A negative ppp is not an error to be absolute-valued away. It says the focus is below (or left of) the vertex and the directrix is above (or right of) it, so the curve opens the other way. The parabola always bends toward the focus and away from the directrix.
  • Treating the directrix as a point. The directrix is a line, so its answer is an equation like y=−3y = -3y=−3 or x=4x = 4x=4, never a coordinate pair. The mirror image of the same slip is quoting the focus as a line.
  • Mixing up the two standard forms. (x−h)2=4p(y−k)(x-h)^2 = 4p(y-k)(x−h)2=4p(y−k) squares xxx and opens up or down; (y−k)2=4p(x−h)(y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h)(y−k)2=4p(x−h) squares yyy and opens left or right. Whichever variable is squared, the curve moves along the other one, and only the form with yyy squared fails the vertical line test.

Key takeaways

  • A parabola is the set of points equidistant from a fixed point (the focus) and a fixed line (the directrix), where the focus is not on the directrix. The axis is the perpendicular from the focus to the directrix, and the vertex is the midpoint between them.
  • The distance formula PQ=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2PQ = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}PQ=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​ is the Pythagorean theorem; the distance from a point to a horizontal line y=my = my=m is ∣y0−m∣|y_0 - m|∣y0​−m∣.
  • Squaring the distance condition for focus (0,p)(0,p)(0,p) and directrix y=−py = -py=−p gives x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py, that is y=14px2y = \tfrac{1}{4p}x^2y=4p1​x2. So the geometric locus is exactly the quadratic graph, with a=14pa = \tfrac{1}{4p}a=4p1​ and p=14ap = \tfrac{1}{4a}p=4a1​.
  • Vertical axis: (x−h)2=4p(y−k)(x-h)^2 = 4p(y-k)(x−h)2=4p(y−k), focus (h,k+p)(h, k+p)(h,k+p), directrix y=k−py = k - py=k−p; it opens up when p>0p > 0p>0 and down when p<0p < 0p<0.
  • Horizontal axis: (y−k)2=4p(x−h)(y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h)(y−k)2=4p(x−h), focus (h+p,k)(h+p, k)(h+p,k), directrix x=h−px = h - px=h−p; it opens right when p>0p > 0p>0 and left when p<0p < 0p<0, and it is not the graph of a function of xxx.
  • The focal width is ∣4p∣|4p|∣4p∣, the chord through the focus parallel to the directrix. The focal distance of a point on x2=4pyx^2 = 4pyx2=4py is ∣y+p∣|y + p|∣y+p∣, which is simply y+py + py+p when p>0p > 0p>0.
  • Every ray parallel to the axis reflects through the focus, which is what a dish, a headlight, and a telescope mirror are built on.

The full proof that every parallel ray reflects through the focus

The proof needs one fact from physics, that a ray reflects off a curve at equal angles to the tangent line. It also needs one fact from geometry, that the set of points equidistant from two points is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them: the same kind of locus as the parabola’s own definition, but built from a point and a point instead of a point and a line.

Rays parallel to the axis reflect through the focus#

Let PPP be any point of the parabola, let FFF be the focus, and let DDD be the foot of the perpendicular from PPP to the directrix. The segment PDPDPD therefore measures PPP‘s distance to the directrix. The definition says PF=PDPF = PDPF=PD, so PPP is equidistant from the two points FFF and DDD, which places PPP on the perpendicular bisector ℓ\ellℓ of the segment FDFDFD.

First, ℓ\ellℓ is the tangent line to the parabola at PPP. Compare ℓ\ellℓ against every point of the parabola, not just PPP. Let RRR be any point of the parabola. By the parabola’s own definition, RF=dist⁡(R,d)RF = \operatorname{dist}(R, d)RF=dist(R,d). But DDD is only one particular point of the directrix, while the distance from RRR to the directrix is measured along the perpendicular through RRR, so dist⁡(R,d)≤RD\operatorname{dist}(R, d) \le RDdist(R,d)≤RD, with equality only when RRR sits directly above DDD, which for this parabola happens at R=PR = PR=P and nowhere else. Chaining the two gives

RF≤RD,RF \le RD ,RF≤RD,

for every point RRR of the parabola, with equality exactly at R=PR = PR=P. A point satisfying RF≤RDRF \le RDRF≤RD is at least as close to FFF as to DDD, so it sits in the closed half of the plane on FFF‘s side of ℓ\ellℓ, the perpendicular bisector of FDFDFD, landing exactly on ℓ\ellℓ only when RF=RDRF = RDRF=RD. So the whole parabola lies in that closed half-plane, touching its boundary ℓ\ellℓ at the single point PPP and nowhere else. A line that meets a curve at one point and keeps the whole curve on a single side of itself is called a supporting line, and ℓ\ellℓ is one. Here we use, without reproving it, a standard fact about smooth convex curves: each point has exactly one supporting line, and that line is the tangent there. A parabola is visibly smooth and bends only one way, so it qualifies, and ℓ\ellℓ must be that tangent, the line the law of reflection measures its equal angles against.

Now send in the ray. The ray that travels parallel to the axis toward the directrix and strikes the curve at PPP arrives along the line PDPDPD. The reason is that PDPDPD is perpendicular to the directrix and so is parallel to the axis. Reflect that ray in the tangent line ℓ\ellℓ, which is what “equal angles to the tangent” means. Reflection in ℓ\ellℓ fixes PPP and swaps FFF and DDD, because ℓ\ellℓ is the perpendicular bisector of FDFDFD and that is precisely the mirror exchanging its two endpoints. So the line PDPDPD is carried to the line PFPFPF, and since FFF and DDD sit on opposite sides of ℓ\ellℓ, the reflected ray leaves PPP heading toward FFF.

The point PPP was an arbitrary point of the curve, and FFF did not move during the argument. So every ray parallel to the axis, wherever it strikes, is sent through the same point FFF. Reflection is reversible, so the converse holds too: a source placed at the focus sends out a beam of rays all parallel to the axis.

∎

This is also why the shape cannot be faked in practice. A shallow circular mirror is nearly parabolic near its centre, so it brings rays landing near the middle roughly to a point, but the further out a ray strikes, the further it misses. A true parabola sends every parallel ray through the focus, however far out it strikes, which is exactly what a telescope mirror or a dish antenna needs. The converse is true too, that essentially no other curve shares this property at every point and in every direction, but that stronger claim needs its own proof and is not shown here.

A bit of history (optional)

A polished bowl aimed at the sun can set wood alight, and ancient engineers wanted that bright patch as tight as possible. A shallow bowl will not oblige: rays landing near the middle gather, but rays striking the rim arrive somewhere else, and the patch smears into a blur.

Diocles, a Greek geometer writing around 200 BCE, asked which curve refuses to smear. His book On Burning Mirrors answers it: shape the mirror from a parabola, and every ray parallel to the axis is thrown through one point, however far out along the curve it strikes. He worked this out with no algebra at all, straight from the geometry of the cut cone.

The demand has not changed since, only what is being gathered: starlight into a telescope eyepiece, a radio signal onto a dish antenna’s receiver, or a headlight bulb’s light sent out as a parallel beam. Diocles’ claim is the one proved in the Go deeper section above. The tangent at a point is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the focus to the foot of the perpendicular on the directrix, and reflecting in that line sends every parallel ray home.

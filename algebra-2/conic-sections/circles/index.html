Circles

Intuition

A circle is not really a formula. It is a rule about distance: fix a point, fix a length, and collect every point in the plane that sits exactly that far away. That one sentence is the entire object, and everything in this lesson is that sentence pushed through algebra. Parabolas already squared a distance condition once, against a point and a line; a circle keeps only the point and fixes the distance itself, which is the simplest version of that same move. Square the condition, complete the square, and read the geometry back out: every curve in this chapter turns on that method, and this lesson is where you see it in its plainest form.

Learning goals

  • Square the distance condition to get center-radius form
  • Complete the square to convert a circle's general form into center-radius form
  • Read the center and classify circle, point, or nothing from DDD, EEE, and FFF
  • Count line intersections with the discriminant
  • Classify two circles by the distance between centers

The distance condition

Fix a point C(h,k)C(h, k)C(h,k) and a positive length rrr. The circle with center CCC and radius rrr is the set of all points P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y) whose distance from CCC equals rrr. Written as a sentence about distance, that is the whole definition:

PC=r.PC = r.PC=r.

Here is one such circle: center C(1,2)C(1, 2)C(1,2), radius 555, with a point P(4,6)P(4, 6)P(4,6) on it.

To turn “exactly rrr away” into algebra, use the right triangle the grid hands you for free. Travel from C(h,k)C(h, k)C(h,k) straight across to the corner (x,k)(x, k)(x,k), then straight up to P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y), the way the diagram above does it for C(1,2)C(1, 2)C(1,2) and P(4,6)P(4, 6)P(4,6): across 333, up 444, and 32+42=523^2 + 4^2 = 5^232+42=52. The across step has length ∣x−h∣|x - h|∣x−h∣, the up step has length ∣y−k∣|y - k|∣y−k∣, and they meet at a right angle, so the Pythagorean theorem gives the square of the direct path for any center and point, not just this one:

PC2=(x−h)2+(y−k)2.PC^2 = (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 .PC2=(x−h)2+(y−k)2.

The absolute-value bars vanish under the squares, because squaring destroys a sign. That single line is the distance formula, and it is all the geometry this lesson needs.

A point lies on the circle exactly when (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2#

Let the circle have center (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) and radius r>0r > 0r>0, and let P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y) be any point of the plane. By the definition, PPP lies on the circle exactly when PC=rPC = rPC=r.

PCPCPC and rrr are both nonnegative, and squaring a nonnegative number is reversible: two nonnegative quantities are equal exactly when their squares are equal, since each number is the positive square root of its own square. So PC=rPC = rPC=r says exactly the same thing as PC2=r2PC^2 = r^2PC2=r2.

Replace PC2PC^2PC2 with the Pythagorean expression from above:

(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 .(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.

Because every step was reversible, this equation holds for a point exactly when that point sits rrr away from (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), that is, exactly when the point lies on the circle.

∎

This is the center-radius form (also called standard form) of a circle:

(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 .(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.

Read it carefully in both directions, because the form hides two traps. The form subtracts the center coordinates, so (x+3)(x + 3)(x+3) means h=−3h = -3h=−3, not +3+3+3; rewrite x+3x + 3x+3 as x−(−3)x - (-3)x−(−3) whenever the sign is in doubt. And the number on the right is r2r^2r2, not rrr, so the radius of (x−1)2+(y−2)2=20(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 20(x−1)2+(y−2)2=20 is 20=25\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}20​=25​, not 202020. Centered at the origin the form collapses to the tidy x2+y2=r2x^2 + y^2 = r^2x2+y2=r2.

Worked example 1 A circle from the endpoints of a diameter

The points (−2,3)(-2, 3)(−2,3) and (4,11)(4, 11)(4,11) are the ends of a diameter. Find the circle.

The center is the midpoint of the diameter. Halfway along a segment you have covered half of its horizontal run and half of its vertical rise, so each midpoint coordinate is the average of the two endpoint coordinates:

(h,k)=(−2+42,  3+112)=(1,7).(h, k) = \left( \frac{-2 + 4}{2}, \; \frac{3 + 11}{2} \right) = (1, 7).(h,k)=(2−2+4​,23+11​)=(1,7).

Completing the square once, with letters

Multiply out the center-radius form and the circle goes into hiding. Expanding (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2 gives x2−2hx+h2+y2−2ky+k2=r2x^2 - 2hx + h^2 + y^2 - 2ky + k^2 = r^2x2−2hx+h2+y2−2ky+k2=r2, and moving everything to one side collects into

x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0,D=−2h,E=−2k,F=h2+k2−r2.x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0, \qquad D = -2h, \quad E = -2k, \quad F = h^2 + k^2 - r^2 .x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0,D=−2h,E=−2k,F=h2+k2−r2.

This is the general form. The center and the radius are still in there, but they are scrambled across three coefficients. Recovering them means completing the square on the xxx-terms and on the yyy-terms separately, and that is a move you already own from the quadratics chapter. Try it once on a specific equation, then once more with letters, so the letter version hands you a ready-made shortcut for every equation after this one.

The steps above work on any equation of that shape. Repeat them with letters instead of numbers, and the pattern turns into a shortcut you can read off without redoing the arithmetic by hand.

Completing the square converts x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0 into center-radius form#

Group the xxx-terms and the yyy-terms, and move the constant to the right:

(x2+Dx)+(y2+Ey)=−F.(x^2 + Dx) + (y^2 + Ey) = -F .(x2+Dx)+(y2+Ey)=−F.

Complete each square. Half of the xxx-coefficient is D2\tfrac{D}{2}2D​, and (x+D2)2=x2+Dx+D24\left(x + \tfrac{D}{2}\right)^2 = x^2 + Dx + \tfrac{D^2}{4}(x+2D​)2=x2+Dx+4D2​, so the group x2+Dxx^2 + Dxx2+Dx is short of a perfect square by exactly D24\tfrac{D^2}{4}4D2​. (x2+Dx+D24)+(y2+Ey+E24)=−F+D24+E24.\left(x^2 + Dx + \frac{D^2}{4}\right) + \left(y^2 + Ey + \frac{E^2}{4}\right) = -F + \frac{D^2}{4} + \frac{E^2}{4}.(x2+Dx+4D2​)+(y2+Ey+4E2​)=−F+4D2​+4E2​.

Circle, single point, or nothing at all

Completing the square always lands you at (x−h)2+(y−k)2=C(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = C(x−h)2+(y−k)2=C for some number CCC on the right, and nothing forces CCC to be positive. Squares of real numbers are never negative, so a sum of two of them behaves in three sharply different ways.

Watch the three cases appear by turning one dial. Take x2+y2−4x+6y+F=0x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 6y + F = 0x2+y2−4x+6y+F=0 and vary only FFF. The test value is 16+36−4F=52−4F16 + 36 - 4F = 52 - 4F16+36−4F=52−4F, so completing the square gives (x−2)2+(y+3)2=13−F(x - 2)^2 + (y + 3)^2 = 13 - F(x−2)2+(y+3)2=13−F every time, and the center stays at (2,−3)(2, -3)(2,−3) no matter what FFF is. Raise FFF and the circle shrinks: at F=9F = 9F=9 the radius is 222, and at F=12F = 12F=12 it is 111. At F=13F = 13F=13 the circle has closed down onto its own center, and past that it is gone.

That progression always runs the same way, whatever the equation. If C>0C > 0C>0, the equation is an honest circle of radius C\sqrt{C}C​. If C=0C = 0C=0, then a sum of two squares is zero, which happens only when each square is zero separately. That forces x=hx = hx=h and y=ky = ky=k, so the graph has collapsed to the single point (h,k)(h, k)(h,k). If C<0C < 0C<0, the equation asks a sum of two squares to be negative, which no real point can do. So when C<0C < 0C<0 the graph is empty: there is no curve, no point, nothing to draw.

So the claim “every equation of the form x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0 is a circle” is simply false. If completing the square reaches (x−2)2+(y+3)2=−7(x - 2)^2 + (y + 3)^2 = -7(x−2)2+(y+3)2=−7, the right side is negative: −7\sqrt{-7}−7​ names no real radius, and the equation has no real graph. Check the sign of the right side before you claim a radius.

Where a line meets a circle

A line and a circle can miss each other, touch at one point, or cut through at two. Which of the three happens is not a matter of drawing carefully: it is decided by a discriminant you already know how to compute. See it happen on one circle first, with three parallel lines.

The pattern behind that picture is a single substitution, repeatable for any line and any circle. Substitute the line y=mx+qy = mx + qy=mx+q into the origin-centered circle x2+y2=r2x^2 + y^2 = r^2x2+y2=r2 and collect powers of xxx:

x2+(mx+q)2=r2  ⟹  (1+m2)x2+2mq x+(q2−r2)=0.x^2 + (mx + q)^2 = r^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; (1 + m^2)x^2 + 2mq\,x + (q^2 - r^2) = 0 .x2+(mx+q)2=r2⟹(1+m2)x2+2mqx+(q2−r2)=0.

This substitution always produces a genuine quadratic in xxx: its leading coefficient is 1+m21 + m^21+m2, which is at least 111 and never zero. A quadratic has at most two real roots, and the line assigns exactly one yyy to each xxx, so distinct roots give distinct points. That is why a line can cross a circle at most twice, no matter where the circle is centered. A vertical line x=ax = ax=a has no slope to substitute; put x=ax = ax=a into the circle instead, which again leaves at most two real solutions for yyy.

That reasoning holds for a circle centered anywhere, not just at the origin, and it covers the vertical-line case in full. Read the complete argument in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full argument

Which of the three cases you get is settled by the discriminant of that quadratic, which for this origin-centered circle collapses to something tidy:

Δ=(2mq)2−4(1+m2)(q2−r2)=4[ r2(1+m2)−q2 ].\Delta = (2mq)^2 - 4(1 + m^2)(q^2 - r^2) = 4\left[\, r^2(1 + m^2) - q^2 \,\right].Δ=(2mq)2−4(1+m2)(q2−r2)=4[r2(1+m2)−q2].

If Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0 there are two roots and the line is a secant, cutting the circle twice. If Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 there is a repeated root and the line is a tangent, touching at exactly one point: tangency is nothing more exotic than a double root. If Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 there are no real roots, and the line misses the circle entirely.

What generalizes is the method, not that closed form. The tidy expression above was computed from x2+y2=r2x^2 + y^2 = r^2x2+y2=r2 and holds only there. For a circle centered anywhere else, do not reach for it: substitute the line, collect the quadratic, and take the discriminant of the quadratic you actually have.

Where two circles meet

Two circles are settled the same way, by a single number: the distance ddd between their centers, weighed against the radii r1r_1r1​ and r2r_2r2​. Picture sliding one ring toward the other and watching the contact change.

That progression is controlled by a single number: compare the distance ddd between centers with the sum r1+r2r_1 + r_2r1​+r2​ and the difference ∣r1−r2∣|r_1 - r_2|∣r1​−r2​∣. While d>r1+r2d > r_1 + r_2d>r1​+r2​ the rings cannot reach each other, so they share no points. At d=r1+r2d = r_1 + r_2d=r1​+r2​ they reach exactly and touch at one point, tangent from the outside. Closer still, whenever ∣r1−r2∣<d<r1+r2|r_1 - r_2| < d < r_1 + r_2∣r1​−r2​∣<d<r1​+r2​, they overlap and cross at two points. At d=∣r1−r2∣d = |r_1 - r_2|d=∣r1​−r2​∣, with radii that differ, the smaller ring rests inside the larger and touches it once from within. And once d<∣r1−r2∣d < |r_1 - r_2|d<∣r1​−r2​∣ the smaller circle is swallowed whole, floating strictly inside the larger with no contact at all.

One case sits outside that pattern. If the two circles share the same center and the same radius, so d=0d = 0d=0 and r1=r2r_1 = r_2r1​=r2​, they are not two circles but one, and every point on it is shared.

So two circles can fail to meet for two opposite reasons: they can be too far apart, or one can be buried inside the other. Closer does not always mean more contact, which is the detail most people miss.

r1+r2=9,∣r1−r2∣=1.r_1 + r_2 = 9, \qquad |r_1 - r_2| = 1 .r1​+r2​=9,∣r1​−r2​∣=1.

Closer centers do not guarantee more contact.

When two distinct circles cross, their intersection points are cheap to find, because both equations carry the same x2+y2x^2 + y^2x2+y2. Subtract one general form from the other and those squared terms cancel. If the centers differ, the remaining xxx- and yyy-terms do not both cancel too, so what is left is a line: any point on both circles satisfies both equations, so it satisfies their difference, which means every shared point lies on that line. Solve the line against either circle and you have them. It also settles the count: a line meets a circle at most twice, so two distinct circles share at most two points.

The general form has one more use worth knowing about. Because DDD, EEE, and FFF each appear to the first power, an equation built from a known point (x0,y0)(x_0, y_0)(x0​,y0​) is linear in those three unknowns. Three points, so long as they do not all lie on one straight line, give three linear equations and pin down exactly one circle.

Two points leave a whole family of circles to choose from; three noncollinear points force exactly one. Work through the linear system, from setup to the final circle, in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Flipping the sign of the center. The form subtracts, so (x+5)2+(y−2)2=9(x + 5)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 9(x+5)2+(y−2)2=9 has center (−5,2)(-5, 2)(−5,2). Rewrite x+5x + 5x+5 as x−(−5)x - (-5)x−(−5) whenever you are unsure.
  • Reporting the wrong number as the radius. The right side of center-radius form is r2r^2r2, not rrr: a right side of 202020 means r=20=25r = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}r=20​=25​. And given the two endpoints of a diameter, the radius is half the distance between them, not the whole distance.
  • Forgetting to divide by the leading coefficient. In 3x2+3y2−12x+6y−9=03x^2 + 3y^2 - 12x + 6y - 9 = 03x2+3y2−12x+6y−9=0 you must divide the whole equation by 333 before completing the square. The center and radius formulas assume the squared terms have coefficient 111.
  • Announcing a radius without checking the sign. If completing the square leaves (x−2)2+(y+3)2=−7(x - 2)^2 + (y + 3)^2 = -7(x−2)2+(y+3)2=−7, there is no radius and no graph. A negative right side means the real plane holds no solutions at all, and a right side of 000 means a single point, not a circle.
  • Assuming every second-degree equation is a circle. A circle needs equal, nonzero coefficients on x2x^2x2 and y2y^2y2, and no xyxyxy term. Unequal coefficients describe some other curve, or a degenerate case with no points at all (as in x2+2y2=−1x^2 + 2y^2 = -1x2+2y2=−1), but never a circle.
  • Stopping at the xxx-values. When a line meets a circle, solving the quadratic gives xxx-values. Substitute each back into the line to produce an actual point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y).

Key takeaways

  • A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance rrr from a fixed center (h,k)(h, k)(h,k). Squaring that distance condition gives the center-radius form (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2, and the equation holds for a point if and only if that point is on the circle.
  • Expanding gives the general form x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0. Completing the square once, with letters, converts it back to (x+D2)2+(y+E2)2=D2+E2−4F4\left(x + \tfrac{D}{2}\right)^2 + \left(y + \tfrac{E}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{D^2 + E^2 - 4F}{4}(x+2D​)2+(y+2E​)2=4D2+E2−4F​: the center is always (−D2,−E2)\left(-\tfrac{D}{2}, -\tfrac{E}{2}\right)(−2D​,−2E​), and the sign of D2+E2−4FD^2 + E^2 - 4FD2+E2−4F decides everything, positive for a circle of radius 12D2+E2−4F\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{D^2 + E^2 - 4F}21​D2+E2−4F​, zero for the single point (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), negative for no real points at all.
  • A circle’s equation has equal, nonzero coefficients on x2x^2x2 and y2y^2y2 and no xyxyxy term. If that shared coefficient is not 111, divide the equation by it first.
  • Substituting a line into a circle always yields a genuine quadratic, so a line meets a circle in at most two points. The discriminant counts them: positive gives a secant, zero gives a tangent (a double root), and negative gives no intersection.
  • Two circles are counted by the distance ddd between their centers. Distinct circles share no points when d>r1+r2d > r_1 + r_2d>r1​+r2​ or d<∣r1−r2∣d < |r_1 - r_2|d<∣r1​−r2​∣ (one inside the other), exactly one point when ddd equals either bound, and two points strictly between them. Equal centers and equal radii describe the same circle, with every point shared.

Why a line meets a circle at most twice, from any center

A line meets a circle in at most two points#

Substituting y=mx+qy = mx + qy=mx+q into x2+y2=r2x^2 + y^2 = r^2x2+y2=r2 collects into (1+m2)x2+2mq x+(q2−r2)=0(1 + m^2)x^2 + 2mq\,x + (q^2 - r^2) = 0(1+m2)x2+2mqx+(q2−r2)=0. Its leading coefficient is 1+m21 + m^21+m2, and since m2≥0m^2 \geq 0m2≥0 for every real slope, 1+m2≥11 + m^2 \geq 11+m2≥1, which is never zero. So the substitution always produces a genuine quadratic in xxx, never a linear equation in disguise, and a quadratic has at most two real roots.

Each root is one intersection point and no more, because the line assigns exactly one yyy to each xxx through y=mx+qy = mx + qy=mx+q. Distinct roots therefore give distinct points, and there can be at most two of them.

Nothing in that argument used the center, so the same reasoning covers a circle sitting anywhere. Substituting the same line into (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2 and collecting powers of xxx gives

(1+m2)x2+[ 2m(q−k)−2h ]x+[ h2+(q−k)2−r2 ]=0,(1 + m^2)x^2 + \left[\, 2m(q - k) - 2h \,\right] x + \left[\, h^2 + (q - k)^2 - r^2 \,\right] = 0,(1+m2)x2+[2m(q−k)−2h]x+[h2+(q−k)2−r2]=0,

whose leading coefficient is again 1+m21 + m^21+m2, never zero. A vertical line x=ax = ax=a has no slope to substitute, but it behaves the same way. Substituting x=ax = ax=a turns the circle into (y−k)2=r2−(a−h)2(y - k)^2 = r^2 - (a - h)^2(y−k)2=r2−(a−h)2, which has two, one, or no real solutions for yyy. Either way, no line can cross any circle three times.

Which lines are tangent to a circle of a given slope

Worked example Which lines y=x+qy = x + qy=x+q are tangent to x2+y2=25x^2 + y^2 = 25x2+y2=25?

Tangency means the substituted quadratic has a repeated root, so build the quadratic and set its discriminant to zero. With slope m=1m = 1m=1:

x2+(x+q)2=25  ⟹  2x2+2qx+(q2−25)=0.x^2 + (x + q)^2 = 25 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x^2 + 2qx + (q^2 - 25) = 0 .x2+(x+q)2=25⟹2x2+2qx+(q2−25)=0.

Finding the circle through three given points

The general form looks uglier than the center-radius form, but it earns its keep in one situation the tidy form cannot handle. Treat DDD, EEE, and FFF in x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0 as the unknowns and a known point (x0,y0)(x_0, y_0)(x0​,y0​) as the data: this equation is linear in those unknowns, because DDD, EEE, and FFF each appear to the first power, multiplied by constants. Each point the circle must pass through gives one linear equation, so three points give three linear equations in three unknowns, a system you already know how to solve.

Worked example The circle through (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1), (2,4)(2, 4)(2,4), and (5,3)(5, 3)(5,3)

Substitute each point into x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0 and simplify, keeping DDD, EEE, and FFF as the unknowns:

(1,1):D+E+F=−2,(2,4):2D+4E+F=−20,(5,3):5D+3E+F=−34.\begin{aligned} (1, 1) &: \quad D + E + F = -2, \\ (2, 4) &: \quad 2D + 4E + F = -20, \\ (5, 3) &: \quad 5D + 3E + F = -34 . \end{aligned}(1,1)(2,4)(5,3)​:D+E+F=−2,:2D+4E+F=−20,:5D+3E+F=−34.​

Eliminate FFF by subtracting the first equation from the other two, which leaves a 2×22 \times 22×2 system:

D+3E=−18,4D+2E=−32.D + 3E = -18, \qquad 4D + 2E = -32 .D+3E=−18,4D+2E=−32.

The second simplifies to 2D+E=−162D + E = -162D+E=−16. x2+y2−6x−4y+8=0.x^2 + y^2 - 6x - 4y + 8 = 0 .x2+y2−6x−4y+8=0.

Complete the square to see it: (x−3)2+(y−2)2=−8+9+4=5(x - 3)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = -8 + 9 + 4 = 5(x−3)2+(y−2)2=−8+9+4=5, a circle with center (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) and radius 5\sqrt{5}5​. Verify with the data you were given. The squared distance from (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) to (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) is 4+1=54 + 1 = 54+1=5, to (2,4)(2, 4)(2,4) is 1+4=51 + 4 = 51+4=5, and to (5,3)(5, 3)(5,3) is 4+1=54 + 1 = 54+1=5. All three points sit exactly 5\sqrt{5}5​ from the center, so all three are on the circle.

Three points determine the circle, provided they do not all lie on one line: if they do, the elimination collapses and no circle exists. Two points leave a whole family of circles to choose from, and four points are usually one demand too many for any single circle to satisfy.

A bit of history (optional)

Fix one point, fix one distance, and collect everything that far away. This lesson turned that rule into an equation. But is it the only rule that draws a circle?

Apollonius of Perga, a Greek geometer working around 200 BCE, found a second one, and it looks nothing like the first. He is the man who gave the ellipse, the parabola and the hyperbola the names they still carry. So he had reason to hunt for odd ways of describing a familiar curve. His rule uses two fixed points rather than one. Collect every point whose distance to the first is exactly twice its distance to the second.

Nothing in that description sounds round. There is no center named anywhere in it, and no radius either. The condition is a ratio rather than a length, and the two fixed points are not even treated alike. Yet the points that satisfy it form a perfect circle, and neither of the two fixed points sits at its center. Any positive ratio behaves the same way, with one exception. At a ratio of exactly 111 the two distances are equal, and the set straightens out into a line: the perpendicular bisector you would draw between the points.

You can settle all of this yourself, with nothing beyond this lesson. Write both distances with the distance formula, square both sides, and gather the terms. The x2x^2x2 and the y2y^2y2 arrive with equal coefficients and no xyxyxy term between them, which is exactly the fingerprint you learned to read.

Ellipses Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

Push two pins into a board, and pin the two ends of a piece of string to them so that the string hangs slack. Then pull the string taut with the tip of a pencil. Now drag the pencil all the way around, keeping the string tight. The curve you get is an ellipse. Nothing about the pencil changes as it travels: the string never stretches. So the two pieces running from the pencil to the pins always add up to the same length, which is the length of the whole string. That one sentence, “the two distances add up to a constant,” is the whole definition. Everything else in this lesson, including the equation x2a2+y2b2=1\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1a2x2​+b2y2​=1, is what that sentence looks like after you write it in coordinates and clean it up.

Learning goals

  • Define an ellipse by a constant sum of focal distances
  • Square twice to reach x2a2+y2b2=1\tfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1a2x2​+b2y2​=1
  • Read b2=a2−c2b^2 = a^2 - c^2b2=a2−c2 as a right triangle
  • Find the major axis under the larger denominator
  • Shift the center by replacing xxx with x−hx - hx−h
  • Measure eccentricity e=cae = \tfrac{c}{a}e=ac​, zero for a circle

Two pins and a string

The circle in the previous lesson was built from one fixed point: every point of the circle is the same distance rrr from the center. An ellipse is built from two fixed points, called the foci (singular: focus). A point is on the ellipse when its distances to the two foci add to a constant.

The distance condition

Try it with actual numbers first, so the letters that follow have something to point at. Put the pins at (−6,0)(-6, 0)(−6,0) and (6,0)(6, 0)(6,0), six units either side of the center, and use a string 202020 units long. Where does the curve cross the yyy-axis? By symmetry, a point directly above or below the center is the same distance from each pin, so each distance is half the string, 101010. That distance is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose horizontal leg is the 666-unit gap to a focus, so the vertical leg yyy satisfies 62+y2=1026^2 + y^2 = 10^262+y2=102, giving y=±8y = \pm 8y=±8. The curve crosses the yyy-axis at both (0,8)(0, 8)(0,8) and (0,−8)(0, -8)(0,−8), and the three numbers 101010, 666, and 888 are exactly what the letters aaa, ccc, and bbb turn out to mean once the same reasoning runs in general.

Now set up coordinates so the two foci sit symmetrically on the xxx-axis, at F1(−c,0)F_1(-c, 0)F1​(−c,0) and F2(c,0)F_2(c, 0)F2​(c,0) with c≥0c \ge 0c≥0, and call the constant total length of the string 2a2a2a. Writing the constant as 2a2a2a rather than as a single letter costs nothing now and saves a division by two later.

You need one tool to turn distances into algebra, and it is just the Pythagorean theorem. The segment from (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) to (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​) is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the horizontal gap ∣x2−x1∣|x_2 - x_1|∣x2​−x1​∣ and the vertical gap ∣y2−y1∣|y_2 - y_1|∣y2​−y1​∣, so its length is (x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​. With that, the point P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y) lies on the ellipse exactly when

(x+c)2+y2⏟r1  =  PF1  +  (x−c)2+y2⏟r2  =  PF2  =  2a.\underbrace{\sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2}}_{r_1 \;=\; PF_1} \;+\; \underbrace{\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2}}_{r_2 \;=\; PF_2} \;=\; 2a.r1​=PF1​(x+c)2+y2​​​+r2​=PF2​(x−c)2+y2​​​=2a.

Before doing any algebra, notice that aaa and ccc cannot be just anything. Any trip from F1F_1F1​ out to PPP and back to F2F_2F2​ is at least as long as the direct trip from F1F_1F1​ to F2F_2F2​, which has length 2c2c2c. That is the triangle inequality, and it says r1+r2≥2cr_1 + r_2 \ge 2cr1​+r2​≥2c, with equality only when PPP sits on the segment between the foci. A string exactly 2c2c2c long only traces that segment, and a string shorter than 2c2c2c cannot reach both pins at once, so no point satisfies the condition. Only a string longer than the gap between the pins leaves slack to trace a genuine closed curve.

So a real ellipse forces a>c≥0a > c \ge 0a>c≥0. Keep that inequality in view: it is what makes the next step legal.

From the string to the equation

The distance condition is exactly x2a2+y2b2=1\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1a2x2​+b2y2​=1#

(x+c)2+y2  =  2a−(x−c)2+y2.\sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} \;=\; 2a - \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2}.(x+c)2+y2​=2a−(x−c)2+y2​.

What aaa, bbb, and ccc mean

Read the geometry straight off x2a2+y2b2=1\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1a2x2​+b2y2​=1.

Set y=0y = 0y=0: then x2=a2x^2 = a^2x2=a2, so x=±ax = \pm ax=±a. The points (±a,0)(\pm a, 0)(±a,0) are the vertices, and the segment joining them is the major axis, of length 2a2a2a. So the length of the string equals the length of the major axis, which is a fact worth remembering.

Set x=0x = 0x=0: then y2=b2y^2 = b^2y2=b2, so y=±by = \pm by=±b. The points (0,±b)(0, \pm b)(0,±b) are the co-vertices, and the segment joining them is the minor axis, of length 2b2b2b.

Since b2=a2−c2b^2 = a^2 - c^2b2=a2−c2 and c≥0c \ge 0c≥0, we get b≤ab \le ab≤a: the ellipse really is at least as wide as it is tall when the foci are on the xxx-axis. And a2=b2+c2a^2 = b^2 + c^2a2=b2+c2 is not a formula to memorize, it is a right triangle you can point at. Draw its two legs from the center: straight up to a co-vertex (0,b)(0, b)(0,b), and straight across to a focus (c,0)(c, 0)(c,0). Those legs are bbb and ccc. The third side joins the co-vertex directly to the focus, and that is the hypotenuse, with length b2+c2=a2=a\sqrt{b^2 + c^2} = \sqrt{a^2} = ab2+c2​=a2​=a. The distance from a co-vertex to each focus is therefore exactly aaa, and the two of them add to 2a2a2a, exactly as the string demands. The co-vertex is the one point where you can see the string length with no calculation at all.

Because aaa is the hypotenuse of that triangle, no side can beat it: a≥ba \ge ba≥b and a>ca > ca>c, with a=ba = ba=b precisely when c=0c = 0c=0, which is the circle. So outside that one case aaa is strictly the largest of the three, and that single observation kills most of the sign errors people make with ellipses.

Which variable gets a2a^2a2

Nothing forced the foci onto the xxx-axis. Put them at (0,−c)(0, -c)(0,−c) and (0,c)(0, c)(0,c) instead and run the same derivation with the roles of xxx and yyy interchanged. Every step is identical, so the result is identical with xxx and yyy swapped:

So there is one rule to internalize, and it is the source of more wrong answers in this topic than anything else:

a2a^2a2 is always the larger denominator, and the major axis runs along the axis of the variable that sits above it. Moving the center off the origin

An ellipse does not have to be centered at the origin. From Transformations of Graphs you already know the move: replacing xxx by x−hx - hx−h shifts the graph right by hhh, and replacing yyy by y−ky - ky−k shifts it up by kkk. Doing both to the standard form slides the whole ellipse so its center lands at (h,k)(h, k)(h,k):

(x−h)2a2+(y−k)2b2=1(major axis horizontal),\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1 \qquad\text{(major axis horizontal)},a2(x−h)2​+b2(y−k)2​=1(major axis horizontal), (x−h)2b2+(y−k)2a2=1(major axis vertical).\frac{(x - h)^2}{b^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{a^2} = 1 \qquad\text{(major axis vertical)}.b2(x−h)2​+a2(y−k)2​=1(major axis vertical).

Shifting cannot change any length, so aaa, bbb, and ccc mean exactly what they meant before. Everything is now measured from the center: the vertices are aaa units from (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) along the major axis. The co-vertices are bbb units from the center along the minor axis, and the foci are ccc units from the center along the major axis.

From a general equation to standard form

A general equation like 9x2+4y2+54x−8y+49=09x^2 + 4y^2 + 54x - 8y + 49 = 09x2+4y2+54x−8y+49=0 hides an ellipse. To expose it, complete the square in xxx and in yyy, exactly as in the Completing the Square lesson, and then divide so the right side becomes 111.

Worked example 2 Put 9x2+4y2+54x−8y+49=09x^2 + 4y^2 + 54x - 8y + 49 = 09x2+4y2+54x−8y+49=0 in standard form

Eccentricity, and the circle hiding inside every ellipse

Two ellipses can both have a=5a = 5a=5 and look nothing alike: one nearly round, one nearly flat. What distinguishes them is how far the foci sit from the center compared with the size of the curve. That ratio is the eccentricity:

e=ca.e = \frac{c}{a}.e=ac​.

Since 0≤c<a0 \le c < a0≤c<a, the eccentricity always satisfies 0≤e<10 \le e < 10≤e<1. It also controls the shape completely, because c=eac = eac=ea and therefore

b2=a2−c2=a2−e2a2=a2(1−e2),ba=1−e2.b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = a^2 - e^2a^2 = a^2\left(1 - e^2\right), \qquad \frac{b}{a} = \sqrt{1 - e^2}.b2=a2−c2=a2−e2a2=a2(1−e2),ab​=1−e2​.

At the flat end, pushing eee toward 111 pushes ccc toward aaa, which squeezes bbb toward 000 and flattens the ellipse against its major axis. At the round end something better happens. Take e=0e = 0e=0, which means c=0c = 0c=0. The two foci merge into a single point at the center, b2=a2−0=a2b^2 = a^2 - 0 = a^2b2=a2−0=a2 gives b=ab = ab=a, and the equation becomes

x2a2+y2a2=1⟺x2+y2=a2,\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^2 + y^2 = a^2,a2x2​+a2y2​=1⟺x2+y2=a2,

a circle of radius aaa. The definition degenerates just as gracefully: with F1=F2=OF_1 = F_2 = OF1​=F2​=O, the condition r1+r2=2ar_1 + r_2 = 2ar1​+r2​=2a becomes 2⋅PO=2a2 \cdot PO = 2a2⋅PO=2a, that is PO=aPO = aPO=a, which is precisely “every point is distance aaa from the center.” A circle is not a different kind of curve. It is the ellipse whose two foci have collided, and the circle lesson is the special case e=0e = 0e=0 of this one.

Eccentricity is how astronomers describe orbits. Earth’s orbit has e≈0.017e \approx 0.017e≈0.017, so close to a circle that a drawing of it at page size is indistinguishable from one. Halley’s comet has e≈0.97e \approx 0.97e≈0.97, a long thin cigar of an orbit. Both are ellipses with the sun at a focus.

Building an ellipse from its geometry

Most problems hand you geometric facts (foci, a vertex, a string length, an eccentricity) and ask for the equation. The recipe never changes: find the center, decide which axis is major, then get any two of aaa, bbb, ccc and use a2=b2+c2a^2 = b^2 + c^2a2=b2+c2 for the third.

Worked example 3 Foci (2,−1)(2, -1)(2,−1) and (2,7)(2, 7)(2,7), with focal distances summing to 101010

The center is halfway between the foci, so average the coordinates:

(2+22,  −1+72)=(2,3).\left(\frac{2 + 2}{2}, \; \frac{-1 + 7}{2}\right) = (2, 3).(22+2​,2−1+7​)=(2,3).

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Calling the first denominator a2a^2a2. In x29+y225=1\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 19x2​+25y2​=1 the value of a2a^2a2 is 252525, not 999. Always take a2a^2a2 to be the larger denominator, then let it tell you which axis is major. Getting this backwards makes every later answer wrong.
  • Adding when you should subtract. For an ellipse, c2=a2−b2c^2 = a^2 - b^2c2=a2−b2. Computing c2=a2+b2c^2 = a^2 + b^2c2=a2+b2 is the single most common slip here. Look at the right triangle: aaa is the hypotenuse, so no side beats it (a≥ba \ge ba≥b and a>ca > ca>c), and ccc is found by taking away.
  • Putting the foci on the minor axis. The foci always lie on the major axis, inside the curve, at distance c<ac < ac<a from the center. If your foci landed on the short axis, you swapped aaa and bbb.
  • Stopping before the right side is 111. After completing the square, 9(x+3)2+4(y−1)2=369(x + 3)^2 + 4(y - 1)^2 = 369(x+3)2+4(y−1)2=36 is not standard form. Divide by 363636 first; only then are the denominators a2a^2a2 and b2b^2b2.
  • Sign slips in the center. In (x+3)24+(y−1)29=1\frac{(x + 3)^2}{4} + \frac{(y - 1)^2}{9} = 14(x+3)2​+9(y−1)2​=1 the center is (−3,1)(-3, 1)(−3,1), because x+3x + 3x+3 means x−(−3)x - (-3)x−(−3). Read the center as the value that makes each squared term zero.
  • Confusing aaa with 2a2a2a. The string length, the sum of the focal distances, and the major axis are all 2a2a2a. The semi-major axis is aaa. Halve or double at the right moment.
  • Thinking a circle is something else. A circle is the ellipse with c=0c = 0c=0, a=ba = ba=b, and e=0e = 0e=0. It is not an exception to the definition, it sits inside the definition at that special value.

A bit of history (optional)

For two thousand years astronomers agreed on one thing. Whatever the heavens were doing, they were doing it with circles. A planet could ride a circle whose center rode a second circle, and a third could be stacked on if the fit went bad, but the parts were always circles. Mars was the planet that would not cooperate.

The measurements were the problem, because they were too good to explain away. Tycho Brahe, a Danish astronomer with the sharpest eyes of his century, had logged the position of Mars night after night for decades, all of it before the telescope. Johannes Kepler inherited those notebooks and spent years bending circles around them. His best attempt missed by eight minutes of arc, a quarter of the width of the full moon. Anyone else’s data could have absorbed an error that size. Brahe’s could not.

So Kepler threw the circles away. What fits, he announced in 1609, is an ellipse with the sun at one focus. He had named those two inner points himself, borrowing the Latin word for a hearth, because light or sound leaving one of them, once it bounces off the curve, gathers at the other. That is the fireplace, and it is why a whisper crosses a domed room.

Now read the orbit with this lesson’s number. Mars has an eccentricity of about 0.090.090.09, so the curve is very nearly round, and drawn on a page it would pass for a circle. What betrayed it was the sun, sitting visibly off center. Push that number all the way down to zero and the two foci collide into one, which is the circle the astronomers wanted. Zero is the one value Mars would not take.

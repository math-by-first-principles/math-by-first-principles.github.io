Rationalizing and Radical Conjugates

Intuition

You have multiplied by a conjugate before, and nobody told you that is what it was called. To divide by the complex number 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i you multiplied top and bottom by 3−2i3 - 2i3−2i, because that product is the real number 131313. To divide by 3+223 + 2\sqrt{2}3+22​ you multiply top and bottom by 3−223 - 2\sqrt{2}3−22​, because that product is the rational number 111. Those are not two tricks that happen to rhyme. They are one move: multiply by the partner that drags the product back into the plain number system you started in. The result is that dividing by an awkward object becomes dividing by an ordinary number. Learn the move that way and you can build the partner yourself when the familiar sign flip fails. And it does fail, the first time you meet a cube root.

Learning goals

  • Multiply by the radical conjugate to land back in the rationals
  • Compare the move with complex division by a conjugate
  • Show closure, since the result is again p+qdp + q\sqrt dp+qd​
  • Complete the power to clear a single nnnth root
  • Use a three-term multiplier for a cube-root binomial
  • Aim at the numerator when that is what blocks you

Why the conjugate makes the product real

Back in the complex numbers you met the conjugate z‾=a−bi\overline{z} = a - biz=a−bi of z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi, and the one identity that made it useful:

z z‾=(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2−(bi)2=a2+b2=∣z∣2.z\,\overline{z} = (a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2 .zz=(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2−(bi)2=a2+b2=∣z∣2.

The cross terms −abi-abi−abi and +abi+abi+abi cancelled, and the only surviving appearance of iii was through i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, which is real. So zz‾z\overline{z}zz is a real number, and since aaa and bbb are real, a2+b2a^2 + b^2a2+b2 is zero only when a=b=0a = b = 0a=b=0, that is, only when z=0z = 0z=0 itself.

That is exactly what let you divide. To evaluate 13+2i\frac{1}{3 + 2i}3+2i1​ you multiplied by 3−2i3−2i\frac{3-2i}{3-2i}3−2i3−2i​, which is 111, so the value did not change, and the denominator turned real:

13+2i=13+2i⋅3−2i3−2i=3−2i9+4=313−213 i.\frac{1}{3 + 2i} = \frac{1}{3+2i}\cdot\frac{3 - 2i}{3 - 2i} = \frac{3 - 2i}{9 + 4} = \frac{3}{13} - \frac{2}{13}\,i .3+2i1​=3+2i1​⋅3−2i3−2i​=9+43−2i​=133​−132​i.

Look at what that computation actually proved, because it is more than an answer. It proved that the reciprocal of a complex number is again a complex number in standard form a+bia + bia+bi. The conjugate is what carried out the proof: it converted a division by an awkward object into a division by an ordinary real number, 131313.

The same move over the rational numbers

Now fix a positive integer ddd that is not a perfect square, so that d\sqrt{d}d​ is irrational, and look at the numbers you can build from it:

α=a+bd,a,b rational.\alpha = a + b\sqrt{d}, \qquad a, b \text{ rational.}α=a+bd​,a,b rational.

You have met this number already. In Complex and Irrational Roots you gave the surd partner of α\alphaα its own symbol,

α∗=a−bd,\alpha^{*} = a - b\sqrt{d},α∗=a−bd​,

precisely because the bar was taken by complex conjugation. There the swap d↦−d\sqrt{d} \mapsto -\sqrt{d}d​↦−d​ paired up the roots of a rational polynomial. Here it is going to clear a denominator. Same map, new job, and this lesson calls it the radical conjugate.

One practical note before the proof, because the sign can trip you. The swap flips the surd term and leaves the rational term alone, so applied to 3−1\sqrt{3} - 13​−1, which is really −1+3-1 + \sqrt{3}−1+3​, it returns α∗=−1−3\alpha^{*} = -1 - \sqrt{3}α∗=−1−3​. In practice nobody multiplies by that. They flip the sign between the two terms and multiply by 3+1\sqrt{3} + 13​+1, which is exactly −α∗-\alpha^{*}−α∗. Both choices rationalize, and both give the same final answer, because the extra factor of −1-1−1 shows up in the numerator and the denominator at once and cancels. The second choice simply spares you a negative denominator to tidy afterwards, so it is the one used throughout this lesson whenever a denominator is written surd-first.

α α∗\alpha\,\alpha^{*}αα∗ is rational#

Let aaa and bbb be rational and let ddd be a positive integer that is not a perfect square, so d\sqrt{d}d​ is irrational. Multiply α=a+bd\alpha = a + b\sqrt{d}α=a+bd​ by α∗=a−bd\alpha^{*} = a - b\sqrt{d}α∗=a−bd​ and watch the same cancellation as in the complex case:

α α∗=(a+bd)(a−bd)=a2−abd+abd−b2(d)2=a2−b2d.\alpha\,\alpha^{*} = (a + b\sqrt{d})(a - b\sqrt{d}) = a^2 - ab\sqrt{d} + ab\sqrt{d} - b^2(\sqrt{d})^2 = a^2 - b^2 d .αα∗=(a+bd​)(a−bd​)=a2−abd​+abd​−b2(d​)2=a2−b2d.

The two cross terms are equal and opposite, so they cancel, and d\sqrt{d}d​ survives only through (d)2=d(\sqrt{d})^2 = d(d​)2=d. Both a2a^2a2 and b2db^2 db2d are rational, so α α∗=a2−b2d\alpha\,\alpha^{*} = a^2 - b^2 dαα∗=a2−b2d is rational.

∎

That product is zero only when aaa and bbb are both zero, which is exactly when α\alphaα itself is zero. (If bbb were not zero, a2−b2d=0a^2 - b^2 d = 0a2−b2d=0 would force d\sqrt{d}d​ to equal a ratio of rational numbers, and d\sqrt{d}d​ is irrational.) So every nonzero α\alphaα leaves a nonzero rational number sitting at α α∗\alpha\,\alpha^{*}αα∗, and that is the number you are about to divide by.

The parenthetical above compresses a short proof by contradiction. Read the full version in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Read the full nonzero argument

Both facts are load-bearing. Rationality is what clears the radical; being nonzero for nonzero α\alphaα is what guarantees you are not dividing by zero while you do it. Put them together and the reciprocal falls out:

1a+bd=1a+bd⋅a−bda−bd=a−bda2−b2d=aa2−b2d⏟rational  +  (−ba2−b2d)⏟rationald.\frac{1}{a + b\sqrt{d}} = \frac{1}{a + b\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{a - b\sqrt{d}}{a - b\sqrt{d}} = \frac{a - b\sqrt{d}}{a^2 - b^2 d} = \underbrace{\frac{a}{a^2 - b^2 d}}_{\text{rational}} \;+\; \underbrace{\left(\frac{-b}{a^2 - b^2 d}\right)}_{\text{rational}}\sqrt{d} .a+bd​1​=a+bd​1​⋅a−bd​a−bd​​=a2−b2da−bd​​=rationala2−b2da​​​+rational(a2−b2d−b​)​​d​.

Read the right-hand side slowly, because it says far more than “the radical is gone.” It says the reciprocal of a number of the form a+bda + b\sqrt{d}a+bd​ is another number of exactly that form. The family is closed under division. Rationalizing a denominator is not tidying up after yourself; it is the proof of that closure, and the proof is constructive: it hands you the answer.

The same cancellation handles a denominator built from two square roots, like 5−2\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{2}5​−2​, which is not of the shape a+bda + b\sqrt{d}a+bd​ with aaa rational. Multiply it by 5+2\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{2}5​+2​ and the cross terms cancel exactly as before, leaving (5)2−(2)2=5−2=3(\sqrt{5})^2 - (\sqrt{2})^2 = 5 - 2 = 3(5​)2−(2​)2=5−2=3. In general, for positive rationals m≠nm \ne nm=n, the pair m±n\sqrt{m} \pm \sqrt{n}m​±n​ multiplies to m−nm - nm−n, which is rational and nonzero. (The condition m≠nm \ne nm=n matters, because at m=nm = nm=n this particular pairing collapses: the m−n\sqrt{m} - \sqrt{n}m​−n​ partner of 2+2\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2}2​+2​ is 2−2=0\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{2} = 02​−2​=0, and multiplying by that form of “1” is really multiplying by 00\frac{0}{0}00​. Nothing is wrong with the number 222\sqrt{2}22​ itself; it is a single radical, so you clear it the single-radical way, by multiplying by 2\sqrt{2}2​.)

Worked example 1 The same computation twice, in two number systems

Rationalize 13+2i\frac{1}{3 + 2i}3+2i1​ and 13+22\frac{1}{3 + 2\sqrt{2}}3+22​1​, side by side, and notice that only the squared term changes.

The second answer is worth a sanity check, since it looks too clean: 3+22≈5.82843 + 2\sqrt{2} \approx 5.82843+22​≈5.8284 and 3−22≈0.17163 - 2\sqrt{2} \approx 0.17163−22​≈0.1716, and indeed 5.8284×0.1716≈15.8284 \times 0.1716 \approx 15.8284×0.1716≈1. Check your understanding

Both reciprocals above came out again in the same shape you started with: a+bia+bia+bi, or p+qdp+q\sqrt dp+qd​. Why does clearing a conjugate denominator always land you back in that shape?

Answer choices

The conjugate trick works anywhere a new object satisfies a relation in which it is squared: i2=−1i^2=-1i2=−1, (2)2=2(\sqrt2)^2=2(2​)2=2. Squaring cannot tell a sign apart from its opposite, so flipping that sign cancels every cross term. That is not just clearing the denominator; it is closure, the proof that dividing by a+bia+bia+bi or a+bda+b\sqrt da+bd​ always lands back on another number of exactly that same form.

One principle, three jobs

Set the two computations next to each other and the shared skeleton is impossible to miss.

SettingAwkward divisorMultiply byProductWhich lands in
complex numbersa+bia + bia+bia−bia - bia−bia2+b2a^2 + b^2a2+b2the real numbers
surdsa+bda + b\sqrt{d}a+bd​a−bda - b\sqrt{d}a−bd​a2−b2da^2 - b^2 da2−b2dthe rational numbers

Why does a mere sign flip do so much work in both rows? Because in both rows the new object satisfies a relation in which it appears squared: i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 and (d)2=d(\sqrt{d})^2 = d(d​)2=d. Squaring is blind to sign, so flipping the sign of the new object leaves that defining relation untouched. In the product, every term carrying an odd power of the new object shows up twice with opposite signs and cancels. In the same product, every term carrying an even power was already back in the base system. Nothing irrational, and nothing imaginary, can survive.

There is a third job you have already seen this same sign flip do. When you studied the roots of polynomials, you proved that a real polynomial carries its nonreal roots in conjugate pairs. You also proved that a rational polynomial carries its surd roots in pairs the same way: if a+bda + b\sqrt{d}a+bd​ is a root, so is a−bda - b\sqrt{d}a−bd​. The engine of that proof was that conjugation slides through sums and products, so applying it to P(α)=0P(\alpha) = 0P(α)=0 term by term leaves the coefficients alone and turns α\alphaα into α∗\alpha^{*}α∗. That is the same map as the one you are using here, doing a different job. Pairing roots and clearing denominators look like unrelated chores; they are one symmetry, seen from two sides.

Where the sign flip stops working

A student who memorised “flip the sign in the middle” is about to be stranded. A student who learned “find the multiplier that completes a power and lands the product back in the rational numbers” is not. Here is the cliff.

A single cube root. Take 123\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}32​1​. There is no sign to flip, because there is only one term. And look again at what you actually did for 12\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}2​1​: multiplying 2\sqrt{2}2​ by 2\sqrt{2}2​ works because it completes the square, not because it flips anything. So complete the cube instead. Rational exponents make the bookkeeping obvious:

23⋅43=21/3⋅22/3=21=2⟹123=432.\sqrt[3]{2}\cdot\sqrt[3]{4} = 2^{1/3}\cdot 2^{2/3} = 2^{1} = 2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}} = \frac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{2} .32​⋅34​=21/3⋅22/3=21=2⟹32​1​=234​​.

The general rule follows from the same exponent arithmetic. For a>0a > 0a>0 and a whole number n≥2n \geq 2n≥2, a1/n⋅a(n−1)/n=a1=aa^{1/n}\cdot a^{(n-1)/n} = a^{1} = aa1/n⋅a(n−1)/n=a1=a, so

1an=a n−1na.\frac{1}{\sqrt[n]{a}} = \frac{\sqrt[n]{a^{\,n-1}}}{a} .na​1​=anan−1​​.

You need whatever power is missing to fill the root up to a whole one, which is the single idea behind every “multiply by 2\sqrt{2}2​” you have ever done.

A cube-root binomial. Now take 11+23\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt[3]{2}}1+32​1​ and try the memorised sign flip:

(1+23)(1−23)=1−(23)2=1−43.(1 + \sqrt[3]{2})(1 - \sqrt[3]{2}) = 1 - (\sqrt[3]{2})^2 = 1 - \sqrt[3]{4} .(1+32​)(1−32​)=1−(32​)2=1−34​.

Still irrational. You have not removed a radical; you have swapped one for another. The reason is worth stating exactly, because it tells you what to do instead. A square root has only one irrational power below the point where it turns rational: d\sqrt{d}d​ is irrational and (d)2=d(\sqrt{d})^2 = d(d​)2=d is rational. So a square root leaves exactly one bad term to kill, and one sign flip kills it. A cube root has two: both 23\sqrt[3]{2}32​ and (23)2=43(\sqrt[3]{2})^2 = \sqrt[3]{4}(32​)2=34​ are irrational, and only (23)3=2(\sqrt[3]{2})^3 = 2(32​)3=2 is rational. A single sign flip cannot cancel two independent bad terms. You need a multiplier with three terms, engineered so that everything except the cubes cancels.

That multiplier is the one hiding inside the sum-of-cubes identity. Expand it and watch the middle collapse:

(x+y)(x2−xy+y2)=x3−x2y+xy2+x2y−xy2+y3=x3+y3.(x + y)(x^2 - xy + y^2) = x^3 - x^2y + xy^2 + x^2y - xy^2 + y^3 = x^3 + y^3 .(x+y)(x2−xy+y2)=x3−x2y+xy2+x2y−xy2+y3=x3+y3.

Every intermediate term appears twice with opposite signs. Only the cubes survive, and cubes are exactly what turn a cube root rational. Put x=1x = 1x=1 and y=23y = \sqrt[3]{2}y=32​:

(1+23)(1−23+43)=13+(23)3=1+2=3,(1 + \sqrt[3]{2})\left(1 - \sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt[3]{4}\right) = 1^3 + \left(\sqrt[3]{2}\right)^3 = 1 + 2 = 3 ,(1+32​)(1−32​+34​)=13+(32​)3=1+2=3, 11+23=1−23+433.\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt[3]{2}} = \frac{1 - \sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt[3]{4}}{3} .1+32​1​=31−32​+34​​.

The minus case runs on the twin identity (x−y)(x2+xy+y2)=x3−y3(x - y)(x^2 + xy + y^2) = x^3 - y^3(x−y)(x2+xy+y2)=x3−y3, with every sign in the multiplier now positive. In full generality, for rational aaa, bbb, and ddd,

(a+bd3)(a2−abd3+b2d23)=a3+b3d,\left(a + b\sqrt[3]{d}\right)\left(a^2 - ab\sqrt[3]{d} + b^2\sqrt[3]{d^2}\right) = a^3 + b^3 d ,(a+b3d​)(a2−ab3d​+b23d2​)=a3+b3d,

and, since aaa, bbb, and ddd are all rational, the right-hand side is rational. The same nonzero guarantee carries over from the square-root case, by the same kind of argument: if aaa and bbb are rational and d3\sqrt[3]{d}3d​ is irrational, then a3+b3d=0a^3 + b^3 d = 0a3+b3d=0 forces a=b=0a = b = 0a=b=0. A nonzero denominator still produces a nonzero rational to divide by.

Read the full cube-root nonzero argument

The principle did all the work: ask which multiplier makes every radical term cancel, and the algebra answers. The sign flip was never the point; it was just what the answer happened to look like when the root was a square root.

Rationalizing the numerator

The principle says “clear the awkward part out of the way,” and it never said the awkward part has to be downstairs. Sometimes the numerator is the problem, and then you aim the same move at it.

How big is 101−100\sqrt{101} - \sqrt{100}101​−100​? Subtracting two nearly equal numbers is a terrible way to find out by hand. The leading digits destroy each other, so you would need many digits of 101\sqrt{101}101​ before even the first digit of the answer settled down. Rationalize the numerator instead, by multiplying top and bottom by 101+10\sqrt{101} + 10101​+10:

101−10=(101−10)(101+10)101+10=101−100101+10=1101+10.\sqrt{101} - 10 = \frac{\left(\sqrt{101} - 10\right)\left(\sqrt{101} + 10\right)}{\sqrt{101} + 10} = \frac{101 - 100}{\sqrt{101} + 10} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{101} + 10} .101​−10=101​+10(101​−10)(101​+10)​=101​+10101−100​=101​+101​.

The delicate cancellation is gone. In its place is a division by a number you barely need to know, and a crude bound on 101\sqrt{101}101​ is now enough to pin the answer tightly. The same rewrite in general,

n+1−n=1n+1+n,\sqrt{n+1} - \sqrt{n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}} ,n+1​−n​=n+1​+n​1​,

also explains something you may have noticed on a number line. Consecutive square roots crowd closer and closer together as nnn grows, because the denominator on the right grows without bound while the numerator stays at 111.

Notice what this does to the school rule. Here the useful form is the one with the radical in the denominator, and the form to be avoided is the one with the radical upstairs. “Rationalize the denominator” is a presentation convention, not a law of nature.

Why bother, when a calculator exists

This deserves an honest answer rather than a slogan, because the usual justification stopped being true around 1975.

The historical reason was hand computation. Suppose a table tells you 2=1.41421356\sqrt{2} = 1.414213562​=1.41421356 and you want 12\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}2​1​. Taken literally, that is a long division of 111 by an eight-digit decimal, done by hand, and it is miserable. Rationalize it first and the same table entry gives the answer by halving:

12=22=1.414213562=0.70710678.\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \frac{1.41421356}{2} = 0.70710678 .2​1​=22​​=21.41421356​=0.70710678.

One is a page of long division, the other is a line of halving. Multiply that saving across every computation in a book of tables and you see why nineteenth-century textbooks drilled the rule until it felt like a moral principle. The calculator quietly removed that reason, and the rule stayed.

The reason that survives is canonical form. Rationalizing puts every number of this kind into one standard shape, p+qdp + q\sqrt{d}p+qd​ with ppp and qqq rational, for a fixed ddd. That shape is unique: no other rational pair p′,q′p', q'p′,q′ names the same number. (If a second pair worked, subtracting the two forms would make d\sqrt{d}d​ equal a ratio of rational numbers, and d\sqrt{d}d​ is irrational.)

Everything below rests on that one fact. Read the full argument for why no two different rational pairs can name the same number, in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Read the full uniqueness proof

That fact is what makes the convention worth keeping. Two expressions can look nothing alike and be the same number, and until you reduce them to canonical form, “they look different” is evidence of nothing. Consider these three:

43+5,3−5,25+1.\frac{4}{3 + \sqrt{5}}, \qquad 3 - \sqrt{5}, \qquad \frac{2}{\sqrt{5} + 1} .3+5​4​,3−5​,5​+12​.

Rationalize the third: 25+1=2(5−1)5−1=5−12=−12+125\frac{2}{\sqrt{5} + 1} = \frac{2(\sqrt{5} - 1)}{5 - 1} = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2} = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5}5​+12​=5−12(5​−1)​=25​−1​=−21​+21​5​. Now compare canonical forms. The first two are both 3+(−1)53 + (-1)\sqrt{5}3+(−1)5​, so they are equal. Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to square the coefficient. The conjugate product is (a+bd)(a−bd)=a2−b2d(a + b\sqrt{d})(a - b\sqrt{d}) = a^2 - b^2 d(a+bd​)(a−bd​)=a2−b2d, not a2−bda^2 - bda2−bd and not a2−b2da^2 - b^2\sqrt{d}a2−b2d​. For 3+223 + 2\sqrt{2}3+22​ the denominator is 9−4(2)=19 - 4(2) = 19−4(2)=1, not 9−2(2)=59 - 2(2) = 59−2(2)=5.
  • Getting the sign of the product wrong. Complex conjugates add, because i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 turns −b2i2-b^2i^2−b2i2 into +b2+b^2+b2. Radical conjugates subtract, because (d)2=d(\sqrt{d})^2 = d(d​)2=d. Mixing up which row you are in produces a2+b2da^2 + b^2 da2+b2d, which is wrong.
  • Multiplying only the denominator. Multiplying the bottom by a−bda - b\sqrt{d}a−bd​ and leaving the top alone changes the number. You must multiply by a−bda−bd\frac{a - b\sqrt{d}}{a - b\sqrt{d}}a−bd​a−bd​​, which is 111.
  • Flipping a sign at a cube root. (1+23)(1−23)=1−43(1 + \sqrt[3]{2})(1 - \sqrt[3]{2}) = 1 - \sqrt[3]{4}(1+32​)(1−32​)=1−34​, still irrational. A cube root needs the three-term multiplier from the sum or difference of cubes, because it has two irrational powers to kill, not one.
  • Stopping before the fraction is reduced. 45−7\frac{4}{5 - \sqrt{7}}5−7​4​ becomes 4(5+7)18\frac{4(5 + \sqrt{7})}{18}184(5+7​)​, which still has a common factor of 222. The finished answer is 10+279\frac{10 + 2\sqrt{7}}{9}910+27​​.

  • It is the same move as complex division: zz‾=a2+b2z\overline{z} = a^2 + b^2zz=a2+b2 is real. In both cases you multiply by the partner that lands the product back in the base number system.   • Rationalizing proves closure: 1a+bd=a−bda2−b2d\frac{1}{a + b\sqrt{d}} = \frac{a - b\sqrt{d}}{a^2 - b^2 d}a+bd​1​=a2−b2da−bd​​ is again of the form p+qdp + q\sqrt{d}p+qd​.

The full argument that a cube-root denominator is never accidentally zero

Suppose aaa and bbb are rational, d3\sqrt[3]{d}3d​ is irrational, and a3+b3d=0a^3 + b^3 d = 0a3+b3d=0. Were bbb nonzero, we could write d=(−ab)3d = \left(-\frac{a}{b}\right)^3d=(−ba​)3. Because every real number has exactly one real cube root, that would make d3=−ab\sqrt[3]{d} = -\frac{a}{b}3d​=−ba​, a ratio of rational numbers and therefore rational, contradicting that d3\sqrt[3]{d}3d​ is irrational. So b=0b = 0b=0, and then a3=0a^3 = 0a3=0 gives a=0a = 0a=0. A nonzero denominator of this form is always safe to divide by, by the same reasoning as the square-root case.

For a fixed d, each number has exactly one p + q root d form

For a fixed nonsquare ddd, each number has exactly one p+qdp + q\sqrt{d}p+qd​ form#

Let ddd be a positive integer that is not a perfect square, so d\sqrt{d}d​ is irrational, and suppose

p+qd=p′+q′dp + q\sqrt{d} = p' + q'\sqrt{d}p+qd​=p′+q′d​

A bit of history (optional)

Some equations look small and are not. Ask for whole numbers xxx and yyy that satisfy x2−61y2=1x^2 - 61y^2 = 1x2−61y2=1. It reads like something you could stumble on by trying a few values. You cannot: the smallest yyy that works is 226,153,980226{,}153{,}980226,153,980, and the matching xxx runs to ten digits.

That equation was solved in India around the year 1150 by Bhaskara, with a method that cycles small near misses upward until one of them lands exactly on 111. Europe did not manage it for another five hundred years.

Now look at what a solution really is. The expression x2−61y2x^2 - 61y^2x2−61y2 is the product of x+y61x + y\sqrt{61}x+y61​ and x−y61x - y\sqrt{61}x−y61​, which are radical conjugates. Landing on 111 therefore means finding a number of that shape whose conjugate is also its reciprocal. Bhaskara was hunting conjugate pairs nine hundred years ago.

You met the small version of his equation in this lesson without being told. 3+223 + 2\sqrt{2}3+22​ times 3−223 - 2\sqrt{2}3−22​ is 9−89 - 89−8, which is 111, and that is why that denominator cleared so cleanly.

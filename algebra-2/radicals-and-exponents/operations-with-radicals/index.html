Operations with Radicals

Intuition

Is 2+3\sqrt2 + \sqrt32​+3​ equal to 5\sqrt55​? A tempting guess says yes, and it is wrong. The numbers disagree: 2+3≈3.146\sqrt2 + \sqrt3 \approx 3.1462​+3​≈3.146, while 5≈2.236\sqrt5 \approx 2.2365​≈2.236. The mistake is treating the radical sign as though it reaches inside a sum and operates on the radicands. It does not. A radical is just a number, and numbers add the way numbers have always added. So the real question is not “what is the rule for adding radicals” but “which radicals can be collected together.” The answer is a law you have owned since you first met like terms. In everything that follows, 2\sqrt22​ behaves exactly like an xxx.

Learning goals

  • Combine like radicals by the distributive law
  • Simplify before deciding whether two radicals are alike
  • Multiply and divide radicals by handling coefficients and radicands separately
  • Pair a radical sum with its matching difference to erase the radicals
  • Switch to rational exponents when the indices differ

Radicals add like terms, not like radicands

Start by killing the tempting rule with numbers you can check by hand. If a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt ba​+b​ really equalled a+b\sqrt{a+b}a+b​, then taking a=9a = 9a=9 and b=16b = 16b=16 would give:

9+16=3+4=7,but9+16=25=5.\sqrt9 + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7, \qquad \text{but} \qquad \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5.9​+16​=3+4=7,but9+16​=25​=5.

Seven is not five, so the rule is false. That one pair of perfect squares is enough to prove it.

Now look at what does work:

2+2=(1+1)2=22.\sqrt2 + \sqrt2 = (1 + 1)\sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2.2​+2​=(1+1)2​=22​.

Read the middle step carefully, because it is the whole lesson. Nothing about square roots was used there. That is the distributive law, cu+du=(c+d)ucu + du = (c + d)ucu+du=(c+d)u, applied with u=2u = \sqrt2u=2​. The same law that turns 3x+5x3x + 5x3x+5x into 8x8x8x turns 32+523\sqrt2 + 5\sqrt232​+52​ into 828\sqrt282​, and for the same reason:

32+52=(3+5)2=82.3\sqrt2 + 5\sqrt2 = (3 + 5)\sqrt2 = 8\sqrt2.32​+52​=(3+5)2​=82​.

Two radicals are like radicals when they have the same index and the same radicand. The index and the radicand together name the unit; the number in front is how many of that unit you have. That test only decides the question once both radicals are already in simplest form, a restriction the next section shows is doing real work. So 2\sqrt22​ and 424\sqrt242​ are like radicals, while 2\sqrt22​ and 3\sqrt33​ are not (different radicands). And 2\sqrt22​ and 23\sqrt[3]{2}32​ are not like radicals either (same radicand, different indices, so they are not even the same size: 2≈1.414\sqrt2 \approx 1.4142​≈1.414 and 23≈1.260\sqrt[3]{2} \approx 1.26032​≈1.260).

Only like radicals collect, because only like radicals share the common factor that the distributive law needs. There is no rule for adding 2\sqrt22​ and 3\sqrt33​ for the same reason there is no rule for adding xxx and yyy. That is because the sum x+yx + yx+y is already as simple as it gets.

The picture shows that the two quantities differ, but it does not say by how much, or whether the false rule might rescue itself in some lucky case. Algebra settles both questions at once.

a+b=a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt b = \sqrt{a+b}a​+b​=a+b​ holds only when a=0a = 0a=0 or b=0b = 0b=0#

Try the argument on a=1a = 1a=1 and b=4b = 4b=4 first. Suppose 1+4=1+4\sqrt1 + \sqrt4 = \sqrt{1+4}1​+4​=1+4​ and square both sides. The left side expands by the distributive law: (1+4)2=(1)2+214+(4)2=1+4+2⋅2=9(\sqrt1 + \sqrt4)^2 = (\sqrt1)^2 + 2\sqrt1\sqrt4 + (\sqrt4)^2 = 1 + 4 + 2\cdot2 = 9(1​+4​)2=(1​)2+21​4​+(4​)2=1+4+2⋅2=9. The right side squares to 1+4=51 + 4 = 51+4=5. Since 9≠59 \ne 59=5, the equation fails for this pair, the same way it failed for 999 and 161616 above.

Now repeat those same steps with letters, so the argument covers every pair at once. Let a≥0a \ge 0a≥0 and b≥0b \ge 0b≥0, so every square root here is real, and suppose a+b=a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt b = \sqrt{a + b}a​+b​=a+b​. Square both sides. The left side expands the same way:

(a+b)2=(a)2+2ab+(b)2.(\sqrt a + \sqrt b)^2 = (\sqrt a)^2 + 2\sqrt a \sqrt b + (\sqrt b)^2.(a​+b​)2=(a​)2+2a​b​+(b​)2.

Since a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0, the product rule gives ab=ab\sqrt a \sqrt b = \sqrt{ab}a​b​=ab​, so the left side is a+b+2aba + b + 2\sqrt{ab}a+b+2ab​. The right side squares to a+ba + ba+b. The supposed identity therefore forces

a+b+2ab=a+b.a + b + 2\sqrt{ab} = a + b.a+b+2ab​=a+b.

Cancel a+ba + ba+b from both sides: 2ab=02\sqrt{ab} = 02ab​=0, so ab=0\sqrt{ab} = 0ab​=0, so ab=0ab = 0ab=0. A product is zero only when one of its factors is, so a=0a = 0a=0 or b=0b = 0b=0.

Check the other direction too. If a=0a = 0a=0, the claim reads 0+b=0+b\sqrt0 + \sqrt b = \sqrt{0 + b}0​+b​=0+b​, which is just b=b\sqrt b = \sqrt bb​=b​, and b=0b = 0b=0 works the same way. So the identity holds for exactly those pairs, and no others.

The squaring step also says how far apart the two sides are whenever a,b>0a, b > 0a,b>0. The left side’s square is a+b+2aba + b + 2\sqrt{ab}a+b+2ab​; the right side’s is only a+ba + ba+b. That leftover 2ab2\sqrt{ab}2ab​ is exactly what a student writing a+b=a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt b = \sqrt{a+b}a​+b​=a+b​ throws away.

It measures a gap between squares, not between the original numbers. Back at a=9a = 9a=9 and b=16b = 16b=16, the two sides themselves are 777 and 555, a gap of 222. But 2ab=242\sqrt{ab} = 242ab​=24 is the gap between their squares, 494949 and 252525.

Simplify first, then decide what is alike

Here is the trap that makes the like-radical test harder than it looks. Consider 12+27\sqrt{12} + \sqrt{27}12​+27​. The radicands are 121212 and 272727, which are different, so the two radicals are unlike and the sum cannot be collected. That conclusion is wrong.

Simplify each radical first, pulling out the largest perfect square:

12=4⋅3=23,27=9⋅3=33.\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 3} = 2\sqrt3, \qquad \sqrt{27} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 3} = 3\sqrt3.12​=4⋅3​=23​,27​=9⋅3​=33​.

They were like radicals all along, wearing a disguise. Now the distributive law finishes the job:

12+27=23+33=(2+3)3=53.\sqrt{12} + \sqrt{27} = 2\sqrt3 + 3\sqrt3 = (2 + 3)\sqrt3 = 5\sqrt3.12​+27​=23​+33​=(2+3)3​=53​.

So state the test carefully. Put every radical in simplest form, and only then compare. Two radical terms in simplest form combine into a single term exactly when they have the same index and the same radicand. Applied to radicals you have not yet simplified, that test can mislead you: 12\sqrt{12}12​ and 27\sqrt{27}27​ look unlike and are nonetheless the same unit five times over. Simplifying is not cosmetic housekeeping before addition. It is the step that tells you whether addition is possible at all.

The index has to match as well, and simplifying can expose a shared index just as easily. Cube roots collect among themselves: since 163=8⋅23=223\sqrt[3]{16} = \sqrt[3]{8 \cdot 2} = 2\sqrt[3]{2}316​=38⋅2​=232​ and 543=27⋅23=323\sqrt[3]{54} = \sqrt[3]{27 \cdot 2} = 3\sqrt[3]{2}354​=327⋅2​=332​, we get 163+543=523\sqrt[3]{16} + \sqrt[3]{54} = 5\sqrt[3]{2}316​+354​=532​. But 2+23\sqrt2 + \sqrt[3]{2}2​+32​ stays exactly as written. The radicands agree and the indices do not, so there is no common unit to factor out.

Worked example 1 Simplify 50+18−8\sqrt{50} + \sqrt{18} - \sqrt{8}50​+18​−8​

None of the three radicands is a perfect square, and all three are different, so nothing can be decided yet. Simplify each one by splitting off its largest square factor.

50=25⋅2=52,18=9⋅2=32,8=4⋅2=22.\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} = 5\sqrt2, \qquad \sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 2} = 3\sqrt2, \qquad \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 2} = 2\sqrt2.50​=25⋅2​=52​,18​=9⋅2​=32​,8​=4⋅2​=22​.

Every term turned out to be a multiple of the same unit 2\sqrt22​, so the distributive law applies to all three at once:

52+32−22=(5+3−2)2=62.5\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt2 - 2\sqrt2 = (5 + 3 - 2)\sqrt2 = 6\sqrt2.52​+32​−22​=(5+3−2)2​=62​.

As a sanity check, 50+18−8≈7.071+4.243−2.828=8.486\sqrt{50} + \sqrt{18} - \sqrt{8} \approx 7.071 + 4.243 - 2.828 = 8.48650​+18​−8​≈7.071+4.243−2.828=8.486, and 62≈8.4856\sqrt2 \approx 8.48562​≈8.485. The two agree, as they must; the last digit only differs because each square root was rounded before adding.

Multiplying radicals

Multiplication is where radicals are cooperative, thanks to the product rule from the last lesson: for a≥0a \ge 0a≥0 and b≥0b \ge 0b≥0,

a⋅b=ab.\sqrt a \cdot \sqrt b = \sqrt{ab}.a​⋅b​=ab​.

Multiplying two radical terms uses that rule twice: multiply the coefficients, multiply the radicands, then simplify what comes out. A product of two unsimplifiable radicals can still hide a square.

(26)(310)=(2⋅3)(6⋅10)=660=64⋅15=1215.(2\sqrt6)(3\sqrt{10}) = (2 \cdot 3)(\sqrt6 \cdot \sqrt{10}) = 6\sqrt{60} = 6\sqrt{4 \cdot 15} = 12\sqrt{15}.(26​)(310​)=(2⋅3)(6​⋅10​)=660​=64⋅15​=1215​.

In general, because multiplication is commutative and associative,

(ca)(db)=(c⋅d)(a⋅b)=cdab(a,b≥0).(c\sqrt a)(d\sqrt b) = (c \cdot d)(\sqrt a \cdot \sqrt b) = cd\sqrt{ab} \qquad (a, b \ge 0).(ca​)(db​)=(c⋅d)(a​⋅b​)=cdab​(a,b≥0).

That hypothesis a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0 is not decoration. Take a=−4a = -4a=−4 and b=−9b = -9b=−9. Over the real numbers −4\sqrt{-4}−4​ does not exist, so there is nothing to multiply. Even in the complex numbers, where −4=2i\sqrt{-4} = 2i−4​=2i and −9=3i\sqrt{-9} = 3i−9​=3i, the rule collapses:

−4⋅−9=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6,while(−4)(−9)=36=6.\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6, \qquad \text{while} \qquad \sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6.−4​⋅−9​=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6,while(−4)(−9)​=36​=6.

The two answers differ in sign, so a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0 is what keeps the product rule honest.

Two special cases fall straight out. Squaring undoes the root, (a)2=a(\sqrt a)^2 = a(a​)2=a for a≥0a \ge 0a≥0, because that is exactly what the principal square root is defined to do, so (53)2=25⋅3=75(5\sqrt3)^2 = 25 \cdot 3 = 75(53​)2=25⋅3=75. And multiplying a radical against a sum works like multiplying anything else against a sum: distribute it across each term.

3(6+15)=18+45=32+35.\sqrt3(\sqrt6 + \sqrt{15}) = \sqrt{18} + \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt5.3​(6​+15​)=18​+45​=32​+35​.

Products of two binomials work exactly as they did for polynomials: expand every term against every term, then collect like radicals.

(2+3)(5−3)=10−23+53−(3)2=10+33−3=7+33.(2 + \sqrt3)(5 - \sqrt3) = 10 - 2\sqrt3 + 5\sqrt3 - (\sqrt3)^2 = 10 + 3\sqrt3 - 3 = 7 + 3\sqrt3.(2+3​)(5−3​)=10−23​+53​−(3​)2=10+33​−3=7+33​.

Now one product deserves to be singled out. Multiply a sum of two radicals by the difference of the same two radicals, called its conjugate, and the middle terms cancel by the difference-of-squares pattern:

(a+b)(a−b)=(a)2−(b)2=a−b(a,b≥0).(\sqrt a + \sqrt b)(\sqrt a - \sqrt b) = (\sqrt a)^2 - (\sqrt b)^2 = a - b \qquad (a, b \ge 0).(a​+b​)(a​−b​)=(a​)2−(b​)2=a−b(a,b≥0).

Look at what that says. Two irrational numbers can multiply to a plain integer: (7+3)(7−3)=7−3=4(\sqrt7 + \sqrt3)(\sqrt7 - \sqrt3) = 7 - 3 = 4(7​+3​)(7​−3​)=7−3=4. Every radical is gone. The same works with a rational term attached, (p+qc)(p−qc)=p2−q2c(p + q\sqrt c)(p - q\sqrt c) = p^2 - q^2 c(p+qc​)(p−qc​)=p2−q2c, so (3+25)(3−25)=9−4⋅5=−11(3 + 2\sqrt5)(3 - 2\sqrt5) = 9 - 4 \cdot 5 = -11(3+25​)(3−25​)=9−4⋅5=−11. You will use this same move in the chapter’s last lesson to clear a radical out of a denominator. For now, just register the fact: pairing a radical sum with its conjugate destroys the radical.

Worked example 2 Expand (23+5)(3−35)(2\sqrt3 + \sqrt5)(\sqrt3 - 3\sqrt5)(23​+5​)(3​−35​)

In two of the four products a radical meets its twin and vanishes: 23⋅3=2⋅3=62\sqrt3 \cdot \sqrt3 = 2 \cdot 3 = 623​⋅3​=2⋅3=6 and 5⋅(−35)=−3⋅5=−15\sqrt5 \cdot (-3\sqrt5) = -3 \cdot 5 = -155​⋅(−35​)=−3⋅5=−15. Dividing radicals

Division follows the same pattern with the same kind of hypothesis. For a≥0a \ge 0a≥0 and b>0b > 0b>0,

ab=ab.\frac{\sqrt a}{\sqrt b} = \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}.b​a​​=ba​​.

The reason is short. The quotient a/b\sqrt a / \sqrt ba​/b​ is nonnegative, and squaring it gives (a)2/(b)2=a/b(\sqrt a)^2 / (\sqrt b)^2 = a / b(a​)2/(b​)2=a/b. The principal square root of a/ba/ba/b is by definition the one nonnegative number whose square is a/ba/ba/b, so the quotient must be it. We need b>0b > 0b>0 rather than b≥0b \ge 0b≥0 only to keep from dividing by zero.

In practice this turns ugly-looking quotients into clean ones:

722=36=6,122137=4217=43.\frac{\sqrt{72}}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt{36} = 6, \qquad \frac{12\sqrt{21}}{3\sqrt7} = 4\sqrt{\frac{21}{7}} = 4\sqrt3.2​72​​=36​=6,37​1221​​=4721​​=43​.

When the division does not come out clean, as with 2/3\sqrt2 / \sqrt32​/3​, you are left with a radical sitting in the denominator. That is not wrong, merely unfinished, and the chapter’s last lesson is devoted to tidying it up.

Roots of different indices

Now the case where the product rule is genuinely powerless. What is 2⋅23\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt[3]{2}2​⋅32​?

The product rule needs a common index, and here there is none: one factor is a square root, the other a cube root. No amount of staring will fix that, because the rule simply does not apply. What does apply is an earlier lesson’s translation into rational exponents, where a root of index nnn becomes an exponent of 1/n1/n1/n:

2⋅23=21/2⋅21/3=21/2+1/3=25/6=256=326.\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt[3]{2} = 2^{1/2} \cdot 2^{1/3} = 2^{1/2 + 1/3} = 2^{5/6} = \sqrt[6]{2^5} = \sqrt[6]{32}.2​⋅32​=21/2⋅21/3=21/2+1/3=25/6=625​=632​.

Powers of the same base multiply by adding exponents, and adding 1/21/21/2 and 1/31/31/3 forces you to find a common denominator. That common denominator, 666, is the common index the radicals were missing. The same computation in radical clothing says: rewrite 2=23/6=86\sqrt2 = 2^{3/6} = \sqrt[6]{8}2​=23/6=68​ and 23=22/6=46\sqrt[3]{2} = 2^{2/6} = \sqrt[6]{4}32​=22/6=64​, and now that the indices agree the product rule finally applies, giving 86⋅46=326\sqrt[6]{8} \cdot \sqrt[6]{4} = \sqrt[6]{32}68​⋅64​=632​. Same answer, and it shows what “common index” means on the page.

The radicands do not even have to be powers of one base. The common-index idea still carries it:

2⋅33=23/6⋅32/6=236⋅326=8⋅96=726.\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt[3]{3} = 2^{3/6} \cdot 3^{2/6} = \sqrt[6]{2^3} \cdot \sqrt[6]{3^2} = \sqrt[6]{8 \cdot 9} = \sqrt[6]{72}.2​⋅33​=23/6⋅32/6=623​⋅632​=68⋅9​=672​.

This is the payoff of that earlier lesson. Fractional exponents are not just a different way to write a root you already knew. They are what turns a question about incompatible radical signs into a question about adding fractions, which you already know how to do.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Writing a+b=a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt b = \sqrt{a + b}a​+b​=a+b​. For a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0 this holds only when aaa or bbb is 000. Check any pair of positive perfect squares to see it fail.
  • Declaring radicals unlike before simplifying them. 12\sqrt{12}12​ and 27\sqrt{27}27​ look unlike, but their simplest forms, 232\sqrt323​ and 333\sqrt333​, are like radicals. Always simplify first.
  • Combining the radicands of like radicals. 35+453\sqrt5 + 4\sqrt535​+45​ is 757\sqrt575​, not 7107\sqrt{10}710​ or 7257\sqrt{25}725​: the radicand is the unit, and counting more of it does not change the unit.
  • Ignoring the index. 2\sqrt22​ and 23\sqrt[3]{2}32​ share a radicand but are different numbers, so they are not like radicals. Multiplying them needs rational exponents, not the product rule.
  • Using the product rule on two negative radicands. ab=ab\sqrt a \sqrt b = \sqrt{ab}a​b​=ab​ needs a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0. With one negative radicand it still holds using the complex convention: −49=2i⋅3=6i\sqrt{-4}\sqrt9 = 2i \cdot 3 = 6i−4​9​=2i⋅3=6i, and (−4)(9)=−36=6i\sqrt{(-4)(9)} = \sqrt{-36} = 6i(−4)(9)​=−36​=6i agree. It fails only once both radicands are negative.
  • Squaring a sum term by term. (a+b)2(\sqrt a + \sqrt b)^2(a​+b​)2 is a+2ab+ba + 2\sqrt{ab} + ba+2ab​+b, not a+ba + ba+b: the cross term is the same one that makes a+b=a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt b = \sqrt{a+b}a​+b​=a+b​ false.

Key takeaways

  • Like radicals share the same index and radicand, and combine by the distributive law, cn+dn=(c+d)nc\sqrt n + d\sqrt n = (c + d)\sqrt ncn​+dn​=(c+d)n​. The test only applies once both radicals are in simplest form.
  • Simplify before you compare. Radicals that look unlike, such as 12\sqrt{12}12​ and 27\sqrt{27}27​, can share a unit once simplified.
  • a+b=a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt b = \sqrt{a + b}a​+b​=a+b​ is false for every positive aaa and bbb: squaring shows the two squares differ by the cross term 2ab2\sqrt{ab}2ab​.
  • Product and quotient rules need a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0 for a product, and a≥0a \ge 0a≥0, b>0b > 0b>0 for a quotient. Multiply or divide coefficients with coefficients and radicands with radicands, then simplify.
  • Conjugate pairs erase radicals: (a+b)(a−b)=a−b(\sqrt a + \sqrt b)(\sqrt a - \sqrt b) = a - b(a​+b​)(a​−b​)=a−b. Squaring the same sum does the opposite, doubling the cross term instead of cancelling it.
  • Different indices need rational exponents. Rewrite each radical as a power. For a shared base, add the exponents once they share a denominator, which becomes the common index. For different bases, use that common denominator to rewrite both indices before combining the radicands.

A bit of history (optional)

For a long time a number like 2\sqrt22​ was called deaf. The insult is genuinely old, and the trail behind it is worth following.

Greek geometers could prove that the diagonal of a square shares no common measure with its side. They called such a length alogos. The word carries two meanings at once, without ratio and without speech. Scholars translating that mathematics into Arabic preserved the pun and wrote asamm, meaning deaf. The Latin translators who followed them, working in the twelfth century, appear to have carried only the literal sense forward. They settled on surdus, the plain word for a person who cannot hear. It reached English as surd, and it has never quite gone away.

Notice what the name is complaining about, because the complaint is exactly this lesson’s subject. Such a number cannot be said as a ratio of two whole numbers, and it will not collapse into anything tidier than itself. That refusal is what you have spent the lesson honouring. When you decline to turn 2+3\sqrt2 + \sqrt32​+3​ into a single radical, you are not leaving the problem half finished. You are reporting that the sum was already finished when you met it.

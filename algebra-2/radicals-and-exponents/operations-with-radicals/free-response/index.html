    1. Radicals that collect like terms . Foundational, 8 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 8 pts

    Three radical terms are written with three different-looking radicands. Whether any of them combine cannot be read off the page as it stands; it depends on what each radical becomes once it is fully simplified.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Simplify 54\sqrt{54}54​ and 96\sqrt{96}96​, pulling out the largest perfect-square factor of each radicand.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 2 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Using your simplified forms from part A, together with 24=26\sqrt{24}=2\sqrt624​=26​, simplify 54+96−24\sqrt{54}+\sqrt{96}-\sqrt{24}54​+96​−24​ as far as it will go.

      Carry your own answer forward Use your own simplified forms for 54\sqrt{54}54​ and 96\sqrt{96}96​ from part A, even if they differ from the ones above, together with 24=26\sqrt{24}=2\sqrt624​=26​ given here.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A classmate looks at 54+96−24\sqrt{54}+\sqrt{96}-\sqrt{24}54​+96​−24​, sees three different radicands (545454, 969696, 242424), and concludes that none of the terms can combine. Name the test the classmate applied too early, and state precisely when two radical terms actually combine.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing about square roots is needed for the addition itself once every term is expressed the same way; the last step is ordinary combining of like terms.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

      Hunt for the largest perfect square dividing each radicand: 999 divides 545454, and 161616 divides 969696.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask what a radicand as originally written actually shows about the number underneath it, and whether that is the same thing its simplified form shows.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    54=36\sqrt{54}=3\sqrt654​=36​ and 96=46\sqrt{96}=4\sqrt696​=46​.

    Part B

    565\sqrt656​.

    Part C

    The classmate tested the radicands before simplifying. Two radical terms combine exactly when, each already in simplest form, they share the same index and the same radicand; here all three reduce to a multiple of 6\sqrt66​.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Split each radicand into a perfect square times whatever is left over, choosing the largest perfect square available.

    Since 54=9⋅654 = 9 \cdot 654=9⋅6 and 999 is a perfect square:

    54=9 6=36.\sqrt{54} = \sqrt9\,\sqrt6 = 3\sqrt6.54​=9​6​=36​.

    Since 96=16⋅696 = 16 \cdot 696=16⋅6 and 161616 is a perfect square:

    96=16 6=46.\sqrt{96} = \sqrt{16}\,\sqrt6 = 4\sqrt6.96​=16​6​=46​.

    Both leftover radicands are 666, which has no square factor beyond 111, so neither simplifies further.

    Part B

    All three terms are now multiples of the same radical, so the distributive law collects them by their coefficients alone.

    54+96−24=36+46−26=(3+4−2)6=56.\sqrt{54}+\sqrt{96}-\sqrt{24} = 3\sqrt6 + 4\sqrt6 - 2\sqrt6 = (3+4-2)\sqrt6 = 5\sqrt6.54​+96​−24​=36​+46​−26​=(3+4−2)6​=56​.

    As a check, 54+96−24≈7.348+9.798−4.899=12.247\sqrt{54}+\sqrt{96}-\sqrt{24} \approx 7.348 + 9.798 - 4.899 = 12.24754​+96​−24​≈7.348+9.798−4.899=12.247, and 56≈12.2475\sqrt6 \approx 12.24756​≈12.247.

    Part C

    The classmate's error is not in the rule quoted. Two radical terms in simplest form really do need a matching index and radicand to combine, and 545454, 969696, and 242424 really are three different numbers.

    The error is in when that comparison was run. A radicand as first written does not yet show what number the radical actually is; only the simplified form does. Here every one of the three terms is secretly a multiple of 6\sqrt66​:

    54=9⋅6,96=16⋅6,24=4⋅6.54 = 9\cdot6,\qquad 96=16\cdot6,\qquad 24=4\cdot6.54=9⋅6,96=16⋅6,24=4⋅6.

    Once that common factor is exposed, the like-radical test has something to compare, and it says the three terms do combine. Running the test on the raw radicands instead of the simplified ones is what produced the wrong conclusion.

    In one line

    54=36\sqrt{54}=3\sqrt654​=36​ and 96=46\sqrt{96}=4\sqrt696​=46​, so 54+96−24=56\sqrt{54}+\sqrt{96}-\sqrt{24}=5\sqrt654​+96​−24​=56​. The classmate's test was run on the radicands before simplifying; only the simplified forms show that all three terms are multiples of 6\sqrt66​, which is why they do combine.

    The answer

    Part A

    30330\sqrt3303​.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Multiply the coefficients together and the radicands together, then simplify.

    (32)(56)=1512=15⋅23=303.(3\sqrt2)(5\sqrt6) = 15\sqrt{12} = 15\cdot2\sqrt3 = 30\sqrt3.(32​)(56​)=1512​=15⋅23​=303​.

    Distribute every term of the first factor against every term of the second.

    32⋅22=12,32⋅(−7)=−314,7⋅22=214,7⋅(−7)=−7.3\sqrt2\cdot2\sqrt2 = 12, \qquad 3\sqrt2\cdot(-\sqrt7) = -3\sqrt{14}, \qquad \sqrt7\cdot2\sqrt2 = 2\sqrt{14}, \qquad \sqrt7\cdot(-\sqrt7) = -7.32​⋅22​=12,32​⋅(−7​)=−314​,7​⋅22​=214​,7​⋅(−7​)=−7.

    (32+7)(22−7)=5−14.(3\sqrt2+\sqrt7)(2\sqrt2-\sqrt7) = 5 - \sqrt{14}.(32​+7​)(22​−7​)=5−14​.

    Part C

    In part B the two factors carried different coefficients on 2\sqrt22​ (a 333 and a 222), so the two cross terms, −314-3\sqrt{14}−314​ and +214+2\sqrt{14}+214​, were unequal in size and only partly cancelled, leaving −14-\sqrt{14}−14​ behind.

    32⋅(−7)=−314,7⋅32=314,3\sqrt2\cdot(-\sqrt7) = -3\sqrt{14}, \qquad \sqrt7\cdot3\sqrt2 = 3\sqrt{14},32​⋅(−7​)=−314​,7​⋅32​=314​,

    which are exact opposites and cancel completely. What remains is (32)2−(7)2=18−7=11(3\sqrt2)^2-(\sqrt7)^2 = 18-7=11(32​)2−(7​)2=18−7=11, with no radical term at all. Matching the coefficient is what turns a partial cancellation into a total one.

    3. Two plates cut to an exact radical size . Application, 10 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 10 pts

    A metal shop cuts rectangular plates to an exact specification rather than a rounded decimal, so a plate's edge lengths are sometimes given as radical expressions in centimetres. Two plates go out today, built the same way but combined differently.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Plate 1 has length (43+2)(4\sqrt3+\sqrt2)(43​+2​) cm and width (3+22)(\sqrt3+2\sqrt2)(3​+22​) cm. Find its area, in simplest form, with units.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Plate 2 has length (35+11)(3\sqrt5+\sqrt{11})(35​+11​) cm and width (35−11)(3\sqrt5-\sqrt{11})(35​−11​) cm. Find its area, in simplest form, with units.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Plate 1's area still carries a radical, but Plate 2's area came out a whole number even though every one of its edge lengths is irrational. Explain, using how each plate's width relates to its length, why only Plate 2's area is rational.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Both areas come from the same operation, multiplying a length by a width that both carry radicals, but the two products behave very differently once expanded.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

      Distribute every term of the length against every term of the width; two of the four resulting products will be plain numbers, and two will carry a radical.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Write out what the width equals in terms of the length's own two radical pieces, for each plate separately, before asking about the cross terms.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    (16+96) cm2(16+9\sqrt6)\text{ cm}^2(16+96​) cm2.

    Part B

    34 cm234\text{ cm}^234 cm2.

    Part C

    Plate 2's width repeats its own length's two radicals with only the sign flipped, a true conjugate pair, so multiplying it by the length makes the two cross terms exact opposites that cancel, leaving a2−b2a^2-b^2a2−b2. Plate 1's width is not built that way, so its cross terms survive.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The area of a rectangle is length times width, so distribute the two binomials.

    43⋅3=12,43⋅22=86,2⋅3=6,2⋅22=4.4\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt3 = 12, \qquad 4\sqrt3\cdot2\sqrt2 = 8\sqrt6, \qquad \sqrt2\cdot\sqrt3 = \sqrt6, \qquad \sqrt2\cdot2\sqrt2 = 4.43​⋅3​=12,43​⋅22​=86​,2​⋅3​=6​,2​⋅22​=4.

    Add the four pieces, combining the rational terms and the radical terms separately:

    (43+2)(3+22)=(12+4)+(86+6)=16+96 cm2.(4\sqrt3+\sqrt2)(\sqrt3+2\sqrt2) = (12+4) + (8\sqrt6+\sqrt6) = 16 + 9\sqrt6 \text{ cm}^2.(43​+2​)(3​+22​)=(12+4)+(86​+6​)=16+96​ cm2.

    Part B

    The width repeats the length's two radicals with only the connecting sign flipped, so this is the conjugate product (a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2(a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2 with a=35a=3\sqrt5a=35​ and b=11b=\sqrt{11}b=11​.

    (35+11)(35−11)=(35)2−(11)2=45−11=34 cm2.(3\sqrt5+\sqrt{11})(3\sqrt5-\sqrt{11}) = (3\sqrt5)^2 - (\sqrt{11})^2 = 45 - 11 = 34 \text{ cm}^2.(35​+11​)(35​−11​)=(35​)2−(11​)2=45−11=34 cm2.

    Both edge lengths are positive, since 35≈6.713\sqrt5\approx6.7135​≈6.71 is larger than 11≈3.32\sqrt{11}\approx3.3211​≈3.32.

    Part C

    Compare how each width was built from its length.

    In Plate 2, the width (35−11)(3\sqrt5-\sqrt{11})(35​−11​) uses the exact same two radicals as the length (35+11)(3\sqrt5+\sqrt{11})(35​+11​), with only the connecting sign reversed. That is precisely the conjugate pattern, and part B showed why it matters: the two cross terms in the expansion are negatives of each other and cancel, leaving only the rational terms:

    35⋅(−11)+11⋅35=−355+355=0.3\sqrt5\cdot(-\sqrt{11}) + \sqrt{11}\cdot3\sqrt5 = -3\sqrt{55}+3\sqrt{55} = 0.35​⋅(−11​)+11​⋅35​=−355​+355​=0.

    In Plate 1, the width (3+22)(\sqrt3+2\sqrt2)(3​+22​) is not the length (43+2)(4\sqrt3+\sqrt2)(43​+2​) with a sign flipped; the coefficients on 3\sqrt33​ and 2\sqrt22​ do not match between the two factors at all.     86+6=96≠0,8\sqrt6 + \sqrt6 = 9\sqrt6 \neq 0,86​+6​=96​=0,

    and a radical remains in the area. The difference is entirely in whether the width is the length's conjugate, not in anything about the numbers being irrational.

    In one line

    The answer

    Part A

    5545\sqrt[4]5545​.

    Part B

    25615\sqrt[15]{256}15256​.

    Part C

    Each radical factor must itself be a real number before it can be rewritten as a power. An odd-index root of a negative number is real (e.g. −163=−163\sqrt[3]{-16}=-\sqrt[3]{16}3−16​=−316​), but an even-index root of a negative number is not, so −164\sqrt[4]{-16}4−16​ has no real value and −164⋅−163\sqrt[4]{-16}\cdot\sqrt[3]{-16}4−16​⋅3−16​ cannot even be started.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Rewrite both factors as powers of the same base. Since 125=53125=5^3125=53,

    1254=53/4,5=51/2.\sqrt[4]{125} = 5^{3/4}, \qquad \sqrt5 = 5^{1/2}.4125​=53/4,5​=51/2.

    Multiplying powers of the same base adds the exponents, over their common denominator 444:

    53/4⋅51/2=53/4+2/4=55/4.5^{3/4}\cdot5^{1/2} = 5^{3/4+2/4} = 5^{5/4}.53/4⋅51/2=53/4+2/4=55/4.

    An exponent past 111 means a whole factor of 555 comes out, since 5/4=1+1/45/4 = 1+1/45/4=1+1/4:

    55/4=51⋅51/4=554.5^{5/4} = 5^1\cdot5^{1/4} = 5\sqrt[4]5.55/4=51⋅51/4=545​.

    Part B

    Write both factors as powers of 222 and add the exponents over the common denominator 151515:

    21/3⋅21/5=25/15+3/15=28/15.2^{1/3}\cdot2^{1/5} = 2^{5/15+3/15} = 2^{8/15}.21/3⋅21/5=25/15+3/15=28/15.

    Since 8/158/158/15 is already less than 111, no whole power comes out this time, and the exponent converts back to a radical of index 151515:

    28/15=2815=25615.2^{8/15} = \sqrt[15]{2^8} = \sqrt[15]{256}.28/15=1528​=15256​.

    Part C

    Both parts A and B start by rewriting a radical as a power of its base, 53/45^{3/4}53/4 or 21/32^{1/3}21/3, and that step only makes sense if the radical already names a real number in the first place; the exponent notation is just a new name for something that already exists.

    Whether a radical names a real number depends on the index's parity together with the radicand's sign, not on the sign of the base by itself. Take −164\sqrt[4]{-16}4−16​. If it named a real number rrr, then r4=−16r^4=-16r4=−16:

    r4=−16.r^4 = -16.r4=−16.

    But a fourth power of a real number is never negative, so no real rrr satisfies that equation. −164\sqrt[4]{-16}4−16​ is not a real number, and there is nothing to relabel as (−16)1/4(-16)^{1/4}(−16)1/4.

    The odd-index factor in the very same product behaves completely differently. An odd power keeps the sign of its base, so every real number, negative ones included, is some real number's cube:

    (−163)3=−16.\left(-\sqrt[3]{16}\right)^3 = -16.(−316​)3=−16.

    So −163=−163\sqrt[3]{-16}=-\sqrt[3]{16}3−16​=−316​ is a perfectly good real number, even though its radicand is the same −16-16−16 that broke the fourth root.

    The product −164⋅−163\sqrt[4]{-16}\cdot\sqrt[3]{-16}4−16​⋅3−16​ therefore fails at its very first factor, before any exponent has been added or any base examined for its sign. The requirement is not that a base be nonnegative in general; it is that each radical being converted must already be real, which for an even index forces a nonnegative radicand and for an odd index requires nothing at all.

    In one line

    1254⋅5=554\sqrt[4]{125}\cdot\sqrt5=5\sqrt[4]54125​⋅5​=545​; 23⋅25=25615\sqrt[3]2\cdot\sqrt[5]2=\sqrt[15]{256}32​⋅52​=15256​; and the technique requires each radical factor to already be a real number: an even-index root of a negative number, such as −164\sqrt[4]{-16}4−16​, names no real number at all, while an odd-index root of that same negative number, such as −163\sqrt[3]{-16}3−16​, is perfectly real.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The quotient rule combines the two roots into the root of a single quotient.

    1626=1626=27=9 3=33.\frac{\sqrt{162}}{\sqrt6} = \sqrt{\frac{162}{6}} = \sqrt{27} = \sqrt9\,\sqrt3 = 3\sqrt3.6​162​​=6162​​=27​=9​3​=33​.

    Part B

    Divide the coefficients and the radicands separately, then apply the quotient rule to what is left under the roots.

    1820038=1832008=625=6⋅5=30.\frac{18\sqrt{200}}{3\sqrt8} = \frac{18}{3}\sqrt{\frac{200}{8}} = 6\sqrt{25} = 6\cdot5 = 30.38​18200​​=318​8200​​=625​=6⋅5=30.

    Part C

    The quotient rule always applies the same way, regardless of what the resulting radicand turns out to be:

    53=53.\frac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt3} = \sqrt{\frac53}.3​5​​=35​​.

    The three parts differ only in what that radicand is. In part B the radicand became 252525, a perfect square outright, so the root evaluated to a whole number with nothing left over. In part A the radicand became 272727, which is not itself a perfect square but does have a perfect-square factor, 27=9⋅327=9\cdot327=9⋅3, so the root only partly resolved, leaving 333\sqrt333​.

    Here the radicand is 5/35/35/3, which is not even a whole number, let alone a perfect square. There is no perfect-square factor to look for in a fraction that is not an integer, so the question that resolved parts A and B does not even apply here, and the radical stays exactly where the quotient rule left it.

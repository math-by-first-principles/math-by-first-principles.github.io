Rational Exponents

Intuition

An exponent began life as a count. a5a^5a5 means five copies of aaa multiplied together, and the 555 counts the copies. So what could a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 mean? “Multiply aaa by itself half a time” is not an instruction anyone can carry out; it is not even a sentence with a meaning. The honest answer is that a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 has no meaning until somebody gives it one, so the value is not discovered, it is chosen. What makes this lesson worth your time is that the choice turns out to be forced. Insist that the exponent laws you already trust keep working, and for a positive aaa, exactly one value of a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 survives. It is a\sqrt{a}a​.

Learning goals

  • Derive a1/n=ana^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}a1/n=na​ for a>0a > 0a>0 from insisting the power law survives
  • Name the principal root, and say when a real one exists
  • Compute am/na^{m/n}am/n by rooting first to keep numbers small
  • Restrict the exponent laws to positive bases
  • Decide when a negative base's rational power, in lowest terms, is real

An exponent that is no longer a count

For a positive integer nnn, the power ana^nan is repeated multiplication, and from that single meaning you proved the laws you have used ever since:

aman=am+n,aman=am−n,(am)n=amn,(ab)n=anbn.a^{m}a^{n} = a^{m+n}, \qquad \frac{a^{m}}{a^{n}} = a^{m-n}, \qquad \left(a^{m}\right)^{n} = a^{mn}, \qquad (ab)^{n} = a^{n}b^{n}.aman=am+n,anam​=am−n,(am)n=amn,(ab)n=anbn.

Each one is a bookkeeping fact about counting factors. Line up mmm copies of aaa next to nnn copies of aaa and you are holding m+nm+nm+n copies, which is the first law.

Now notice something you may never have questioned. You also write a0=1a^0 = 1a0=1 and a−3=1/a3a^{-3} = 1/a^3a−3=1/a3, and neither of those is a count. Zero copies and negative-three copies of aaa are not multiplications anybody can perform. Those values were not discovered by counting; they were selected, and the thing that selected them was the demand that the first law survive. If am+n=amana^{m+n} = a^{m}a^{n}am+n=aman is to keep holding when one exponent is 000, then

am⋅a0=am+0=am,a^{m} \cdot a^{0} = a^{m+0} = a^{m},am⋅a0=am+0=am,

and dividing by ama^{m}am (safe, since a≠0a \neq 0a=0) leaves a0=1a^{0} = 1a0=1. No other value is available. The same pressure fixes negative exponents: a−n⋅an=a−n+n=a0=1a^{-n} \cdot a^{n} = a^{-n+n} = a^{0} = 1a−n⋅an=a−n+n=a0=1, so a−na^{-n}a−n has to be the reciprocal 1/an1/a^{n}1/an.

That is the move this lesson repeats one more time. Every extension of the exponent notation past “repeated multiplication” has been made by asking a single question: what value would let the laws keep working? Answer it for the exponent 1/n1/n1/n and you have rational exponents.

The radical symbol, made precise

You have been writing  \sqrt{\ } ​ for a while now. It sat under the quadratic formula, it measured the modulus of a complex number, and it named the irrational roots of polynomials. Nothing about the symbol is new here. What has never been pinned down is which number the symbol names, and that is suddenly about to matter.

Call rrr an nnn-th root of aaa when rn=ar^{n} = arn=a. The trouble is that this description does not single out one number:

  • 222 and −2-2−2 are both square roots of 444, since 22=42^2 = 422=4 and (−2)2=4(-2)^2 = 4(−2)2=4.
  • −2-2−2 is the only real cube root of −8-8−8.
  • −4-4−4 has no real square root at all, because the square of a real number is never negative.

A symbol has to name one number, so we make a choice, and the choice is called the principal root.

For a≥0a \geq 0a≥0 and any integer n≥2n \geq 2n≥2, the symbol an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ means the nonnegative real number whose nnn-th power is aaa. For odd nnn, a negative aaa has exactly one real nnn-th root and it is negative, so an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ names that number: −83=−2\sqrt[3]{-8} = -23−8​=−2. For even nnn, a negative aaa has no real nnn-th root, so an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ names nothing real at all.

This is why 9\sqrt{9}9​ is 333 and not −3-3−3, and not ±3\pm 3±3 either. The radical hands you exactly one number. That is also the reason the quadratic formula has to write ±b2−4ac\pm\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}±b2−4ac​ with the sign spelled out in front. If you want both roots, you have to ask for both, because the symbol by itself only ever gives you one.

Underneath the convention is a fact worth proving, because the entire rest of the lesson leans on it.

A positive number has exactly one positive nnn-th root#

That such a root exists is a property of the real number line. The reals have no gaps, so for a positive aaa the number we call an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ is genuinely there waiting to be named. You have already met one of them, 2\sqrt{2}2​, and you know it is irrational. Take existence as given. What we can prove with the algebra already in hand is that there is never more than one.

Suppose 0≤r<s0 \leq r < s0≤r<s. Recall the factorization you met when dividing polynomials,

sn−rn=(s−r)(sn−1+sn−2r+⋯+s rn−2+rn−1),s^{n} - r^{n} = (s - r)\left(s^{n-1} + s^{n-2}r + \cdots + s\,r^{n-2} + r^{n-1}\right),sn−rn=(s−r)(sn−1+sn−2r+⋯+srn−2+rn−1),

which is exactly what long division of sn−rns^n - r^nsn−rn by s−rs - rs−r produces. Look at the two factors. The first, s−rs - rs−r, is positive because s>rs > rs>r. The second factor is a sum of nnn terms, and every one of them is nonnegative. Each term is a product of copies of r≥0r \geq 0r≥0 and s>0s > 0s>0, and the leading term sn−1s^{n-1}sn−1 is strictly positive. A sum of nonnegative terms with at least one positive term is positive, so that second factor is positive too. A positive number times a positive number is positive, so sn−rn>0s^{n} - r^{n} > 0sn−rn>0.

In words: raising to the nnn-th power preserves strict order on the nonnegative numbers. Different nonnegative numbers therefore have different nnn-th powers, so no two distinct nonnegative numbers can both be nnn-th roots of the same aaa. Existence plus that argument gives exactly one, and an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ is its name.

The odd case follows by mirroring. When nnn is odd, (−r)n=−rn(-r)^{n} = -r^{n}(−r)n=−rn, so the negative nnn-th roots are the reflections of the positive ones, and a negative number has exactly one real nnn-th root.

∎

Read that headline once more, because it is the hinge of the whole lesson: a positive number has exactly one positive nnn-th root. A negative number has no positive nnn-th root at all, so the moment the base goes negative there is nothing left for this lemma to pin down. Hold that thought.

What a1/na^{1/n}a1/n is forced to be

Here is the central argument. We are free to define a1/na^{1/n}a1/n however we like, but we are not going to be free for long.

For a>0a > 0a>0, the value of a1/na^{1/n}a1/n is forced#

Suppose we extend the notation ara^{r}ar to rational exponents in some way, we do not care how, subject to only two demands. Both already hold for the integer exponents we are extending, so we are asking for nothing new.

The first demand is that the power law (ar)s=ars\left(a^{r}\right)^{s} = a^{rs}(ar)s=ars keeps holding. The second is that a positive base keeps giving a positive result, which is true of every integer power of a positive number.

Write x=a1/nx = a^{1/n}x=a1/n. The second demand says x>0x > 0x>0. The first demand, applied with r=1/nr = 1/nr=1/n and s=ns = ns=n, says

xn=(a1/n)n=a1n⋅n=a1=a.x^{n} = \left(a^{1/n}\right)^{n} = a^{\frac{1}{n} \cdot n} = a^{1} = a.xn=(a1/n)n=an1​⋅n=a1=a.

So xxx is a positive real number whose nnn-th power is aaa. By the lemma above there is exactly one such number, and its name is an\sqrt[n]{a}na​. Therefore

a1/n=an,a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a},a1/n=na​,

and there was never a second candidate. Any definition obeying the two demands must produce this number: it is the only value a1/na^{1/n}a1/n is allowed to take. Whether this choice goes on to obey the exponent laws in general is a separate claim, and it is what the proofs below establish.

∎

Notice exactly what each demand bought. The power law forced xn=ax^{n} = axn=a, and when nnn is even that still leaves two candidates: for a=4a = 4a=4 and n=2n = 2n=2, both 222 and −2-2−2 square to 444. Positivity is what picked one of them. Drop either demand and the definition stops being forced, which is a useful reminder that “the definition is natural” is a claim needing an argument, not a feeling.

The general rational exponent

Once a1/na^{1/n}a1/n is nailed down for a>0a > 0a>0, the rest of the rational exponents come along for free. Keep that hypothesis in view, because it is doing real work: everything in this section is stated for a positive base. The last section of the lesson shows precisely how it all collapses without one.

So let aaa be positive, let mmm be an integer, and let nnn be a positive integer. Apply the power law twice and you get two recipes:

am/n=a1n⋅m=(a1/n)m=(an)m,a^{m/n} = a^{\frac{1}{n} \cdot m} = \left(a^{1/n}\right)^{m} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m},am/n=an1​⋅m=(a1/n)m=(na​)m, am/n=am⋅1n=(am)1/n=amn.a^{m/n} = a^{m \cdot \frac{1}{n}} = \left(a^{m}\right)^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}}.am/n=am⋅n1​=(am)1/n=nam​.

Take the root first and then the power, or take the power first and then the root. Those are different computations, and they had better land on the same number, or the symbol am/na^{m/n}am/n means nothing.

There is a second worry, and it is easy to miss. The fractions 26\tfrac{2}{6}62​ and 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ are the same rational number. So a2/6a^{2/6}a2/6 and a1/3a^{1/3}a1/3 must be the same value, even though one recipe says “sixth root, then square” and the other says “cube root, then leave alone.” Nothing so far guarantees that.

Both worries are real. For a>0a > 0a>0 both are settled, and by the same lemma.

Definition. For a>0a > 0a>0, an integer mmm, and a positive integer nnn,   am/n=(an)m\;a^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m}am/n=(na​)m.

For a>0a > 0a>0, am/na^{m/n}am/n is well defined#

The two routes agree. Let r=anr = \sqrt[n]{a}r=na​, so r>0r > 0r>0 and rn=ar^{n} = arn=a. Using nothing but the integer exponent laws,

(rm)n=rmn=(rn)m=am.\left(r^{m}\right)^{n} = r^{mn} = \left(r^{n}\right)^{m} = a^{m}.(rm)n=rmn=(rn)m=am.

Also rm>0r^{m} > 0rm>0, since every integer power of a positive number is positive. So rmr^{m}rm is a positive number whose nnn-th power is ama^{m}am. And ama^{m}am is positive, so by the lemma it has exactly one positive nnn-th root, which is written amn\sqrt[n]{a^{m}}nam​. Two names for one number force (an)m=amn\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}}(na​)m=nam​, so root-then-power and power-then-root give the same answer.

The form of the fraction does not matter. Any fraction with a positive denominator is the lowest-terms fraction with numerator and denominator both multiplied by the same positive integer kkk. So it is enough to check that scaling numerator and denominator by kkk changes nothing. Let s=anks = \sqrt[nk]{a}s=nka​, so s>0s > 0s>0 and snk=as^{nk} = asnk=a. Then (sk)n=skn=a\left(s^{k}\right)^{n} = s^{kn} = a(sk)n=skn=a and sk>0s^{k} > 0sk>0, so sks^{k}sk is a positive nnn-th root of aaa, and by the lemma it must be the one: sk=ans^{k} = \sqrt[n]{a}sk=na​. Now compute both sides,

amk/(nk)=(ank)mk=(sk)m=(an)m=am/n.a^{mk/(nk)} = \left(\sqrt[nk]{a}\right)^{mk} = \left(s^{k}\right)^{m} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m} = a^{m/n}.amk/(nk)=(nka​)mk=(sk)m=(na​)m=am/n.

So the symbol am/na^{m/n}am/n names one number no matter which fraction you write for the exponent, and no matter which of the two routes you compute it by. Both halves of the argument used the same fact: a>0a > 0a>0 has exactly one positive nnn-th root.

∎

Computing with rational exponents

In practice, always take the root first. Both routes agree, so you may as well pick the one that keeps the numbers small. For instance, 645/664^{5/6}645/6 is (646)5=25=32\left(\sqrt[6]{64}\right)^{5} = 2^5 = 32(664​)5=25=32, whereas the other route asks you to face 6456=1,073,741,8246\sqrt[6]{64^{5}} = \sqrt[6]{1{,}073{,}741{,}824}6645​=61,073,741,824​ to reach the same 323232.

Worked example 1 Evaluate 82/38^{2/3}82/3 and 163/416^{3/4}163/4

For 82/38^{2/3}82/3, read the denominator as the root and the numerator as the power. The denominator is 333, so take the cube root of 888 first:

82/3=(83)2=22=4.8^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^{2} = 2^{2} = 4.82/3=(38​)2=22=4.

The other route confirms it, at the cost of larger arithmetic: 823=643=4\sqrt[3]{8^{2}} = \sqrt[3]{64} = 4382​=364​=4. The two agree, exactly as the well-definedness proof promised.

For 163/416^{3/4}163/4, the denominator 444 says fourth root, and 164=2\sqrt[4]{16} = 2416​=2 because 24=162^4 = 1624=16:

163/4=(164)3=23=8.16^{3/4} = \left(\sqrt[4]{16}\right)^{3} = 2^{3} = 8.163/4=(416​)3=23=8.

Note that the exponent 34\tfrac3443​ is less than 111, and sure enough 888 is smaller than 161616. A fractional exponent between 000 and 111 shrinks a base bigger than 111.

Worked example 2 Evaluate 32−2/532^{-2/5}32−2/5 and (278)−2/3\left(\tfrac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3}(827​)−2/3

A negative exponent still means “take the reciprocal,” exactly as it did for integers. Deal with the sign first, then the fraction.

32−2/5=1322/5=1(325)2=122=14.32^{-2/5} = \frac{1}{32^{2/5}} = \frac{1}{\left(\sqrt[5]{32}\right)^{2}} = \frac{1}{2^{2}} = \frac{1}{4}.32−2/5=322/51​=(532​)21​=221​=41​.

The base 323232 is positive and the answer 14\tfrac1441​ is positive. A negative exponent flips a number over; it never makes it negative.

For the fraction base, flipping the fraction is the cleanest way to absorb the minus sign:

(278)−2/3=(827)2/3=(8273)2=(23)2=49.\left(\frac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3} = \left(\frac{8}{27}\right)^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{\frac{8}{27}}\right)^{2} = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{2} = \frac{4}{9}.(827​)−2/3=(278​)2/3=(3278​​)2=(32​)2=94​.

With both bases positive, the cube root of a fraction is the cube root of the top over the cube root of the bottom. That is the law (ab)r=arbr\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{r} = \tfrac{a^{r}}{b^{r}}(ba​)r=brar​ doing its job.

The laws survive, with one standing hypothesis

Everything you know about exponents now extends to rational exponents. Throughout this list, aaa and bbb are positive and rrr and sss are any rational numbers:

aras=ar+s,aras=ar−s,(ar)s=ars,a^{r}a^{s} = a^{r+s}, \qquad \frac{a^{r}}{a^{s}} = a^{r-s}, \qquad \left(a^{r}\right)^{s} = a^{rs},aras=ar+s,asar​=ar−s,(ar)s=ars, (ab)r=arbr,(ab)r=arbr,a−r=1ar.(ab)^{r} = a^{r}b^{r}, \qquad \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{r} = \frac{a^{r}}{b^{r}}, \qquad a^{-r} = \frac{1}{a^{r}}.(ab)r=arbr,(ba​)r=brar​,a−r=ar1​.

These are not new assumptions. They are consequences of the integer laws plus the definition, and the proof of the first one shows how all of them go. Push everything down to a single positive number and let the integer laws do the work.

For a>0a > 0a>0 and rational r,sr, sr,s: aras=ar+sa^{r}a^{s} = a^{r+s}aras=ar+s#

Write r=mnr = \dfrac{m}{n}r=nm​ and s=pqs = \dfrac{p}{q}s=qp​ with integers m,pm, pm,p and positive integers n,qn, qn,q. Put both over the common denominator nqnqnq, which the well-definedness result licenses:

r=mqnq,s=pnnq.r = \frac{mq}{nq}, \qquad s = \frac{pn}{nq}.r=nqmq​,s=nqpn​.

Now let t=anqt = \sqrt[nq]{a}t=nqa​, a single positive number with tnq=at^{nq} = atnq=a. Straight from the definition, ar=tmqa^{r} = t^{mq}ar=tmq and as=tpna^{s} = t^{pn}as=tpn. Both are ordinary integer powers of ttt, so the integer product law applies to them without any fuss:

aras=tmq⋅tpn=tmq+pn=amq+pnnq=ar+s,a^{r}a^{s} = t^{mq} \cdot t^{pn} = t^{mq + pn} = a^{\frac{mq + pn}{nq}} = a^{r+s},aras=tmq⋅tpn=tmq+pn=anqmq+pn​=ar+s,

where the last step is just adding the two fractions. Every move was an integer-exponent law applied to the positive number ttt. The remaining laws follow the same pattern: rewrite each rational power as an integer power of a common root, and the integer laws finish the job.

∎

Where the laws break: a negative base

That hypothesis a>0a > 0a>0 has been standing in front of every result in this lesson. Here is what it is protecting you from.

Take the base a=−8a = -8a=−8 and the exponent 26\tfrac{2}{6}62​, and compute it three perfectly reasonable looking ways.

Reduce the exponent first. 26=13\tfrac26 = \tfrac1362​=31​, and (−8)1/3=−2(-8)^{1/3} = -2(−8)1/3=−2, since (−2)3=−8(-2)^{3} = -8(−2)3=−8. Reducing a fraction changes nothing about which rational number it names, so this step is honest arithmetic. −2-2−2 is the real value of (−8)2/6(-8)^{2/6}(−8)2/6.

Power first, then root. ((−8)2)1/6=641/6=2\left((-8)^{2}\right)^{1/6} = 64^{1/6} = 2((−8)2)1/6=641/6=2, since 26=642^{6} = 6426=64. This looks just as legal, but it is not: it pulls the base through the outer power, (am)1/n=am/n\left(a^{m}\right)^{1/n} = a^{m/n}(am)1/n=am/n, and that identity was proved only for a>0a > 0a>0. For a=−8a = -8a=−8 it is not licensed, and it hands back the wrong number.

Root first, then power. ((−8)1/6)2\left((-8)^{1/6}\right)^{2}((−8)1/6)2, and it stops dead: −8-8−8 has no real sixth root, because an even power of a real number is never negative. This is the tell. If the power-first route were legitimate, this order should reach the same place; instead it cannot even start, which is the sign that the manipulation smuggled something past you.

One route gives the real value, −2-2−2. The other two are not alternative real values; one is an invalid shortcut and the other exposes why.

Why does the power-first shortcut fail? Go back and reread the proofs. Every one of them, the forcing argument and both halves of well-definedness, spent the same fact: a positive number has exactly one positive nnn-th root. A negative number has no positive nnn-th root whatsoever, so there is nothing for that lemma to pin down, and pulling a negative base through the outer power stops being licensed. The hypothesis a>0a > 0a>0 printed above the law list is precisely the hypothesis the proofs consumed.

So what is still safe? Reducing the exponent is always safe: 26\tfrac2662​ and 13\tfrac1331​ are the same rational number no matter the base, so (−8)2/6(-8)^{2/6}(−8)2/6 and (−8)1/3(-8)^{1/3}(−8)1/3 must agree, and both equal −2-2−2. What is not safe is pulling the base through the power law. Both ((−8)2)1/6=2\left((-8)^{2}\right)^{1/6} = 2((−8)2)1/6=2 and (−8)1/3=−2(-8)^{1/3} = -2(−8)1/3=−2 are true statements about real numbers, but only the second one equals (−8)2/6(-8)^{2/6}(−8)2/6. The false step is claiming ((−8)2)1/6\left((-8)^{2}\right)^{1/6}((−8)2)1/6 equals (−8)2/6(-8)^{2/6}(−8)2/6: that identity, (am)1/n=am/n\left(a^{m}\right)^{1/n} = a^{m/n}(am)1/n=am/n, was proved only for a>0a > 0a>0.

This gives a general rule. For a<0a < 0a<0 and a rational exponent mn\tfrac{m}{n}nm​ already written in lowest terms, there is no fraction left to reduce, so the only route is root then power: am/n=(a1/n)ma^{m/n} = \left(a^{1/n}\right)^{m}am/n=(a1/n)m. That route exists exactly when a1/na^{1/n}a1/n is a real number, which needs nnn odd. So am/na^{m/n}am/n is real when nnn, the reduced denominator, is odd, and has no real value when nnn is even. Try it on a numerator bigger than 111:

(−8)4/3=((−8)1/3)4=(−2)4=16,(-8)^{4/3} = \left((-8)^{1/3}\right)^{4} = (-2)^{4} = 16,(−8)4/3=((−8)1/3)4=(−2)4=16,

real, because 43\tfrac4334​ is already in lowest terms with odd denominator 333. But (−16)5/2(-16)^{5/2}(−16)5/2 has no real value at all: 52\tfrac5225​ is already in lowest terms, its denominator 222 is even, and −16-16−16 has no real square root to raise to the fifth power.

The same crack runs through the radical form of the laws. The rule ab=ab\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​b​=ab​ needs aaa and bbb to be nonnegative. Push it to a=b=−1a = b = -1a=b=−1, using the convention −1=i\sqrt{-1} = i−1​=i from the complex numbers chapter, and the left side is i⋅i=i2=−1i \cdot i = i^{2} = -1i⋅i=i2=−1 while the right side is 1=1\sqrt{1} = 11​=1. The law does not survive the trip.

That last computation is worth being precise about, now that you have complex numbers. “Not a real number” no longer means “meaningless”: −8-8−8 genuinely has six complex sixth roots, and you could pick one. But nothing in this lesson proved a single exponent law for them, and the i⋅ii \cdot ii⋅i calculation shows the laws really do fail out there. Leaving the positive reals means leaving the system in which these rules were proved. Complex exponents are a serious subject, and they are not this one. Use a>0a > 0a>0 whenever you use a law, and treat an odd root of a negative number as a value you look up rather than a value you manipulate.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading the exponent as a division. a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 is not a/2a/2a/2. Check it on a number: 161/2=416^{1/2} = 4161/2=4, while 16/2=816/2 = 816/2=8. The denominator of the exponent is a root, not a divisor.
  • Adding when the law says multiply, or multiplying when it says add. Multiplying two powers adds the exponents, so x1/2⋅x1/3=x5/6x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/3} = x^{5/6}x1/2⋅x1/3=x5/6. Raising a power to a power multiplies them, so (x1/2)1/3=x1/6\left(x^{1/2}\right)^{1/3} = x^{1/6}(x1/2)1/3=x1/6. Those are different operations and different answers.
  • Taking the power before the root. Both routes agree for a>0a > 0a>0, so choose the easy one. 645/6=(646)5=25=3264^{5/6} = \left(\sqrt[6]{64}\right)^{5} = 2^{5} = 32645/6=(664​)5=25=32 beats wrestling with 6456=1,073,741,8246\sqrt[6]{64^{5}} = \sqrt[6]{1{,}073{,}741{,}824}6645​=61,073,741,824​.
  • Believing x2=x\sqrt{x^{2}} = xx2​=x. The radical returns the nonnegative root, so x2=∣x∣\sqrt{x^{2}} = |x|x2​=∣x∣. Test it: (−3)2=9=3\sqrt{(-3)^{2}} = \sqrt{9} = 3(−3)2​=9​=3, not −3-3−3. Only (x)2=x\left(\sqrt{x}\right)^{2} = x(x​)2=x, and that one needs x≥0x \geq 0x≥0 before you can even write it.
  • Pulling a negative base through the power law. (−8)2/6=(−8)1/3=−2(-8)^{2/6} = (-8)^{1/3} = -2(−8)2/6=(−8)1/3=−2 is fine: reducing the fraction is just arithmetic. But (−8)2/6=((−8)2)1/6(-8)^{2/6} = \left((-8)^{2}\right)^{1/6}(−8)2/6=((−8)2)1/6 is not; that move pulls the base through the outer power, a rule proved only for a>0a > 0a>0, and it gives the wrong answer, 222.
  • Letting a negative exponent make the answer negative. With a positive base it never does: 32−2/5=1432^{-2/5} = \tfrac{1}{4}32−2/5=41​, a small positive number. The minus sign flips the value over, it does not flip its sign.

Key takeaways

  • a1/na^{1/n}a1/n cannot be a count of factors, so its value is chosen. Demanding that (ar)s=ars\left(a^{r}\right)^{s} = a^{rs}(ar)s=ars survive, and that positive bases give positive results, forces (a1/n)n=a\left(a^{1/n}\right)^{n} = a(a1/n)n=a, hence a1/n=ana^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}a1/n=na​. One candidate, no freedom left.
  • Principal roots. For a≥0a \geq 0a≥0, an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ is the one nonnegative nnn-th root. For odd nnn, a negative aaa has exactly one real nnn-th root and it is negative. For even nnn, a negative aaa has none.
  • For a>0a > 0a>0,   am/n=(an)m=amn\;a^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}}am/n=(na​)m=nam​, and the value does not depend on the route or on how the fraction is written. Take the root first; the arithmetic stays small.
  • All six exponent laws extend to rational exponents under the standing hypothesis that the bases are positive.
  • Every proof leaned on one fact: a positive number has exactly one positive nnn-th root, which is why pulling a negative base through the power law fails: ((−8)2)1/6=2\left((-8)^{2}\right)^{1/6} = 2((−8)2)1/6=2, but that is not (−8)2/6(-8)^{2/6}(−8)2/6. For a<0a < 0a<0 with mn\tfrac{m}{n}nm​ already in lowest terms, am/n=(a1/n)ma^{m/n} = \left(a^{1/n}\right)^{m}am/n=(a1/n)m is still real exactly when nnn is odd: (−8)2/6=(−8)1/3=−2(-8)^{2/6} = (-8)^{1/3} = -2(−8)2/6=(−8)1/3=−2.
  • x2=∣x∣\sqrt{x^{2}} = |x|x2​=∣x∣, not xxx. The radical always hands back the nonnegative root.

A bit of history (optional)

A root and a power used to be different kinds of thing. A power was a count of factors, written as a small number up in the corner. A root was an operation, written with a sign of its own. Nothing let you combine the two, because the notations did not speak to each other.

Isaac Newton settled that in a pair of letters written in 1676. They were addressed to Henry Oldenburg, the man through whom mathematicians across Europe wrote to one another. In them Newton simply wrote a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 and a3/2a^{3/2}a3/2 and a−2a^{-2}a−2. Then he used those powers exactly as he used a2a^2a2 and a3a^3a3, with no ceremony and no apology.

He was not making a point about notation. He was deep in long calculations that ran on endless sums, and he needed every exponent to obey the same few rules, whole or not. Writing a root as a power was what let one rule cover both.

That is the argument of this lesson, arriving three and a half centuries early. The value of a1/na^{1/n}a1/n was never discovered. It was chosen, and what chose it was the demand that the laws keep working.

Simplifying Radical Expressions

Intuition

You have been writing x\sqrt{\phantom{x}}x​ for chapters now: in the quadratic formula, in the discriminant, in the modulus of a complex number, in the irrational roots of a polynomial. The last lesson finally pinned down what the symbol means, in order to define a1/na^{1/n}a1/n, but that definition was moving fast toward a different destination. This lesson slows down and puts it to work. One of the rules you have been using on trust, ab=ab\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \sqrt bab​=a​b​, is false as soon as aaa and bbb are both negative, and you now own enough machinery to watch it fail. Every result here, including the identity x2=∣x∣\sqrt{x^2} = |x|x2​=∣x∣ that trips up more students than any other in algebra, follows from the same decision the last lesson made: the radical sign must name exactly one number.

Learning goals

  • Read a\sqrt aa​ as the principal root, one number not two
  • Write xnn\sqrt[n]{x^n}nxn​ as ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ for even nnn
  • Apply the product and quotient rules, nonnegative for even index, nonzero divisor for odd
  • Identify the three conditions for simplest radical form
  • Reduce a common factor of index and exponent
  • Reject a+b=a+b\sqrt{a+b} = \sqrt a + \sqrt ba+b​=a​+b​

One symbol, one number

Start with a question you can already answer. The equation

x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9

has two real solutions, x=3x = 3x=3 and x=−3x = -3x=−3. Both of them square to 999, so both of them deserve the name “a square root of 999.” That is a fact about the equation, and nothing in this lesson changes it.

Now ask a different question: what number does the symbol 9\sqrt 99​ stand for? It cannot stand for both of them. A symbol that might mean 333 or might mean −3-3−3 is useless, because then 9+9\sqrt 9 + \sqrt 99​+9​ could be 666, or 000, or −6-6−6. With that ambiguity in play, no equation containing a radical would say anything definite. A symbol has to name one definite value to be useful at all.

So a choice was made, and it is the foundation of everything below. It is the same choice the last lesson made in order to define a1/na^{1/n}a1/n; here it is restated on its own, because everything from this point on depends on it directly.

Definition: the principal square root

For a≥0a \ge 0a≥0, the symbol a\sqrt aa​ denotes the unique non-negative real number whose square is aaa.

So 9=3\sqrt 9 = 39​=3, and only 333. The other square root of 999 has to be written out as −9=−3-\sqrt 9 = -3−9​=−3. Two consequences follow at once, and they are worth saying out loud:

  • A real square root is never negative. Whenever stuff\sqrt{\text{stuff}}stuff​ is a real number, it equals something ≥0\ge 0≥0. This one sentence will decide the whole next section.
  • “Solve x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9” and “evaluate 9\sqrt 99​” are different questions. The first has two answers, the second has one.

That difference is exactly why the quadratic formula carries a ±\pm± in front of its radical:

x=−b±b2−4ac2a.x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​.

The radical hands back only the non-negative root, so the formula has to ask for the other one explicitly. The ±\pm± you have been writing since chapter 4 is not decoration. It is there to repair, by hand, the second root that the symbol deliberately throws away.

The word “unique” in the definition is doing real work, so here is why it holds. Picture rrr starting at 000 and growing. As rrr grows, rnr^nrn grows too, sweeping upward through every non-negative value exactly once on the way, never doubling back. So rnr^nrn can equal aaa at only one value of rrr: there is exactly one non-negative number whose nnn-th power is aaa, which is what makes calling it the principal root legitimate.

The paragraph above gives the reason informally. The formal proof, which rules out two different non-negative numbers sharing an nnn-th power, is in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Roots of any index

Everything above generalises. In the expression an\sqrt[n]{a}na​, the number nnn is the index and aaa is the radicand. A square root is the case n=2n = 2n=2, where the index is left unwritten.

The definition splits into two cases, and the split is caused entirely by signs.

Odd index. Cube every real number and you get every real number back: 23=82^3 = 823=8, (−2)3=−8(-2)^3 = -8(−2)3=−8. An odd power preserves sign, so a negative number has a negative cube root and a positive number has a positive one. In each of those cases there is exactly one real root. No choice has to be made and nothing has to be excluded:

83=2,−83=−2,−325=−2.\sqrt[3]{8} = 2, \qquad \sqrt[3]{-8} = -2, \qquad \sqrt[5]{-32} = -2.38​=2,3−8​=−2,5−32​=−2.

Even index. An even power destroys sign: r4≥0r^4 \ge 0r4≥0 for every real rrr. So a negative radicand has no real even root at all, and a positive radicand has two of them, ±r\pm r±r. Both problems get the same treatment as before: negative radicands are excluded, and of the two roots the non-negative one is chosen. For an even index, an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ names a real number exactly when a≥0a \ge 0a≥0, and that number is never negative:

164=2,646=2,−164 is not a real number.\sqrt[4]{16} = 2, \qquad \sqrt[6]{64} = 2, \qquad \sqrt[4]{-16} \ \text{is not a real number}.416​=2,664​=2,4−16​ is not a real number.

The last one is not meaningless, and chapter 5 is the reason. You know that −1=i\sqrt{-1} = i−1​=i, so a negative radicand under a square root has a perfectly good complex value. Keep the distinction sharp: for the rest of this lesson, every rule involving an even index is stated over the real numbers with a non-negative radicand. An odd index carries no such restriction, as the cube roots above already showed. Hold onto that, because the next section is where ignoring it, for an even index, costs you.

The square root of a square is an absolute value

Here is the single most important identity in the lesson, and the one most often written down wrong. Simplify x2\sqrt{x^2}x2​.

The tempting answer is xxx: the square and the root should undo each other. Test it at x=−3x = -3x=−3:

(−3)2=9=3,butx=−3.\sqrt{(-3)^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3, \qquad \text{but} \qquad x = -3.(−3)2​=9​=3,butx=−3.

So x2=x\sqrt{x^2} = xx2​=x is simply false at x=−3x = -3x=−3. And you should have seen it coming, because of the first consequence of the definition: x\sqrt{\phantom{x}}x​ never returns a negative number, while xxx is negative half the time. An expression that is always ≥0\ge 0≥0 cannot equal an expression that is sometimes <0< 0<0.

What you want is the thing that leaves non-negative numbers alone and flips negative ones. You have had a name for it since chapter 1.

For every real xxx, x2=∣x∣\sqrt{x^2} = |x|x2​=∣x∣#

By definition, x2\sqrt{x^2}x2​ is the non-negative real number whose square is x2x^2x2, and there is only one such number, by the uniqueness fact established above. So the whole job is to show that ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ has those two properties, and uniqueness then forces ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ to be that number.

First, ∣x∣≥0|x| \ge 0∣x∣≥0. That is immediate from the definition of absolute value, which reports a distance from zero.

Second, ∣x∣2=x2|x|^2 = x^2∣x∣2=x2. Check the two cases the absolute value splits into. If x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, then ∣x∣=x|x| = x∣x∣=x, so ∣x∣2=x2|x|^2 = x^2∣x∣2=x2. If x<0x < 0x<0, then ∣x∣=−x|x| = -x∣x∣=−x, so

∣x∣2=(−x)2=(−1)2x2=x2.|x|^2 = (-x)^2 = (-1)^2 x^2 = x^2.∣x∣2=(−x)2=(−1)2x2=x2.

Either way ∣x∣2=x2|x|^2 = x^2∣x∣2=x2.

So ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ is a non-negative number whose square is x2x^2x2. The principal square root of x2x^2x2 is the only non-negative number whose square is x2x^2x2. Therefore x2=∣x∣\sqrt{x^2} = |x|x2​=∣x∣, for every real xxx, with no exceptions and no side conditions.

∎

Notice what this does and does not say. It does not say the square and the root fail to undo each other; it says they undo each other up to sign. The absolute value is the bookkeeping that fixes the sign. The two agree precisely on the non-negative numbers: x2=x\sqrt{x^2} = xx2​=x holds exactly when x≥0x \ge 0x≥0. (In one direction, x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 gives ∣x∣=x|x| = x∣x∣=x. In the other, x2=x\sqrt{x^2} = xx2​=x makes xxx equal to something non-negative, so x≥0x \ge 0x≥0. Both directions check out, which is what “exactly when” demands.)

The same reasoning runs at every index, and the index’s parity decides the answer:

xnn=∣x∣  (n even),xnn=x  (n odd).\sqrt[n]{x^n} = |x| \ \ (n \text{ even}), \qquad \sqrt[n]{x^n} = x \ \ (n \text{ odd}).nxn​=∣x∣  (n even),nxn​=x  (n odd).

An even root is forced to be non-negative, so it needs the bars. An odd root is free to be negative and carries the sign of xxx by itself, so bars would be wrong: (−2)33=−83=−2\sqrt[3]{(-2)^3} = \sqrt[3]{-8} = -23(−2)3​=3−8​=−2, exactly xxx, not ∣x∣|x|∣x∣.

One refinement saves a lot of unnecessary bars. When you pull an even power apart, what matters is the exponent that comes out:

x4=x2,x6=∣x∣3,x10=∣x∣5.\sqrt{x^4} = x^2, \qquad \sqrt{x^6} = |x|^3, \qquad \sqrt{x^{10}} = |x|^5 .x4​=x2,x6​=∣x∣3,x10​=∣x∣5.

In the first, x2x^2x2 is already non-negative for every real xxx, so bars would change nothing and are left off. In the others the exponent coming out is odd, so the result could be negative and the bars are mandatory. Bars are needed when, and only when, the exponent that emerges is odd (and the variable is unrestricted).

Worked example 1 Simplify 20x2\sqrt{20x^2}20x2​, where xxx is any real number

Check the domain first. The radicand is 20x220x^220x2, and x2≥0x^2 \ge 0x2≥0 for every real xxx, so 20x2≥020x^2 \ge 020x2≥0 always. Nothing restricts xxx, which means the answer has to be correct for negative xxx too.

Split off the largest perfect square inside, which is 4x24x^24x2:

20x2=4x2⋅5=4x2 5.\sqrt{20x^2} = \sqrt{4x^2 \cdot 5} = \sqrt{4x^2}\,\sqrt{5}.20x2​=4x2⋅5​=4x2​5​.

(The product rule is doing that split, and both factors are non-negative, so it is allowed. The next section is about what happens when they are not.) Now 4x2=22x2=2∣x∣\sqrt{4x^2} = \sqrt{2^2}\sqrt{x^2} = 2|x|4x2​=22​x2​=2∣x∣, using the identity just proved:

20x2=2∣x∣5.\sqrt{20x^2} = 2|x|\sqrt 5 .20x2​=2∣x∣5​.

The bars are not optional here. Test x=−1x = -1x=−1: the original is 20≈4.47\sqrt{20} \approx 4.4720​≈4.47, a positive number. The candidate answer 2x52x\sqrt 52x5​ would give −25≈−4.47-2\sqrt5 \approx -4.47−25​≈−4.47, the wrong sign. The product rule, and the hypothesis it depends on

The rule you have been using on trust is the product rule, and it comes with a hypothesis attached:

ab=a b,provided a≥0 and b≥0.\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \, \sqrt b, \qquad \text{provided } a \ge 0 \text{ and } b \ge 0 .ab​=a​b​,provided a≥0 and b≥0.

That hypothesis is usually skated over, as though it were a formality. Watch where it enters the proof, because in a moment you are going to remove it and watch the rule die.

If a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0, then ab=ab\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \sqrt bab​=a​b​#

Write p=ap = \sqrt ap=a​ and q=bq = \sqrt bq=b​. These exist as real numbers because aaa and bbb are non-negative, which is the first use of the hypothesis.

By the definition of the principal root, p≥0p \ge 0p≥0 and q≥0q \ge 0q≥0, and also p2=ap^2 = ap2=a and q2=bq^2 = bq2=b. Their product pqpqpq then satisfies two things. Its square is

(pq)2=p2q2=ab,(pq)^2 = p^2 q^2 = ab,(pq)2=p2q2=ab,

and it is non-negative, since p≥0p \ge 0p≥0 and q≥0q \ge 0q≥0 make pq≥0pq \ge 0pq≥0. That is the second use of the hypothesis, and it is the one that matters.

So pqpqpq is a non-negative real number whose square is ababab. By the uniqueness fact established earlier, there is only one such number, and its name is ab\sqrt{ab}ab​. Therefore pq=abpq = \sqrt{ab}pq=ab​, which is exactly ab=ab\sqrt a \sqrt b = \sqrt{ab}a​b​=ab​.

∎

The proof splits into two claims: that pqpqpq squares to ababab, and that pqpqpq is non-negative. The first claim never uses the sign of aaa or bbb at all. The second one does, and it is the claim that identifies pqpqpq as the principal root rather than merely a square root. Pull the hypothesis and the first half of the argument survives while the second half collapses. So the product pqpqpq lands on a square root of ababab, just possibly the wrong one of the two.

That is a prediction, and you have the tools to test it.

Watching the rule fail

Take a=b=−1a = b = -1a=b=−1. Compute both sides.

The left side is easy, and it is entirely real:

ab=(−1)(−1)=1=1.\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{1} = 1 .ab​=(−1)(−1)​=1​=1.

The right side uses the imaginary unit from chapter 5, where −1=i\sqrt{-1} = i−1​=i and i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1:

a b=−1⋅−1=i⋅i=i2=−1.\sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{-1}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = i \cdot i = i^2 = -1 .a​b​=−1​⋅−1​=i⋅i=i2=−1.

One side is 111 and the other is −1-1−1. The product rule is not “risky” here, or “undefined,” or a matter of taste. For a=b=−1a = b = -1a=b=−1 it is false, and you have just checked it with arithmetic you already own.

The diagnosis is exactly what the proof predicted. The number −1-1−1 that the right side produced is a square root of ab=1ab = 1ab=1, because (−1)2=1(-1)^2 = 1(−1)2=1. It is simply the wrong square root: it is the negative one, and x\sqrt{\phantom{x}}x​ is committed to the non-negative one. The hypothesis a,b≥0a, b \ge 0a,b≥0 is what guarantees the product pqpqpq lands on the correct root, and with two negative factors that guarantee is gone.

The failure is not a one-off:

−4⋅−9=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6,while(−4)(−9)=36=6.\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6, \qquad \text{while} \qquad \sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6 .−4​⋅−9​=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6,while(−4)(−9)​=36​=6.

Off by a sign again. With one negative factor there is no problem at all: −4⋅9=(2i)(3)=6i\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{9} = (2i)(3) = 6i−4​⋅9​=(2i)(3)=6i, and (−4)(9)=−36=6i\sqrt{(-4)(9)} = \sqrt{-36} = 6i(−4)(9)​=−36​=6i agrees. It takes two negative factors to break the rule, and two negative factors break it every time, always by a factor of −1-1−1.

The two examples above show the failure happening. The full case-by-case algebra, covering every real aaa and bbb at once, is in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

The practical rule that follows is short, and it is the one to carry into every problem with a negative radicand: convert to iii first, then multiply. Never combine two negative radicands under one radical sign.

Nothing in that proof cared that the index was 222. It needed only that a non-negative radicand has exactly one non-negative nnn-th root, which holds for every nnn, as established earlier. Replace each square root by an nnn-th root and the argument runs word for word, so the rule holds at every index:

abn=an bn,provided a≥0 and b≥0.\sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]{a}\,\sqrt[n]{b}, \qquad \text{provided } a \ge 0 \text{ and } b \ge 0 .nab​=na​nb​,provided a≥0 and b≥0.

So the odd-index product rule ab3=a3 b3\sqrt[3]{ab} = \sqrt[3]{a}\,\sqrt[3]{b}3ab​=3a​3b​ holds for all real aaa and bbb, negatives included. There is no second root competing for the name, so there is nothing to get wrong.

The quotient rule

Division behaves the same way, and the proof is the same argument with a quotient in place of a product. If a≥0a \ge 0a≥0 and b>0b > 0b>0, then a/b\sqrt a / \sqrt ba​/b​ is non-negative and its square is a/ba/ba/b, so it is the principal square root of a/ba/ba/b:

ab=ab,a≥0, b>0.\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt a}{\sqrt b}, \qquad a \ge 0, \ b > 0 .ba​​=b​a​​,a≥0, b>0.

The condition b>0b > 0b>0 is stronger than b≥0b \ge 0b≥0 only because you cannot divide by zero. For an even index, the same statement holds with a non-negative aaa and a positive bbb:

abn=anbn,n even, a≥0, b>0.\sqrt[n]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}}, \qquad n \text{ even}, \ a \ge 0, \ b > 0 .nba​​=nb​na​​,n even, a≥0, b>0.

For an odd index there is no nonnegative-output convention to protect, exactly as with the product rule, so the restriction relaxes to the one thing you can never do, dividing by zero: aaa can be any real number and bbb only has to be nonzero.

abn=anbn,n odd, b≠0.\sqrt[n]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}}, \qquad n \text{ odd}, \ b \ne 0 .nba​​=nb​na​​,n odd, b=0.

Both rules are worth reading in both directions. Left to right, they take a radical apart: 9/25=3/5\sqrt{9/25} = 3/59/25​=3/5. Right to left, they put one together, which is often the faster move:

502=502=25=5.\frac{\sqrt{50}}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{50}{2}} = \sqrt{25} = 5 .2​50​​=250​​=25​=5.

Simplest radical form

Radicals, like fractions, have a standard form, so that two people who simplify the same expression write down the same thing. A radical expression is in simplest radical form when all three of these hold.

  1. No perfect nnn-th power factors remain under the radical. 72\sqrt{72}72​ fails this, because 363636 divides 727272.
  2. No radical appears in a denominator. 12\dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}2​1​ fails this.
  3. The index and the exponents under the radical share no common factor. x46\sqrt[6]{x^4}6x4​ fails this, because 666 and 444 share the factor 222.

Condition 2 is a real condition, but its repair is a technique of its own, and it closes this chapter (Rationalizing and Radical Conjugates). Leave it alone for now; every exercise here is arranged so that conditions 1 and 3 are the whole job.

Answer choices

Check all three conditions against each option. 18\sqrt{18}18​ fails condition 1: the perfect square 999 divides 181818, so it simplifies to 323\sqrt232​. 86\sqrt[6]{8}68​ fails condition 3: the index 666 and the exponent inside, 333 (since 8=238 = 2^38=23), share the factor 333, so it reduces to 2\sqrt22​. 53\dfrac{5}{\sqrt3}3​5​ fails condition 2, a radical in the denominator. Condition 1: extract perfect powers. Factor the radicand, pull out everything that appears as a perfect nnn-th power, and leave the rest inside. Two routes work. Hunt for the largest perfect nnn-th power that divides the radicand, which is fast when you spot it. The other route is to factor into primes and take out one factor for every group of nnn equal ones, which never fails.

Worked example 2 Put 72\sqrt{72}72​ in simplest radical form

Route 1: the largest perfect square. The perfect squares are 1,4,9,16,25,36,49,…1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, \ldots1,4,9,16,25,36,49,…, and the largest one dividing 727272 is 363636, since 72=36⋅272 = 36 \cdot 272=36⋅2. Both factors are non-negative, so the product rule applies:

72=36⋅2=36 2=62.\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{36}\,\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt 2 .72​=36⋅2​=36​2​=62​.

Route 2: prime factorization. If the largest square is not obvious, break the radicand into primes and group them in pairs, since a pair of equal primes is a perfect square:

72=23⋅32=(2⋅2)(3⋅3)(2).72 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 = (2 \cdot 2)(3 \cdot 3)(2).72=23⋅32=(2⋅2)(3⋅3)(2).

Each pair sends one copy of itself outside the radical, and the leftover 222 stays in:

72=2⋅3⋅2=62.\sqrt{72} = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \sqrt 2 = 6\sqrt 2 .72​=2⋅3⋅2​=62​.

Both routes agree, as they must. A quick sanity check: 62≈6(1.414)=8.496\sqrt2 \approx 6(1.414) = 8.4962​≈6(1.414)=8.49, and 8.492≈728.49^2 \approx 728.492≈72.

The answer 626\sqrt262​ is not “less simplified” than 72\sqrt{72}72​ because it looks longer. It is more useful. It separates the exact whole-number part from the irrational part, which exposes that 72\sqrt{72}72​ and 2\sqrt{2}2​ are multiples of the same irrational number. Two expressions written in simplest form can be compared at a glance, and the next lesson turns that into arithmetic.

At a higher index, the grouping size changes but nothing else does. For a cube root you take out one factor per group of three.

Worked example 3 Simplify −54a43\sqrt[3]{-54a^4}3−54a4​, where aaa is any real number

The index is odd, so a negative radicand is fine and no absolute values will appear. Factor the radicand, splitting off perfect cubes:

−54a4=(−27)(2) (a3)(a),-54a^4 = (-27)(2)\,(a^3)(a),−54a4=(−27)(2)(a3)(a),

since −54=−27⋅2-54 = -27 \cdot 2−54=−27⋅2 and a4=a3⋅aa^4 = a^3 \cdot aa4=a3⋅a. Both −27=(−3)3-27 = (-3)^3−27=(−3)3 and a3a^3a3 are perfect cubes, so they come out:

−54a43=−273 a33 2a3=−3⋅a⋅2a3=−3a2a3.\sqrt[3]{-54a^4} = \sqrt[3]{-27}\,\sqrt[3]{a^3}\,\sqrt[3]{2a} = -3 \cdot a \cdot \sqrt[3]{2a} = -3a\sqrt[3]{2a} .3−54a4​=3−27​3a3​32a​=−3⋅a⋅32a​=−3a32a​.

No bars anywhere. a33=a\sqrt[3]{a^3} = a3a3​=a exactly, for every real aaa, because an odd root keeps the sign it was given. Writing ∣a∣|a|∣a∣ here would be an error, not a precaution: at a=−1a = -1a=−1 the original is −543≈−3.78\sqrt[3]{-54} \approx -3.783−54​≈−3.78, and −3a2a3=3−23≈−3.78-3a\sqrt[3]{2a} = 3\sqrt[3]{-2} \approx -3.78−3a32a​=33−2​≈−3.78 matches, while −3∣a∣2a3-3|a|\sqrt[3]{2a}−3∣a∣32a​ would give +3.78+3.78+3.78.

Worked example 4 Simplify 50x3y4\sqrt{50x^3y^4}50x3y4​, where x≥0x \ge 0x≥0

Start with the domain, because it does half the work. The index is even, so the radicand must be non-negative. With x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, every factor here is already non-negative for any real yyy: 50>050 > 050>0, x3≥0x^3 \ge 0x3≥0, and y4≥0y^4 \ge 0y4≥0. So the expression is a real number no matter what yyy is, and that is the hypothesis the rest of this example leans on.

Split each factor into a perfect square times a remainder:

50x3y4=(25⋅2)(x2⋅x)(y4),50x^3y^4 = (25 \cdot 2)(x^2 \cdot x)(y^4),50x3y4=(25⋅2)(x2⋅x)(y4),

so the perfect squares are 252525, x2x^2x2, and y4y^4y4, and what stays behind is 2x2x2x:

50x3y4=25 x2 y4 2x=5⋅∣x∣⋅y2⋅2x.\sqrt{50x^3y^4} = \sqrt{25}\,\sqrt{x^2}\,\sqrt{y^4}\,\sqrt{2x} = 5 \cdot |x| \cdot y^2 \cdot \sqrt{2x} .50x3y4​=25​x2​y4​2x​=5⋅∣x∣⋅y2⋅2x​.

Now clean up the two absolute values that could have appeared. Since x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, ∣x∣=x|x| = x∣x∣=x. And y4=y2\sqrt{y^4} = y^2y4​=y2 needs no bars in the first place, because the exponent coming out is even and y2≥0y^2 \ge 0y2≥0 for every real yyy:

50x3y4=5xy22x.\sqrt{50x^3y^4} = 5xy^2\sqrt{2x} .50x3y4​=5xy22x​.

The general habit is worth naming. Ask what the domain forces before you ask whether you need bars. Bars are needed only for a variable whose sign is genuinely unknown, and an odd power under an even root usually pins the sign down for you.

Condition 3: reduce the index. Rational exponents, from the previous lesson, make this mechanical. Rewrite the radical as a power, reduce the fraction, and translate back.

Worked example 5 Simplify 86\sqrt[6]{8}68​ and 94\sqrt[4]{9}49​

For 86\sqrt[6]{8}68​, write the radicand as a power of 222 and convert to a rational exponent:

86=236=(23)1/6=23/6=21/2=2.\sqrt[6]{8} = \sqrt[6]{2^3} = \left(2^3\right)^{1/6} = 2^{3/6} = 2^{1/2} = \sqrt 2 .68​=623​=(23)1/6=23/6=21/2=2​.

The same move handles 94\sqrt[4]{9}49​:

94=324=32/4=31/2=3.\sqrt[4]{9} = \sqrt[4]{3^2} = 3^{2/4} = 3^{1/2} = \sqrt 3 .49​=432​=32/4=31/2=3​.

For a variable base, keep the base non-negative so the rational-exponent rules apply. With x≥0x \ge 0x≥0,

x46=x4/6=x2/3=x23,\sqrt[6]{x^4} = x^{4/6} = x^{2/3} = \sqrt[3]{x^2},6x4​=x4/6=x2/3=3x2​,

One rule that does not exist

The product and quotient rules distribute a radical over multiplication and division, under the sign conditions above. It is tempting to assume the same courtesy for addition, but no such rule exists. a+b\sqrt{a + b}a+b​ is not a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt ba​+b​, for any aaa and bbb where both sides are defined and unequal to the trivial case. Test it once and the gap is obvious:

9+16=25=5,while9+16=3+4=7.\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5, \qquad \text{while} \qquad \sqrt 9 + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7.9+16​=25​=5,while9​+16​=3+4=7.

Five is not seven. There is no algebra to fix, because there was never a rule here to apply; addition and subtraction simply do not distribute over a radical the way multiplication and division do.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Writing x2=x\sqrt{x^2} = xx2​=x. This is the big one. A square root is never negative, so it cannot equal xxx when xxx is negative. The identity is x2=∣x∣\sqrt{x^2} = |x|x2​=∣x∣, and it costs one pair of bars to be right: at x=−3x = -3x=−3, (−3)2=3\sqrt{(-3)^2} = 3(−3)2​=3, not −3-3−3.
  • Using bars where they do not belong. For an odd index there is no sign to fix: x33=x\sqrt[3]{x^3} = x3x3​=x, bars and all would be wrong. And if the exponent that comes out is even, as in x4=x2\sqrt{x^4} = x^2x4​=x2, the result is already non-negative and the bars are redundant. Bars go on exactly when an odd exponent emerges from an even root taken over an unrestricted variable.
  • Applying ab=ab\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \sqrt bab​=a​b​ with two negative factors. −4⋅−9\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9}−4​⋅−9​ is −6-6−6, not 666. Convert each radical to iii form before multiplying. This rule has a hypothesis, and the hypothesis is load-bearing.
  • Saying x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9 has one solution, or that 9=±3\sqrt 9 = \pm 39​=±3. Keep the two questions apart. The equation has two solutions (333 and −3-3−3); the symbol 9\sqrt 99​ names one number (333). The ±\pm± in the quadratic formula exists to put the second one back.
  • Splitting a sum. There is no rule a+b=a+b\sqrt{a + b} = \sqrt a + \sqrt ba+b​=a​+b​, and it is not close to true: 9+16=25=5\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 59+16​=25​=5, while 9+16=3+4=7\sqrt 9 + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 79​+16​=3+4=7. Radicals distribute over products and quotients under the conditions above; there is no such rule for sums or differences.
  • Stopping too early. 72=218\sqrt{72} = 2\sqrt{18}72​=218​ is true but not simplified, because 181818 still hides the perfect square 999. Take out the largest perfect power, or factor into primes so nothing can hide.

Key takeaways

  • a\sqrt aa​ denotes the principal root: the one non-negative number whose square is aaa. For a>0a > 0a>0, the equation x2=ax^2 = ax2=a has two real solutions, but the symbol names only one of them; for a=0a = 0a=0 there is just the single solution x=0x = 0x=0.
  • x2=∣x∣\sqrt{x^2} = |x|x2​=∣x∣ for every real xxx, and x2=x\sqrt{x^2} = xx2​=x exactly when x≥0x \ge 0x≥0. In general xnn=∣x∣\sqrt[n]{x^n} = |x|nxn​=∣x∣ for even nnn and xnn=x\sqrt[n]{x^n} = xnxn​=x for odd nnn.
  • An odd root accepts any real radicand and keeps its sign; an even root needs a non-negative radicand (over the reals) and returns a non-negative value.
  • Product and quotient rules: abn=anbn\sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]a\sqrt[n]bnab​=na​nb​ and a/bn=an/bn\sqrt[n]{a/b} = \sqrt[n]a / \sqrt[n]bna/b​=na​/nb​ need a≥0a \ge 0a≥0 and b≥0b \ge 0b≥0 (with b>0b > 0b>0 for the quotient) at an even index; at an odd index both hold for every real aaa, with only b≠0b \ne 0b=0 required for the quotient. The even-index hypothesis is load-bearing, not decorative: with both factors negative the product rule is false at a square root, since −1−1=−1\sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1} = -1−1​−1​=−1 while (−1)(−1)=1\sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = 1(−1)(−1)​=1. Convert to iii first, then multiply.
  • Simplest radical form: no perfect nnn-th power factors left inside, no radical in a denominator, and no common factor between the index and the exponents. Reduce that common factor with rational exponents, as in 86=23/6=2\sqrt[6]{8} = 2^{3/6} = \sqrt 268​=23/6=2​.
  • There is no rule for sums. a+b\sqrt{a+b}a+b​ is not a+b\sqrt a + \sqrt ba​+b​.

Why a non-negative number has exactly one non-negative n-th root

A non-negative number has exactly one non-negative nnn-th root#

Fix an integer n≥2n \ge 2n≥2 and a real number a≥0a \ge 0a≥0. That some non-negative real rrr satisfies rn=ar^n = arn=a is a fact about the completeness of the real numbers. As rrr climbs from 000 upward, rnr^nrn climbs continuously from 000 upward and passes through every non-negative value on the way, so it passes through the non-negative number aaa. What needs proving is that it passes through aaa only once.

Suppose two non-negative reals rrr and sss both satisfy rn=sn=ar^n = s^n = arn=sn=a, and suppose they are different. Relabel them if necessary so that r<sr < sr<s. Now use a factorization you met with polynomials:

sn−rn=(s−r)(sn−1+sn−2r+⋯+srn−2+rn−1).s^n - r^n = (s - r)\left(s^{n-1} + s^{n-2}r + \cdots + sr^{n-2} + r^{n-1}\right).sn−rn=(s−r)(sn−1+sn−2r+⋯+srn−2+rn−1).

Look at the two factors on the right. The first, s−rs - rs−r, is positive, because r<sr < sr<s. The second is a sum of products of non-negative numbers, so no term in it is negative, and its leading term sn−1s^{n-1}sn−1 is strictly positive because s>r≥0s > r \ge 0s>r≥0 forces s>0s > 0s>0. A sum of non-negative terms with at least one positive term is positive.

A positive number times a positive number is positive, so sn−rn>0s^n - r^n > 0sn−rn>0, which says sn≠rns^n \ne r^nsn=rn. That contradicts rn=sn=ar^n = s^n = arn=sn=a. No two distinct non-negative reals can share an nnn-th power, so the non-negative nnn-th root of aaa is unique, and calling it the principal root is legitimate.

∎

Why the product rule fails exactly when both factors are negative

Two examples showed the rule failing whenever both aaa and bbb are negative. Here is why that is the only way it fails, for every real aaa and bbb.

Use the chapter-5 convention −c=ic\sqrt{-c} = i\sqrt c−c​=ic​ for c>0c > 0c>0. If at most one of aaa and bbb is negative, say a=−ma = -ma=−m with m>0m > 0m>0 and b≥0b \ge 0b≥0, then

a b=im b=imb,andab=−mb=imb,\sqrt a \, \sqrt b = i\sqrt m \, \sqrt b = i\sqrt{mb}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{-mb} = i\sqrt{mb},a​b​=im​b​=imb​,andab​=−mb​=imb​,

so the two sides agree. But if both are negative, a=−ma = -ma=−m and b=−nb = -nb=−n with m,n>0m, n > 0m,n>0, then

a b=(im)(in)=i2mn=−mn,whileab=mn,\sqrt a \, \sqrt b = (i\sqrt m)(i\sqrt n) = i^2 \sqrt{mn} = -\sqrt{mn}, \qquad \text{while} \qquad \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{mn} ,a​b​=(im​)(in​)=i2mn​=−mn​,whileab​=mn​,

and those two are negatives of each other, never equal (unless both are 000, which two negative numbers cannot be). So ab=ab\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \sqrt bab​=a​b​ holds for every real aaa and bbb except when both are negative, and in that one case it fails by exactly a factor of −1-1−1.

A bit of history (optional)

Here is a mistake worth more than most correct answers. It was printed in 1770, in an algebra textbook by Leonhard Euler, a Swiss mathematician who is still among the most prolific ever to work in the field.

Euler was using the rule you have been using all lesson. The root of a product is the product of the roots. He applied it to two negative numbers, and concluded that −2\sqrt{-2}−2​ times −3\sqrt{-3}−3​ is 6\sqrt66​. By the conventions now standard the answer is −6-\sqrt6−6​, the other square root of the same number. Euler had walked straight into the trap this lesson is built around.

It is not hard to see how. In 1770 nobody had yet insisted that the radical sign must name one particular number. The square root of a negative was still handled on instinct rather than by definition. So the rule looked unconditional, because nothing in the notation announced the hypothesis it was quietly resting on.

That is why the clause “provided a≥0a \ge 0a≥0 and b≥0b \ge 0b≥0” is not textbook throat clearing. It is the whole difference between the answer you now know how to defend and the one that one of the greatest mathematicians in history wrote down.

Literal Equations and Formulas

Intuition

The previous lesson reduced every linear equation to ax=bax = bax=b and read its answer straight off the two numbers aaa and bbb. There is one solution when a≠0a \neq 0a=0, none when a=0a = 0a=0 but b≠0b \neq 0b=0, and every real number when both are zero. That worked because aaa and bbb were numbers you could look at. This lesson asks the same question when aaa and bbb are letters, and one quiet fact changes everything. Which letter you are solving for is a choice you make, not something the equation tells you. In ax=bax = bax=b you might be solving for xxx with aaa and bbb handed to you, or solving for aaa with xxx and bbb handed to you. Those are the same three symbols posing two genuinely different problems. And the moment you divide by the letter aaa to write x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​, you have quietly assumed aaa is not zero. Unlike a number, a letter will never confirm that for you. So before you trust a rearrangement, check whether it divided by something that could be zero. When it did, the honest answer is a short list of cases, not one formula. When it did not, one formula is the whole story.

Learning goals

  • Choose which letter is the unknown and which are parameters
  • Split ax=bax = bax=b into its three parameter cases
  • Check whether a divisor could be zero before dividing, and attach that condition to your answer
  • Factor out the target and put the nonzero condition on the whole factor

The unknown and the parameters

Take the distance formula d=rtd = rtd=rt. Solve it for ttt and you divide by rrr, which needs r≠0r \neq 0r=0, reaching t=drt = \frac{d}{r}t=rd​. Solve the very same formula for rrr instead and you divide by ttt, which needs t≠0t \neq 0t=0, reaching r=dtr = \frac{d}{t}r=td​. Same three letters, two different questions, because a different letter was chosen to be isolated each time.

A literal equation is an equation with more than one letter in it, and d=rtd = rtd=rt is one. You already know the mechanics of rearranging one: isolate the letter you want by undoing the operations around it, treating the other letters as fixed numbers. What d=rtd = rtd=rt shows is the idea this lesson is built on. In any literal equation, one letter is singled out to be solved for, and every other letter stands for a fixed but unstated number. The letter you solve for is the unknown. Each letter held fixed while you solve is a parameter.

Here is the point that is easy to miss: nothing about the symbol aaa makes it a parameter, and nothing about xxx makes it the unknown. The roles are assigned by the question, not carried by the letters. Take the equation ax=bax = bax=b that closed the previous lesson. Solve it for xxx, treating aaa and bbb as parameters, and you divide by aaa, which needs a≠0a \neq 0a=0, to reach x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​. Solve the very same equation for aaa instead, treating xxx and bbb as parameters, and you divide by xxx, which needs x≠0x \neq 0x=0, to reach a=bxa = \frac{b}{x}a=xb​. Same three symbols, two different problems, because a different letter was chosen to be the unknown. Deciding which letter is the unknown is the first move you make, before any algebra happens at all.

By long convention, letters near the start of the alphabet (aaa, bbb, ccc, and often kkk) are used for parameters. By the same convention, letters near the end (xxx, yyy, zzz) are used for the unknown, which is why ax=bax = bax=b reads so naturally as an instruction to solve for xxx. But that convention is only a courtesy to the reader. The mathematics is identical whichever letter you decide to isolate. A good part of this lesson is learning to see the choice rather than let the alphabet make it for you.

Dividing by a letter you cannot inspect

When the coefficient was a number, classifying ax=bax = bax=b took a glance. When the coefficient is a parameter, the same classification is still true, but you can no longer see which case you are in. That single change is what turns solving a literal equation into a case analysis, so it is worth stating the classification carefully with aaa and bbb understood as parameters.

The previous lesson already proved this in full, and nothing about that proof assumed aaa and bbb were literal numbers rather than letters standing for fixed values, so it carries over unchanged. If a≠0a \neq 0a=0, dividing by aaa gives the single solution x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​. If a=0a = 0a=0, the equation collapses to 0=b0 = b0=b: no solution when b≠0b \neq 0b=0, every real number when b=0b = 0b=0. Either a=0a = 0a=0 or it is not, and when a=0a = 0a=0 either b=0b = 0b=0 or it is not, so those three cases are the whole story again here.

What is new is that you cannot carry the classification out by inspection. With a number in front of xxx you simply look and see which case holds. With a parameter you cannot look, because aaa is a letter whose value you were never told. Writing x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​ in one confident line is a bet that you are in the first case, and this lesson is about checking that bet instead of placing it silently.

Check your understanding

You solve ax=bax = bax=b for xxx, and a classmate writes x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​ as the complete answer. For which values of the parameters is that single line the whole story?

Answer choices

Dividing both sides by aaa is legal only when a≠0a \neq 0a=0.

a≠0  ⇒  x=baa \neq 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{b}{a}a=0⇒x=ab​

When a=0a = 0a=0 the expression ba\frac{b}{a}ab​ is undefined, and the equation instead has no solution (when b≠0b \neq 0b=0) or every real number as a solution (when b=0b = 0b=0). So the single line is the whole answer only on the region a≠0a \neq 0a=0.

For which values of the parameter?

The most revealing use of the classification runs the logic backward. Instead of being handed fixed numbers aaa and bbb, you are handed a whole family of equations that depends on a parameter kkk. The question is this: for which values of kkk does the equation have one solution, no solution, or infinitely many? The recipe is always the same. Reduce the equation to the form (coefficient) x=(constant)(\text{coefficient})\,x = (\text{constant})(coefficient)x=(constant), where the coefficient and the constant now depend on kkk, and then apply the trichotomy to those two expressions. The following pair looks almost identical, yet the pair is chosen to show that the coefficient alone does not settle the answer.

Worked example 1 For which kkk does kx+3=2x+kkx + 3 = 2x + kkx+3=2x+k have one, none, or infinitely many solutions?

Treat kkk as the parameter and xxx as the unknown. Collect the xxx terms on the left and the constants on the right:

kx−2x=k−3,(k−2)x=k−3.kx - 2x = k - 3, \qquad (k - 2)x = k - 3.kx−2x=k−3,(k−2)x=k−3.

The coefficient of xxx is k−2k - 2k−2 and the constant is k−3k - 3k−3. Apply the classification to them.

If k≠2k \neq 2k=2, the coefficient k−2k - 2k−2 is nonzero, so there is exactly one solution:

x=k−3k−2.x = \frac{k - 3}{k - 2}.x=k−2k−3​.

If k=2k = 2k=2, the coefficient is zero and the constant is k−3=−1k - 3 = -1k−3=−1, so the equation becomes 0⋅x=−10 \cdot x = -10⋅x=−1. No value of xxx works, and the solution set is ∅\varnothing∅.

Could any kkk give infinitely many solutions? That would need the coefficient and the constant to vanish together, k−2=0k - 2 = 0k−2=0 and k−3=0k - 3 = 0k−3=0 at once, which asks kkk to be both 222 and 333. Impossible. So this family has one solution for every k≠2k \neq 2k=2 and no solution at k=2k = 2k=2, and infinitely many solutions simply never occur.

Worked example 2 For which kkk does kx+6=2x+3kkx + 6 = 2x + 3kkx+6=2x+3k have one, none, or infinitely many solutions?

Again treat kkk as the parameter. Collect the xxx terms and the constants:

kx−2x=3k−6,(k−2)x=3(k−2).kx - 2x = 3k - 6, \qquad (k - 2)x = 3(k - 2).kx−2x=3k−6,(k−2)x=3(k−2).

The coefficient is the same k−2k - 2k−2 as before, but the constant is now 3(k−2)3(k - 2)3(k−2), which carries that same factor.

If k≠2k \neq 2k=2, the coefficient is nonzero, and dividing gives

x=3(k−2)k−2=3.x = \frac{3(k - 2)}{k - 2} = 3.x=k−23(k−2)​=3.

The factor k−2k - 2k−2 cancels, so the solution is x=3x = 3x=3 for every k≠2k \neq 2k=2, the identical value no matter which such kkk you were handed.

If k=2k = 2k=2, the coefficient is zero and the constant is 3(k−2)=03(k - 2) = 03(k−2)=0 as well, so the equation becomes 0⋅x=00 \cdot x = 00⋅x=0, true for every xxx. The solution set is all of R\mathbb{R}R. This time no value of kkk produces “no solution,” because the instant the coefficient k−2k - 2k−2 vanishes, the constant 3(k−2)3(k - 2)3(k−2) vanishes with it.

Put the two families side by side. Their coefficient of xxx is the same expression, k−2k - 2k−2. So the coefficient by itself cannot be what decides the outcome, since one family loses its solution at k=2k = 2k=2 and the other gains a whole line of them. The tiebreaker is the constant at the exceptional value. In the first family the constant is −1-1−1 at k=2k = 2k=2, nonzero, so 0⋅x=−10 \cdot x = -10⋅x=−1 is a contradiction. In the second the constant is 000 at k=2k = 2k=2, so 0⋅x=00 \cdot x = 00⋅x=0 is an identity. That is exactly the fork from the previous lesson: once the coefficient aaa is zero, it is whether b≠0b \neq 0b=0 or b=0b = 0b=0 that separates no solution from every real number. The coefficient tells you when you are standing on the boundary k=2k = 2k=2; the constant at that boundary tells you which way you fall off it.

Check your understanding

For which value of kkk does kx−8=4x−2kkx - 8 = 4x - 2kkx−8=4x−2k have infinitely many solutions?

Answer choices

Collect the unknown on one side and the constants on the other.

kx−4x=8−2k  ⇒  (k−4)x=−2(k−4)kx - 4x = 8 - 2k \;\Rightarrow\; (k - 4)x = -2(k - 4)kx−4x=8−2k⇒(k−4)x=−2(k−4)

Infinitely many solutions need the coefficient and the constant to vanish at once. Both k−4k - 4k−4 and −2(k−4)-2(k - 4)−2(k−4) are zero exactly when k=4k = 4k=4, giving 0⋅x=00 \cdot x = 00⋅x=0. For every other kkk there is the single solution x=−2x = -2x=−2.

When a formula hides a condition

Solving a real formula for one of its variables can produce a condition the original never visibly carried. That condition is not arbitrary, and it is not a ritual to attach to every answer: it shows up only at a step where you divide by an expression that could be zero, the same danger the classification spelled out. Before performing such a step, check whether the expression you are about to divide by could be zero, and name the condition if it can. Naming the condition is not the end of the story, though: the value that zeroes the divisor does not simply vanish from consideration, it sends you back to the ax=bax = bax=b classification, evaluated on the original relation at that value, to find out whether it leaves no solution or every value of the target.

Worked example 3 Solve C=59(F−32)C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)C=95​(F−32) for FFF

It is worth seeing a rearrangement that produces no condition at all, so the restriction never looks like a reflex. The Celsius-to-Fahrenheit relationship is

C=59(F−32).C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32).C=95​(F−32).

Solve for FFF. Multiply both sides by the reciprocal 95\frac{9}{5}59​, then add 323232:

95C=F−32,F=95C+32.\frac{9}{5}C = F - 32, \qquad F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32.59​C=F−32,F=59​C+32.

Every step here either multiplied by the nonzero constant 95\frac{9}{5}59​ or added the constant 323232. Nowhere did we divide by an expression that could be zero, so this rearrangement adds no new condition and the formula works for every temperature. In a rearrangement built only from adding constants and multiplying or dividing, a new condition appears exactly where you divide by an expression that can vanish. Divide by a genuine constant and no new condition appears.

Worked example 4 Solve the thin-lens equation for the image distance vvv

The thin-lens equation from optics ties the focal length fff of a lens to the object distance uuu and the image distance vvv:

1f=1u+1v.\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}.f1​=u1​+v1​.

Solve it for vvv. Isolate the 1v\frac{1}{v}v1​ term by subtracting 1u\frac{1}{u}u1​ from both sides, then combine the right side over a common denominator:

1v=1f−1u=u−ffu.\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{u - f}{fu}.v1​=f1​−u1​=fuu−f​.

The last step is to take the reciprocal of both sides, and a reciprocal exists only when the quantity being flipped is nonzero. So check first: u−ffu=0\frac{u - f}{fu} = 0fuu−f​=0 exactly when u=fu = fu=f. For every other uuu, the reciprocal is defined, and taking it gives

v=fuu−f,u≠f.v = \frac{fu}{u - f}, \qquad u \neq f.v=u−ffu​,u=f.

The original already carries hidden restrictions, since fff, uuu, and vvv all sit in denominators and so must be nonzero. On top of those, the rearranged formula divides by u−fu - fu−f, so it adds the new demand u≠fu \neq fu=f. That extra condition is telling the truth about lenses. An object placed exactly at the focal length, u=fu = fu=f, produces no image at any finite distance, because the rays leave the lens parallel, so there is no vvv to report. The algebra discovered the physics: at u=fu = fu=f the denominator u−fu - fu−f is zero and vvv does not exist.

Worked example 5 Solve the parallel-resistor formula for R2R_2R2​

Two resistors R1R_1R1​ and R2R_2R2​ wired in parallel, both with positive resistance, act like a single resistor RRR given by

R=R1R2R1+R2.R = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}.R=R1​+R2​R1​R2​​.

Solve for R2R_2R2​. Clear the fraction by multiplying both sides by R1+R2R_1 + R_2R1​+R2​, then gather every term containing the target R2R_2R2​ on one side, and factor it out, the collect-and-factor move you already know:

R(R1+R2)=R1R2,R R1=R1R2−R R2,R R1=R2(R1−R).R(R_1 + R_2) = R_1 R_2, \qquad R\,R_1 = R_1 R_2 - R\,R_2, \qquad R\,R_1 = R_2(R_1 - R).R(R1​+R2​)=R1​R2​,RR1​=R1​R2​−RR2​,RR1​=R2​(R1​−R).

Isolating R2R_2R2​ now means dividing by R1−RR_1 - RR1​−R, and that division is valid only when R1−R≠0R_1 - R \neq 0R1​−R=0, that is, R1≠RR_1 \neq RR1​=R. Checking that first,

R2=R R1R1−R,R1≠R.R_2 = \frac{R\,R_1}{R_1 - R}, \qquad R_1 \neq R.R2​=R1​−RRR1​​,R1​=R.

Once more the condition is physically honest: for positive R1R_1R1​ and R2R_2R2​, the combined resistance RRR of a parallel pair is always smaller than either resistor alone. So RRR can never equal R1R_1R1​, and asking for a fixed RRR equal to R1R_1R1​ describes an impossible circuit.

Check your understanding

The sum of an infinite geometric series is S=a1−rS = \dfrac{a}{1 - r}S=1−ra​, valid when −1<r<1-1 < r < 1−1<r<1. Solving it for the ratio rrr gives r=S−aSr = \dfrac{S - a}{S}r=SS−a​. Which nonzero condition does this rearrangement introduce?

Answer choices

Rearrange and watch the divisor.

S(1−r)=a  ⇒  S−Sr=a  ⇒  r=S−aSS(1 - r) = a \;\Rightarrow\; S - Sr = a \;\Rightarrow\; r = \frac{S - a}{S}S(1−r)=a⇒S−Sr=a⇒r=SS−a​

The last step divides by SSS, so the formula r=S−aSr = \frac{S - a}{S}r=SS−a​ is valid only when S≠0S \neq 0S=0. At S=0S = 0S=0 the equation S−Sr=aS - Sr = aS−Sr=a collapses to 0=a0 = a0=a, the familiar fork: no rrr works when a≠0a \neq 0a=0, and every rrr works when a=0a = 0a=0 (still subject to the series' own requirement −1<r<1-1 < r < 1−1<r<1). The series itself already needs −1<r<1-1 < r < 1−1<r<1 to have a sum at all; dividing by SSS is the one new division this rearrangement performs.

The condition belongs to the factor

You already know that when the target appears in several terms you collect those terms, factor the target out, and only then divide. The one thing to get right here is where the nonzero condition lands. It lands on the whole factor you divide by, not on the individual letters inside it.

Worked example 6 Solve ax+b=cx+dax + b = cx + dax+b=cx+d for xxx

The unknown xxx starts on both sides. Collect its terms on the left and the constants on the right:

ax−cx=d−b.ax - cx = d - b.ax−cx=d−b.

Both terms on the left contain xxx, so factor it out. The factor left behind is a−ca - ca−c:

(a−c)x=d−b.(a - c)x = d - b.(a−c)x=d−b.

Now xxx appears once, multiplied by a−ca - ca−c. Dividing by that whole factor is valid only when a−c≠0a - c \neq 0a−c=0, so check that first, then divide:

x=d−ba−c,a≠c.x = \frac{d - b}{a - c}, \qquad a \neq c.x=a−cd−b​,a=c.

The condition is a≠ca \neq ca=c, a single statement about the factor a−ca - ca−c. It is not ”a≠0a \neq 0a=0 and c≠0c \neq 0c=0.” You are dividing by a−ca - ca−c, not by aaa and not by ccc, so the only quantity that must stay nonzero is the difference. Coefficients a=5a = 5a=5 and c=5c = 5c=5 break the formula even though neither is zero, while a=0a = 0a=0 and c=3c = 3c=3 are perfectly fine, because there a−c=−3a - c = -3a−c=−3 is nonzero.

And when the forbidden case does happen, when a=ca = ca=c, the equation has not disappeared; it has returned to the classification. Setting a=ca = ca=c turns (a−c)x=d−b(a - c)x = d - b(a−c)x=d−b into 0⋅x=d−b0 \cdot x = d - b0⋅x=d−b, the familiar fork one more time: no solution when d≠bd \neq bd=b, and every real number when d=bd = bd=b. The nonzero condition on a formula and the boundary case of the case analysis are one fact seen from two sides. Dividing by a−ca - ca−c writes the answer as a formula valid on the region a≠ca \neq ca=c. And the trichotomy takes over precisely on the line a=ca = ca=c, where the formula is not allowed to go.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a letter has a fixed role. Which letter is the unknown and which are parameters is decided by the question, not by the alphabet. The same ax=bax = bax=b can be solved for xxx (giving x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​, needing a≠0a \neq 0a=0) or for aaa (giving a=bxa = \frac{b}{x}a=xb​, needing x≠0x \neq 0x=0).
  • Dividing by an expression without checking whether it could be zero. Writing x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​ as the whole answer to ax=bax = bax=b silently assumes a≠0a \neq 0a=0. The honest answer also covers a=0a = 0a=0, which gives no solution when b≠0b \neq 0b=0 and every real number when b=0b = 0b=0.
  • Putting the condition on the wrong thing. Solving ax+b=cx+dax + b = cx + dax+b=cx+d needs a≠ca \neq ca=c, one condition on the factor a−ca - ca−c, not the two separate conditions a≠0a \neq 0a=0 and c≠0c \neq 0c=0.
  • Attaching a condition to every rearrangement. A restriction appears only where you divide by something that can be zero. The formula F=95C+32F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32F=59​C+32 adds no condition on CCC, because nothing in it can vanish.

Key takeaways

  • In a literal equation you choose one letter to be the unknown; the rest are parameters, fixed but unstated numbers. The choice is yours, not a property of the symbols.
  • Solving ax=bax = bax=b for xxx has no single answer: one solution x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​ when a≠0a \neq 0a=0, no solution when a=0a = 0a=0 and b≠0b \neq 0b=0, and every real number when a=0a = 0a=0 and b=0b = 0b=0. With a number you can see which case you are in; with a parameter you cannot, so carry all three cases.
  • To find which parameter values give one, none, or infinitely many solutions, reduce to (coefficient) x=(constant)(\text{coefficient})\,x = (\text{constant})(coefficient)x=(constant) and apply that same classification. The coefficient marks the boundary, and the constant at the boundary decides the fork.
  • A new nonzero condition appears exactly where a rearrangement divides by an expression that can vanish, such as u≠fu \neq fu=f in v=fuu−fv = \frac{fu}{u - f}v=u−ffu​. A rearrangement that only adds constants or divides by constants, like F=95C+32F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32F=59​C+32, adds no new condition.
  • When the target sits in several terms, factor it out and put the condition on the whole factor: ax+b=cx+dax + b = cx + dax+b=cx+d gives x=d−ba−cx = \frac{d - b}{a - c}x=a−cd−b​ with a≠ca \neq ca=c, and at a=ca = ca=c the trichotomy takes over.

A bit of history (optional)

You cannot reason about ax=bax = bax=b until you can write down the aaa. For most of the history of algebra there was no way to do that. A method was stated in words and shown once on particular numbers, and the reader copied the pattern with fresh ones. The unknown might be granted a letter. A known amount was rarely given one of its own to keep across every equation it appeared in.

François Viète changed that in 159115911591, and this lesson runs on the change. He gave the known quantities letters of their own, so a whole relationship could be set down, held still, and rearranged as a single object.

What he could not give them was freedom. His letters stood for geometric magnitudes, lengths and areas and volumes, and a term had to keep its dimension. A length could be added only to a length, an area only to an area. Nothing in that world stood for zero either, because a length of nothing is not a length at all.

So the branch this lesson turns on was closed to him. He could write the case where you divide by aaa and reach x=bax = \frac{b}{a}x=ab​. He could not write the case where aaa is nothing whatever, and the equation slumps into 0⋅x=b0 \cdot x = b0⋅x=b with either no solution or every real number. The letters arrived first, and permission to ask what happens at zero took generations more. Every time you stop and ask whether aaa could be zero, you are spending a permission he never had.

Linear Inequalities

Intuition

Two lessons ago you pinned down what makes a step on an equation trustworthy. A reversible step on an equation is guaranteed to produce an equivalent equation, one with the identical solution set, since it can neither add a solution nor drop one. Every legal move on an inequality faces the same demand to preserve the solution set, but here reversibility is no longer the test. An inequality also carries an order, a claim about which side is larger, and a step is safe when it keeps faith with that order. That is the real subject of this lesson, so meet the hazard before any rules. Take x<3x < 3x<3, whose solutions are every number below 333, and multiply both sides by xxx. Out comes x2<3xx^2 < 3xx2<3x. That new inequality is true exactly on 0<x<30 < x < 30<x<3: the value x=−1x = -1x=−1 fails it, since 1<−31 < -31<−3 is false, and x=4x = 4x=4 fails it too, since 16<1216 < 1216<12 is false, while x=1x = 1x=1 passes. So every solution at or below 000 has silently disappeared. One clean-looking step destroyed part of the solution set. This lesson is about which steps respect that order and are therefore honest, and why the famous negative flip is forced rather than decreed. The lesson is also about why multiplying by something whose sign you cannot name is not a move at all.

Learning goals

  • Keep the direction under addition or positive scaling, reverse it under negative scaling
  • Derive the reversal from a<ba < ba<b meaning b−a>0b - a > 0b−a>0
  • Refuse to multiply by an expression of unknown sign in one step
  • Split ax>bax > bax>b four ways on the parameters
  • Read and as intersection and or as union

Which moves keep the solution set

Solving an inequality means the same disciplined thing that solving an equation did. Replace the inequality, step by step, with a simpler one that has the identical solution set, until the set can be read off directly. Two inequalities are equivalent when they have exactly the same solution set, and the whole task is to travel through a chain of equivalent inequalities. So the governing question is the one from lesson 1, asked again. Which operations are guaranteed to turn an inequality into an equivalent one? Sorting the operations answers it.

Adding the same expression to both sides, as long as that expression is defined for every xxx, gives an equivalent inequality. Adding slides both sides the same distance in the same direction, so it never changes which side is larger, and the order (and with it the symbol) is preserved. Adding a constant, or a whole term like 3x3x3x, is fine, because each is defined for every real number. Adding something like 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​, undefined at x=0x = 0x=0, would not qualify.

Multiplying or dividing both sides by a positive constant keeps both the solution set and the direction of the symbol. It is reversible, undone by dividing or multiplying by that same positive constant. And scaling by a positive factor stretches or shrinks every distance from zero without ever carrying a number across zero. Because nothing crosses zero, the left-to-right order of the two sides is preserved, so the direction survives.

Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative constant preserves the solution set only if you also reverse the direction of the symbol. This is the move usually handed to students as a rule to memorize, and here is a one-line demonstration that the reversal is not optional. Take x<3x < 3x<3 and multiply both sides by −1-1−1 without touching the symbol, giving −x<−3-x < -3−x<−3. That step is defined for every xxx, and multiplying by −1-1−1 again returns you to x<3x < 3x<3. So the step is perfectly reversible, yet its solution set is every number above 333, while the original was every number below 333. Reversible, and wrong. So reversibility cannot be the standard: the multiplier was negative, and holding the direction fixed silently changed the answer. Reverse the symbol instead, to −x>−3-x > -3−x>−3, and the two sides agree with the original again. The reversal is a forced consequence, not a convention, and the derivation shows exactly why it has to happen.

The same rule solves an ordinary inequality, not just a labeled step. Solve −3x>12-3x > 12−3x>12 for xxx: divide both sides by −3-3−3 and reverse the direction, giving x<−4x < -4x<−4. Check both sides of that boundary: at x=−5x = -5x=−5 the original reads 15>1215 > 1215>12, true, while at x=−3x = -3x=−3 it reads 9>129 > 129>12, false, so the answer really does change sides exactly at −4-4−4. And −4-4−4 itself is not a solution, since it gives 12>1212 > 1212>12, which is false, confirming the flip and why the boundary is not included in the solution set.

Why multiplying by a negative constant reverses the direction#

Suppose a<ba < ba<b, so on the number line aaa sits to the left of bbb. Multiplying every number by −1-1−1 reflects the whole line through 000, sending each point to its mirror image on the far side. A reflection turns left into right, so whichever of aaa and bbb started on the left comes out on the right: their order reverses, giving −b<−a-b < -a−b<−a. That is exactly the picture below.

The same fact confirms itself algebraically. It is enough to understand multiplication by −1-1−1, because any negative constant is −1-1−1 times a positive one, and multiplying by that positive factor is harmless, since a positive scaling keeps the order, as shown above. Saying a<ba < ba<b is the same as saying the gap b−ab - ab−a is a positive number, since bbb lies above aaa. Multiplying aaa and bbb by −1-1−1 produces −a-a−a and −b-b−b, and comparing them through their difference,

(−a)−(−b)=b−a>0,(-a) - (-b) = b - a > 0,(−a)−(−b)=b−a>0,

shows −a-a−a is now the larger of the two, that is −b<−a-b < -a−b<−a again. Nothing was chosen; the flip is forced by the single fact that b−ab - ab−a was positive to begin with.

Dividing by a negative constant is no different, since dividing by −k-k−k with k>0k > 0k>0 is multiplying by the positive number 1k\frac{1}{k}k1​ and then by −1-1−1. Of those two factors only the final −1-1−1 does any flipping, so the direction reverses exactly once.

∎

Multiplying both sides by 000 is a catastrophe of a different kind. It turns any inequality into a statement about 000 and 000 alone: 0<00 < 00<0, 0>00 > 00>0, or 0≠00 \ne 00=0 come out false no matter what xxx was, while 0≤00 \le 00≤0 or 0≥00 \ge 00≥0 come out true no matter what xxx was. Either way, the result no longer remembers anything about the numbers you started with. As with equations, where multiplying by 000 collapsed everything to 0=00 = 00=0, the step keeps no record of the original and cannot be undone.

Now the hazard the opening promised. Multiplying or dividing by a constant is safe once you know its sign: positive keeps the direction, negative reverses it, and only 000 is forbidden. But suppose the thing you multiply by is not a constant at all, but an expression like xxx or x−2x - 2x−2 whose sign changes with xxx. Then you genuinely cannot say what the step does, because the answer depends on a value you have not fixed. Where the expression is positive the direction should stay, where it is negative the direction should flip, and where it is 000 the inequality is destroyed. A single stroke of the pen cannot be all three at once. So multiplying both sides of an inequality by an expression of unknown sign is not a legal single step, not until that sign has been pinned down. It is not one operation but a fork in the road, and writing a single line as though the fork were not there is what loses solutions.

This is the inequality version of the warning from the equation lessons, sharpened. For an equation the fatal question about a multiplier was “can this expression be zero,” because zero was the one value where multiplying could not be undone. For an inequality that question is still fatal, and a second one joins it: “can this expression be negative.” That second question matters because a negative multiplier silently reverses a direction you have left pointing the wrong way. An expression of unknown sign can be negative and can be zero, so it fails both tests at once. That is why an unknown-sign multiplier is doubly barred. The same double failure is why the honest way through, as the rest of the lesson shows, is to split into cases where the sign is known.

Worked example 1 What the opening step destroyed

Return to x<3x < 3x<3 multiplied by xxx. The multiplier xxx has no fixed sign, so the step should have forked. Where x>0x > 0x>0, multiplying keeps the direction and gives x2<3xx^2 < 3xx2<3x; where x<0x < 0x<0, multiplying reverses it and gives x2>3xx^2 > 3xx2>3x; and at x=0x = 0x=0 the inequality 0<00 < 00<0 is simply false. Writing x2<3xx^2 < 3xx2<3x for every xxx quietly used the first branch everywhere, so it can be trusted only where x>0x > 0x>0.

Solving x2<3xx^2 < 3xx2<3x properly confirms exactly the boundary the opening found by testing: the inequality holds precisely on 0<x<30 < x < 30<x<3, the same interval where x=−1x = -1x=−1 and x=4x = 4x=4 failed and x=1x = 1x=1 passed.

Compare the two sets. The original x<3x < 3x<3 has solution set (−∞,3)(-\infty, 3)(−∞,3), while the multiplied inequality has solution set (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3). Every solution with x≤0x \le 0x≤0 was thrown away. Those lost solutions are exactly the values where the multiplier xxx was negative or zero, the very places the single step had no right to speak for. The destroyed solutions were lost at the fork that was never taken.

The parameter case, solving ax > b

Lesson 2 took the equation ax=bax = bax=b, let aaa be a parameter you could not inspect, and found that solving it forced a split into cases at a=0a = 0a=0. The strict inequality ax>bax > bax>b is the direct sequel. Everything from lesson 2 returns, and one thing is added: the sign of aaa now decides not only whether you may divide but which way the symbol points afterward. So the split is no longer just “can I divide” but “divide and keep, divide and flip, or cannot divide at all.”

The four cases of ax>bax > bax>b#

Solve ax>bax > bax>b for xxx, with aaa and bbb fixed but unknown. Everything turns on the sign of aaa.

If a>0a > 0a>0, divide both sides by the positive number aaa. Dividing by a positive keeps the direction, so the solution is x>bax > \frac{b}{a}x>ab​, the interval (ba,∞)\left(\frac{b}{a}, \infty\right)(ab​,∞).

If a<0a < 0a<0, divide both sides by the negative number aaa. Dividing by a negative reverses the direction, so the solution is x<bax < \frac{b}{a}x<ab​, the interval (−∞,ba)\left(-\infty, \frac{b}{a}\right)(−∞,ab​). The flip here is not a rule applied from memory; it is the forced consequence proved above, now triggered because this case placed a negative number under the division.

If a=0a = 0a=0, there is nothing to divide by, and the left side axaxax is 000 for every xxx. The inequality collapses to 0>b0 > b0>b, a statement with no xxx in it, whose truth depends only on bbb. When b<0b < 0b<0, the claim 0>b0 > b0>b is true, so every real number is a solution and the solution set is all of R\mathbb{R}R. When b≥0b \ge 0b≥0, the claim 0>b0 > b0>b is false, so no value of xxx works and the solution set is ∅\varnothing∅.

These cases are exhaustive, because aaa is positive, negative, or zero, and in the zero case bbb is negative or it is not. Notice where the dividing line falls. The boundary sits at b=0b = 0b=0, and b=0b = 0b=0 lands on the empty-set side, because 0>00 > 00>0 is false. This is a genuine difference from the equation ax=bax = bax=b of lesson 2, where the a=0a = 0a=0 fork was b=0b = 0b=0 against b≠0b \neq 0b=0. In lesson 2’s a=0a = 0a=0 case the value b=0b = 0b=0 gave every real number, since 0=00 = 00=0 is true. A strict inequality reads its boundary the other way: b=0b = 0b=0 passes an equals test but fails a strictly-greater test.

∎

The two cases where the boundary itself is a genuine ray are worth seeing side by side, because what separates them is smaller than the algebra suggests. Take ba=−3\frac{b}{a} = -3ab​=−3 and shade the figure below to the right: that is x>−3x > -3x>−3, the answer when aaa is positive. Now change nothing except the side. The boundary has not moved, the circle is still hollow, and the picture is now x<−3x < -3x<−3, the answer when aaa is negative. Dividing by a negative did not compute a different number. It kept the same number and turned the solution set around it inside out.

The illegal move, up close

The unknown-sign warning from the first section has a classic trap all its own. Consider 1x<1\frac{1}{x} < 1x1​<1. One caution before we touch it: this is not a linear inequality, and this section is not here to teach a general method for solving inequalities like it, only to show the illegal move in full daylight and the one habit that fixes it. The tempting step is to clear the fraction by multiplying both sides by xxx, turning 1x<1\frac{1}{x} < 1x1​<1 into 1<x1 < x1<x and concluding x>1x > 1x>1. Test that conclusion against the original at x=−1x = -1x=−1: the original reads 1−1=−1<1\frac{1}{-1} = -1 < 1−11​=−1<1, which is true, so x=−1x = -1x=−1 is a genuine solution, yet x=−1>1x = -1 > 1x=−1>1 is false. The step multiplied by xxx as though xxx were positive, and every negative solution fell through the gap. Multiplying by xxx is the unknown-sign move, and the cure is to split by the sign of xxx, exactly as the refusal rule from earlier in this lesson demands.

Worked example 4 Solve 1x<1\dfrac{1}{x} < 1x1​<1 by cases on the sign of xxx

The denominator forbids x=0x = 0x=0 outright, since 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​ is undefined there, so x=0x = 0x=0 cannot be a solution for any reason. That leaves two honest cases, and in each the sign of xxx is known, so multiplying by xxx is a legal, single-direction step.

Case x>0x > 0x>0. Multiplying both sides by the positive number xxx keeps the direction:

1x<1  ⇒  1<x.\frac{1}{x} < 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 < x.x1​<1⇒1<x.

So x>1x > 1x>1. Every such value is already positive, so the whole ray x>1x > 1x>1 survives this case.

Case x<0x < 0x<0. Multiplying both sides by the negative number xxx reverses the direction:

1x<1  ⇒  1>x.\frac{1}{x} < 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 > x.x1​<1⇒1>x.

This says x<1x < 1x<1, which every negative number already satisfies, so the entire case x<0x < 0x<0 works.

Collect the two cases. The solutions are the negative numbers together with the numbers above 111:

x<0orx>1,x < 0 \quad\text{or}\quad x > 1,x<0orx>1,

that is, the union (−∞,0)∪(1,∞)(-\infty, 0) \cup (1, \infty)(−∞,0)∪(1,∞). The naive step kept only the x>1x > 1x>1 half and lost the whole (−∞,0)(-\infty, 0)(−∞,0) ray, precisely the case where multiplying by xxx should have flipped the symbol.

Compound inequalities as set operations

You already solve compound inequalities mechanically. Here is the single idea that ties them to this lesson. A compound joins two conditions, and each condition has its own solution set; the compound’s solution set is built from those two by a set operation. An and takes the intersection: a value solves ”PPP and QQQ” exactly when it lies in both solution sets, so you keep only what the two share. An or takes the union: a value solves ”PPP or QQQ” when it lies in at least one, so you sweep together everything either piece allows. The chained form a<x<ba < x < ba<x<b is simply the “and” of x>ax > ax>a and x<bx < bx<b, its solution the overlap of two rays.

Seeing a compound as a set operation makes its degenerate answers obvious, and they echo lesson 1’s identity and contradiction exactly. If two “and” conditions share nothing, their intersection is empty and the compound has no solution. That is why a chained statement like 3<x<13 < x < 13<x<1 names no numbers at all: it asks for x>3x > 3x>3 and x<1x < 1x<1 at once. Those two rays (3,∞)(3, \infty)(3,∞) and (−∞,1)(-\infty, 1)(−∞,1) do not overlap, so the intersection is ∅\varnothing∅. A union runs the other way, since two “or” pieces can together cover the whole line, and then every real number is a solution. And if solving either piece cancels the variable and leaves a bare statement, that piece is all of R\mathbb{R}R when the statement is true and ∅\varnothing∅ when it is false. That is the same identity-or-contradiction verdict from the equation lesson, now feeding into the intersection or union.

Worked example 5 An 'and' compound whose pieces do not overlap

Solve x−2>3x - 2 > 3x−2>3 and x+4<5x + 4 < 5x+4<5 together. Solve each piece on its own first. The left piece gives

x−2>3  ⇒  x>5,x - 2 > 3 \;\Rightarrow\; x > 5,x−2>3⇒x>5,

with solution set (5,∞)(5, \infty)(5,∞). The right piece gives

x+4<5  ⇒  x<1,x + 4 < 5 \;\Rightarrow\; x < 1,x+4<5⇒x<1,

with solution set (−∞,1)(-\infty, 1)(−∞,1).

Now intersect, because the two are joined by “and.” A solution must be greater than 555 and less than 111 at the same time, and no number is both, so the intersection is empty. The compound has no solution, ∅\varnothing∅. Written as a chain it would read 5<x<15 < x < 15<x<1, ends pointing in conflict, which is the visible signature of an empty “and.”

For contrast, the same two pieces joined by “or” would ask for x>5x > 5x>5 or x<1x < 1x<1. And their union (−∞,1)∪(5,∞)(-\infty, 1) \cup (5, \infty)(−∞,1)∪(5,∞) is a large set, everything except the band from 111 to 555. The word between the pieces, “and” or “or,” is the entire difference between an empty answer and a nearly full line.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying by the variable to clear a fraction. Multiplying both sides of 1x<1\frac{1}{x} < 1x1​<1 by xxx treats xxx as positive and loses every negative solution, because the multiplier xxx has no fixed sign here. Not every expression holding a variable is like this; x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1, for instance, is positive for every xxx and may be multiplied freely. Check whether the sign of the multiplier is actually known before you multiply, and split into cases only when it is not.
  • Treating the flip as a rule to memorize. Reversing the symbol is not an arbitrary law; it is forced, because a<ba < ba<b makes b−ab - ab−a positive and taking opposites turns that into −b<−a-b < -a−b<−a. If you ever doubt the direction, fall back on that fact rather than a slogan.
  • Flipping for a negative that is not the multiplier. Only the sign of the number you multiply or divide both sides by can flip the symbol. A negative constant sitting elsewhere in the problem, or a subtraction, never does.
  • Forgetting the a=0a = 0a=0 branches of ax>bax > bax>b. Writing only x>bax > \frac{b}{a}x>ab​ or x<bax < \frac{b}{a}x<ab​ quietly assumes a≠0a \neq 0a=0. When a=0a = 0a=0 the inequality is 0>b0 > b0>b, which gives every real number if b<0b < 0b<0 and no solution if b≥0b \ge 0b≥0.
  • Reading a strict boundary like an equals boundary. In the a=0a = 0a=0 branch, ax=bax = bax=b with b=0b = 0b=0 gave every real number, but ax>bax > bax>b with a=0a = 0a=0 and b=0b = 0b=0 gives no solution, because 0=00 = 00=0 is true while 0>00 > 00>0 is false. Switch to the non-strict ax≥bax \ge bax≥b and b=0b = 0b=0 gives every real number again, since 0≥00 \ge 00≥0 is true.
  • Confusing “and” with “or”. An “and” compound is the intersection of the two solution sets, and an “or” compound is their union. A chain like 3<x<13 < x < 13<x<1 is an empty “and,” not a typo to be repaired by swapping the ends.

Key takeaways

  • Two inequalities are equivalent when they have the same solution set, but a safe step is not merely a reversible one: it must respect order. Keep the direction under a strictly increasing operation like adding a constant or scaling by a positive; reverse it under a strictly decreasing one like scaling by a negative.
  • Adding any expression defined for all xxx, and multiplying or dividing by a positive constant, preserve the solution set and the direction. Multiplying or dividing by a negative constant preserves the set but reverses the direction, while multiplying by 000 destroys the inequality.
  • The reversal is a consequence, not a rule: a<ba < ba<b means b−a>0b - a > 0b−a>0, and since (−a)−(−b)=b−a>0(-a) - (-b) = b - a > 0(−a)−(−b)=b−a>0 you get −b<−a-b < -a−b<−a, which is just the reflection of the line through 000.
  • Multiplying by an expression of unknown sign is not a legal move, because it can be positive, negative, or zero. For equations the fatal question was “can it be zero,” and for inequalities “can it be negative” joins it.

A bit of history (optional)

Two of the most familiar marks in mathematics have no certain author.

The signs <<< and >>> reached print in 163116311631, in an algebra book credited to Thomas Harriot, an English mathematician. He had been dead for ten years by then. The later editor, Walter Warner, gathered the loose pages together and saw the book through the press.

That would be a small footnote if the exact signs turned up somewhere in Harriot’s own hand. In the plain, uncrossed shape that reached print, they do not. A large body of his manuscripts survives, and it does not show that pair of marks in that exact printed form. Who settled on that printed pair, a decade after he stopped writing, is unknown. Warner may simply have introduced them himself while preparing the book, and nobody is in a position to rule it out.

You will still find Harriot called their inventor, stated flatly, in textbooks and on websites. The honest version is shorter and less satisfying. The symbols entered mathematics through that one book, and who first drew them is unknown.

Set that beside what this lesson did with the signs. It refused to hand you the reversal as a rule to accept on somebody’s word. It derived the reversal, out of the single fact that a<ba < ba<b makes b−ab - ab−a positive. A mark whose own author is a guess is a poor thing to take on authority, and here you never had to.

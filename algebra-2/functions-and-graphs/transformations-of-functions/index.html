Transformations of Graphs

Intuition

In an earlier course you learned a stack of transformation rules and where each one moves a graph: adding outside slides it up, subtracting inside slides it right, and the inside changes always look backward. You met those as facts to memorize. This lesson does not add new rules; it shows that every one of them comes from the same idea. A transformation is a map applied in exactly one of two places, to the number you feed fff before fff acts, or to the answer fff returns after it acts. That split explains every sign and every direction in this lesson, including why f(2x−3)f(2x - 3)f(2x−3) does not shift by 333 and why the same finished graph can come from more than one transformation.

Learning goals

  • Separate outside moves on the output from inside moves that run backward on the input
  • Apply the master formula to send (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) to its image
  • Factor the inside first, so f(2x−3)f(2x - 3)f(2x−3) shifts by 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​
  • Show that two moves need not commute
  • Read symmetry as a transformation that changes nothing

Two places a number can act

Start with one concrete case. Suppose (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) lies on the graph of y=f(x)y = f(x)y=f(x), and you are told y=f(x−3)+1y = f(x - 3) + 1y=f(x−3)+1. The old input 222 reappears when the inside equals it, so solve x−3=2x - 3 = 2x−3=2 to get the new input x=5x = 5x=5. The old height 555 becomes 5+1=65 + 1 = 65+1=6 once the outside adds to it. So this rule sends the point to (5,6)(5, 6)(5,6): the −3-3−3 changed which input you asked about, and the +1+1+1 changed only the height once you already had an answer. That is the whole lesson in miniature, one number acting on the question you ask fff, another acting on the answer it gives back.

Write a general transformed rule in the shape you will unpack for the rest of the lesson:

y=a f(b(x−h))+k.y = a\,f\big(b(x - h)\big) + k.y=af(b(x−h))+k.

There are exactly two places a number can sit, and they behave completely differently.

The numbers aaa and kkk act on the output, the value fff has already produced. You take the answer f(⋅)f(\cdot)f(⋅), multiply it by aaa, then add kkk. These are the moves that behave the way you expect: multiply the height, then raise it. Call this the outside, the part applied after fff.

The numbers bbb and hhh act on the input, the number handed to fff before fff does anything. The rule first forms x−hx - hx−h, then multiplies by bbb, and only then feeds the result to fff. These are the moves that make the graph go the opposite way from what the symbols seem to say. Call this the inside, the part applied before fff.

Each side applies one step to a number: on the inside you change xxx before handing it to fff, and on the outside you change the number fff hands back. Writing f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)) for “do ggg, then do fff” is the same substitution you practiced in Function Notation, so here we only ever say “apply this, then fff, then that,” one step at a time. The whole lesson is learning to read those two sides correctly.

Why the inside runs backward

Start with the inside, because it is where the confusion lives. Take a pure horizontal shift, g(x)=f(x−h)g(x) = f(x - h)g(x)=f(x−h), and ask not what the formula looks like but where the points actually go.

An inside map moves the graph opposite to its sign#

Let g(x)=f(x−h)g(x) = f(x - h)g(x)=f(x−h). A point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) lies on the graph of ggg exactly when y=g(x)=f(x−h)y = g(x) = f(x - h)y=g(x)=f(x−h), and that says precisely that the point (x−h,y)(x - h, y)(x−h,y) lies on the graph of fff. Read that correspondence starting from fff. Take a point sitting at horizontal position uuu on the graph of fff, namely (u,f(u))(u, f(u))(u,f(u)). It shows up on the graph of ggg at the input xxx where the inside equals uuu, that is where x−h=ux - h = ux−h=u, so x=u+hx = u + hx=u+h. Its height f(u)f(u)f(u) is unchanged. Every point therefore moves from position uuu to position u+hu + hu+h, a slide to the right by hhh when h>0h > 0h>0, even though the formula shows a minus sign.

The same reading handles a pure scaling, g(x)=f(bx)g(x) = f(bx)g(x)=f(bx) with b>0b > 0b>0. Here (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) is on ggg exactly when y=f(bx)y = f(bx)y=f(bx), which says (bx,y)(bx, y)(bx,y) is on fff. A point at position uuu on fff reappears on ggg where bx=ubx = ubx=u, that is at x=ubx = \dfrac{u}{b}x=bu​, again at the same height. So the horizontal position of every point is divided by bbb, which pulls points toward the vertical axis when b>1b > 1b>1 and pushes them away when 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1.

∎

Because this is the move most often read backward, fix the vocabulary now and keep it fixed. The inside factor bbb divides every input. When b>1b > 1b>1 that pulls the graph toward the vertical axis, and we call it a horizontal compression by bbb. When 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1 the inputs grow instead and the graph is stretched away from the vertical axis. Throughout this lesson the number named in a compression is always the input multiplier bbb, never its reciprocal. So “a horizontal compression by bbb” always means “the inputs are divided by bbb.”

Here is the one idea that explains the whole inside, and it is worth saying out loud. An outside map is applied to the answer fff already gave, so it does exactly what it says: multiply the answer, add to the answer. An inside map is applied to the question, the number you are about to hand fff. So to find where a point lands you must ask which xxx makes the inside equal the old input, and answering that means solving for xxx. The solving is what turns “subtract hhh” into “move right by hhh” and “multiply by bbb” into “divide by bbb.”

The master formula

Both sides fold into a single statement about where a point goes. It is the tool the rest of the lesson uses, so derive it once and keep it.

Where every point of the graph lands#

Fix the general rule y=a f(b(x−h))+ky = a\,f\big(b(x - h)\big) + ky=af(b(x−h))+k with b≠0b \ne 0b=0, and take any point (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) on the graph of fff, so v=f(u)v = f(u)v=f(u). We find the point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) it becomes on the new graph.

The new rule reproduces the height v=f(u)v = f(u)v=f(u) when the number handed to fff equals uuu, that is when b(x−h)=ub(x - h) = ub(x−h)=u. This is an equation in xxx, and solving it means dividing by bbb and then adding hhh:

b(x−h)=u ⇒ x−h=ub ⇒ x=ub+h.b(x - h) = u \ \Rightarrow\ x - h = \frac{u}{b} \ \Rightarrow\ x = \frac{u}{b} + h.b(x−h)=u ⇒ x−h=bu​ ⇒ x=bu​+h.

At that input the output is a f(u)+k=av+ka\,f(u) + k = a v + kaf(u)+k=av+k, read straight off the outside. So the point (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) on the graph of fff lands at

(u,v) ⟼ (ub+h,  av+k).(u, v) \ \longmapsto\ \left(\frac{u}{b} + h,\ \, a v + k\right).(u,v) ⟼ (bu​+h, av+k).

∎

Every rule from the earlier course is one line of this formula. Setting a=b=1a = b = 1a=b=1 and h=0h = 0h=0 gives (u, v+k)(u,\, v + k)(u,v+k), a vertical shift by kkk. Setting a=b=1a = b = 1a=b=1 and k=0k = 0k=0 gives (u+h, v)(u + h,\, v)(u+h,v), a horizontal shift right by hhh. Setting b=1b = 1b=1, h=0h = 0h=0, k=0k = 0k=0 gives (u, av)(u,\, a v)(u,av), a vertical scaling by aaa, which also reflects across the xxx-axis when a<0a < 0a<0. Setting a=1a = 1a=1, h=0h = 0h=0, k=0k = 0k=0 gives (ub, v)\left(\tfrac{u}{b},\, v\right)(bu​,v), which divides every input position by bbb (a horizontal compression when b>1b > 1b>1) and reflects across the yyy-axis when b<0b < 0b<0. The one formula carries all of them, and the two halves never mix. The input data uuu is touched only by bbb and hhh, and the output data vvv only by aaa and kkk.

That separation reaches the domain and range too, the data Relations and Functions insisted was part of a function. The inputs uuu of fff become ub+h\tfrac{u}{b} + hbu​+h, so the domain is divided by bbb and shifted by hhh. The outputs vvv become av+ka v + kav+k, so the range is scaled by aaa and shifted by kkk. In general the transformed domain is the set of every value ub+h\tfrac{u}{b} + hbu​+h for uuu in the original domain, and the transformed range is the set of every value av+kav + kav+k for vvv in the original range, the same rule that moves every other point on the graph. When the domain or range is a plain interval, a negative aaa reverses the order of the range’s endpoints, since it turns the larger output into the smaller one, and a negative bbb does the same to the domain’s endpoints.

Order matters, and the two sides disagree

The master formula hides a subtlety that causes more errors than any sign. When a side carries two operations, the order in which you apply them to the graph is not free.

On the outside, the graph order matches the arithmetic order. The rule y=af(x)+ky = a f(x) + ky=af(x)+k says compute f(x)f(x)f(x), then multiply by aaa, then add kkk. As graph moves that reads scale by aaa first, then shift by kkk, in that order, because the arithmetic is done in that order to every height.

On the inside, the graph order is the reverse of the arithmetic order, and the reason is the solving step. The rule y=f(b(x−h))y = f\big(b(x - h)\big)y=f(b(x−h)) processes xxx by first subtracting hhh, then multiplying by bbb, then handing the result to fff. But the master formula shows the point lands at ub+h\tfrac{u}{b} + hbu​+h, which you build from uuu by first dividing by bbb (the horizontal scaling) and then adding hhh (the shift right). Scaling comes first, shift second, the opposite of the arithmetic order. That reversal is forced: to solve b(x−h)=ub(x - h) = ub(x−h)=u for xxx you undo the multiplication before you undo the subtraction, and undoing in reverse is what solving an equation always does.

This reversal is exactly why the inside must be factored into the form b(x−h)b(x - h)b(x−h) before you can read the shift. Skipping that step produces the single most common error in all of this material.

Worked example 3 The factoring trap: reading f(2x - 3)

The rule y=f(2x−3)y = f(2x - 3)y=f(2x−3) is not ”f(2x)f(2x)f(2x) shifted right 333.” To read it correctly, factor the inside so it matches the shape b(x−h)b(x - h)b(x−h):

2x−3=2(x−32).2x - 3 = 2\left(x - \tfrac{3}{2}\right).2x−3=2(x−23​).

Now the slots are visible: b=2b = 2b=2 and h=32h = \tfrac{3}{2}h=23​. So the graph is a horizontal compression by 222 (the inputs are divided by 222) and a shift right by 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​, not by 333.

Check it against the master formula, which never needs the factoring. A point (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) on fff lands where 2x−3=u2x - 3 = u2x−3=u, that is at

x=u+32=u2+32.x = \frac{u + 3}{2} = \frac{u}{2} + \frac{3}{2}.x=2u+3​=2u​+23​.

The shift added to the scaled input is 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​, matching the factored reading. The tempting wrong answer, “shift right 333,” comes from reading the −3-3−3 before dividing out the 222, which reverses the required order of the two inside moves. The inside multiplier 222 has to be pulled out first.

When the order of two moves changes the result

The order rule has a sharp consequence: doing two moves in one order can give a genuinely different function from doing them in the other order.

Take a vertical stretch by 222 and a vertical shift up 333, and apply them to y=f(x)y = f(x)y=f(x) in each order. Stretch first, then shift, and you get 2f(x)2f(x)2f(x) and then 2f(x)+32f(x) + 32f(x)+3. Shift first, then stretch, and you get f(x)+3f(x) + 3f(x)+3 and then 2(f(x)+3)=2f(x)+62\big(f(x) + 3\big) = 2f(x) + 62(f(x)+3)=2f(x)+6. These are not the same rule:

2f(x)+3 ≠ 2f(x)+6.2f(x) + 3 \ \ne\ 2f(x) + 6.2f(x)+3 = 2f(x)+6.

The gap is the extra 333 that the second stretch also multiplied. So “stretch then shift” and “shift then stretch” are two different transformations, and the graphs land in different places.

One graph, many transformations

It is tempting to believe the finished graph tells you which transformation produced it. It does not, and seeing why is one of the honest surprises of this lesson.

Take f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2 and compress it horizontally by 222:

f(2x)=(2x)2=4x2.f(2x) = (2x)^2 = 4x^2.f(2x)=(2x)2=4x2.

Now instead stretch the same fff vertically by 444:

4f(x)=4x2.4f(x) = 4x^2.4f(x)=4x2.

They are the same function, y=4x2y = 4x^2y=4x2, reached by two different transformations. So you may truthfully say ”y=4x2y = 4x^2y=4x2 is y=x2y = x^2y=x2 compressed horizontally by 222,” and equally truthfully say “it is y=x2y = x^2y=x2 stretched vertically by 444.” What you may not say is that the transformation is unique, or that the graph reveals which move was applied. Both descriptions are correct, and the graph cannot choose between them.

This is not special to x2x^2x2: for positive bbb, every pure power function f(x)=xdf(x) = x^df(x)=xd satisfies f(bx)=(bx)d=bdxd=bdf(x)f(bx) = (bx)^d = b^d x^d = b^d f(x)f(bx)=(bx)d=bdxd=bdf(x), so a horizontal compression by bbb can always be redescribed as an outside scaling by bdb^dbd (for example, f(2x)=8f(x)f(2x) = 8f(x)f(2x)=8f(x) when f(x)=x3f(x) = x^3f(x)=x3). Pure power functions are one important family with this property, and adding a constant already breaks it. For f(x)=x2+1f(x) = x^2 + 1f(x)=x2+1,

f(2x)=4x2+1,butc f(x)=c x2+cf(2x) = 4x^2 + 1, \qquad \text{but} \qquad c\,f(x) = c\,x^2 + cf(2x)=4x2+1,butcf(x)=cx2+c

can never equal 4x2+14x^2 + 14x2+1, since matching the x2x^2x2 term needs c=4c = 4c=4 while matching the constant needs c=1c = 1c=1. So here the horizontal compression f(2x)f(2x)f(2x) is not any vertical stretch at all, and the two kinds of scaling are genuinely different operations. The figure shows this second case, where f(2x)f(2x)f(2x) and 2f(x)2f(x)2f(x) are plainly different curves.

Symmetry is a transformation that does nothing

The chapter’s earlier lesson defined a function as even when f(−x)=f(x)f(-x) = f(x)f(−x)=f(x) and odd when f(−x)=−f(x)f(-x) = -f(x)f(−x)=−f(x), both requiring a domain symmetric about 000 so that −x-x−x is available whenever xxx is. Read through the lens of this lesson, those definitions are statements about a single inside transformation, reflecting the input, written y=f(−x)y = f(-x)y=f(−x).

An even function is one that reflecting the input leaves completely unchanged: f(−x)=f(x)f(-x) = f(x)f(−x)=f(x) says the reflected graph y=f(−x)y = f(-x)y=f(−x) is the very same graph as y=f(x)y = f(x)y=f(x). So evenness is precisely invariance of the graph under reflection across the yyy-axis. An odd function is one where reflecting the input has the same effect as reflecting the output: f(−x)=−f(x)f(-x) = -f(x)f(−x)=−f(x) says the inside reflection y=f(−x)y = f(-x)y=f(−x) equals the outside reflection y=−f(x)y = -f(x)y=−f(x). Doing both at once, reflecting input and output together, is a 180∘180^\circ180∘ rotation about the origin, and it returns the original graph, since −f(−x)=f(x)-f(-x) = f(x)−f(−x)=f(x). So oddness is invariance of the graph under that rotation.

Both statements need the symmetric domain to even be asked, exactly as the earlier lesson proved, and neither says more than it should. In particular an odd function need not pass through the origin: it does when 000 is in its domain, because then f(0)=−f(0)f(0) = -f(0)f(0)=−f(0) forces f(0)=0f(0) = 0f(0)=0. But f(x)=1xf(x) = \tfrac{1}{x}f(x)=x1​ is odd with 000 absent from its domain, and it never meets the origin. Symmetry, in this chapter’s language, is just the special case where a particular transformation fixes the graph.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading a horizontal shift without factoring. y=f(2x−3)y = f(2x - 3)y=f(2x−3) is not ”f(2x)f(2x)f(2x) shifted right 333.” Factor the inside as 2(x−32)2\left(x - \tfrac{3}{2}\right)2(x−23​): the shift is right 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​, because the factor bbb must be pulled out before the shift hhh is visible.
  • Applying the inside moves in arithmetic order. On the inside the graph order is the reverse of the arithmetic order, since finding where a point lands means solving the inside for xxx. Scale first, then shift, the opposite of “subtract then multiply.”
  • Assuming two moves commute. 2f(x)+32f(x) + 32f(x)+3 (stretch then shift) and 2(f(x)+3)=2f(x)+62(f(x) + 3) = 2f(x) + 62(f(x)+3)=2f(x)+6 (shift then stretch) are different functions. Reordering a stretch and a shift, or a reflection and a shift, generally changes the result.
  • Believing the graph names its transformation. For f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2, the graph y=4x2y = 4x^2y=4x2 is both a horizontal compression by 222 and a vertical stretch by 444. The finished graph does not reveal which was applied, so never claim the transformation is unique.
  • Misreading symmetry. Testing even or odd requires −x-x−x to be in the domain whenever xxx is; on an unsymmetric domain the question cannot be asked at all. And an odd function passes through the origin only when 000 is in its domain: f(x)=1xf(x) = \tfrac{1}{x}f(x)=x1​ is odd yet never meets the origin.

Key takeaways

  • Every transformation acts in one of two places: the outside (aaa, kkk) on the output after fff, acting exactly as written, or the inside (bbb, hhh) on the input before fff, which runs backward because finding where a point lands means solving the inside for xxx.
  • Master formula: under y=a f(b(x−h))+ky = a\,f\big(b(x - h)\big) + ky=af(b(x−h))+k, a point (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) on fff lands at (ub+h, av+k)\left(\tfrac{u}{b} + h,\ a v + k\right)(bu​+h, av+k). The domain is divided by bbb and shifts by hhh; the range scales by aaa and shifts by kkk.
  • Order matters, and factoring reveals it. Outside moves apply in arithmetic order (scale then shift); on the inside you must factor to the form b(x−h)b(x - h)b(x−h) first, because the graph applies the two moves in the reverse order, scale then shift, not “subtract then multiply.”
  • Two moves need not commute, on the outside or the inside: 2f(x)+3≠2(f(x)+3)2f(x) + 3 \ne 2(f(x) + 3)2f(x)+3=2(f(x)+3), and a reflection combined with a shift disagrees about order the same way.
  • A graph does not determine its transformation. For f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2, y=4x2y = 4x^2y=4x2 is both f(2x)f(2x)f(2x) and 4f(x)4f(x)4f(x); pure power functions are one family with this property, and it fails for x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1.
  • Symmetry is invariance: on a domain symmetric about 000, fff is even exactly when reflecting the input fixes the graph (f(−x)=f(x)f(-x) = f(x)f(−x)=f(x)). On that same domain, fff is odd exactly when reflecting the input matches reflecting the output (f(−x)=−f(x)f(-x) = -f(x)f(−x)=−f(x)).

A bit of history (optional)

By the middle of the nineteenth century geometry had come apart into rival versions, school geometry, projective geometry, and others, each with its own theorems and no shared account of what made them all geometry. Felix Klein answered the question in 1872, newly a professor at Erlangen. His answer moved the subject off the figures and onto the motions: choose a set of transformations that can be undone and combined, and a geometry is the study of whatever that set leaves unchanged. Change the set and you change the subject.

The Erlangen Programme, as it came to be called, is this lesson raised one level: a transformation is a map applied before fff or after it, and two of them combine by doing one and then the other. You have already met the smallest case of Klein’s question. An even function is a graph left unchanged by reflecting the input; an odd function is a graph left unchanged by a half turn about the origin. Symmetry, in Klein’s language, is the case where a transformation changes nothing.

Systems in Three Variables

Intuition

Take three equations in three unknowns, such as x+y+z=4x + y + z = 4x+y+z=4, x+2y+3z=5x + 2y + 3z = 5x+2y+3z=5, and 2x+3y+z=122x + 3y + z = 122x+3y+z=12. A solution is a triple (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z) that makes all three equations true at once, and the goal is to find every such triple. You can already eliminate a variable to drop to a smaller system, solve that, then substitute back; this lesson sharpens the question. What does the whole set of solutions look like, and why does the arithmetic always land on one of a short list of answers? In two variables one equation drew a line; in three it draws a flat sheet, a plane. A system of three such equations is three planes in space, and a solution is a point that lies on all three. Three planes can cross at one point, share a whole line, coincide on a single sheet, or miss each other entirely. The elimination routine below finds the solution and tells you, from the arithmetic alone, which of those four outcomes you are in.

Learning goals

  • Picture each equation as a plane in three-dimensional space
  • Eliminate one variable at a time to reach triangular form, then back-substitute
  • Tell a false row (no solution) apart from a vanishing row (a free variable)
  • Sort the outcome into a point, a line, a plane, or no solution
  • Parameterize a line or a plane with one letter per free variable

From a line to a plane

A linear equation in three variables has the standard form

ax+by+cz=d,ax + by + cz = d,ax+by+cz=d,

with numbers aaa, bbb, ccc, ddd and at least one of aaa, bbb, ccc nonzero, and each variable to the first power only. Its solutions are ordered triples (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z), three numbers at once. Compare the counting. In two variables an equation ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c leaves one degree of freedom: solve for whichever variable has a nonzero coefficient, and the other is free to be anything, so the solutions sweep out a line. (If b≠0b \neq 0b=0, that means picking xxx freely and solving for yyy.) In three variables an equation ax+by+cz=dax + by + cz = dax+by+cz=d leaves two degrees of freedom: solve for one variable using whichever coefficient is nonzero, and the other two are free, so the solutions sweep out a two-dimensional sheet, a plane. (If c≠0c \neq 0c=0, that means picking xxx and yyy freely and solving for zzz.) Going up one variable lifts the picture up one dimension.

A system of three linear equations stacks three of these and asks for the triples satisfying all three at once. Geometrically it stacks three planes and asks which points lie on every one. That is why solving a three-variable system is the study of how planes can intersect.

The elimination algorithm: reduce to triangular form

You met elimination as a way to cancel a variable by adding or subtracting equations. The previous lessons also proved the key fact we lean on: replacing one equation by itself plus a multiple of another changes nothing about the solution set. The upgrade here is to stop combining pairs at random and instead aim at a target shape. Use the first equation to remove xxx from the other two, then use the new second equation to remove yyy from the third. This only works if the equation you are eliminating with actually contains the variable you are clearing; if it does not, for instance the first equation happens to have no xxx term, swap it with a lower equation that does. Swapping the order of two equations changes nothing about the solution set, so it is always allowed. What remains is a triangular (staircase) system: the first equation still carries all three unknowns, the second carries only two, the third carries only one.

The bottom equation has one unknown, so you solve it outright. Carry that value up into the second equation and solve for its second unknown. Carry both up into the first equation and solve for the last. This forward sweep to triangular form, then the climb back up, is a fixed procedure you can run without inventing anything. Two lessons from now you will drop the letters and record only the coefficients in a rectangular array called a matrix, and this identical sweep becomes a purely mechanical routine. For now keep the letters, so the reasoning stays visible.

A word problem in three unknowns

Three unknowns generally need three independent facts to pin down one value for each, and each fact becomes one equation. Independent just means each fact adds real information: repeating something you already know does not count as a fourth fact. The elimination routine solves the resulting system the same way, whether the third fact is a full weighted total or a plain relationship between the unknowns.

Worked example 2 A three-way coffee blend

A roaster builds a 121212-pound blend from three coffees. The three coffees are a house roast at 666 dollars per pound, a dark roast at 999 dollars per pound, and a decaf at 121212 dollars per pound. The blend costs 102102102 dollars in all, and the roaster uses as much house roast as dark and decaf combined. How many pounds of each go in?

Let hhh, ddd, and kkk be the pounds of house, dark, and decaf. The three facts become

(1) h+d+k=12,(2) 6h+9d+12k=102,(3) h=d+k.(1)\ h + d + k = 12, \qquad (2)\ 6h + 9d + 12k = 102, \qquad (3)\ h = d + k.(1) h+d+k=12,(2) 6h+9d+12k=102,(3) h=d+k.

Divide equation (2)(2)(2) by 333 to shrink the numbers, and rewrite equation (3)(3)(3) with all terms on one side:

(2′) 2h+3d+4k=34,(3′) h−d−k=0.(2')\ 2h + 3d + 4k = 34, \qquad (3')\ h - d - k = 0.(2′) 2h+3d+4k=34,(3′) h−d−k=0.

Use (3′)(3')(3′) to clear hhh. Subtract (3′)(3')(3′) from (1)(1)(1), and subtract 222 times (3′)(3')(3′) from (2′)(2')(2′):

(1)−(3′): 2d+2k=12 ⇒ d+k=6,(2′)−2(3′): 5d+6k=34.(1) - (3'): \ 2d + 2k = 12 \ \Rightarrow \ d + k = 6, \qquad (2') - 2(3'): \ 5d + 6k = 34.(1)−(3′): 2d+2k=12 ⇒ d+k=6,(2′)−2(3′): 5d+6k=34.

(5d+6k)−5(d+k)=34−30,k=4.(5d + 6k) - 5(d + k) = 34 - 30, \qquad k = 4.(5d+6k)−5(d+k)=34−30,k=4.

6(6)+9(2)+12(4)=36+18+48=102 ✓.6(6) + 9(2) + 12(4) = 36 + 18 + 48 = 102 \ \checkmark.6(6)+9(2)+12(4)=36+18+48=102 ✓.

The blend takes 666 pounds of house roast, 222 pounds of dark, and 444 pounds of decaf.

The four possible outcomes

Every example so far ended at one point, but that is only the most common ending. Recall the two-variable result from the previous lesson: a system there is consistent (has a solution) or inconsistent (none). A consistent system is in turn either independent (exactly one solution) or dependent (infinitely many). The infinite case had a single shape, a whole line where two equations described the same line. Space adds a dimension, and with it a new possibility. A dependent system in three variables can leave the solutions filling a line or filling an entire plane, so the catalog grows from three entries to four.

How do you tell which of the four you are looking at, just from the arithmetic? Read the triangular form. The next three examples work out a line, a false row, and a plane in turn; after that we can explain why these four outcomes, and no others, are the only ones possible.

Degenerate systems and how to read them

When the sweep ends with anything other than one value pinned down for each variable, the arithmetic tells you which shape you have. A false row (0=k0 = k0=k with k≠0k \neq 0k=0) means no solution, and it overrides everything else. If no row is false, then a vanishing row (0=00 = 00=0) is a redundant constraint, so a variable is free and you parameterize. To parameterize, name each free variable with a letter and solve for the rest in terms of it.

Worked example 3 A vanishing row: the solutions form a line

Solve

(1) x+3y+z=3,(2) −x+y+z=1,(3) −x+5y+3z=5.(1)\ x + 3y + z = 3, \qquad (2)\ -x + y + z = 1, \qquad (3)\ -x + 5y + 3z = 5.(1) x+3y+z=3,(2) −x+y+z=1,(3) −x+5y+3z=5.

Worked example 4 A false row: no solution

Solve

(1) x+y+2z=4,(2) 2x+3y+z=5,(3) 3x+4y+3z=12.(1)\ x + y + 2z = 4, \qquad (2)\ 2x + 3y + z = 5, \qquad (3)\ 3x + 4y + 3z = 12.(1) x+y+2z=4,(2) 2x+3y+z=5,(3) 3x+4y+3z=12.

(2)−2(1): y−3z=−3,(3)−3(1): y−3z=0.(2) - 2(1): \ y - 3z = -3, \qquad (3) - 3(1): \ y - 3z = 0.(2)−2(1): y−3z=−3,(3)−3(1): y−3z=0.

0=3.0 = 3.0=3.

This row is false, so no triple satisfies all three equations. The system is inconsistent, and the three planes enclose space without ever sharing a common point.

Check your understanding

Elimination on a three-equation system produces the rows x+y+z=2x + y + z = 2x+y+z=2, y−z=1y - z = 1y−z=1, and 0=50 = 50=5. What is the solution set?

Answer choices

The row 0=50 = 50=5 is false: no triple can make 000 equal 555. A false row means the system has no solution, and it overrides every other row, even if another row happened to vanish too. Once you find one false row, you never need to look at the rest.

Worked example 5 Two vanishing rows: the solutions form a plane

Solve

(1) 2x−y+z=3,(2) −4x+2y−2z=−6,(3) 6x−3y+3z=9.\begin{aligned} (1)\ 2x - y + z &= 3, \\ (2)\ -4x + 2y - 2z &= -6, \\ (3)\ 6x - 3y + 3z &= 9. \end{aligned}(1) 2x−y+z(2) −4x+2y−2z(3) 6x−3y+3z​=3,=−6,=9.​

Look before computing. Equation (2)(2)(2) is exactly −2-2−2 times equation (1)(1)(1), and equation (3)(3)(3) is exactly 333 times equation (1)(1)(1). Elimination confirms it: adding 222 times (1)(1)(1) to (2)(2)(2) gives 0=00 = 00=0, and subtracting 333 times (1)(1)(1) from (3)(3)(3) gives 0=00 = 00=0. Both extra rows vanish, so only one real constraint survives. With two variables left undetermined, the solution set is a whole plane, namely the single plane 2x−y+z=32x - y + z = 32x−y+z=3 that all three equations describe.

Parameterize with two letters. Solve for yyy, whose coefficient is −1-1−1 so no fractions appear, and let the other two run free as x=sx = sx=s and z=tz = tz=t:

(x,y,z)=(s, 2s+t−3, t),(x, y, z) = (s,\ 2s + t - 3,\ t),(x,y,z)=(s, 2s+t−3, t),

one triple for each pair (s,t)(s, t)(s,t). Two free letters sweep out a two-dimensional sheet, which is exactly what it means for the answer to be a plane.

Why exactly these four?

Why exactly these four outcomes, and never a stray answer like two points? The triangular form settles it. The count that matters is how many rows out of the triangular system still carry real information, that is, how many did not vanish into 0=00 = 00=0.

Why the solution set is a point, a line, a plane, or nothing#

Run the forward elimination until the system is triangular, exactly as in the examples above. Each step replaces one equation by itself plus a multiple of another, and the earlier lessons proved that such a step neither loses a solution nor invents one. So the triangular system has exactly the solutions the original did. Read it from the bottom up.

If any row comes out false (0=k0 = k0=k with k≠0k \neq 0k=0), no triple can satisfy it, so the solution set is empty. That case is done; assume from here that no row is false.

Otherwise, count the surviving equations, the rows that did not vanish into 0=00 = 00=0. Each surviving equation pins down one variable once the variables below it are known, the same way back-substitution worked in every worked example above. Every variable that no surviving equation pins down is free: you may give it any value you like, and each choice produces a different solution.

Surviving equationsFree variablesSolution set
30a single point
21a line (one parameter)
12a plane (two parameters)

A count of 000 surviving equations would need all three original equations to vanish, which cannot happen for a genuine system of three equations. So a consistent system always lands in one of the three rows above, and together with the empty case, the complete catalog is point, line, plane, or nothing. It can never be exactly two solutions, or any other finite count past one, because as soon as one variable is free, running it through infinitely many values produces infinitely many solutions.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Eliminating a different variable in the two pairs. Aim the whole forward sweep at one variable first. If you clear xxx from the second equation but yyy from the third, the two rows that are left carry different pairs of unknowns, so they are not yet the matching two-variable system the next step needs.
  • Reading 0=00 = 00=0 as no solution, or 0=k0 = k0=k as infinitely many. A vanishing row (0=00 = 00=0) is a redundant equation: provided no row is false, it frees a variable and the system has infinitely many solutions. A false row (0=k0 = k0=k with k≠0k \neq 0k=0) means none, and it wins even if another row vanished. Keep the two straight.
  • Confusing a line of solutions with a plane of solutions. One vanishing row leaves two constraints and one free variable, a line. Two vanishing rows leave one constraint and two free variables, a plane. Count the free variables, not just whether the answer is “infinitely many.”
  • Reporting a plane or line as if it were a single triple. When the answer is infinite, you must parameterize: introduce ttt (or sss and ttt) and give the full family like (t/2, 1−t/2, t)(t/2,\ 1 - t/2,\ t)(t/2, 1−t/2, t), not one sample point.
  • Stopping at two coordinates. The sweep hands you the bottom variable, then the middle one. Climb all the way to the top equation and solve for the last unknown before stating the triple.
  • Checking in only the equation you set aside. A slip can occur in any step, not just the one you set aside last. Checking that one equation, as most examples above do, catches many arithmetic errors, but substituting into all three original equations, as Worked Example 1 does, is the surest way to catch a slip anywhere in the sweep. For a parameterized line or plane, confirm algebraically that each original equation holds for every value of the parameter.

Key takeaways

  • A linear equation ax+by+cz=dax + by + cz = dax+by+cz=d has a plane of solutions, so a three-equation system is three planes and a solution is a point on all three.
  • Solve by forward elimination to triangular form, then back-substitute. Clear xxx from the lower two equations, clear yyy from the bottom one, solve the single remaining equation, and climb back up.
  • The solution set is exactly one of four shapes: a point (three surviving equations), a line (two surviving equations, one free variable), a plane (one surviving equation, two free variables), or empty. It can never be two points or any finite count past one.
  • The algebra announces the shape. Unique values mean a point, and a false row 0=k0 = k0=k means empty. If no row is false, a vanishing row 0=00 = 00=0 is a redundant equation that frees a variable, so you parameterize the line or plane.
  • To parameterize, name each free variable with a letter and solve each surviving equation for one variable, giving a family like (t/2, 1−t/2, t)(t/2,\ 1 - t/2,\ t)(t/2, 1−t/2, t) for a line or (s, 2s+t−3, t)(s,\ 2s + t - 3,\ t)(s, 2s+t−3, t) for a plane.

A bit of history (optional)

A surveyor measures the same corner of a field again and again, and the readings never quite agree, since no instrument is perfect. Fitting one set of numbers to every reading at once means solving a large system of linear equations, and for a century that work was done by hand.

Wilhelm Jordan wrote the handbook German surveyors worked from, and his 1888 edition laid elimination out as a fixed routine with a set order and a place on the page for every step. His one change is worth knowing: instead of stopping once the system is triangular and climbing back up, he kept clearing, removing each variable from the rows above it too. For a system with a single solution, that leaves every row reading one variable equals one number directly, with nothing left to climb; a degenerate system still simplifies the same way, it just keeps whatever zero rows, false rows, or free variables the sweep already found. That version, now called Gauss-Jordan elimination, trades a little extra arithmetic in the middle for none at the end when there is a unique solution to find. Your forward sweep above is the first half of it; back-substitution is the half a surveyor’s routine wrote out of the job.

Matrices and Systems of Equations Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

When you solved a system by elimination, you wrote the variables again and again, but look closely at what your pencil actually did. You scaled equations and added them, and the letters xxx and yyy just came along for the ride. The arithmetic happened entirely on the coefficients. A matrix is what you get when you stop copying the letters and keep only the grid of numbers. It is bookkeeping, a way to run elimination without rewriting xxx and yyy on every line. That is the whole idea, and everything in this lesson is elimination you already know, wearing a tidier coat.

Learning goals

  • Write an augmented matrix with the variables stripped away
  • Apply the three row operations, each reversible
  • Reduce to row-echelon form, then back-substitute
  • Read a contradiction row as no solution
  • Use a pivot in every variable column to tell one solution from infinitely many

From equations to a grid of numbers

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, written inside square brackets. Nothing more mysterious than that. Here is one with two rows and three columns:

[238547]\left[\begin{array}{ccc} 2 & 3 & 8 \\ 5 & 4 & 7 \end{array}\right][25​34​87​]

The reason a matrix is useful right now is that a system of linear equations is already a grid of numbers in disguise. Take the system

2x+3y=8,5x+4y=7.\begin{aligned} 2x + 3y &= 8, \\ 5x + 4y &= 7. \end{aligned}2x+3y5x+4y​=8,=7.​

Line up the variables in the same order in every equation, keep the constants on the right, and read off the coefficients row by row. The two coefficients on the left of each equation become the first two columns, and the constant becomes a third column. To remember that the third column plays a different role, we draw a vertical bar in front of it. The result is the augmented matrix of the system:

[238547]\left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 2 & 3 & 8 \\ 5 & 4 & 7 \end{array}\right][25​34​87​]

The bar stands for the equals signs. Everything left of it is a coefficient of a variable; the single column to its right holds the constants. Drop the bar and the first two columns on their own form the coefficient matrix. The augmented matrix carries strictly more information, because it remembers the right-hand sides too, which is why it is the one we reduce.

Two small conventions keep the translation honest. A variable that is written with no number in front of it has coefficient 111, so x−y=1x - y = 1x−y=1 becomes the row [ 1 −1∣1 ][\,1 \ {-1} \mid 1\,][1 −1∣1], coefficients and constant together. A variable that is missing from an equation has coefficient 000. That 000 is not optional: it holds the column open so the other coefficients stay in their correct places, exactly the placeholder job a zero does in place value. Write x+z=4x + z = 4x+z=4 inside a three-variable system and its row is [ 1 0 1∣4 ][\,1 \ 0 \ 1 \mid 4\,][1 0 1∣4], with the 000 marking the absent yyy.

Stacking the rows, keeping x,y,zx, y, zx,y,z in the same three columns and the constants in a final column, gives

[10152−134]\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 1 & 5 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 & 4 \end{array}\right][12​0−1​13​54​]

The second option drops the placeholder 000 for the missing yyy and lets the zzz-coefficient slide into yyy's column instead, exactly the slip the Common mistakes section warns about. The third flips the sign of yyy's coefficient in row 222. The fourth writes the constants first instead of last.

Dimensions and entries

A matrix with mmm rows and nnn columns is called an m×nm \times nm×n matrix (say ”mmm by nnn”), always rows first. The augmented matrix above is 2×32 \times 32×3. To point at one number inside a matrix AAA, we name its row and its column: the entry in row iii and column jjj is written aija_{ij}aij​. That notation uses the same letter as the matrix, but lowercase, with the row number first and the column number second. So in

A=[238547],A = \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 2 & 3 & 8 \\ 5 & 4 & 7 \end{array}\right],A=[25​34​87​],

the entry a11=2a_{11} = 2a11​=2 sits in the top left, a13=8a_{13} = 8a13​=8 is the first constant, and a21=5a_{21} = 5a21​=5 is the coefficient of xxx in the second equation. Row first, column second, every time. That is the entire vocabulary you need; matrices carry much more machinery in later chapters, but here the matrix exists only to organize elimination, so this is where we stop.

The three row operations

Elimination lets you do three things to a system without changing which values of the variables solve it. Written on the augmented matrix, where each row is one equation, they become the three row operations:

  1. Swap two rows. (Ri↔RjR_i \leftrightarrow R_jRi​↔Rj​.) You are just listing the equations in a different order.
  2. Multiply a row by a nonzero constant. (Ri→kRiR_i \to kR_iRi​→kRi​ with k≠0k \ne 0k=0.) You are scaling one whole equation, both sides at once.
  3. Add a multiple of one row to another. (Ri→Ri+kRjR_i \to R_i + kR_jRi​→Ri​+kRj​.) This is the elimination step itself: you add a chosen multiple of one equation to a second so that a variable cancels in the second.

These are the only moves you are allowed, and every one of them is reversible, which is the property that matters. Swapping back undoes a swap; dividing by kkk undoes a multiply; subtracting the same multiple undoes an addition. Reversibility is what protects the whole system, together, not row by row: if a set of values satisfies every equation before the move, it still satisfies every equation after, and if it satisfies every equation after the move, undoing the move recovers the original system, so those values must have solved that too. Nothing gets in or out of the solution set either way. Multiplying by 000 is the one move left out, because it is not reversible: it turns an equation into 0=00 = 00=0, which is true no matter what, and there is no way back to what the row used to say.

The paragraph above gives the reason in plain words. The Go deeper section checks it equation by equation, including exactly why the third operation cannot lose or gain a solution.

k≠0 is required for Ri→kRik \ne 0 \text{ is required for } R_i \to kR_ik=0 is required for Ri​→kRi​

Swapping rows and adding a multiple of one row to another are always legal, and multiplying by −2-2−2 is fine because −2≠0-2 \ne 0−2=0.

Row-echelon form and back-substitution

The goal of the row operations is to reach row-echelon form, the matrix version of the triangular shape you drove systems toward in the previous lessons. The leading entry of a row is its first nonzero number, and we call it a pivot. A matrix is in row-echelon form when each pivot sits strictly to the right of the pivot in the row above, and any all-zero rows lie at the bottom. For a square system with a single solution this makes a clean staircase: the entries below the diagonal are all zero.

Once the matrix is triangular, you translate the bottom row back into an equation and solve it, then work upward. Each row you climb into has only one new variable in it. The variables to the right of that row’s pivot were already pinned down by the rows below. The entries to the left of the pivot are zero, by the staircase shape itself. So you substitute the values you already have and solve for that one remaining variable. Working from the bottom up like this is called back-substitution, and it is the reason the triangular shape is worth chasing.

Row reduction in action

Worked example 1 Solve a 2×22\times22×2 system by row reduction

Solve the system

x+2y=5,3x−y=1.\begin{aligned} x + 2y &= 5, \\ 3x - y &= 1. \end{aligned}x+2y3x−y​=5,=1.​

Write the augmented matrix, keeping the variables in the order x,yx, yx,y:

[1253−11]\left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 3 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right][13​2−1​51​]

The first column already has a 111 on top, a convenient pivot. Clear the 333 beneath it by replacing R2R_2R2​ with R2−3R1R_2 - 3R_1R2​−3R1​. That means subtracting 333 times every entry of row 111, the constant included, from the matching entry of row 222:

When a row gives it away

Not every system has a single solution. In the previous lessons you saw that a linear system always has exactly one solution, no solution, or infinitely many. In matrix form, no solution announces itself in a single tell-tale row; infinitely many takes a little more reading, which the next two examples and the rule after them will show you how to do.

Worked example 3 Spot the no-solution and infinitely-many cases

Start with a system that has no solution:

x+y=2,2x+2y=5.\begin{aligned} x + y &= 2, \\ 2x + 2y &= 5. \end{aligned}x+y2x+2y​=2,=5.​

Reduce its augmented matrix by replacing R2R_2R2​ with R2−2R1R_2 - 2R_1R2​−2R1​:

[112225]  ⟶  [112001]\left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 5 \end{array}\right] \;\longrightarrow\; \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right][12​12​25​]⟶[10​10​21​]

Translate the bottom row back into an equation. Its coefficients are all zero, but the constant is not: it reads 0x+0y=10x + 0y = 10x+0y=1, that is, 0=10 = 10=1. No values of xxx and yyy can make that true, so the system has no solution. A row of the form [ 0  0 ∣ c ][\,0 \ \ 0 \ \mid\ c\,][0  0 ∣ c] with c≠0c \ne 0c=0 is the matrix shouting contradiction.

Now change a single number so the system has infinitely many solutions:

x+y=2,2x+2y=4.\begin{aligned} x + y &= 2, \\ 2x + 2y &= 4. \end{aligned}x+y2x+2y​=2,=4.​

The same operation R2→R2−2R1R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1R2​→R2​−2R1​ now gives:

[112224]  ⟶  [112000]\left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 4 \end{array}\right] \;\longrightarrow\; \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right][12​12​24​]⟶[10​10​20​]

This time the bottom row is entirely zero, standing for 0=00 = 00=0. That is true for every choice of values, so it adds no constraint at all: the second equation was really just the first one doubled. What remains is the single equation x+y=2x + y = 2x+y=2, and any pair of numbers that adds to 222 satisfies it: try (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0), or (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1), or (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2), and each one checks out. To describe every such pair at once, let yyy stand for any number, and give it the name ttt so it is clear it is no longer fixed; then x=2−tx = 2 - tx=2−t matches it. Every (2−t, t)(2 - t,\, t)(2−t,t) is a solution, one for each value of ttt. The system is dependent, with a whole line of solutions.

The pattern is worth stating plainly, because it is how you diagnose a system straight from its reduced matrix. First check for a contradiction: a row of zeros on the left with a nonzero constant on the right, [ 0 ⋯ 0∣c ][\,0 \ \cdots\ 0 \mid c\,][0 ⋯ 0∣c] with c≠0c \ne 0c=0, means no solution, full stop. If no such row appears, the system is consistent, and the count of solutions comes down to one thing: does every variable column have a pivot? If it does, each variable is pinned to a single value, and the solution is unique. If some variable column has no pivot, that variable is free. You assign each free variable a parameter, one per pivot-free column, and solve for the pivoted variables in terms of those parameters, giving infinitely many solutions.

A row that is entirely zero, [ 0 ⋯ 0∣0 ][\,0 \ \cdots\ 0 \mid 0\,][0 ⋯ 0∣0], carries no information on its own: it just means one equation added nothing new. It often shows up alongside the infinitely-many case, since one equation adding nothing is one less piece of information to pin a variable down with, but it is the missing pivot that decides the solution count, not the zero row itself. A zero row can appear in a system with more equations than unknowns, one of them redundant, while every unknown still has its own pivot from the other equations, giving one solution despite the zero row.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the constant column. The augmented matrix must keep the right-hand sides, separated by the bar. Reduce only the coefficient matrix and you lose track of what each equation equals.
  • Dropping a zero coefficient. A variable missing from an equation still needs a 000 in its column, holding the place so the other coefficients line up. Skipping it shifts entries into the wrong columns.
  • Multiplying a row by zero. Scaling a row is legal only by a nonzero constant. Multiplying by 000 wipes an equation out to 0=00 = 00=0 and discards a real constraint.
  • Only partly updating a row. In R2→R2−3R1R_2 \to R_2 - 3R_1R2​→R2​−3R1​, every entry of row 222 changes, including the constant. Change the coefficients but forget the constant (or the reverse) and the row no longer stands for a true equation.
  • Misreading the two special rows. [ 0  0∣c ][\,0 \ \ 0 \mid c\,][0  0∣c] with c≠0c \ne 0c=0 means no solution, while a fully zero row [ 0  0∣0 ][\,0 \ \ 0 \mid 0\,][0  0∣0] adds no new constraint. They look almost alike but say opposite things, so check the constant.
  • Calling a zero row the reason for infinitely many solutions. A zero row only means one equation was redundant. The actual decision is the pivots: infinitely many solutions means some variable column has no pivot, not that a row happens to be all zero.
  • Reading a triangular matrix top-down. Back-substitution starts at the bottom row, where a single variable is pinned down, and climbs up. Starting at the top leaves you with too many unknowns at once.

Key takeaways

  • A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers; an m×nm \times nm×n matrix has mmm rows and nnn columns, and aija_{ij}aij​ is the entry in row iii, column jjj.
  • The augmented matrix of a system holds each equation’s coefficients as a row, with the constants in a final column set off by a bar. It is the coefficients of the system with the variables stripped away.
  • The three row operations are swapping two rows, multiplying a row by a nonzero constant, and adding a multiple of one row to another. All three are exactly the moves of elimination, and each is reversible, so none of them changes the solution set.
  • Reduce to row-echelon (triangular) form, then back-substitute from the bottom row upward to read off the solution.
  • A reduced row [ 0 ⋯ 0∣c ][\,0 \ \cdots\ 0 \mid c\,][0 ⋯ 0∣c] with c≠0c \ne 0c=0 means no solution. Otherwise, a pivot in every variable’s column means a unique solution, and a variable column with no pivot means that variable is free, giving infinitely many solutions. A fully zero row adds no information by itself; it does not decide the count.

Why the three row operations preserve the solution set

The main lesson gives the reason in plain words: every row operation is reversible, so it cannot create or destroy a solution. Here is the same claim checked equation by equation.

Each row operation preserves the solution set#

A solution of a system is a choice of values for the variables that makes every equation true at once. We check each operation against that definition.

Reordering the rows changes nothing about the equations themselves, only the order in which they are listed. So a choice of values that satisfies all of them before the swap satisfies all of them after. The solution set is untouched.

Multiplying row RiR_iRi​ by a nonzero constant kkk replaces the equation EEE with kEkEkE. Any values that make EEE true make kEkEkE true, since multiplying both sides of a true equation by kkk keeps it true. Conversely, any values that make kEkEkE true also make EEE true, because k≠0k \ne 0k=0 lets you divide both sides by kkk and recover EEE. So EEE and kEkEkE have precisely the same solutions, and the rest of the system is unchanged. This is exactly where the word nonzero earns its place. Multiplying by 000 would turn EEE into 0=00 = 00=0, which every choice of values satisfies, and the original equation could never be recovered. That step would throw a genuine constraint away.

Adding kkk times row RjR_jRj​ to row RiR_iRi​ replaces the equation EiE_iEi​ with Ei+kEjE_i + kE_jEi​+kEj​, while EjE_jEj​ itself stays in the system. Suppose some values satisfy the original system. Then they make both EiE_iEi​ and EjE_jEj​ true, so they make Ei+kEjE_i + kE_jEi​+kEj​ true as well, and they still satisfy the untouched EjE_jEj​. So every old solution is a solution of the new system. For the reverse, suppose some values satisfy the new system. They make Ei+kEjE_i + kE_jEi​+kEj​ true and, because EjE_jEj​ is still present, they make EjE_jEj​ true too. Subtracting kkk times EjE_jEj​ from Ei+kEjE_i + kE_jEi​+kEj​ returns EiE_iEi​, so those values satisfy EiE_iEi​ as well. Every new solution is therefore an old solution. The two systems have identical solution sets.

Since each operation leaves the solution set exactly as it was, any sequence of them does too. Whatever you reduce the matrix to, you may read its solutions as the solutions of the system you started with.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

Two thousand years ago in China, a clerk facing three unknowns wrote nothing down. There was no letter xxx, no equals sign, and no page of algebra to fill. There was a board, and a bag of small counting rods.

Each equation became one column of rods laid on the board. Set the columns side by side and the whole system was there, a rectangle of numbers with nothing else in it. The procedure that reduced the rectangle had a name, fangcheng, and a home: the eighth chapter of the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art.

Every clerk running it met the same obstacle. Clearing an unknown means subtracting one column from another, and sooner or later that asks you to take a larger number from a smaller. The board answered with color. Rods came in two colors, and the second color counted an amount owed rather than held. Negative quantities were ordinary working tools on that table, because the method could not run without them.

Now consider what that rectangle really is. The unknowns have disappeared completely, and the coefficients are laid out in a grid. The permitted moves are to scale a line of numbers and to subtract it from another, the same two moves this lesson calls row operations, done on columns instead of rows because the rods laid each equation sideways. That is the augmented matrix of this lesson, turned on its side and kept in rods on a table rather than in ink on a page.

Systems in Two Variables

Intuition

In your first algebra course, a system of equations was a puzzle with an answer: substitute or eliminate, land on a single pair like (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2), and move on. That worked, but it hid a question worth asking. What kinds of answers are even possible? Could a system have exactly two solutions, or seventeen? It turns out the answer is surprisingly limited. A linear system in two variables can end in only three ways, with one solution, none at all, or infinitely many, and nothing else can ever happen. This lesson is about that result and why it is forced. The mechanics of solving are review from here. The new idea is to ask what shape the whole solution set is allowed to take, not just what one solution looks like.

Learning goals

  • Read the solution set as one point, none, or a line
  • Prove no other count is possible for two lines
  • Detect the case from how elimination ends
  • Classify a system from its coefficient ratios and name it consistent, inconsistent, independent, or dependent
  • Describe an infinite solution set explicitly, as (x,2x−3)(x, 2x - 3)(x,2x−3)

A system asks where two lines meet

You already know that a single linear equation in two variables, such as 2x+3y=122x + 3y = 122x+3y=12, does not have one answer. Its solutions are all the pairs (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that make it true, and when you plot them they form a straight line. The equation and the line are two views of the same object: the line is the solution set of that one equation.

A system stacks two such equations and asks for the pairs that satisfy both at once:

2x+3y=12,x−y=1.2x + 3y = 12, \qquad x - y = 1.2x+3y=12,x−y=1.

A pair solves the system exactly when it lies on the first line and on the second. So the solution set of the system is precisely the set of points the two lines have in common, their intersection. The whole question “how many solutions can a system have” becomes a geometry question you can picture: in how many points can two lines in a plane meet?

Three shapes, and no others

Take any two lines drawn in the same plane. Run through the possibilities. They might cross at a single point. They might run parallel and never touch. Or the two equations might, once simplified, describe the very same line lying on top of itself. Those three pictures give one shared point, no shared point, and every point shared. What is remarkable is that this list is complete: no arrangement of two straight lines produces exactly two crossings, or exactly five. The reason is short and worth seeing in full.

A linear system in two variables has one, no, or infinitely many solutions, and nothing else#

A solution of the system is a point lying on both lines, so the solution set is exactly the collection of points the two lines share. Two lines are either the same line or two genuinely different lines, so handle those cases.

If the two equations describe the same line, then every point of that line lies on both. Since a line has infinitely many points, the system then has infinitely many solutions.

If the two lines are different, they can share at most one point. Here is why. Suppose, for contradiction, that two different lines passed through two different shared points. A straight line is completely determined by any two of its points: through two given points there is exactly one line. So both lines, passing through the same two points, would have to be the identical line, contradicting that they are different. Two distinct lines therefore meet in at most one point, which leaves only two options: they cross at exactly one point, or they never meet at all.

Collecting the cases, the solution set is the whole line (same line), a single point (distinct lines that cross), or empty (distinct lines that never meet). A count of “two or more, but finitely many” is impossible, because the two-points argument forbids two distinct lines from sharing even two points. That is the entire list.

∎

Check your understanding

Two different lines are claimed to cross at two different points, PPP and QQQ. Why is that impossible?

Answer choices

A straight line is completely determined by any two of its points: through two given points there is exactly one line. If both of the original lines passed through PPP and QQQ, both would have to be that one determined line, making them the same line, not two different ones. That contradiction is why two distinct lines can share at most one point.

The three outcomes are easy to tell apart once each line is in slope-intercept form y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b, because the slope mmm controls the direction. Different slopes force a single crossing. Equal slopes with different intercepts give parallel lines that never meet. Equal slopes with the same intercept are the same line drawn twice.

Reading the three cases off the algebra

You do not need a graph to know which case you are in. Substitution and elimination already report it, if you listen to what they output. Both methods work by removing one variable to get a single equation, and that final equation lands in exactly one of three states, matching the three pictures one for one. Think of elimination less as a way to grind out an answer and more as a detector for which case a system is in.

Start with the case you expect most often, a single crossing point.

Worked example 1 One solution: the algebra ends at a value

Solve the system

2x+3y=12,x−y=1.2x + 3y = 12, \qquad x - y = 1.2x+3y=12,x−y=1.

The slopes differ (−23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ against 111), so expect a single crossing. Eliminate yyy by scaling the second equation by 333 so the yyy terms become opposites:

3(x−y)=3(1)  ⟹  3x−3y=3.3(x - y) = 3(1) \;\Longrightarrow\; 3x - 3y = 3.3(x−y)=3(1)⟹3x−3y=3.

Add this to the first equation. The +3y+3y+3y and −3y-3y−3y cancel:

(2x+3y)+(3x−3y)=12+3  ⟹  5x=15  ⟹  x=3.(2x + 3y) + (3x - 3y) = 12 + 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; 5x = 15 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3.(2x+3y)+(3x−3y)=12+3⟹5x=15⟹x=3.

Back-substitute into x−y=1x - y = 1x−y=1 to get 3−y=13 - y = 13−y=1, so y=2y = 2y=2. The elimination ended at a genuine value, x=3x = 3x=3, which then pinned yyy to a single number. That is the signature of the one-solution case:

(x,y)=(3,2).(x, y) = (3, 2).(x,y)=(3,2).

A quick check confirms it: 2(3)+3(2)=122(3) + 3(2) = 122(3)+3(2)=12 and 3−2=13 - 2 = 13−2=1, both true.

Now watch what changes when the lines are parallel. The arithmetic does something that looks alarming the first time you see it, but it is exactly the report you want.

Worked example 2 No solution: the algebra ends at a falsehood

Solve the system

2x−y=3,4x−2y=1.2x - y = 3, \qquad 4x - 2y = 1.2x−y=3,4x−2y=1.

Rewrite each in slope-intercept form to see the geometry: the first is y=2x−3y = 2x - 3y=2x−3 and the second is y=2x−12y = 2x - \tfrac{1}{2}y=2x−21​. Same slope 222, different intercepts, so the lines are parallel and should share no point. Watch elimination confirm it. Scale the first equation by 222:

2(2x−y)=2(3)  ⟹  4x−2y=6.2(2x - y) = 2(3) \;\Longrightarrow\; 4x - 2y = 6.2(2x−y)=2(3)⟹4x−2y=6.

Subtract the second equation, 4x−2y=14x - 2y = 14x−2y=1, from this. Both variables cancel at once:

(4x−2y)−(4x−2y)=6−1  ⟹  0=5.(4x - 2y) - (4x - 2y) = 6 - 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; 0 = 5.(4x−2y)−(4x−2y)=6−1⟹0=5.

The statement 0=50 = 50=5 is false, and no choice of xxx or yyy can make it true, because xxx and yyy have already vanished from it. So there is no pair satisfying both equations. The system has no solution, which is precisely the parallel-lines picture.

The last case is the strangest of the three, because the answer is not a point and not “nothing” but an entire line’s worth of solutions. When elimination wipes out both variables and leaves a statement that is simply true, the second equation was telling you nothing new.

Worked example 3 Infinitely many: describing the whole solution set

Solve the system

6x−3y=9,2x−y=3.6x - 3y = 9, \qquad 2x - y = 3.6x−3y=9,2x−y=3.

Divide the first equation by 333 and it becomes 2x−y=32x - y = 32x−y=3, identical to the second. The two equations are the same line wearing different clothes. Elimination makes this explicit: scale the second by 333 to get 6x−3y=96x - 3y = 96x−3y=9, then subtract the first:

(6x−3y)−(6x−3y)=9−9  ⟹  0=0.(6x - 3y) - (6x - 3y) = 9 - 9 \;\Longrightarrow\; 0 = 0.(6x−3y)−(6x−3y)=9−9⟹0=0.

The statement 0=00 = 00=0 is always true, so the second equation added no restriction the first had not already imposed. Every point on the shared line solves the system, so there are infinitely many solutions.

“Infinitely many” is not a complete answer, though; you should say which pairs. Solve the common line 2x−y=32x - y = 32x−y=3 for yyy to get y=2x−3y = 2x - 3y=2x−3. Then any xxx you like is allowed, and yyy is forced to match, so the solution set is every pair of the form

(x, 2x−3)as x ranges over all real numbers.(x,\ 2x - 3) \quad \text{as } x \text{ ranges over all real numbers.}(x, 2x−3)as x ranges over all real numbers.

For instance x=0x = 0x=0 gives (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3), and x=4x = 4x=4 gives (4,5)(4, 5)(4,5); both satisfy the original two equations. Writing the answer as (x, 2x−3)(x,\ 2x - 3)(x, 2x−3) captures all of them at once.

These three endings are the whole story, and they are the algebraic mirror of the geometry.

Why elimination can only end in a value, a falsehood, or an identity#

Take a system in which each equation genuinely involves both variables. Eliminate one variable, say yyy, by scaling the two equations so their yyy-coefficients match and then subtracting. The yyy terms cancel by design, and what remains is one equation whose only variable is xxx. Exactly one of two things happens to the xxx term.

Either some nonzero multiple of xxx survives, giving an equation of the form px=qpx = qpx=q with p≠0p \ne 0p=0. Then x=qpx = \tfrac{q}{p}x=pq​ is forced to a single value, back-substitution forces one matching yyy, and the system has exactly one solution.

Or the xxx term cancels along with the yyy term, leaving a bare numerical statement 0=k0 = k0=k. That cancellation happens exactly when the two equations’ xxx and yyy coefficients are already proportional, so kkk is left to record whether the constant terms respect that same proportion. If k≠0k \ne 0k=0 the statement 0=k0 = k0=k is false and the system has no solution. If k=0k = 0k=0 the statement 0=00 = 00=0 holds for every pair, and the second equation is a rescaling of the first. The two then describe one line, and every point on it is a solution, infinitely many.

So the only possible endings are px=qpx = qpx=q (one solution), 0=k≠00 = k \ne 00=k=0 (none), and 0=00 = 00=0 (infinitely many), matching crossing lines, parallel lines, and one repeated line. There is no fourth ending.

∎

0=0 is always true0 = 0 \ \text{is always true}0=0 is always true

An always-true ending with no variables left means the second equation added no information the first did not already have: the two equations describe the same line, and every point on that shared line is a solution, infinitely many in all. Reaching 0=00 = 00=0 is not a mistake; it is the report that tells you the case.

Consistent, inconsistent, dependent, independent

Because these three cases come up constantly, they carry standard names, and the names split the idea along two independent questions.

The first question is whether any solution exists at all. A system with at least one solution is consistent; a system with no solution is inconsistent. The parallel-lines system from Worked Example 2, which ended in 0=50 = 50=5, is inconsistent.

The second question, asked only when the system is consistent, is whether the two equations carry genuinely different information. If the equations describe different lines, they pin down a single crossing point, and the system is independent. If one equation is just a rescaling of the other, so they describe the same line, the system is dependent, and it has infinitely many solutions. Combining the two questions gives exactly the three cases:

  • Consistent and independent: one solution (lines cross).
  • Consistent and dependent: infinitely many solutions (same line).
  • Inconsistent: no solution (parallel lines).

When both equations are written in standard form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c with nonzero coefficients and constant terms, you can classify a system at a glance by comparing the ratios of matching coefficients. Two lines have the same slope exactly when a1a2=b1b2\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​, and they are the same line when the constants keep that ratio too:

a1a2≠b1b2\frac{a_1}{a_2} \ne \frac{b_1}{b_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​cross onceexactly oneconsistent, independent

Forcing a case: solving for a parameter

Once you can read the case off the coefficients, you can run the question backward. Instead of being handed a fixed system and asked how many solutions it has, you are handed a system with an unknown constant in it. You are then asked to choose that constant so that a particular case occurs. This is where the classification earns its keep.

Worked example 4 Choose k so the system has no solution, then infinitely many

For which values of kkk does this system have no solution, and for which does it have infinitely many?

3x−y=4,6x−2y=k.3x - y = 4, \qquad 6x - 2y = k.3x−y=4,6x−2y=k.

Look at the left-hand sides. The second, 6x−2y6x - 2y6x−2y, is exactly twice the first, 3x−y3x - y3x−y. So no matter what kkk is, the two lines have the same slope; they can only ever be parallel or identical, never crossing. Which one it is depends on the constant. Scale the first equation by 222:

2(3x−y)=2(4)  ⟹  6x−2y=8.2(3x - y) = 2(4) \;\Longrightarrow\; 6x - 2y = 8.2(3x−y)=2(4)⟹6x−2y=8.

Compare with the second equation, 6x−2y=k6x - 2y = k6x−2y=k. Subtracting gives

0=8−k.0 = 8 - k.0=8−k.

If k=8k = 8k=8, this is 0=00 = 00=0, the two equations are the same line, and there are infinitely many solutions. If k≠8k \ne 8k=8, this is 0=(nonzero)0 = (\text{nonzero})0=(nonzero), the lines are parallel, and there is no solution.

When the unknown sits in a coefficient instead of a constant, it controls the slope, and the reasoning shifts to matching slopes. The next checkpoint is that version.

A vertical line has no slope to compare, but it still crosses the first line's non-vertical slope exactly once.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading 0=50 = 50=5 as an error instead of an answer. When both variables cancel and leave a false numeric statement, you have not made a mistake; you have proved the lines are parallel. The correct response is “no solution,” not “start over.”
  • Reading 0=00 = 00=0 as "x=0x = 0x=0" or “no solution.” An always-true ending means infinitely many solutions, not zero of them and not a single value. It is the opposite of the 0=50 = 50=5 case.
  • Stopping at “infinitely many.” That names the case but does not describe the solutions. Solve the shared line for one variable and report the pairs, for example (x, 2x−3)(x,\ 2x - 3)(x, 2x−3) for every real xxx.
  • Confusing dependent with inconsistent. Dependent systems are consistent: they have solutions, a whole line of them. Inconsistent systems have none. Both can show equal slopes, so check the constant term to tell them apart.
  • Assuming any nonzero coefficient change keeps one solution. A coefficient sets a line’s slope through the ratio of the xxx- and yyy-coefficients, so changing one can change the slope, and if it makes the slopes equal, the count jumps from one straight to zero or infinity. Not every coefficient change does this, but you cannot assume one is safe without checking; the number of solutions is not a continuous dial.

Key takeaways

  • The solution set of a two-variable linear system is the intersection of two lines. That intersection is therefore one point, no points, or a whole line: one solution, no solution, or infinitely many, and nothing else.
  • Two distinct lines meet in at most one point (two shared points would force them to be the same line), which is what rules out any other count.
  • Elimination detects the case by its ending: px=qpx = qpx=q with p≠0p \ne 0p=0 gives one solution, 0=k≠00 = k \ne 00=k=0 gives none, and 0=00 = 00=0 gives infinitely many.
  • Consistent means at least one solution, inconsistent means none; a consistent system is independent (one solution, different lines) or dependent (infinitely many, same line).
  • When every coefficient and constant is nonzero, compare the three ratios a1a2\frac{a_1}{a_2}a2​a1​​, b1b2\frac{b_1}{b_2}b2​b1​​, and c1c2\frac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​ in standard form. The first two ratios unequal means one solution, the first two equal but the constant ratio different means none, and all three equal means infinitely many. If a ratio is undefined, fall back on elimination instead.
  • Describe an infinite solution set explicitly, such as (x, 2x−3)(x,\ 2x - 3)(x, 2x−3) for all real xxx, rather than leaving it as “infinitely many.”

A bit of history (optional)

How many times can two shapes cross?

Start with the shapes you already know. Two distinct straight lines cross once, or never. A straight line can cut a circle twice, or graze it, or miss it entirely. It can never cut a circle three times. So the number of crossings has a ceiling, and the ceiling depends on the shapes.

Etienne Bezout, a French mathematician, published the general answer in 1779. Every curve carries a degree. Take any term, add the powers inside it, and the largest total is the degree, so xyxyxy counts as two. This lesson states a simplified version of his rule, for the ordinary crossings you would actually mark on a real graph: multiply the two degrees, and the product is the most such crossings that pair can ever have. (The full theorem is exact rather than a ceiling, but only once you also count using complex-number coordinates, add in “points at infinity” where parallel lines meet, and count each touching point by its multiplicity, how many times the curves coincide there, rather than as a single point. A plain graze counts twice; a tighter tangency between higher-degree curves can count higher still. None of that extra machinery is needed for the two straight lines below.) One condition rides along: the curves must have no piece in common, since a curve laid on top of another meets it everywhere. A line has degree one and a circle degree two, so a line meets a circle at most twice. Two lines have degree one apiece, and one multiplied by one is one.

That final case is the theorem you proved in this lesson. Two different lines share at most one point. So a system of two linear equations cannot possibly have exactly two solutions, or exactly five, whatever coefficients you choose.

Notice that Bezout promises only a ceiling once you stay with ordinary real points, not an exact count. Ordinary pairs fall short of it constantly, and this lesson shows the two ways that happens. Parallel lines miss the ceiling entirely: they share no ordinary point, though the full theorem would still count one shared point “at infinity” that no graph ever shows. Two copies of a single line share every point, which is exactly the case the “no piece in common” condition rules out to begin with, so the ceiling never applied there at all. Those are the awkward cases in any count of crossings, and here they are your no solution and your infinitely many.

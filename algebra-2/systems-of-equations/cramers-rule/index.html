Determinants and Cramer's Rule Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

You have solved plenty of systems one at a time, pushing numbers around until the answer fell out. Here is a different idea. Solve the general two-variable system once, with letters standing in for the coefficients, and keep that answer on the shelf. Every specific system you ever meet is then just the stored answer with numbers dropped in. When you carry out that one calculation, a single combination of the coefficients turns up in the same spot both times. That spot is the denominator for xxx, and then again the denominator for yyy. That one number decides whether the system has a single solution or is degenerate, and which kind of degenerate takes one more look. It earns a name and a symbol of its own. It is called the determinant.

Learning goals

  • Find ad−bcad - bcad−bc in elimination, and use D≠0D \ne 0D=0 to test uniqueness
  • Compute a 2×22 \times 22×2 determinant, and a 3×33 \times 33×3 determinant by cofactor expansion
  • Apply Cramer's rule to two- and three-variable systems, replacing a column with the constants
  • Choose row reduction over Cramer's rule for large systems

Solving the general two-variable system

Watch the pattern appear on real numbers before meeting the letters. Solve

4x+y=13,x−y=2.\begin{aligned} 4x + y &= 13, \\ x - y &= 2. \end{aligned}4x+yx−y​=13,=2.​

To clear yyy, multiply the first equation by −1-1−1 (the yyy-coefficient of the second equation) and the second equation by 111 (the yyy-coefficient of the first). That is the same move you are about to see done with letters:

−4x−y=−13,x−y=2.\begin{aligned} -4x - y &= -13, \\ x - y &= 2. \end{aligned}−4x−yx−y​=−13,=2.​

Subtract the second line from the first. The yyy terms cancel, and xxx is left with the coefficient −5-5−5:

−5x=−15,sox=3,and theny=13−4(3)=1.-5x = -15, \qquad \text{so} \qquad x = 3, \qquad \text{and then} \qquad y = 13 - 4(3) = 1.−5x=−15,sox=3,and theny=13−4(3)=1.

Where did that −5-5−5 come from? From the four coefficients 4,1,1,−14, 1, 1, -14,1,1,−1, combined as 4×(−1)−1×14 \times (-1) - 1 \times 14×(−1)−1×1. Hold on to that combination. It is about to reappear, unchanged, when the same two steps are repeated with letters instead of numbers.

Take the most general system of two linear equations in two unknowns. Write the coefficients as letters so the result works for every system at once:

ax+by=e,cx+dy=f.\begin{aligned} ax + by &= e, \\ cx + dy &= f. \end{aligned}ax+bycx+dy​=e,=f.​

Solve it by elimination, with one thing to watch for. The multipliers about to appear, ddd and bbb, are letters standing for coefficients that could themselves be 000 for some particular system, unlike the nonzero multiplier the row operations of the last lesson required. That is not a problem here, because at this stage all that is needed is a one-way fact: multiply both sides of a true equation by any number, zero included, and the result is still a true equation. So whatever the actual solution turns out to be, it must satisfy the combination about to be built, whether or not ddd or bbb happens to be zero. Whether the reverse holds, whether the values this process produces actually solve the system, is checked separately below, and only for the case where the process can be completed, D≠0D \ne 0D=0. To clear yyy, multiply the first equation by ddd and the second by bbb, so the yyy terms match:

adx+bdy=ed,bcx+bdy=bf.\begin{aligned} adx + bdy &= ed, \\ bcx + bdy &= bf. \end{aligned}adx+bdybcx+bdy​=ed,=bf.​

Subtract the second line from the first. The bdybdybdy terms cancel, and xxx is left with the coefficient ad−bcad - bcad−bc:

(ad−bc) x=ed−bf.(ad - bc)\,x = ed - bf.(ad−bc)x=ed−bf.

Check it against the numbers you already found: with a=4a = 4a=4, b=1b = 1b=1, c=1c = 1c=1, d=−1d = -1d=−1, this coefficient is exactly the −5-5−5 you computed by hand above.

Now do the mirror image to clear xxx. Multiply the first equation by ccc and the second by aaa, letting the same one-way reasoning cover ccc and aaa whether or not either happens to be zero, then subtract the first from the second, and the same coefficient ad−bcad - bcad−bc reappears in front of yyy:

(ad−bc) y=af−ce.(ad - bc)\,y = af - ce.(ad−bc)y=af−ce.

Look at what these two results share. The quantity ad−bcad - bcad−bc sits in front of both xxx and yyy, which means it becomes the denominator the moment you divide to solve:

x=ed−bfad−bc,y=af−cead−bc.x = \frac{ed - bf}{ad - bc}, \qquad y = \frac{af - ce}{ad - bc}.x=ad−bced−bf​,y=ad−bcaf−ce​.

One combination of the four coefficients controls both answers. If it is not zero, you can divide and the system has a clean solution. If it is zero, the division is illegal, and that tells you the two equations no longer pin down one single point. A number that important should not stay nameless.

The determinant and its bar notation

The shared quantity ad−bcad - bcad−bc is the determinant of the four coefficients, arranged in the square grid in which they appear:

∣abcd∣=ad−bc.\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc.​ac​bd​​=ad−bc.

The straight bars are the determinant symbol. The rule they stand for is short: multiply down the main diagonal (aaa times ddd), then subtract the product up the other diagonal (bbb times ccc). We write the coefficient determinant of our system as DDD, so D=ad−bcD = ad - bcD=ad−bc.

These bars are not absolute value

You met vertical bars once before, in ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣, the absolute value. The determinant bars look the same but mean something different, and it is worth keeping them apart from the start. Absolute value takes a single number and returns its nonnegative size, so ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ is never negative. A determinant takes a whole square array of numbers and returns a single signed number, which is very often negative. For example, ∣1231∣=1−6=−5\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1 - 6 = -5​13​21​​=1−6=−5. What tells the two apart is what sits between the bars: one plain number means absolute value, a square array of numbers means a determinant.

Cramer’s rule for a two-variable system

Look again at the answers we derived,

x=ed−bfad−bc,y=af−cead−bc,x = \frac{ed - bf}{ad - bc}, \qquad y = \frac{af - ce}{ad - bc},x=ad−bced−bf​,y=ad−bcaf−ce​,

and notice that every numerator is itself a determinant. The denominator is DDD in both. The numerator for xxx is ed−bfed - bfed−bf, which is the determinant you get from DDD by a single column replacement. You take DDD and replace its first column (the xxx-column, aaa and ccc) with the constants eee and fff. The numerator for yyy is af−ceaf - ceaf−ce, the determinant from replacing the second column (the yyy-column, bbb and ddd) with the same constants. Name these column-replacement determinants DxD_xDx​ and DyD_yDy​:

Dx=∣ebfd∣=ed−bf,Dy=∣aecf∣=af−ce.D_x = \begin{vmatrix} e & b \\ f & d \end{vmatrix} = ed - bf, \qquad D_y = \begin{vmatrix} a & e \\ c & f \end{vmatrix} = af - ce.Dx​=​ef​bd​​=ed−bf,Dy​=​ac​ef​​=af−ce.

With that naming, the whole solution collapses into a pattern worth memorizing. This is Cramer’s rule:

x=DxD,y=DyD(D≠0).x = \frac{D_x}{D}, \qquad y = \frac{D_y}{D} \qquad (D \ne 0).x=DDx​​,y=DDy​​(D=0).

Why Cramer's rule gives the exact solution when D≠0D \ne 0D=0#

Look back at the elimination. Starting from the two original equations, honest algebra produced D x=DxD\,x = D_xDx=Dx​ and D y=DyD\,y = D_yDy=Dy​, so every solution of the system must satisfy those two relations. When D≠0D \ne 0D=0 we may divide, and the relations force

x=DxD,y=DyD.x = \frac{D_x}{D}, \qquad y = \frac{D_y}{D}.x=DDx​​,y=DDy​​.

No other values are possible, so the system has at most this one solution. It remains to check that this pair actually solves the system, since forcing the values does not by itself guarantee they work. Substitute them into the first equation and clear the denominator:

a⋅DxD+b⋅DyD=a(ed−bf)+b(af−ce)ad−bc=aed−bcead−bc=e(ad−bc)ad−bc=e.a\cdot\frac{D_x}{D} + b\cdot\frac{D_y}{D} = \frac{a(ed - bf) + b(af - ce)}{ad - bc} = \frac{aed - bce}{ad - bc} = \frac{e(ad - bc)}{ad - bc} = e.a⋅DDx​​+b⋅DDy​​=ad−bca(ed−bf)+b(af−ce)​=ad−bcaed−bce​=ad−bce(ad−bc)​=e.

The middle step used −abf+baf=0-abf + baf = 0−abf+baf=0, so those terms drop and eee factors out of what remains. The same computation on cx+dycx + dycx+dy returns fff. The pair therefore satisfies both equations, and it is the only pair that can, so it is the unique solution.

∎

The determinant test for a unique solution

The derivation quietly answered a deeper question than “what is the solution.” It told us exactly when a solution exists and is unique. Everything hinged on whether we were allowed to divide by DDD, so DDD answers one specific question: does this system have exactly one solution, or not?

A two-variable system has a unique solution exactly when D≠0D \ne 0D=0#

We prove both directions, since “exactly when” is a claim in both. Throughout, recall the two relations that hold for any solution: D x=DxD\,x = D_xDx=Dx​ and D y=DyD\,y = D_yDy=Dy​.

First, if D≠0D \ne 0D=0, the previous proof already produced one and only one solution, x=Dx/Dx = D_x/Dx=Dx​/D and y=Dy/Dy = D_y/Dy=Dy​/D. So D≠0D \ne 0D=0 gives a unique solution.

Now the other direction: suppose D=0D = 0D=0, and show the solution cannot be unique. With D=0D = 0D=0 the two relations read 0=Dx0 = D_x0=Dx​ and 0=Dy0 = D_y0=Dy​, and both must hold for any solution to exist at all. Two cases cover it.

If either Dx≠0D_x \ne 0Dx​=0 or Dy≠0D_y \ne 0Dy​=0, then one of the relations says 0=(a nonzero number)0 = (\text{a nonzero number})0=(a nonzero number), which nothing can make true. The system then has no solution.

If instead Dx=0D_x = 0Dx​=0 and Dy=0D_y = 0Dy​=0 as well, look at the two equations directly, and sort them by how many variable coefficients are zero.

Suppose both equations have every variable coefficient equal to zero, so each reads 0x+0y=(some constant)0x + 0y = (\text{some constant})0x+0y=(some constant). If either constant is nonzero, that one equation alone is already false, so the system has no solution. If both constants are zero, every point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) satisfies both equations, which is still infinitely many solutions, just not confined to a single line.

Suppose instead exactly one equation has every variable coefficient equal to zero, so it reads 0x+0y=(some constant)0x + 0y = (\text{some constant})0x+0y=(some constant) while the other equation keeps a nonzero coefficient. A nonzero constant in the degenerate equation contradicts itself, so the system has no solution. A zero constant there says nothing at all, so the solution set is exactly the line the other, nondegenerate equation describes: infinitely many solutions.

Suppose finally that neither equation is degenerate; both keep a nonzero coefficient on at least one variable. Since D=0D = 0D=0, the coefficient rows (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) and (c,d)(c, d)(c,d) are proportional. Because Dx=Dy=0D_x = D_y = 0Dx​=Dy​=0 forces the constants to scale by that same factor as well, one equation is a constant multiple of the other. The two therefore impose at most one line’s worth of constraint, so the solution set is never a single point. In the consistent case that set is a whole line, giving infinitely many solutions.

Either way, a system with D=0D = 0D=0 has no solution or infinitely many, never exactly one. Combined with the first direction, a system has a unique solution precisely when D≠0D \ne 0D=0.

∎

This is the tidy end of the one-none-infinitely-many trichotomy you met at the start of the chapter. The determinant is a single number that detects which case you are in:

  • D≠0D \ne 0D=0: the two lines cross at one point, so there is exactly one solution.
  • D=0D = 0D=0: for two variables, the two lines are parallel (no solution) or the same line (infinitely many). The determinant cannot tell those two apart by itself, only that the system is degenerate. This two-line picture is specific to two variables; with three variables the same D=0D = 0D=0 test still correctly signals “no solution or infinitely many,” but the geometry has more shapes than “parallel or the same plane,” since three planes can also fail to share a common point without any two of them being parallel.

Be careful with that last point, because it is the easiest thing to overstate. A zero determinant does not mean “no solution.” It means “not a unique solution,” which is either no solution or infinitely many. You need one more look at the constants to decide which.

See the difference on numbers. Pair 3x+y=63x + y = 63x+y=6 with 6x+2y=126x + 2y = 126x+2y=12: the second equation is the first one doubled, so every point on the line 3x+y=63x + y = 63x+y=6 satisfies both, and every determinant is zero, D=Dx=Dy=0D = D_x = D_y = 0D=Dx​=Dy​=0. That is infinitely many solutions. Now pair the same 3x+y=63x + y = 63x+y=6 with 6x+2y=96x + 2y = 96x+2y=9 instead. The coefficients still double, 6=2(3)6 = 2(3)6=2(3) and 2=2(1)2 = 2(1)2=2(1), so D=0D = 0D=0 again, but the constant does not match that doubling, 9≠2(6)9 \ne 2(6)9=2(6), so Dx=6(2)−1(9)=3D_x = 6(2) - 1(9) = 3Dx​=6(2)−1(9)=3, which is not zero. The two lines are parallel and never meet: no solution.

Determinants of three-variable systems

For three unknowns the coefficients form a 3×33 \times 33×3 array, and its determinant is built out of 2×22 \times 22×2 determinants. Expand along the first row: multiply each top entry by the 2×22 \times 22×2 determinant left after crossing out that entry’s own row and column. Then attach the alternating signs to those products in order: plus, minus, plus.

∣abcdefghi∣=a∣efhi∣−b∣dfgi∣+c∣degh∣.\begin{vmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{vmatrix} = a\begin{vmatrix} e & f \\ h & i \end{vmatrix} - b\begin{vmatrix} d & f \\ g & i \end{vmatrix} + c\begin{vmatrix} d & e \\ g & h \end{vmatrix}.​adg​beh​cfi​​=a​eh​fi​​−b​dg​fi​​+c​dg​eh​​.

Each small determinant is the minor of the entry in front of it. The middle term carries a minus sign, which is the single most common slip, so watch for it. This method is called cofactor expansion, and it reduces any 3×33 \times 33×3 determinant to three easy 2×22 \times 22×2 ones.

Cramer’s rule for a three-variable system

Cramer’s rule extends to three unknowns with no new ideas, only more columns. The two-variable version came from combining the equations to make one variable’s coefficient match and cancel, then recording only what that combination implies about any solution, the same one-way reasoning as above, valid whether or not a multiplier lands on zero. Isolating one unknown among three runs through that same combining process, just applied twice instead of once, and lands on the identical pattern: each numerator swaps the constants into one column, with DDD staying the shared denominator throughout. As before, this only pins down what any solution must satisfy; checking that those values actually work, when D≠0D \ne 0D=0, is the same substitution argument as the two-variable proof, just with one more variable. Writing out that longer elimination and check in full would not teach anything the two-variable case has not already shown, so the pattern is stated here rather than rederived from scratch.

Let DDD be the coefficient determinant of

a1x+b1y+c1z=d1,a2x+b2y+c2z=d2,a3x+b3y+c3z=d3.\begin{aligned} a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1 z &= d_1, \\ a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2 z &= d_2, \\ a_3 x + b_3 y + c_3 z &= d_3. \end{aligned}a1​x+b1​y+c1​za2​x+b2​y+c2​za3​x+b3​y+c3​z​=d1​,=d2​,=d3​.​

Form DxD_xDx​, DyD_yDy​, and DzD_zDz​ by replacing the xxx-, yyy-, or zzz-column with the constant column d1,d2,d3d_1, d_2, d_3d1​,d2​,d3​. Then, as long as D≠0D \ne 0D=0,

x=DxD,y=DyD,z=DzD.x = \frac{D_x}{D}, \qquad y = \frac{D_y}{D}, \qquad z = \frac{D_z}{D}.x=DDx​​,y=DDy​​,z=DDz​​.

Cramer’s rule or row reduction?

Cramer’s rule and the row reduction from the previous lesson both handle a square system, the same number of equations as unknowns, so on that common ground, which should you reach for? Row reduction still reaches further: it also handles a system with more equations than unknowns or fewer, the kind Matrices and Systems solved, where Cramer’s rule does not apply at all, since there is no single coefficient determinant to divide by. Restricted to square systems, though, the two have opposite strengths, and the honest answer is that it depends on what you want.

Cramer’s rule is a closed formula: it writes each unknown as one determinant over another, with no back-substitution and no bookkeeping of row swaps. That makes it excellent in two situations. First, when you need only one unknown, you compute just DDD and that variable’s numerator and stop, no matter which unknown it is. Row reduction cannot promise that: how much extra work it costs depends on where the unknown you want sits in the elimination order. Ask for the last variable eliminated and forward elimination alone hands it to you, no back-substitution needed at all; ask for an early one instead and you must first pin down every variable below it, climbing the whole staircase to reach it. Cramer’s rule pays the same one extra determinant regardless of which unknown you picked. Second, for theory, the formula shows plainly how the answer depends on the numbers in the system, which is why D≠0D \ne 0D=0 reads off the unique-solution condition at a glance.

Row reduction wins on scale. A cofactor expansion of an n×nn \times nn×n determinant fans out into smaller determinants and the work grows explosively with nnn, and Cramer needs n+1n + 1n+1 of these determinants. Row reduction grows far more gently, which is why it, not Cramer, is what people and computers use to solve large systems.

Cramer's rule's no-back-substitution advantage matters most when you want only one unknown, not a full solve of a large one, and the two methods are close in effort only for a 2×22 \times 22×2 system.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading the bars as absolute value. A determinant is a signed number and is frequently negative; ∣1231∣=−5\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = -5​13​21​​=−5. The bars mean absolute value only around a single number, never around an array.
  • Reversing the diagonal subtraction. The 2×22 \times 22×2 rule is ad−bcad - bcad−bc, main diagonal minus anti-diagonal, not bc−adbc - adbc−ad. Swapping the order flips the sign of every answer.
  • Forgetting the minus in the middle of a cofactor expansion. The first-row signs are plus, minus, plus. The middle term is subtracted; treating all three as additions is the classic 3×33 \times 33×3 error.
  • Concluding D=0D = 0D=0 means no solution. A zero determinant means the solution is not unique, which is either no solution or infinitely many. Deciding which requires checking the constants.
  • Dividing the wrong way. Cramer’s rule is x=Dx/Dx = D_x / Dx=Dx​/D, the column-replacement determinant over the coefficient determinant, not D/DxD / D_xD/Dx​.
  • Replacing the wrong column. For DxD_xDx​ the constants go into the xxx-column; for DyD_yDy​ they go into the yyy-column. Mixing them up silently swaps your answers.

Key takeaways

  • Solving the general system ax+by=eax + by = eax+by=e, cx+dy=fcx + dy = fcx+dy=f by elimination makes the quantity ad−bcad - bcad−bc appear as the denominator of both unknowns. That quantity is the determinant DDD.
  • The 2×22 \times 22×2 determinant is ∣abcd∣=ad−bc\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc​ac​bd​​=ad−bc, main diagonal minus anti-diagonal. The bars are not absolute value; a determinant can be negative.
  • Cramer’s rule: x=Dx/Dx = D_x / Dx=Dx​/D and y=Dy/Dy = D_y / Dy=Dy​/D, where DxD_xDx​ and DyD_yDy​ replace the xxx- or yyy-column of DDD with the constants. The same pattern gives x,y,zx, y, zx,y,z in the 3×33 \times 33×3 case.
  • A 3×33 \times 33×3 determinant is found by cofactor expansion along the first row, with signs plus, minus, plus.
  • A square system has a unique solution exactly when D≠0D \ne 0D=0 (proved above for two variables; the same test holds for three). When D=0D = 0D=0 the system is degenerate, with either no solution or infinitely many, which ties the determinant to the chapter’s trichotomy.
  • Prefer Cramer’s rule for a single unknown or for theory; prefer row reduction for large systems.

A bit of history (optional)

Give a mathematician five points on a page. Which curve runs through all five of them? That question was live in the seventeen hundreds, and it is harder than it sounds. You pick a family of curves, each one carrying several unknown coefficients. Then you demand that every point satisfy the equation. Each point hands you one linear equation, and the unknowns are the coefficients themselves.

Gabriel Cramer worked on exactly this problem, in Geneva, a city that is now Swiss. He wanted curves, not arithmetic. A system was a nuisance standing in his way, and he had to clear it again and again. So what he wanted was a formula: the answer already written, waiting for numbers to be dropped in.

His book on curves appeared in 1750, and the rule you have just learned is in an appendix at the back. It says nothing whatever about how to solve a system. It says what the answer is. Every unknown is a ratio, with the same quantity underneath every time, and each numerator built by replacing one column with the constants. That shared denominator must not be zero, and it is zero in exactly the cases where no single answer exists.

That is why the rule survives in a course that also teaches row reduction. Row reduction is a procedure, and it delivers numbers. Cramer’s rule is a statement about the answer. That is what you need when the coefficients are letters, or when a single unknown out of three is all you actually want.

  1. Problem 1 A missing corner entry

    Find the real number kkk such that ∣2−34k∣=10\begin{vmatrix} 2&-3\\4&k\end{vmatrix}=10​24​−3k​​=10.

    1. Hint 1

      The determinant is a signed combination of the two diagonal products.

    2. Hint 2

      Write a linear equation involving the unknown entry.

    Answer

    k=−1k=-1k=−1.

    Full solution

    Main diagonal minus the other diagonal gives

    2k−(−3)(4)=102k+12=10k=−1\begin{aligned} 2k-(-3)(4)&=10 \\ 2k+12&=10 \\ k&=-1 \end{aligned}2k−(−3)(4)2k+12k​=10=10=−1​

    Check:

    2(−1)−(−3)(4)=10.2(-1)-(-3)(4)=10.2(−1)−(−3)(4)=10.

    Answer

    k=−1k=-1k=−1.

    Key idea

    A determinant value can determine an unknown matrix entry.

  2. Problem 2 Elimination on a specific system

    For 3x−2y=83x-2y=83x−2y=8 and 5x+y=95x+y=95x+y=9, eliminate yyy by adding the first equation to twice the second. Identify the coefficient of xxx that remains.

    1. Hint 1

      Track the coefficient arithmetic separately from the constants.

    2. Hint 2

      Use main diagonal minus the other diagonal on the original coefficient array.

    Answer

    131313.

    Full solution

    The stated combination gives

    13x=2613x=2613x=26

    The coefficient determinant in the original equation order is

    D=3(1)−(−2)(5)D=3(1)-(-2)(5)D=3(1)−(−2)(5)

    Thus D=13D=13D=13, matching the surviving coefficient, because scaling by d=1d=1d=1 and b=−2b=-2b=−2 is exactly the elimination that produces DDD.

    Since it is nonzero, it permits division and forces one xxx value.

    Answer

    131313.

    Key idea

    The determinant appears as the surviving variable coefficient in elimination.

  3. Problem 3 Three short rows

    Evaluate ∣0−21310142∣\begin{vmatrix}0&-2&1\\3&1&0\\1&4&2\end{vmatrix}​031​−214​102​​ by expanding along its first row.

    1. Hint 1

      Each first-row entry multiplies the determinant formed by removing its row and column.

    2. Hint 2

      The signs across that row alternate plus, minus, plus.

    Answer

    232323.

    Full solution

    The first entry contributes zero.

    The minor under the middle entry is 3(2)−0(1)=63(2)-0(1)=63(2)−0(1)=6, and the last minor is 3(4)−1(1)=113(4)-1(1)=113(4)−1(1)=11.

    D=0−(−2)(6)+1(11)D=23\begin{aligned} D&=0-(-2)(6)+1(11) \\ D&=23 \end{aligned}DD​=0−(−2)(6)+1(11)=23​

    Answer

    232323.

    Key idea

    A negative middle entry still receives the subtraction in cofactor expansion.

  4. Problem 4 A straight-line model

    A model f(t)=at+bf(t)=at+bf(t)=at+b satisfies f(−1)=6f(-1)=6f(−1)=6 and f(2)=−3f(2)=-3f(2)=−3. Use Cramer’s rule to find aaa and bbb.

    1. Hint 1

      The two function values form a system for the model’s coefficients.

    2. Hint 2

      Replace the coefficient column for the unknown you are finding with the two outputs.

    Answer

    a=−3a=-3a=−3, b=3b=3b=3.

    Full solution

    The equations are −a+b=6-a+b=6−a+b=6 and 2a+b=−32a+b=-32a+b=−3.

    In variable order a,ba,ba,b,

    D=(−1)(1)−(1)(2)D=−3Da=(6)(1)−(1)(−3)Da=9\begin{aligned} D&=(-1)(1)-(1)(2) \\ D&=-3 \\ D_a&=(6)(1)-(1)(-3) \\ D_a&=9 \end{aligned}DDDa​Da​​=(−1)(1)−(1)(2)=−3=(6)(1)−(1)(−3)=9​

    The other replacement gives

    Db=(−1)(−3)−(6)(2)Db=−9a=9−3b=−9−3\begin{aligned} D_b&=(-1)(-3)-(6)(2) \\ D_b&=-9 \\ a&=\frac9{-3} \\ b&=\frac{-9}{-3} \end{aligned}Db​Db​ab​=(−1)(−3)−(6)(2)=−9=−39​=−3−9​​

    So a=−3a=-3a=−3 and b=3b=3b=3.

    Check f(−1)f(-1)f(−1):

    −3(−1)+3=6.-3(-1)+3=6.−3(−1)+3=6.

    Check f(2)f(2)f(2):

    −3(2)+3=−3.-3(2)+3=-3.−3(2)+3=−3.

    Answer

    a=−3a=-3a=−3, b=3b=3b=3.

    Key idea

    Cramer’s rule applies when the unknowns are model coefficients as well as coordinates.

  5. Problem 5 An isolated column

    Use Cramer’s rule to find xxx in x+z=4x+z=4x+z=4, 2y−z=32y-z=32y−z=3, and x+y+z=7x+y+z=7x+y+z=7.

    1. Hint 1

      Keep the zero coefficient in each missing-variable position.

    2. Hint 2

      Compute the original determinant and the one with the first column replaced.

    Answer

    x=1x=1x=1.

    Full solution

    In order x,y,zx,y,zx,y,z,

    D=∣10102−1111∣D=\begin{vmatrix}1&0&1\\0&2&-1\\1&1&1\end{vmatrix}D=​101​021​1−11​​

    Cofactor expansion gives

    D=1(3)+1(−2)=1\begin{aligned} D&=1(3)+1(-2) \\ &=1 \end{aligned}D​=1(3)+1(−2)=1​

    Replacing the first column gives

    Dx=∣40132−1711∣D_x=\begin{vmatrix}4&0&1\\3&2&-1\\7&1&1\end{vmatrix}Dx​=​437​021​1−11​​

    Then

    Dx=4(3)+1(3−14)Dx=1x=11\begin{aligned} D_x&=4(3)+1(3-14) \\ D_x&=1 \\ x&=\frac11 \end{aligned}Dx​Dx​x​=4(3)+1(3−14)=1=11​​

    The check can be made with y=3y=3y=3 and z=3z=3z=3: 1+3=41+3=41+3=4, 6−3=36-3=36−3=3, and 1+3+3=71+3+3=71+3+3=7.

    Key idea

    One desired unknown requires its replacement determinant and the shared coefficient determinant.

  6. Problem 6 All three readings

    Use Cramer’s rule to solve x−y=1x-y=1x−y=1, y+z=3y+z=3y+z=3, and 2x+z=72x+z=72x+z=7.

    1. Hint 1

      Write zero coefficients where an equation omits a variable.

    2. Hint 2

      Replace one column at a time, keeping the other two columns fixed.

    Answer

    (x,y,z)=(3,2,1)(x,y,z)=(3,2,1)(x,y,z)=(3,2,1).

    Full solution

    The coefficient determinant is

    D=∣1−10011201∣D=\begin{vmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&1\\2&0&1\end{vmatrix}D=​102​−110​011​​

    Its first-row expansion gives

    D=1(1)−(−1)(−2)=−1\begin{aligned} D&=1(1)-(-1)(-2) \\ &=-1 \end{aligned}D​=1(1)−(−1)(−2)=−1​

    The replacements are

    Dx=∣1−10311701∣D_x=\begin{vmatrix}1&-1&0\\3&1&1\\7&0&1\end{vmatrix}Dx​=​137​−110​011​​ Dy=∣110031271∣D_y=\begin{vmatrix}1&1&0\\0&3&1\\2&7&1\end{vmatrix}Dy​=​102​137​011​​ Dz=∣1−11013207∣D_z=\begin{vmatrix}1&-1&1\\0&1&3\\2&0&7\end{vmatrix}Dz​=​102​−110​137​​

    Expanding along the top row gives

    Dx=1+(3−7)Dx=−3Dy=(3−7)−(−2)Dy=−2Dz=7−6−2Dz=−1\begin{aligned} D_x&=1+(3-7) \\ D_x&=-3 \\ D_y&=(3-7)-(-2) \\ D_y&=-2 \\ D_z&=7-6-2 \\ D_z&=-1 \end{aligned}Dx​Dx​Dy​Dy​Dz​Dz​​=1+(3−7)=−3=(3−7)−(−2)=−2=7−6−2=−1​

    Dividing each by the nonzero D=−1D=-1D=−1 gives (3,2,1)(3,2,1)(3,2,1).

    Each coordinate uses the determinant with its own coefficient column replaced.

  7. Problem 7 Which inputs distinguish settings

    The equations au+b=5au+b=5au+b=5 and av+b=8av+b=8av+b=8 have unknowns a,ba,ba,b and real parameters u,vu,vu,v. Determine when they have a unique solution, and describe what happens otherwise.

    1. Hint 1

      Arrange the coefficients of a,ba,ba,b in a two-row array.

    2. Hint 2

      Consider what the two left sides become when its determinant is zero.

    Answer

    Unique when u≠vu\ne vu=v; no solution when u=vu=vu=v.

    Full solution

    The coefficient determinant is

    D=u−vD=u-vD=u−v

    It is nonzero exactly when u≠vu\ne vu=v.

    If u=vu=vu=v, the left sides are identical, yet the right sides are 555 and 888; subtraction gives 0=30=30=3.

    Thus this particular zero-determinant case is inconsistent.

    Answer

    Unique when u≠vu\ne vu=v; no solution when u=vu=vu=v.

    Key idea

    The coefficient determinant tests uniqueness, while the constants decide a degenerate case.

  8. Problem 8 One vanished numerator

    A two-variable system has D=−6D=-6D=−6 and Dx=0D_x=0Dx​=0. A student says the system has no solution since one determinant is zero. Is that correct? State what is known about xxx and uniqueness.

    1. Hint 1

      Distinguish the coefficient determinant from a replacement determinant.

    2. Hint 2

      Identify which determinant is the divisor in Cramer’s rule.

    Answer

    No; the system has a unique solution and x=0x=0x=0.

    Full solution

    The coefficient determinant is nonzero, so the system is uniquely solvable.

    The first coordinate is

    x=0−6x=\frac0{-6}x=−60​

    Thus x=0x=0x=0.

    A zero numerator permits a zero coordinate; it does not block division.

    Answer

    No; the system has a unique solution and x=0x=0x=0.

    Key idea

    A zero replacement determinant can give a zero coordinate in a unique solution.

  9. Problem 9 Unchanged coefficients

    A square system has coefficient determinant zero. A student changes just one right side and claims this can make the solution unique. Is this possible? Explain.

    1. Hint 1

      Changing a right side does not alter the coefficient matrix.

    2. Hint 2

      Use the uniqueness condition for a square system.

    Answer

    No; the system remains nonunique.

    Full solution

    The coefficient matrix is unchanged, so

    Dnew=0D_{\mathrm{new}}=0Dnew​=0

    A square system has a unique solution exactly when its coefficient determinant is nonzero.

    Changing a right side may switch between no solutions and infinitely many, but cannot produce a unique solution here.

    Answer

    No; the system remains nonunique.

    Key idea

    A change to constants cannot repair a zero coefficient determinant.

  10. Problem 10 One unknown, not the whole solve

    A solver has a dense system of six equations in six unknowns, known to have a unique solution, but needs only the value of one particular unknown, not a full solution. Recommend whether to use Cramer's rule or row reduction, and explain.

    1. Hint 1

      Row reduction's cost for one unknown depends on where it falls in the elimination order; Cramer's rule's cost does not.

    2. Hint 2

      Cramer's rule needs only the coefficient determinant and one replacement determinant for a single unknown, whichever one it is.

    Answer

    Use Cramer's rule.

    Full solution

    Row reduction's cost for one unknown depends on where it falls in the elimination order, and can require back-substituting through most of the other five, even though only one value is wanted.

    Cramer's rule pays a fixed cost no matter which unknown is wanted: the coefficient determinant DDD and the one replacement determinant for that variable, with no back-substitution through the others.

    That fixed cost does not grow with how far the wanted unknown sits from the end of the elimination order, unlike row reduction's.

    Answer

    Use Cramer's rule.

    Key idea

    Needing only one unknown, not a full solve, is when Cramer's rule's fixed cost per unknown matters most against row reduction's cost, which depends on elimination order.

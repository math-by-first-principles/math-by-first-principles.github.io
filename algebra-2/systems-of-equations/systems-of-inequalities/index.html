Systems of Inequalities

Intuition

The last two lessons treated a system’s solution set as an object in its own right. It was a single point, a whole line, or a whole plane once a third variable was in play, or nothing at all when the system is inconsistent. Swap one equation’s equals sign for an inequality symbol, and that single condition no longer pins the answer to one of those: it opens into a whole region instead. Every point inside that region has room to move in any direction and stay inside it, because a limit describes a whole range of allowable choices, not one exact value. Such a limit might be “you have at most 101010 hours” or “spend no more than your budget”. A single inequality’s solution set is a whole two-dimensional region, not a thin curve or a point. This lesson is about those regions: how one inequality splits the plane, and how several of them together carve out the shape you are allowed to live in. It then shows how to find the best value the region allows, out of the infinitely many points it holds.

Learning goals

  • Shade the half-plane a test point satisfies
  • Draw the boundary solid or dashed by the symbol
  • Intersect the half-planes to get the feasible region
  • Find the corners by solving the boundary-line systems
  • Evaluate a linear objective at every corner
  • Allow for an empty or unbounded region

A single inequality carves the plane in two

Start with one linear inequality in two variables, such as x+2y<6x + 2y < 6x+2y<6. Its solutions are all the pairs (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that make it true, and there are infinitely many of them. To see their shape, first draw the boundary line x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6, the set where the two sides are exactly equal. That line does not solve the inequality (equality is not “less than”), but it is the fence between the pairs that do and the pairs that do not.

The key fact is that the line splits the plane into two sides, and each side is entirely solutions or entirely non-solutions, with no mixing. So you never test more than one point: check a single point off the line, and its verdict settles the whole side it belongs to.

That gives the whole procedure for graphing one inequality. Draw the boundary line. Make it solid when the inequality allows equality (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥), because then the line’s own points are solutions. Make it dashed when the inequality does not (<<< or >>>), because then those points are excluded. Then pick a test point not on the line and substitute it. Shade the side the test point lands on if it makes the inequality true, or the other side if it makes it false. The origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) is the easiest test point whenever the line does not pass through it.

Worked example 1 Graph the inequality x+2y<6x + 2y < 6x+2y<6

Begin with the boundary line x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6. Two points fix it. Setting x=0x = 0x=0 gives 2y=62y = 62y=6, so y=3y = 3y=3 and the point (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3); setting y=0y = 0y=0 gives x=6x = 6x=6, the point (6,0)(6, 0)(6,0). The inequality is strict, so the boundary is drawn dashed: points on the line are not solutions.

Now choose which side to shade by testing one point off the line. The origin is not on this line, so use it:

0+2(0)=0,0<6 is true.0 + 2(0) = 0, \quad 0 < 6 \text{ is true}.0+2(0)=0,0<6 is true.

Because (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) satisfies the inequality, the whole side of the line containing the origin is solutions. Shade that side. The solution set is that entire open half-plane, with the dashed boundary excluded.

Why does testing one point settle the whole side? Moving away from the boundary in one direction steadily increases x+2yx + 2yx+2y, and moving the other way steadily decreases it. So a point can flip from making the inequality true to making it false only by crossing the boundary itself, never without it. That is why the origin’s verdict in the example above settled the entire lower-left side at once. Here is that reason made precise, for any boundary ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c.

Why the solutions of ax+by<cax + by < cax+by<c fill one whole side of the line#

The boundary is the line ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c. Take the common case b>0b > 0b>0 first, and solve the boundary for yyy:

y=c−axb.y = \frac{c - ax}{b}.y=bc−ax​.

At a given xxx, this is the height of the line. A point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) sits above the line exactly when its own height beats the line’s height there, y>c−axby > \frac{c - ax}{b}y>bc−ax​. Multiply both sides by the positive number bbb; from the lesson on linear inequalities, a positive multiplier keeps an inequality’s direction, so

by>c−ax,that isax+by>c.by > c - ax, \quad\text{that is}\quad ax + by > c.by>c−ax,that isax+by>c.

So “above the line” is exactly the set where ax+by>cax + by > cax+by>c, and running the same steps with the inequality reversed shows “below the line” is exactly where ax+by<cax + by < cax+by<c. Every point of the plane is above, on, or below the line, so these three conditions partition the plane, and each strict inequality names one full side.

If instead b<0b < 0b<0, multiplying by bbb reverses the direction (again from the linear inequalities lesson), so “above” becomes ax+by<cax + by < cax+by<c: the side attached to each symbol flips. That is precisely why you cannot read the shaded side straight off the symbol without watching the sign of bbb. That direction flip is also why the safe method is to test an actual point. And if b=0b = 0b=0 the boundary is the vertical line x=cax = \frac{c}{a}x=ac​, which splits the plane into x<cax < \frac{c}{a}x<ac​ and x>cax > \frac{c}{a}x>ac​; there you solve for xxx instead of yyy.

In every case the boundary cuts the plane into two sides, one where ax+by>cax + by > cax+by>c and one where ax+by<cax + by < cax+by<c. Testing a single point off the line reveals which side is which.

∎

Answer choices

Test the origin by substituting it into the inequality.

3(0)−0=0,0≥6 is false3(0) - 0 = 0, \quad 0 \ge 6 \text{ is false}3(0)−0=0,0≥6 is false

Since 0≥60 \ge 60≥6 is false, (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) is not a solution, so the origin lies on the side you do not shade. The symbol is ≥\ge≥, which counts the points on the line as solutions, so the boundary is solid; a strict >>> would make it dashed.

Stacking inequalities: the feasible region

A system of inequalities asks for all of its conditions at once. A pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) solves the system only when it solves every inequality, so it must lie in every one of the half-planes at the same time. The solution set is therefore the intersection of the half-planes: the region where all the shaded sides overlap. This is the two-variable echo of the “and” from one-variable inequalities, where joining conditions with “and” meant intersecting their solution sets. The overlap region has a name worth knowing, the feasible region, because in an applied problem it is exactly the set of choices that are actually allowed.

Here is a concrete system to graph. A farm plants xxx acres of wheat and yyy acres of barley. Irrigating an acre of wheat takes 222 hours and an acre of barley 111 hour, with 101010 irrigation hours available, so 2x+y≤102x + y \le 102x+y≤10. Harvesting takes 111 hour per acre for either crop, with 777 harvesting hours available, so x+y≤7x + y \le 7x+y≤7. You cannot plant a negative number of acres, which forces x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and y≥0y \ge 0y≥0. Those four inequalities are the system, and its solution set is every planting plan that respects all of the limits together.

Take the pair (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3). It satisfies three of the four conditions: both coordinates are nonnegative, and 4+3=7≤74 + 3 = 7 \le 74+3=7≤7. But 2(4)+3=112(4) + 3 = 112(4)+3=11, which fails 2x+y≤102x + y \le 102x+y≤10. Satisfying most of the conditions is not enough, so (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3) does not solve the system. Now check (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4): 2(3)+4=10≤102(3) + 4 = 10 \le 102(3)+4=10≤10 and 3+4=7≤73 + 4 = 7 \le 73+4=7≤7, and both coordinates are nonnegative, so (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4) meets every inequality and does belong to the solution set.

Worked example 2 Graph the system and find its corners

The four inequalities are

2x+y≤10,x+y≤7,x≥0,y≥0.2x + y \le 10, \qquad x + y \le 7, \qquad x \ge 0, \qquad y \ge 0.2x+y≤10,x+y≤7,x≥0,y≥0.

The two conditions x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and y≥0y \ge 0y≥0 confine everything to the first quadrant. Within it, graph each slanted boundary and keep the side each inequality allows. Here 2x+y≤102x + y \le 102x+y≤10 holds below its line (test the origin: 0≤100 \le 100≤10 is true), and x+y≤7x + y \le 7x+y≤7 holds below its line as well. Keeping only the common overlap leaves the four-sided feasible region shown below.

Its corners are where two boundaries cross, and each corner is the solution of a two-equation system. The interesting corner is where irrigation and harvesting hours are both used to the full, where 2x+y=102x + y = 102x+y=10 and x+y=7x + y = 7x+y=7 meet. Subtract the second equation from the first to eliminate yyy:

(2x+y)−(x+y)=10−7  ⇒  x=3.(2x + y) - (x + y) = 10 - 7 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 3.(2x+y)−(x+y)=10−7⇒x=3.

Then y=7−3=4y = 7 - 3 = 4y=7−3=4, giving the corner (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4). The other three corners are read off directly: the origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), the point (5,0)(5, 0)(5,0) where 2x+y=102x + y = 102x+y=10 meets the xxx-axis, and (0,7)(0, 7)(0,7) where x+y=7x + y = 7x+y=7 meets the yyy-axis. So the feasible region is the quadrilateral with corners (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), (5,0)(5, 0)(5,0), (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4), and (0,7)(0, 7)(0,7).

The same test runs in reverse, which is how you write a system from a picture. Given a shaded region, each boundary line supplies one inequality: write the line’s equation, then fix the direction by testing a point you know is inside the region. That interior point must satisfy the inequality, so choose the symbol that makes it true. Use ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥ when the boundary is solid, and <<< or >>> when it is dashed. Doing this for every boundary rebuilds the whole system from the drawing.

For example, take the boundary x+y=7x + y = 7x+y=7 from the graph above, drawn solid. Pick a point clearly inside the shaded region, say (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1), and substitute: 1+1=2≤71 + 1 = 2 \le 71+1=2≤7, which is true. So the boundary’s inequality is x+y≤7x + y \le 7x+y≤7, matching the solid line.

Check your understanding

On the same graph, the other boundary 2x+y=102x + y = 102x+y=10 is also drawn solid, and the interior point (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) gives 2(1)+1=32(1) + 1 = 32(1)+1=3. Which inequality does that boundary contribute to the system?

Answer choices

Substituting the interior point gives 333, and 3≤103 \le 103≤10 is the true statement, so that is the inequality this boundary supplies. The boundary is solid, so the symbol includes equality: ≤\le≤, not <<<.

Check your understanding

The two boundary lines x+y=5x + y = 5x+y=5 and 2x−y=42x - y = 42x−y=4 meet at a corner of a feasible region. What are its coordinates?

Answer choices

A corner (also called a vertex) is where two boundaries cross, so solve the two equations as a system. Adding them eliminates yyy.

(x+y)+(2x−y)=5+4  ⇒  3x=9(x + y) + (2x - y) = 5 + 4 \;\Rightarrow\; 3x = 9(x+y)+(2x−y)=5+4⇒3x=9

So x=3x = 3x=3, and then y=5−x=2y = 5 - x = 2y=5−x=2. The corner is (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2). Finding a region's corners is exactly the elimination you already use for systems of equations, run on the boundary lines.

Optimizing over the region: linear programming

A feasible region collects every allowed choice, but usually you want the best one. On the farm, “best” means the most profit. Suppose each acre of wheat earns a profit of 303030 dollars and each acre of barley 202020 dollars. Measuring profit in tens of dollars, the farm wants to maximize the objective

P=3x+2yP = 3x + 2yP=3x+2y

over the feasible region. Maximizing or minimizing a linear objective over a region cut out by linear inequalities is called linear programming. When the region is a bounded polygon with corners, linear programming has a strikingly simple answer, that you need only check the corners.

Why a linear objective is optimized at a corner#

Fix the objective P=3x+2yP = 3x + 2yP=3x+2y. For each number ccc, the equation 3x+2y=c3x + 2y = c3x+2y=c is a straight line, and because they all share the slope −32-\tfrac{3}{2}−23​, the lines for different values of ccc are parallel. They form a family sweeping across the plane, and moving to a larger ccc shifts the line toward the upper right, the direction in which PPP grows.

Maximizing PPP over the feasible region means finding the largest ccc for which the line 3x+2y=c3x + 2y = c3x+2y=c still touches the region. Picture sliding the line in the increasing direction until it is about to leave: the last point it touches is where PPP is greatest on the region.

The feasible region is convex, meaning the straight segment joining any two of its points stays entirely inside it. This holds because each inequality’s half-plane is convex, and the region is their intersection, so a segment lying inside every half-plane lies inside the region. On a convex polygon, the final contact of a sliding line is either a corner or an edge parallel to that line. In the edge case, its endpoints are corners. Either way a corner attains the maximum.

The same argument with the line sliding the other way puts a minimizer at a corner too, which gives the corner point principle. Stated carefully, the principle applies to a feasible region whose boundaries are solid and which has at least one corner. On such a region, wherever an optimum of a linear objective exists, at least one corner attains it. So to optimize over a bounded polygon of that kind, evaluate the objective at each corner and take the best value. If the region is unbounded in the increasing direction, the slide never stops and no maximum exists. If a boundary is dashed, so the region is open, the objective can creep toward a best value it never actually reaches. And when the objective line is parallel to an edge, that whole edge is optimal, though its corners still attain the value. So: on a region with solid boundaries and at least one corner, whenever an optimum exists, a corner is among the points that attain it.

∎

Worked example 3 Find the most profitable plan

The feasible region from the previous example has corners (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), (5,0)(5, 0)(5,0), (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4), and (0,7)(0, 7)(0,7). By the corner point principle, the maximum of P=3x+2yP = 3x + 2yP=3x+2y sits at one of them, so evaluate PPP at all four:

P(0,0)=0,P(5,0)=15,P(0,7)=14,P(3,4)=17.P(0,0) = 0, \quad P(5,0) = 15, \quad P(0,7) = 14, \quad P(3,4) = 17.P(0,0)=0,P(5,0)=15,P(0,7)=14,P(3,4)=17.

The largest value is 171717, at the corner (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4). So the best plan plants 333 acres of wheat and 444 acres of barley, using all 101010 irrigation hours and all 777 harvesting hours. That plan earns a profit of 171717 tens of dollars, that is 170170170 dollars.

Notice that neither lopsided plan wins. Planting as much wheat as possible, (5,0)(5, 0)(5,0), earns 151515, and leaning hard on barley, (0,7)(0, 7)(0,7), earns 141414. The optimum mixes the two crops, which is exactly the kind of balance linear programming is built to find.

When the region is empty or unbounded

Not every system produces a tidy polygon. Two other outcomes carry straight over from the consistent-or-inconsistent vocabulary of the equation lessons, and both matter for optimization.

Worked example 4 A region can be empty or unbounded

Two quick cases show the range of possibilities.

First, an empty region. The system x+y≥5x + y \ge 5x+y≥5 and x+y≤2x + y \le 2x+y≤2 asks for a sum that is at least 555 and at most 222 at the same time. No point can satisfy both, since a number cannot be ≥5\ge 5≥5 and ≤2\le 2≤2 at once, so the two half-planes never overlap and the feasible region is empty, ∅\varnothing∅. This is the two-variable version of an inconsistent system: the constraints contradict one another and no choice can meet them all. There is nothing to optimize.

Second, an unbounded region. The system x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, y≥0y \ge 0y≥0, and x+y≥4x + y \ge 4x+y≥4 keeps everything in the first quadrant that lies on or above the line x+y=4x + y = 4x+y=4, and that region runs outward forever toward the upper right. On it the objective x+yx + yx+y can be made as large as you like. So the objective has no maximum, but it does have a minimum of 444, reached all along the edge x+y=4x + y = 4x+y=4, including the corners (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0) and (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4). So on an unbounded region an optimum can fail to exist in one direction while still existing in the other. Because this region has solid boundaries and corners, its existing minimum is attained at a corner, matching the corner point principle.

Check your understanding

The system x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, y≥0y \ge 0y≥0, x+2y≤4x + 2y \le 4x+2y≤4, and x+2y≥10x + 2y \ge 10x+2y≥10 describes a feasible region. Which best describes it, and what follows for optimizing an objective over it?

Answer choices

The last two constraints demand x+2y≤4x + 2y \le 4x+2y≤4 and x+2y≥10x + 2y \ge 10x+2y≥10 at the same time. No number can be both at most 444 and at least 101010, so those two half-planes never overlap, no matter what the nonnegativity constraints allow. The feasible region is empty, the same way an inconsistent system of equations has no solution. With no point in the region, there is nothing to evaluate the objective at, so no optimum, maximum or minimum, exists.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Shading the side without testing. The symbol alone does not tell you the side once the inequality is not solved for yyy. Substitute a test point (the origin is easiest) and shade the side that makes the statement true. Never guess the side from the direction of the symbol.
  • Testing a point that lies on the boundary. A test point on the line gives equality, which decides nothing. Pick a point clearly off the line; if the boundary passes through the origin, test something like (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0) instead.
  • Mixing up solid and dashed. A solid boundary means the line’s points are included (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥); a dashed boundary means they are excluded (<<< or >>>). The corners of a feasible region only count as solutions when their boundaries are solid.
  • Forgetting the hidden constraints. When xxx and yyy count or measure amounts that cannot be negative, such as acres planted or hours worked, include x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and y≥0y \ge 0y≥0 even when the story never says so. Leaving them out can add phantom corners with negative coordinates.
  • Dividing by a negative without reversing the symbol. If you rewrite an inequality by multiplying or dividing by a negative to solve for yyy, the symbol must flip. Keep the old direction and you will shade the wrong side. This is the same negative-flip rule from the lesson on linear inequalities. A correct rewrite never moves the region itself, only which side the final symbol points to.
  • Hunting for the optimum inside the region. For a nonconstant linear objective on a bounded region with solid boundaries, the optimum is attained on the boundary, and at least one corner reaches it. On such a region, evaluate the corners rather than interior points. Random interior points waste effort and miss the best value.

Key takeaways

  • A single linear inequality in two variables has a half-plane of solutions. The boundary line ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c splits the plane, and every point on one side satisfies the inequality while every point on the other does not.
  • Graph it by drawing the boundary solid for ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥ and dashed for <<< or >>>, then testing one point off the line to decide which side to shade.
  • A system of inequalities has the intersection of the half-planes as its solution set, the feasible region. The region’s corners are the solutions of the boundary-line systems, found by the same substitution or elimination you use for equations.
  • Linear programming maximizes or minimizes a linear objective over a feasible region. By the corner point principle, on a region with solid boundaries and at least one corner, whenever an optimum exists, at least one corner attains it. Evaluate the objective at every corner and take the best.
  • A feasible region can be empty (contradictory constraints, nothing to optimize) or unbounded (an optimum may exist in one direction only). On a region with solid boundaries and at least one corner, whenever an optimum exists, at least one corner attains it.

A bit of history (optional)

A plywood factory has a row of machines and several kinds of board to cut. Any machine can cut any board. But each one is quicker at some jobs than at others. How should the week be shared out among them?

That question reached Leonid Kantorovich in 1939, a young mathematician in the Soviet city of Leningrad. The factory wanted more output from the machines it already owned. He saw at once that guessing was hopeless. The schedules that obey every limit are far too numerous to test one at a time. Then he saw something better. The limits are linear, and so is the quantity to be pushed upward. The acceptable plans fill a region with corners, exactly the sort of region you shaded in this lesson.

Then he did the harder thing, and said in public that whole industries could be run this way. Planning by mathematics was an awkward claim in that country at that moment. His work waited years for an audience.

The subject he opened is called linear programming. The word programming misleads nearly everyone who meets it. It has nothing to do with computers, which barely existed. It uses the older sense of a program: a schedule, a plan, the order in which supplies and people are to move. Linear programming is planning under straight-line limits, and your feasible region is one such plan, drawn small enough to see.

Exponential Growth and Decay

Intuition

An account that pays you 100 dollars a year and an account that pays you 10%10\%10% a year can look identical for one year. The 100 dollars is a fixed amount that never changes. The 10%10\%10% is calculated from whatever is sitting in the account right now, so as the balance grows, the yearly gain grows with it. Growth that feeds on its own current size is exponential growth, and it is why a modest percentage, left alone long enough, eventually overtakes any fixed amount. Run the same idea backwards, losing a fixed percentage every period, and you get exponential decay. That decay governs a car’s resale value, a drug leaving your bloodstream, and the atoms in a radioactive sample.

Learning goals

  • Multiply by the growth factor b=1+rb = 1 + rb=1+r each step
  • Distinguish the rate from the factor, since r=b−1r = b - 1r=b−1
  • Read growth, standstill or decay off bbb
  • Divide the rate and multiply the time when compounding nnn times
  • Chain percent changes by multiplying factors
  • Take a logarithm for doubling time or half-life

That is the whole distinction, and it is worth stating in one line:

  • A fixed amount added each step gives linear change.
  • A fixed percentage added each step gives exponential change.

Everything else in this lesson follows from turning that second sentence into algebra.

Where the model comes from

Take a quantity and increase it by a rate rrr, written as a decimal, so 10%10\%10% means r=0.10r = 0.10r=0.10. The increase is rrr times the current amount, and the new amount is the old amount plus that increase:

Anew  =  Aold+r⋅Aold  =  Aold(1+r).A_{\text{new}} \;=\; A_{\text{old}} + r \cdot A_{\text{old}} \;=\; A_{\text{old}}(1 + r).Anew​=Aold​+r⋅Aold​=Aold​(1+r).

Factoring out AoldA_{\text{old}}Aold​ is the move that makes the whole subject work. One step of growth is one multiplication, by the number 1+r1 + r1+r. That number gets its own name.

The growth rate is rrr, the fraction added per step. The growth factor is b=1+rb = 1 + rb=1+r, the number you multiply by. They are not the same number, and confusing them is the single most common error in this topic. A rate of r=0.07r = 0.07r=0.07 goes with a factor of b=1.07b = 1.07b=1.07. Running it the other way, r=b−1r = b - 1r=b−1, so a factor of b=0.85b = 0.85b=0.85 means r=−0.15r = -0.15r=−0.15, a 15%15\%15% decrease.

Watch the factor pile up. Account E from the opening table multiplies by 1.101.101.10 every year, so year 333 means three multiplications by 1.101.101.10, not one:

1000(1.10)(1.10)(1.10)=1000(1.10)3=1331,1000(1.10)(1.10)(1.10) = 1000(1.10)^3 = 1331,1000(1.10)(1.10)(1.10)=1000(1.10)3=1331,

which is exactly the 133113311331 dollars the table shows. The exponent is just a count of how many times the same factor has been applied, so ttt steps of growth multiply by bbb a total of ttt times:

A(t)=P(1+r)t=Pbt.A(t) = P(1+r)^t = Pb^t.A(t)=P(1+r)t=Pbt.

That pattern holds at every whole number of steps, not just the first three, and the same reasoning proves the reverse too: any quantity that changes by the same percentage every step must follow this exact formula. Growth, decay, and reading the factor

The factor bbb decides the entire character of the model. Take a positive starting amount P>0P > 0P>0 and a positive factor bbb. Then exactly one of three things happens:

  • If b>1b > 1b>1, each step multiplies by more than 111, so the amount grows without bound.
  • If b=1b = 1b=1, each step multiplies by 111, so the amount never changes.
  • If 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1, each step multiplies by less than 111, so the amount decays toward zero.

Decay never actually reaches zero. Multiplying a positive number by 0.70.70.7 leaves a smaller positive number, forever, which is why the decay curve below flattens against the horizontal axis without touching it.

Reading a factor out of a sentence takes care. “Grows by 30%30\%30%” keeps the original and adds 30%30\%30% more, so the factor is 1+0.30=1.301 + 0.30 = 1.301+0.30=1.30. “Falls by 30%30\%30%” keeps 70%70\%70% of the original, so the factor is 1−0.30=0.701 - 0.30 = 0.701−0.30=0.70. “Doubles” is a 100%100\%100% increase, factor 222. “Halves” is a 50%50\%50% decrease, factor 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​.

Now watch what happens when you chain two of them. A price rises 30%30\%30% and then falls 30%30\%30%. Many people expect to land back where they started. Multiply the factors:

1.30×0.70=0.91,1.30 \times 0.70 = 0.91,1.30×0.70=0.91,

a 9%9\%9% net loss. The two 30%30\%30%s are percentages of different amounts, so they do not cancel. Successive percent changes never add; their factors multiply. This is not a quirk of the numbers 1.31.31.3 and 0.70.70.7: a rise of rrr followed by a fall of the same rrr always gives (1+r)(1−r)=1−r2(1+r)(1-r) = 1 - r^2(1+r)(1−r)=1−r2, and squaring makes 1−r21 - r^21−r2 less than 111 for every nonzero rrr, so the pair always leaves a net loss.

Compounding more than once a year

Banks rarely wait a whole year to pay interest. A rate quoted as ”6%6\%6% per year, compounded monthly” means the year is cut into 121212 equal periods and each period pays one twelfth of the annual rate. Nothing new is needed: apply the one-step rule with the period rate.

With an annual rate rrr compounded nnn times a year, each period grows by r/nr/nr/n, so each period multiplies by 1+rn1 + \frac{r}{n}1+nr​. Over ttt years there are ntntnt such periods, and ntntnt multiplications give an exponent of ntntnt:

A  =  P(1+rn)nt.A \;=\; P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.A=P(1+nr​)nt.

Divide the rate, multiply the time. Setting n=1n = 1n=1 recovers A=P(1+r)tA = P(1+r)^tA=P(1+r)t, so the annual formula is just the special case with one period a year. Compounding more often pays a little more, because the interest you earn early starts earning interest itself sooner.

Identify the four ingredients. The principal is P=2000P = 2000P=2000 dollars, the annual rate is r=0.06r = 0.06r=0.06, the number of periods per year is n=12n = 12n=12, and the time is t=5t = 5t=5 years.

The monthly rate is r/nr/nr/n and the number of periods is ntntnt:

rn=0.0612=0.005,nt=12⋅5=60.\frac{r}{n} = \frac{0.06}{12} = 0.005, \qquad nt = 12 \cdot 5 = 60.nr​=120.06​=0.005,nt=12⋅5=60.

So the balance multiplies by 1.0051.0051.005 sixty times:

The account holds about 2697.702697.702697.70 dollars. Compare that with the same rate compounded once a year, 2000(1.06)5≈2676.452000(1.06)^5 \approx 2676.452000(1.06)5≈2676.45 dollars. Doubling time and half-life

So far the unknown has always been the amount. The more interesting question runs the other way: how long until the amount reaches some target? That puts the unknown in the exponent, and the logarithm is precisely the tool built to bring an exponent down.

The doubling time is the time it takes for a growing quantity to reach twice its size. Set A(t)=2PA(t) = 2PA(t)=2P and solve:

Pbt=2P⟹bt=2.P b^t = 2P \quad \Longrightarrow \quad b^t = 2.Pbt=2P⟹bt=2.

Look at what just happened: PPP cancelled. The doubling time does not depend on the starting amount at all. A colony of 101010 bacteria and a colony of 101010 million bacteria with the same growth factor take exactly the same time to double. Taking the logarithm of both sides and using the power rule log⁡(bt)=tlog⁡b\log(b^t) = t\log blog(bt)=tlogb,

tlog⁡b=log⁡2⟹t=log⁡2log⁡b.t \log b = \log 2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad t = \frac{\log 2}{\log b}.tlogb=log2⟹t=logblog2​.

The half-life is the mirror image: the time for a decaying quantity to fall to half its size. The same cancellation happens, leaving bt=12b^t = \tfrac{1}{2}bt=21​, so

t=log⁡12log⁡b.t = \frac{\log \frac{1}{2}}{\log b}.t=logblog21​​.

Here log⁡12\log \frac{1}{2}log21​ is negative, and for decay b<1b < 1b<1 makes log⁡b\log blogb negative too, so the quotient of two negatives is a positive time, as it must be.

Because the doubling time is the same everywhere along the curve, it gives a second, often tidier way to write the model. If the doubling time is ddd, then the per-unit factor bbb satisfies bd=2b^d = 2bd=2, so b=21/db = 2^{1/d}b=21/d and therefore bt=2t/db^t = 2^{t/d}bt=2t/d. The same reasoning with a half-life hhh gives b=(12)1/hb = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{1/h}b=(21​)1/h. In short:

A(t)=P⋅2t/dandA(t)=P(12)t/h.A(t) = P \cdot 2^{t/d} \qquad \text{and} \qquad A(t) = P\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/h}.A(t)=P⋅2t/dandA(t)=P(21​)t/h.

Sanity-check the second one at t=ht = ht=h: the exponent is 111 and the amount is exactly P2\tfrac{P}{2}2P​, which is what “half-life” means.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using the rate where the factor belongs. “Grows 6%6\%6% a year” gives the factor 1.061.061.06, not 0.060.060.06. The model A=P(0.06)tA = P(0.06)^tA=P(0.06)t describes something that loses 94%94\%94% of itself every year.
  • Adding percent changes. A 30%30\%30% rise followed by a 30%30\%30% fall is not a wash. Multiply the factors: 1.30×0.70=0.911.30 \times 0.70 = 0.911.30×0.70=0.91, a 9%9\%9% net loss. Percents are taken from different amounts, so they do not cancel; only the factors combine, and they combine by multiplying.
  • Mishandling the compounding period. With nnn periods a year the rate is divided and the time is multiplied: P(1+rn)ntP\left(1 + \tfrac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}P(1+nr​)nt. Writing P(1+rn)tP\left(1 + \tfrac{r}{n}\right)^{t}P(1+nr​)t or P(1+r)ntP(1+r)^{nt}P(1+r)nt changes the answer.
  • Putting the half-life in the wrong place. A half-life of hhh gives (12)t/h\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/h}(21​)t/h, not (12)ht\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{ht}(21​)ht. Test any candidate at t=ht = ht=h, where the amount must come out to exactly half.
  • Thinking the doubling time depends on the starting amount. Solve Pbt=2PPb^t = 2PPbt=2P and the PPP cancels before you do anything else. Starting bigger does not slow the doubling down.
  • Dividing instead of taking a logarithm. From bt=2b^t = 2bt=2 you cannot write t=2/bt = 2/bt=2/b. The unknown is an exponent, so take a log of both sides: tlog⁡b=log⁡2t\log b = \log 2tlogb=log2.

Key takeaways

  • One step of growth is one multiplication. Increasing by a rate rrr multiplies by the growth factor b=1+rb = 1 + rb=1+r, so ttt steps multiply by btb^tbt and A(t)=P(1+r)tA(t) = P(1+r)^tA(t)=P(1+r)t.
  • The rate and the factor are different numbers: r=0.07r = 0.07r=0.07 pairs with b=1.07b = 1.07b=1.07, and a factor of b=0.85b = 0.85b=0.85 means a 15%15\%15% decrease, since r=b−1r = b - 1r=b−1.
  • With P>0P > 0P>0 the model grows when b>1b > 1b>1, stays put when b=1b = 1b=1, and decays toward zero (never reaching it) when 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1.
  • Compounding nnn times a year divides the rate and multiplies the time: A=P(1+rn)ntA = P\left(1 + \tfrac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}A=P(1+nr​)nt.
  • Percent changes chain by multiplying factors, so a 30%30\%30% gain followed by a 30%30\%30% loss leaves 1−(0.3)2=0.911 - (0.3)^2 = 0.911−(0.3)2=0.91 of the original, not all of it.
  • Time questions put the unknown in the exponent, so take a logarithm. The doubling time is t=log⁡2log⁡bt = \dfrac{\log 2}{\log b}t=logblog2​ and the half-life is t=log⁡12log⁡bt = \dfrac{\log \frac{1}{2}}{\log b}t=logblog21​​, and neither depends on the starting amount PPP.
  • A doubling time ddd or a half-life hhh rewrites the model as A=P⋅2t/dA = P \cdot 2^{t/d}A=P⋅2t/d or A=P(12)t/hA = P\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/h}A=P(21​)t/h, without ever solving for bbb or rrr directly.

Why A(t)=P(1+r)tA(t) = P(1+r)^tA(t)=P(1+r)t holds at every step

After ttt steps, A(t)=P(1+r)tA(t) = P(1+r)^tA(t)=P(1+r)t#

Let PPP be the starting amount, and suppose every step multiplies the current amount by the same factor b=1+rb = 1 + rb=1+r. Write A(t)A(t)A(t) for the amount after ttt steps.

At the start no step has happened, so A(0)=PA(0) = PA(0)=P, which agrees with Pb0=P⋅1=PP b^0 = P \cdot 1 = PPb0=P⋅1=P. Now suppose the formula A(t)=PbtA(t) = P b^tA(t)=Pbt holds after some particular whole number of steps ttt. The next step does one thing only: it multiplies by bbb. So

A(t+1)=A(t)⋅b=Pbt⋅b=Pbt+1,A(t+1) = A(t) \cdot b = P b^t \cdot b = P b^{t+1},A(t+1)=A(t)⋅b=Pbt⋅b=Pbt+1,

which is the same formula with t+1t+1t+1 in place of ttt. The formula is therefore true at step 000, and whenever it is true at one step it is true at the next. So the formula is true at every whole number of steps. Restoring b=1+rb = 1 + rb=1+r gives A(t)=P(1+r)tA(t) = P(1+r)^tA(t)=P(1+r)t.

The converse also holds, and it is worth checking, because it says the two descriptions really are the same description. Suppose a quantity follows A(t)=PbtA(t) = P b^tA(t)=Pbt with P>0P > 0P>0 and b>0b > 0b>0. From one step to the next it changes by

A(t+1)−A(t)A(t)=Pbt+1−PbtPbt=b−1,\frac{A(t+1) - A(t)}{A(t)} = \frac{P b^{t+1} - P b^{t}}{P b^{t}} = b - 1,A(t)A(t+1)−A(t)​=PbtPbt+1−Pbt​=b−1,

which does not depend on ttt. So the quantity changes by the same fraction, the same percentage, at every step. Constant percent change per step and the model PbtP b^tPbt each force the other.

One honest caveat about the exponent. The argument above pins the formula down only at whole numbers of steps, since those are the only steps that were taken. Reading the model at times between steps assumes something more: that growth over a stretch of time depends only on the length of that stretch, not on when it starts. The standard exponential function is built to have exactly that property, so extending A(t)=PbtA(t) = P b^tA(t)=Pbt to every real ttt is the natural, but not the only logically possible, choice consistent with the whole-number steps above. That extra assumption is standard throughout this course and is what lets a half step multiply by b1/2b^{1/2}b1/2.

A bit of history (optional)

A clay tablet from a school in Babylon, a city in what is now Iraq, poses a student this exercise: silver is lent at what scholars read as twenty percent a year, so how long before the debt has doubled? The tablet is close to four thousand years old, and it is asking the question this lesson answers with a logarithm.

The scribe had no logarithms. Scholars have conjectured that the scribe used a column of the growth factor, raised to power after power, run down until two entries straddle double, with the value between them estimated by hand. If that reconstruction is right, the result comes out very slightly wrong, but the underlying method is exactly right. Notice what it never asks for: how much silver was lent. Doubling time does not depend on the starting amount, which is why one column of powers could serve every debt in the city.

Introduction to Logarithms

Intuition

You already know that 23=82^3 = 823=8. Now flip the question around: 222 raised to what power gives 888? The number you land on is still 333, but asking it this way means hunting for an exponent instead of computing one. This lesson gives that reversed question a name.

Here is why the reversal is even possible. The Inverse Functions lesson gave you a test for whether a function can be run backward: it has to be one-to-one, so that no output is ever produced by two different inputs, and then the machine that undoes it is a function in its own right. The exponential y=2xy = 2^xy=2x passes that test, since it climbs steadily, never levels off, and never returns to a height it has already reached. So 2x2^x2x has an inverse whether or not anyone has bothered to name it, and that inverse takes a positive number such as 888 and hands back the exponent that produced it, namely 333, exactly the reversed question above. This lesson names it. The logarithm is not a new operation invented to attack a puzzle; it is the exponential function read backward. Every fact you are about to meet falls out of that one sentence.

Learning goals

  • Define the logarithm as the exponential's inverse, switching between y=log⁡bxy = \log_b xy=logb​x and by=xb^y = xby=x
  • Require the logarithm's whole argument to be positive
  • Cancel with log⁡b(bt)=t\log_b(b^t) = tlogb​(bt)=t and blog⁡bx=xb^{\log_b x} = xblogb​x=x
  • Graph y=log⁡bxy = \log_b xy=logb​x as the exponential reflected across y=xy = xy=x
  • Trap an untidy log between straddling integer powers

Why the exponential can be run backward

Fix a base bbb with b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \ne 1b=1, and consider the exponential function f(x)=bxf(x) = b^xf(x)=bx. From the previous lesson you know three things about it. It is defined for every real number xxx. Its outputs are always positive. And its graph is an unbroken curve that rises steadily when b>1b > 1b>1 and falls steadily when 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1.

“Rises steadily” is the part that matters, because it is what makes the function one-to-one: an output can only repeat if the curve ever turns around, and a curve that only climbs never does. Look at the powers of 222:

2−2=14,2−1=12,20=1,21=2,22=4,23=8, …2^{-2} = \tfrac14, \quad 2^{-1} = \tfrac12, \quad 2^0 = 1, \quad 2^1 = 2, \quad 2^2 = 4, \quad 2^3 = 8, \ \dots2−2=41​,2−1=21​,20=1,21=2,22=4,23=8, …

Each step to the right multiplies the previous output by 222, and multiplying a positive number by something bigger than 111 always makes it bigger. So every output beats the one before it, and no two different exponents can ever land on the same power of 222. Nothing about 222 was special: for any base b>1b > 1b>1, multiplying by bbb always increases a positive number, so bxb^xbx climbs steadily and never repeats a value. A base with 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1 is the mirror image. Multiplying by a fraction between 000 and 111 always shrinks a positive number, so bxb^xbx falls steadily instead, and by the same reasoning it is one-to-one too.

The reasoning above is easy to check at whole-number exponents. Making it airtight at every real exponent, including an irrational one like 2\sqrt{2}2​, takes a few more steps. The full argument is in Go deeper at the end of this lesson.

Being one-to-one tells you the inverse exists. To know what to feed it, you need the range of bxb^xbx, because the Inverse Functions lesson pinned the two down together. The inverse’s domain is the original function’s range, and the inverse’s range is the original function’s domain.

Every power of a positive base is positive, so bxb^xbx never outputs 000 and never outputs a negative number. On the other side, with b>1b > 1b>1 the curve climbs past every bound as xxx grows, and as xxx runs off to the left, bx=1b−xb^x = \tfrac{1}{b^{-x}}bx=b−x1​ shrinks toward 000 without ever landing on it. An unbroken, steadily climbing curve that starts arbitrarily close to 000 and ends arbitrarily large passes through every positive height exactly once. A base with 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1 needs no fresh argument: writing b=1cb = \tfrac{1}{c}b=c1​ with c>1c > 1c>1 turns bxb^xbx into c−xc^{-x}c−x, which produces the same collection of outputs as cxc^xcx, merely in the reverse order, so its range is the positive numbers too. Either way, then:

domain of bx=all real numbers,range of bx=all y>0.\text{domain of } b^x = \text{all real numbers}, \qquad \text{range of } b^x = \text{all } y > 0.domain of bx=all real numbers,range of bx=all y>0.

Read that through the inverse rule and the inverse is already fully described before it has a name. Its inputs are the positive numbers, its outputs are all the real numbers, and the number it returns for a given input is an exponent. All that is left is to write it down.

The logarithm is the name of that inverse

Here is the definition, and it is the whole lesson in one sentence.

Fix a base bbb with b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \ne 1b=1. For a positive number xxx, the logarithm of xxx to the base bbb, written log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x, is the unique real exponent you must put on bbb to get xxx.

Unpacked into an equation, the definition is a two-way street between a logarithmic statement and an exponential one:

y=log⁡bx  ⟺  by=x(b>0,  b≠1,  x>0).y = \log_b x \iff b^y = x \qquad (b > 0,\; b \ne 1,\; x > 0).y=logb​x⟺by=x(b>0,b=1,x>0).

Both directions are true, and both are worth checking. Going left to right, if y=log⁡bxy = \log_b xy=logb​x then yyy is by definition the exponent that turns bbb into xxx, so by=xb^y = xby=x. Going right to left, suppose by=xb^y = xby=x for some real yyy. Then xxx is automatically positive, so it is a legal input for the logarithm, and yyy is an exponent that turns bbb into xxx. There cannot be a second such exponent, because btb^tbt is one-to-one in ttt, so yyy has to be the one the logarithm names, and y=log⁡bxy = \log_b xy=logb​x. The equivalence goes both ways, which is why you may swap either form for the other at any moment without losing information.

Say the definition aloud whenever you use it. "log⁡28\log_2 8log2​8" means ”222 to the what gives 888?” The answer is 333, and so log⁡28=3\log_2 8 = 3log2​8=3.

The two restrictions on the base are not decoration. If b=1b = 1b=1, then 1y=11^y = 11y=1 for every exponent yyy, so the function is a flat line: it is not one-to-one, and it never produces any output except 111. The equation 1y=51^y = 51y=5 has no solution and 1y=11^y = 11y=1 has infinitely many, so no inverse can exist. If bbb were negative, bxb^xbx would not even be a function on the real numbers, since a power like (−4)1/2(-4)^{1/2}(−4)1/2 is not a real number at all. There is nothing there to invert.

Two values come free from the definition and are worth memorising on the spot:

log⁡b1=0(because b0=1),log⁡bb=1(because b1=b).\log_b 1 = 0 \quad (\text{because } b^0 = 1), \qquad \log_b b = 1 \quad (\text{because } b^1 = b).logb​1=0(because b0=1),logb​b=1(because b1=b).

Reading a logarithm straight from the definition

Every evaluation is the same question asked with different numbers, so ask it out loud each time.

Worked example 1 Evaluate log⁡381\log_3 81log3​81, log⁡5125\log_5 \tfrac{1}{25}log5​251​, and log⁡101000\log_{10} 1000log10​1000

For log⁡381\log_3 81log3​81, ask: three to the what gives eighty-one? Run up the powers of 333, which are 333, 999, 272727, 818181:

34=81⟹log⁡381=4.3^4 = 81 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \log_3 81 = 4.34=81⟹log3​81=4.

For log⁡5125\log_5 \tfrac{1}{25}log5​251​, ask: five to the what gives one twenty-fifth? A number smaller than 111 needs a negative exponent, since a negative exponent flips the power over:

5−2=152=125⟹log⁡5125=−2.5^{-2} = \frac{1}{5^2} = \frac{1}{25} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \log_5 \frac{1}{25} = -2.5−2=521​=251​⟹log5​251​=−2.

For log⁡101000\log_{10} 1000log10​1000, ask: ten to the what gives a thousand? Counting the zeros answers it:

103=1000⟹log⁡101000=3.10^3 = 1000 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \log_{10} 1000 = 3.103=1000⟹log10​1000=3.

Notice what the logarithm’s answer actually names. It plays the role of an exponent, not the role of the number you started with, even on the rare occasion the two numbers happen to match. In log⁡381=4\log_3 81 = 4log3​81=4, the 444 is an exponent: the power you put on 333, not a disguised copy of 818181.

When the base and the number are powers of a common smaller number, write both as powers of that number and the exponent falls out.

Worked example 2 Evaluate log⁡84\log_8 4log8​4 and log⁡927\log_9 27log9​27

Let y=log⁡84y = \log_8 4y=log8​4. By the definition this says 8y=48^y = 48y=4. Both 888 and 444 are powers of 222, so rewrite them that way and use the rule (23)y=23y(2^3)^y = 2^{3y}(23)y=23y:

8y=4⟹23y=22.8^y = 4 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 2^{3y} = 2^2.8y=4⟹23y=22.

Two powers of 222 are equal only when their exponents are equal, because 2x2^x2x is one-to-one. So 3y=23y = 23y=2 and y=23y = \tfrac{2}{3}y=32​. Check it against the definition: 82/3=(81/3)2=22=48^{2/3} = \left(8^{1/3}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 482/3=(81/3)2=22=4, as required.

The same move handles log⁡927\log_9 27log9​27. Setting y=log⁡927y = \log_9 27y=log9​27 gives 9y=279^y = 279y=27, and both sides are powers of 333:

32y=33⟹2y=3⟹y=32.3^{2y} = 3^3 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 2y = 3 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad y = \frac{3}{2}.32y=33⟹2y=3⟹y=23​.

A logarithm is under no obligation to be a whole number. Here it is a fraction, and that is perfectly ordinary.

The two cancellation laws

The Inverse Functions lesson ended with a pair of identities: composing a function with its inverse, in either order, gives back what you started with. Written for f(x)=bxf(x) = b^xf(x)=bx and f−1(x)=log⁡bxf^{-1}(x) = \log_b xf−1(x)=logb​x, they read

log⁡b ⁣(bt)=tfor every real t,blog⁡bx=xfor every x>0.\log_b\!\left(b^t\right) = t \quad \text{for every real } t, \qquad b^{\log_b x} = x \quad \text{for every } x > 0.logb​(bt)=tfor every real t,blogb​x=xfor every x>0.

Neither needs the general theory to justify it; each is one line from the definition. For the first, ask what log⁡b(bt)\log_b(b^t)logb​(bt) means: it is the exponent you put on bbb to get btb^tbt. The exponent ttt does that job, and one-to-one-ness says no other exponent does, so the answer is ttt. For the second, log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x is by construction the exponent that turns bbb into xxx; put it on bbb, and you get xxx.

Watch the two domains, because they are not the same. You may write log⁡b(bt)=t\log_b(b^t) = tlogb​(bt)=t for every real ttt, positive or negative, since btb^tbt is a legal input for the logarithm no matter what ttt is. But blog⁡bx=xb^{\log_b x} = xblogb​x=x requires x>0x > 0x>0, because otherwise log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x does not exist and the left side is meaningless. Written as log⁡7 ⁣(7−5)=−5\log_7\!\left(7^{-5}\right) = -5log7​(7−5)=−5 and 7log⁡712=127^{\log_7 12} = 127log7​12=12, the two laws are easy to use and easy to confuse, so keep asking which one you are applying.

The cancellation laws combine with the exponent rules to settle expressions that look far worse than they are. Take 9log⁡359^{\log_3 5}9log3​5. The base 999 is 323^232, so

9log⁡35=(32)log⁡35=(3log⁡35)2=52=25.9^{\log_3 5} = \left(3^2\right)^{\log_3 5} = \left(3^{\log_3 5}\right)^2 = 5^2 = 25.9log3​5=(32)log3​5=(3log3​5)2=52=25.

Nothing there is a new rule. It is the ordinary power-of-a-power rule, (32)L=32L=(3L)2\left(3^2\right)^{L} = 3^{2L} = \left(3^{L}\right)^{2}(32)L=32L=(3L)2 with L=log⁡35L = \log_3 5L=log3​5, followed by the cancellation law, and the tangle collapses to 252525.

What you are allowed to take the logarithm of

The domain question was answered before the logarithm even had a name. Its domain is the range of bxb^xbx, and its range is the domain of bxb^xbx:

domain of log⁡bx=all x>0,range of log⁡bx=all real numbers.\text{domain of } \log_b x = \text{all } x > 0, \qquad \text{range of } \log_b x = \text{all real numbers}.domain of logb​x=all x>0,range of logb​x=all real numbers.

So log⁡20\log_2 0log2​0 and log⁡2(−8)\log_2(-8)log2​(−8) are not small, or negative, or infinite. They do not exist. There is no real exponent yyy with 2y=02^y = 02y=0, because powers of 222 stay strictly positive however far left you go, and there is certainly no exponent with 2y=−82^y = -82y=−8. For a fixed legal base, log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x is defined exactly when x>0x > 0x>0: positive inputs always work, since every positive number is bbb to some power, and nothing else ever works.

In practice this restriction bites on the argument, the quantity sitting inside the logarithm, not on the variable. The function y=log⁡5(x−2)y = \log_5(x - 2)y=log5​(x−2) is defined precisely when x−2>0x - 2 > 0x−2>0, which is to say x>2x > 2x>2. The function y=log⁡2(x2+1)y = \log_2(x^2 + 1)y=log2​(x2+1) is defined for every real xxx, negative xxx included, because x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1 is positive no matter what. Always ask what the logarithm is being handed, and then demand that that be positive.

The exponential’s yyy-intercept (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) becomes the logarithm’s xxx-intercept (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0). So the graph of y=log⁡bxy = \log_b xy=logb​x crosses the xxx-axis at x=1x = 1x=1 for every base, which is just log⁡b1=0\log_b 1 = 0logb​1=0 drawn as a picture. That last pair is the one marked in the figure above, and it is worth stepping the base through every setting to watch it travel. The two marks slide in opposite directions along their curves, always the same distance from the diagonal on opposite sides. Set the base to 111 and the logarithm disappears altogether. That is not a gap in the drawing. Every power of 111 is 111, so y=1xy = 1^xy=1x is the flat line y=1y = 1y=1, one height reached by every input. A rule that sends everything to one place has nothing to undo it. The condition b≠1b \ne 1b=1 in the definition is exactly this state being excluded.

The exponential’s horizontal asymptote y=0y = 0y=0 reflects into a vertical asymptote x=0x = 0x=0 for the logarithm. The curve dives down the side of the yyy-axis, getting arbitrarily low without ever touching the axis. That is the picture of “you cannot take the logarithm of 000”: there is no height at all above x=0x = 0x=0.

The reflection preserves direction too, so log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x increases when b>1b > 1b>1 and decreases when 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1, matching its exponential. But the growth is slow: if log⁡2x=t\log_2 x = tlog2​x=t then x=2tx = 2^tx=2t, so doubling the input only buys a single unit of height, log⁡2(2x)=t+1\log_2(2x) = t + 1log2​(2x)=t+1. That is why log⁡21000\log_2 1000log2​1000 is under 101010, and why the right-hand end of the curve looks almost flat.

Switching forms to solve equations

The equivalence y=log⁡bx  ⟺  by=xy = \log_b x \iff b^y = xy=logb​x⟺by=x is a tool, not just a slogan. Whenever an unknown is trapped in an awkward position, translate the statement into the other form and the unknown steps into the open. There are only three places it can hide.

If the unknown is inside the logarithm, as in log⁡2x=5\log_2 x = 5log2​x=5, go to exponential form: x=25=32x = 2^5 = 32x=25=32.

If the unknown is the base, as in log⁡x81=4\log_x 81 = 4logx​81=4, go to exponential form again: x4=81x^4 = 81x4=81. Now x=3x = 3x=3 and x=−3x = -3x=−3 both satisfy that equation, but a logarithm base must be positive and cannot be 111, so x=3x = 3x=3 is the only answer. The rejected root is not a mistake in the algebra; it is a value the definition forbids.

If the unknown is the exponent, as in 3x=1813^x = \tfrac{1}{81}3x=811​, go to logarithmic form: x=log⁡3181=−4x = \log_3 \tfrac{1}{81} = -4x=log3​811​=−4. This is the case the logarithm was built for, and it is why the whole lesson matters.

Worked example 3 Solve log⁡x8=32\log_x 8 = \tfrac{3}{2}logx​8=23​

Here xxx is the base, so it already has to end up positive and different from 111; keep that requirement in mind and check the answer against it once you have one. Read the equation with the definition. It says: xxx raised to the power 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​ gives 888. Write that down:

x3/2=8.x^{3/2} = 8.x3/2=8.

To free xxx, raise both sides to the reciprocal power 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​, since (x3/2)2/3=x1\left(x^{3/2}\right)^{2/3} = x^{1}(x3/2)2/3=x1:

x=82/3=(81/3)2=22=4.x = 8^{2/3} = \left(8^{1/3}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4.x=82/3=(81/3)2=22=4.

Check the answer in the original statement rather than in your own algebra. Is log⁡48=32\log_4 8 = \tfrac{3}{2}log4​8=23​? That asks whether 43/2=84^{3/2} = 843/2=8, and indeed 43/2=(41/2)3=23=84^{3/2} = \left(4^{1/2}\right)^3 = 2^3 = 843/2=(41/2)3=23=8. It is, and x=4x = 4x=4 is a legal base, being positive and not 111.

Worked example 4 Solve log⁡2 ⁣(x2−3x)=2\log_2\!\left(x^2 - 3x\right) = 2log2​(x2−3x)=2

Switch to exponential form. The equation says that the exponent turning 222 into x2−3xx^2 - 3xx2−3x is 222, so the argument itself must equal 222^222:

x2−3x=22=4.x^2 - 3x = 2^2 = 4.x2−3x=22=4.

That is a quadratic. Move everything to one side and factor:

x2−3x−4=0⟹(x−4)(x+1)=0,x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad (x - 4)(x + 1) = 0,x2−3x−4=0⟹(x−4)(x+1)=0,

Now check both against the domain rule, and check it carefully, because the rule applies to the argument of the logarithm, not to xxx. For x=4x = 4x=4 the argument is 16−12=416 - 12 = 416−12=4, which is positive. For x=−1x = -1x=−1 the argument is 1+3=41 + 3 = 41+3=4, which is also positive. Both values are legal, and both are solutions: log⁡24=2\log_2 4 = 2log2​4=2 in each case.

The temptation is to throw out x=−1x = -1x=−1 on sight because logarithms “cannot take negatives”. That instinct is misplaced. Nothing forbids a negative solution; what is forbidden is a negative argument, and here the argument comes out to 444 either way.

Trapping a logarithm between two integers

Most logarithms are not tidy. No whole number answers ”222 to the what gives 101010?”, and neither does any fraction: log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 turns out to be irrational, an endless, non-repeating decimal, the same kind of number as 2\sqrt{2}2​.

No whole number and no fraction is ever exactly log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10. The full argument for why no fraction can work is in Go deeper at the end of this lesson.

See the proof

That does not make it mysterious. You can pin it between two integers using nothing but powers of 222 and the fact that the logarithm is increasing.

Why is log⁡2\log_2log2​ increasing? The Inverse Functions lesson already settled this in general: reflecting an increasing curve across y=xy = xy=x gives another increasing curve, so the inverse of an increasing function is always increasing. Since 2x2^x2x is increasing, log⁡2x\log_2 xlog2​x is increasing too, and applying it to an inequality keeps the inequality pointing the same way.

Now trap the number. Since 23=82^3 = 823=8 and 24=162^4 = 1624=16,

8<10<16⟹log⁡28<log⁡210<log⁡216⟹3<log⁡210<4.8 < 10 < 16 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \log_2 8 < \log_2 10 < \log_2 16 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 3 < \log_2 10 < 4.8<10<16⟹log2​8<log2​10<log2​16⟹3<log2​10<4.

So log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 sits between 333 and 444, and its true value is about 3.323.323.32. Do not try to guess where in that interval it lands by asking whether 101010 is nearer to 888 or to 161616: the height climbs by one unit every time the argument doubles, not every time it grows by a fixed amount, so arithmetic nearness is the wrong measure.

The interval’s arithmetic middle is 121212, but that is not where the logarithm’s midpoint falls. The computation that finds the real midpoint, and what it says about where log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 sits, is in Go deeper at the end of this lesson.

See the refinement

Knowing which two integers a logarithm lives between is not just a sanity check. It answers real questions on its own, including ones where the honest answer is not the nearer integer. Suppose a server’s request capacity doubles with every upgrade, starting at 111 request per second, and it must be upgraded until it can handle at least 140140140 requests per second. The number of upgrades kkk has to satisfy 2k≥1402^k \ge 1402k≥140, so k≥log⁡2140k \ge \log_2 140k≥log2​140. Trapping the logarithm gives 27=1282^7 = 12827=128 and 28=2562^8 = 25628=256, so 128<140<256128 < 140 < 256128<140<256 and 7<log⁡2140<87 < \log_2 140 < 87<log2​140<8; in fact log⁡2140≈7.13\log_2 140 \approx 7.13log2​140≈7.13, much closer to 777 than to 888. Even so, seven upgrades cap out at 128128128 requests per second, short of 140140140, so the answer has to be rounded up to k=8k = 8k=8 regardless. Trapping a logarithm tells you which two whole numbers are in play; whether the honest answer rounds up or down depends on what the numbers mean in the situation, never on which integer the logarithm sits nearer to.

Answer choices

Find the powers of 333 that straddle 505050.

33=27,34=81⟹27<50<813^3 = 27, \qquad 3^4 = 81 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 27 < 50 < 8133=27,34=81⟹27<50<81

Since log⁡3\log_3log3​ is increasing, applying it to that inequality keeps it pointing the same way, giving 3<log⁡350<43 < \log_3 50 < 43<log3​50<4.

Base 101010 deserves a note of its own. It is used so often that it is written with no base at all, and log⁡x\log xlogx with a bare “log” means log⁡10x\log_{10} xlog10​x from here on. The trapping argument is especially sharp there. A whole number nnn with ddd digits satisfies 10d−1≤n<10d10^{d-1} \le n < 10^{d}10d−1≤n<10d, so applying log⁡\loglog gives

d−1≤log⁡n<d.d - 1 \le \log n < d.d−1≤logn<d.

In other words, the whole-number part of a common logarithm is one less than the digit count. Since 1000≤4500<100001000 \le 4500 < 100001000≤4500<10000, you know without any calculation that log⁡4500\log 4500log4500 is three point something, and sure enough 450045004500 has four digits.

What you cannot do yet is compute log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 or log⁡4500\log 4500log4500 to a decimal, or break a complicated logarithm into simpler ones. That takes rules for combining logarithms, which is the business of the next lesson. Everything in this one came from a single idea: the logarithm is the exponential run backward.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x as some kind of multiplication. It is an exponent and nothing else. The statement log⁡28=3\log_2 8 = 3log2​8=3 carries exactly the same information as 23=82^3 = 823=8.
  • Scrambling the roles in the definition. In log⁡bx=y\log_b x = ylogb​x=y, the base bbb stays the base, xxx is the result, and yyy is the exponent. It says by=xb^y = xby=x, never bx=yb^x = ybx=y.
  • Rejecting a negative solution reflexively. The domain rule restricts the argument, not the variable, so x=−1x = -1x=−1 is a legal solution of log⁡2(x2−3x)=2\log_2(x^2 - 3x) = 2log2​(x2−3x)=2: it produces the positive argument 444.
  • Accepting a negative base. log⁡x81=4\log_x 81 = 4logx​81=4 gives x4=81x^4 = 81x4=81, with roots 333 and −3-3−3, but only x=3x = 3x=3 survives, since a base must be positive and different from 111.
  • Thinking log⁡b0\log_b 0logb​0 is 000, or that a negative number has a negative logarithm. Neither exists at all. No exponent turns a positive base into 000 or into a negative number.
  • Confusing the two cancellation laws. Write log⁡b(bt)=t\log_b(b^t) = tlogb​(bt)=t for every real ttt, but blog⁡bx=xb^{\log_b x} = xblogb​x=x only for x>0x > 0x>0, since otherwise log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x is undefined and there is nothing to cancel.
  • Expecting logarithms to be large. They grow painfully slowly. Doubling the input adds only 111 to a base-222 logarithm, so log⁡21000\log_2 1000log2​1000 is still under 101010.

Key takeaways

  • The exponential bxb^xbx (with b>0b > 0b>0, b≠1b \ne 1b=1) is one-to-one and its outputs are exactly the positive numbers, so it has an inverse. The logarithm is the name of that inverse.
  • The definition, in both directions: y=log⁡bx  ⟺  by=xy = \log_b x \iff b^y = xy=logb​x⟺by=x, for x>0x > 0x>0. Read it as ”log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x is the exponent you put on bbb to get xxx”.
  • The domain of log⁡b\log_blogb​ is all x>0x > 0x>0 and its range is all real numbers, because those are the range and the domain of bxb^xbx. The restriction applies to the argument, not to the variable.
  • Free values and cancellation: log⁡b1=0\log_b 1 = 0logb​1=0, log⁡bb=1\log_b b = 1logb​b=1, log⁡b(bt)=t\log_b(b^t) = tlogb​(bt)=t for every real ttt, and blog⁡bx=xb^{\log_b x} = xblogb​x=x for every x>0x > 0x>0.
  • The graph is the exponential reflected across y=xy = xy=x. It crosses the xxx-axis at (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0), passes through (b,1)(b, 1)(b,1), has the yyy-axis as a vertical asymptote, and increases exactly when b>1b > 1b>1.
  • To solve, switch forms. Then trap an untidy logarithm between the two integers whose powers of bbb straddle the number, as in 8<10<168 < 10 < 168<10<16 giving 3<log⁡210<43 < \log_2 10 < 43<log2​10<4. Round up or down to fit the situation, not to the nearer integer.

Why bxb^xbx never repeats a value, at every real exponent

The main lesson checks this at whole-number exponents. Here is the argument for every real exponent, including an irrational one.

bxb^xbx is one-to-one, so it has an inverse#

Take the case b>1b > 1b>1 first, and let u<vu < vu<v be any two real numbers. Write t=v−ut = v - ut=v−u, so t>0t > 0t>0. The exponent rule for a sum splits the larger power apart:

bv=bu+t=bu⋅bt.b^v = b^{u + t} = b^u \cdot b^t.bv=bu+t=bu⋅bt.

Since bub^ubu is positive, whether bvb^vbv beats bub^ubu comes down to a single question: is btb^tbt bigger than 111? It is, and here is why. When ttt is a positive integer, btb^tbt is a product of ttt factors each larger than 111, so the product is larger than 111. When t=pqt = \tfrac{p}{q}t=qp​ is a positive rational number, suppose for contradiction that bp/q≤1b^{p/q} \le 1bp/q≤1. Raising both sides of an inequality between positive numbers to the qqqth power preserves it, so bp≤1b^p \le 1bp≤1, contradicting the integer case. Hence bp/q>1b^{p/q} > 1bp/q>1. When ttt is irrational, lean on the previous lesson, which is where bxb^xbx at an irrational exponent was constructed in the first place. That power was constructed as the single value the rational exponents squeeze it to. Pick a positive rational rrr smaller than ttt. Every rational exponent between rrr and ttt gives a power of at least brb^rbr, so the value they squeeze btb^tbt to satisfies bt≥br>1b^t \ge b^r > 1bt≥br>1 as well.

So bt>1b^t > 1bt>1 for every t>0t > 0t>0, and therefore

bv=bu⋅bt>bu⋅1=bu.b^v = b^u \cdot b^t > b^u \cdot 1 = b^u.bv=bu⋅bt>bu⋅1=bu.

That is exactly the statement that u<vu < vu<v forces bu<bvb^u < b^vbu<bv: the function is strictly increasing. A strictly increasing function can never take the same value twice, because any two different inputs are ordered, and the smaller one is sent to a strictly smaller output. So bxb^xbx is one-to-one.

For a base with 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1, write b=1cb = \tfrac{1}{c}b=c1​ with c>1c > 1c>1, so that bx=c−xb^x = c^{-x}bx=c−x. If u<vu < vu<v then −u>−v-u > -v−u>−v, and the case just proved gives c−u>c−vc^{-u} > c^{-v}c−u>c−v, which says bu>bvb^u > b^vbu>bv. The function is strictly decreasing, and a strictly decreasing function is one-to-one for the same reason. Either way, bxb^xbx never repeats a value, so it can be run backward.

∎

Why log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 is irrational

No fraction equals log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10#

Suppose one did, say log⁡210=pq\log_2 10 = \tfrac{p}{q}log2​10=qp​ for positive integers ppp and qqq. Then 2p/q=102^{p/q} = 102p/q=10, and raising both sides to the qqqth power gives 2p=10q=2q⋅5q2^p = 10^q = 2^q \cdot 5^q2p=10q=2q⋅5q. Dividing through by 2q2^q2q leaves 5q=2p−q5^q = 2^{p-q}5q=2p−q. The left side is an odd integer bigger than 111. The right side is 222 to an integer power, so it is a fraction below 111 when p<qp < qp<q, exactly 111 when p=qp = qp=q, and an even integer when p>qp > qp>q. It is never an odd integer bigger than 111, so no such ppp and qqq exist, and log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 is irrational.

∎

Refining the trap: why log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 is closer to 333 than to 444

The interval 3<log⁡210<43 < \log_2 10 < 43<log2​10<4 has arithmetic midpoint 3.53.53.5, which corresponds to the argument 23.52^{3.5}23.5, not to the argument’s own midpoint 121212:

23.5=23⋅21/2=82≈11.3.2^{3.5} = 2^3 \cdot 2^{1/2} = 8\sqrt{2} \approx 11.3.23.5=23⋅21/2=82​≈11.3.

Since 10<11.310 < 11.310<11.3 and the logarithm is increasing, log⁡210\log_2 10log2​10 lies below the height 3.53.53.5, in the lower half of the interval (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4), and its true value is about 3.323.323.32. The lesson: the height climbs by one unit every time the argument doubles, not every time it grows by a fixed amount, so the argument at the halfway height is 8⋅16=82\sqrt{8 \cdot 16} = 8\sqrt{2}8⋅16​=82​, the two endpoints’ geometric mean, not their arithmetic mean 121212.

A bit of history (optional)

The logarithm reached the world backwards from the way you have just met it. Its inventor was not inverting anything, because the function bxb^xbx did not yet exist for him to invert.

John Napier was a Scottish landowner who did mathematics in the hours his estate left him, and he gave about twenty years to one problem. Astronomers were losing whole days to the multiplication of seven-digit numbers, and a slip anywhere spoiled the page. His answer, printed in 1614, was a table of paired numbers.

He built it out of a race. Picture two points, each travelling along a line of its own. The first moves at a steady speed and never varies. The second sets off beside it, but it keeps slowing in proportion to the distance it still has to run. So it closes on its target forever without arriving. Read off the two positions at the same instant, and the pair of numbers you write down is one row of his table.

The definition you met today is the logarithm as the exponential read the other way round, and it is more than a century younger than that table. It needs bxb^xbx at every real xxx, and nobody had built that yet. Once you do have it, the moving points can be dismissed and the tables thrown away. The inverse is the entire definition, and every fact in this lesson fell out of it.

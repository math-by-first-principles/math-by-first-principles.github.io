Properties of Logarithms

Intuition

Multiplying two six-digit numbers by hand takes a few minutes and a lot of care. Adding them takes seconds. For three hundred years, astronomers, navigators, and engineers exploited that gap using one tool: a logarithm turns a product into a sum. Look up two numbers in a table, add the two results, look the answer back up, and you have multiplied without multiplying. The rules in this lesson are what make that trade possible, and not one of them is a new fact. Each one is an exponent law you already know, read through the inverse function.

Learning goals

  • Read every rule as an exponent law run backwards
  • Apply the product, quotient and power rules with their domains
  • Change base with log⁡bM=log⁡aMlog⁡ab\log_b M = \tfrac{\log_a M}{\log_a b}logb​M=loga​bloga​M​
  • Expand and condense logarithmic expressions using all three rules
  • Use log⁡10\log_{10}log10​ to count the digits of a huge power

The mirror behind every rule

Everything here rests on the definition you already have. For a base bbb with b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \ne 1b=1, and for M>0M > 0M>0,

log⁡bM=xmeans exactlybx=M.\log_b M = x \quad\text{means exactly}\quad b^x = M.logb​M=xmeans exactlybx=M.

A logarithm is an exponent. It is the exponent you must put on bbb to reach MMM. Two consequences follow immediately and get used constantly: blog⁡bM=Mb^{\log_b M} = Mblogb​M=M for every M>0M > 0M>0, and log⁡b(bx)=x\log_b (b^x) = xlogb​(bx)=x for every real xxx. Those two say that raising bbb to a power and taking a base-bbb logarithm are inverse operations, each undoing the other.

Here is what that gives you with actual numbers. Since 8=238 = 2^38=23 and 4=224 = 2^24=22, their product is 8⋅4=23+2=25=328 \cdot 4 = 2^{3+2} = 2^5 = 328⋅4=23+2=25=32. Multiplying the numbers matched adding their exponents, 333 and 222. But 333 and 222 are also the base-two logarithms of 888 and 444, so that same addition is log⁡28+log⁡24=3+2=5=log⁡232\log_2 8 + \log_2 4 = 3 + 2 = 5 = \log_2 32log2​8+log2​4=3+2=5=log2​32. Multiplying two numbers matched adding their logarithms. That one example is the whole idea of this lesson. The three rules below just say it for any base and any numbers, not only 222, 888, and 444.

To see why it always works, recall the exponent laws, which hold for every real exponent whenever the base is positive:

bx⋅by=bx+y,bxby=bx−y,(bx)p=bxp.b^x \cdot b^y = b^{x+y}, \qquad \frac{b^x}{b^y} = b^{x-y}, \qquad \left(b^x\right)^p = b^{xp}.bx⋅by=bx+y,bybx​=bx−y,(bx)p=bxp.

Each of the three logarithm rules is one of these laws, read backwards. That gives us a single proof strategy, and we will use it three times without variation:

  1. Name the logarithms. Put x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M and y=log⁡bNy = \log_b Ny=logb​N.
  2. Translate to exponents. The definition says bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M and by=Nb^y = Nby=N.
  3. Apply the matching exponent law to combine them.
  4. Translate back by taking log⁡b\log_blogb​ of both sides.

The product rule

For b>0b > 0b>0 with b≠1b \ne 1b=1, and for M>0M > 0M>0 and N>0N > 0N>0:

log⁡b(MN)=log⁡bM+log⁡bN.\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N.logb​(MN)=logb​M+logb​N.

The product rule#

Let x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M and y=log⁡bNy = \log_b Ny=logb​N. Both of these logarithms exist precisely because MMM and NNN are positive, and by the definition of a logarithm the two statements say exactly that bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M and by=Nb^y = Nby=N.

Multiply those two equations together. The left sides give MNMNMN and the right sides give bx⋅byb^x \cdot b^ybx⋅by, so MN=bx⋅byMN = b^x \cdot b^yMN=bx⋅by. The exponent law for a product of powers with the same base collapses the right side into a single power, bx⋅by=bx+yb^x \cdot b^y = b^{x+y}bx⋅by=bx+y, and therefore

MN=b x+y.MN = b^{\,x+y}.MN=bx+y.

Read that last line back through the definition of a logarithm. It says that the exponent you must put on bbb to reach MNMNMN is x+yx + yx+y, which is to say log⁡b(MN)=x+y\log_b(MN) = x + ylogb​(MN)=x+y. (The left side is legitimate: MMM and NNN are positive, so MNMNMN is positive too.) Substituting back what xxx and yyy stood for,

log⁡b(MN)=log⁡bM+log⁡bN.\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N.logb​(MN)=logb​M+logb​N.

∎

The condition M>0M > 0M>0 and N>0N > 0N>0 is not decorative, and it is stronger than requiring the product to be positive. Take M=N=−2M = N = -2M=N=−2. Their product is 444, so the left side log⁡b4\log_b 4logb​4 is a perfectly good number, but log⁡b(−2)\log_b(-2)logb​(−2) does not exist, so the right side is not merely wrong, it is meaningless. The rule needs each factor positive, not just their product.

Here is what the product rule looks like as a picture. Mark the numbers 111 through 101010 on a ruler, but place each number nnn at a distance proportional to log⁡10n\log_{10} nlog10​n from the left end instead of at its own value. On that ruler, distances are logarithms, so adding two logarithms means laying two distances end to end.

The second bar is the distance from 111 to 333, but it has been shifted so it starts at 222. It ends at 666. Multiplying by 333 always moves you the same distance to the right on this scale, no matter where you start, and that fixed distance is log⁡103\log_{10} 3log10​3. A slide rule is exactly two of these scales, and sliding one against the other adds the distances for you.

The quotient rule

For b>0b > 0b>0 with b≠1b \ne 1b=1, and for M>0M > 0M>0 and N>0N > 0N>0:

log⁡b ⁣(MN)=log⁡bM−log⁡bN.\log_b\!\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b N.logb​(NM​)=logb​M−logb​N.

The quotient rule#

This is the product-rule proof with one symbol changed. Let x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M and y=log⁡bNy = \log_b Ny=logb​N, so bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M and by=Nb^y = Nby=N. Divide the two equations instead of multiplying them:

MN=bxby=b x−y,\frac{M}{N} = \frac{b^x}{b^y} = b^{\,x-y},NM​=bybx​=bx−y,

using the exponent law for a quotient of powers with the same base. (N≠0N \ne 0N=0 since N>0N > 0N>0, so the division is legal, and M/N>0M/N > 0M/N>0, so its logarithm exists.) Reading M/N=bx−yM/N = b^{x-y}M/N=bx−y back through the definition gives log⁡b(M/N)=x−y\log_b(M/N) = x - ylogb​(M/N)=x−y, which is

log⁡b ⁣(MN)=log⁡bM−log⁡bN.\log_b\!\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b N.logb​(NM​)=logb​M−logb​N.

∎

One special case is worth keeping in your head. Put M=1M = 1M=1 and use log⁡b1=0\log_b 1 = 0logb​1=0:

log⁡b ⁣(1N)=log⁡b1−log⁡bN=−log⁡bN.\log_b\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\right) = \log_b 1 - \log_b N = -\log_b N.logb​(N1​)=logb​1−logb​N=−logb​N.

Taking a reciprocal on the inside flips the sign on the outside. That single fact converts every division into a subtraction and every “one over” into a minus sign.

The power rule

For b>0b > 0b>0 with b≠1b \ne 1b=1, for M>0M > 0M>0, and for any real number ppp:

log⁡b ⁣(Mp)=plog⁡bM.\log_b\!\left(M^p\right) = p \log_b M.logb​(Mp)=plogb​M.

The power rule#

Same pattern once more. Let x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M, which exists because M>0M > 0M>0, so bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M. Raise both sides to the power ppp:

Mp=(bx)p=b px,M^p = \left(b^x\right)^p = b^{\,px},Mp=(bx)p=bpx,

using the exponent law for a power of a power, which holds for every real exponent because b>0b > 0b>0. Since bpxb^{px}bpx is positive, MpM^pMp is positive and its logarithm exists. Reading the line back through the definition gives log⁡b ⁣(Mp)=px\log_b\!\left(M^p\right) = pxlogb​(Mp)=px, and substituting x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M gives the rule:

log⁡b ⁣(Mp)=plog⁡bM.\log_b\!\left(M^p\right) = p \log_b M.logb​(Mp)=plogb​M.

∎

Notice what the power rule does: an exponent trapped inside a logarithm walks out to the front as a plain multiplier. That is the property that made logarithms indispensable, because it turns the brutal operation of raising to a power into ordinary multiplication.

The domain condition here is the one students lose. The rule requires M>0M > 0M>0. Consider the familiar-looking claim

log⁡b ⁣(x2)=2log⁡bx.\log_b\!\left(x^2\right) = 2 \log_b x.logb​(x2)=2logb​x.

This is true for every x>0x > 0x>0, and it is false for every x<0x < 0x<0, for a reason that is easy to miss: when xxx is negative, x2x^2x2 is still positive. The left side is therefore a perfectly good number, but log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x does not exist for a negative xxx, so the right side is not a number at all. At x=0x = 0x=0 neither side exists. So the equation holds exactly when x>0x > 0x>0, no more and no less.

The repair is an absolute value. For any x≠0x \ne 0x=0 we have x2=∣x∣2x^2 = |x|^2x2=∣x∣2 with ∣x∣>0|x| > 0∣x∣>0, so the power rule applies to ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ and gives the identity that is true for every nonzero xxx:

log⁡b ⁣(x2)=2log⁡b∣x∣.\log_b\!\left(x^2\right) = 2 \log_b |x|.logb​(x2)=2logb​∣x∣.

Check it on x=−3x = -3x=−3: the left side is log⁡b9\log_b 9logb​9, and the right side is 2log⁡b3=log⁡b92\log_b 3 = \log_b 92logb​3=logb​9. They agree, and neither one asks you to take the logarithm of a negative number.

Expanding and condensing

Two skills come out of the three rules, and they are the same skill run in opposite directions.

Expanding takes one logarithm of a complicated expression and breaks it into a sum and difference of simpler logarithms. Work from the outside in: split the top and bottom of a fraction first (quotient rule). Then split the products (product rule), and then bring exponents down to the front (power rule). Roots are exponents in disguise, so y=y1/2\sqrt{y} = y^{1/2}y​=y1/2 before you start.

Condensing runs the film backwards, aiming for one logarithm with no coefficient left outside it. The product and quotient rules combine two bare logarithms, log⁡bM±log⁡bN\log_b M \pm \log_b Nlogb​M±logb​N; they say nothing about a coefficient in front. So the first step is always the power rule: send every coefficient back up as an exponent, turning each term into a bare logarithm. Only then can the product and quotient rules add everything into a single numerator and subtract the rest into a single denominator.

Throughout, assume every variable inside a logarithm is positive. Textbooks say this so often that they stop saying it out loud, but you now know why they must.

Worked example 1 Evaluate log⁡26+log⁡223\log_2 6 + \log_2 \tfrac{2}{3}log2​6+log2​32​ without a calculator

Neither logarithm is a whole number on its own, so evaluating them separately is hopeless. Combine them first with the product rule.

log⁡26+log⁡223=log⁡2 ⁣(6⋅23)=log⁡24.\log_2 6 + \log_2 \frac{2}{3} = \log_2\!\left(6 \cdot \frac{2}{3}\right) = \log_2 4.log2​6+log2​32​=log2​(6⋅32​)=log2​4.

Now the question is what exponent turns 222 into 444, and the answer is 222, since 22=42^2 = 422=4:

log⁡26+log⁡223=2.\log_2 6 + \log_2 \frac{2}{3} = 2.log2​6+log2​32​=2.

The ugly numbers were never the point. The rule let them cancel before we ever had to face them.

Change of base

Every logarithm in the lesson so far has had a friendly base. Real questions are not so polite: a population that triples, an interest rate, a half-life, all produce logarithms in bases nobody has tabulated. Worse, a calculator offers you only a couple of bases, not the one you happen to need.

The fix is the change-of-base formula. For b,a>0b, a > 0b,a>0 with b≠1b \ne 1b=1 and a≠1a \ne 1a=1, and for M>0M > 0M>0:

log⁡bM=log⁡aMlog⁡ab.\log_b M = \frac{\log_a M}{\log_a b}.logb​M=loga​bloga​M​.

Any base you can compute in will do for aaa. The formula converts the base you want into the base you have.

Here is why it works, in three lines. Let x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M, so bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M. Take a base-aaa logarithm of both sides and use the power rule on the left:

log⁡a ⁣(bx)=xlog⁡ab=log⁡aM.\log_a\!\left(b^x\right) = x\log_a b = \log_a M.loga​(bx)=xloga​b=loga​M.

Solve for xxx by dividing by log⁡ab\log_a bloga​b, and since x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M, that gives the formula. The division is always legal, because log⁡ab=0\log_a b = 0loga​b=0 would mean a0=ba^0 = ba0=b, that is b=1b = 1b=1, and b=1b = 1b=1 was excluded as a base from the start. The full derivation, with every legality check spelled out, is in where the change-of-base formula comes from, at the bottom of this lesson.

One special case is worth knowing: swapping the base and the argument inverts the value, so log⁡28=3\log_2 8 = 3log2​8=3 sits alongside log⁡82=13\log_8 2 = \tfrac{1}{3}log8​2=31​. It falls straight out of the formula. The one line of algebra is in why swapping the base and the argument inverts the value, at the bottom of this lesson.

What the rules buy you

The power rule does something no amount of arithmetic can: it measures a number too large to write down. How many digits does 2402^{40}240 have? Multiplying it out is possible but tedious, and for 240002^{4000}24000 it would be hopeless. Logarithms answer the question in one line.

The idea is that a positive integer NNN has ddd digits exactly when 10d−1≤N<10d10^{d-1} \le N < 10^d10d−1≤N<10d. (A three-digit number runs from 100100100 to 999999999, which is 102≤N<10310^2 \le N < 10^3102≤N<103.) So the digit count is read off from where log⁡10N\log_{10} Nlog10​N falls between consecutive whole numbers.

Worked example 6 How many digits does 2402^{40}240 have? Use log⁡102≈0.30103\log_{10} 2 \approx 0.30103log10​2≈0.30103

Take the base-10 logarithm and let the power rule pull the exponent down:

log⁡10 ⁣(240)=40log⁡102≈40(0.30103)=12.0412.\log_{10}\!\left(2^{40}\right) = 40 \log_{10} 2 \approx 40(0.30103) = 12.0412.log10​(240)=40log10​2≈40(0.30103)=12.0412.

That value sits between 121212 and 131313. Because 10t10^t10t grows as ttt grows, this pins the number itself:

1012<240<1013.10^{12} < 2^{40} < 10^{13}.1012<240<1013.

A number that is at least 101210^{12}1012 but below 101310^{13}1013 has exactly 131313 digits. So 2402^{40}240 has 131313 digits, and indeed 240=1,099,511,627,7762^{40} = 1{,}099{,}511{,}627{,}776240=1,099,511,627,776.

The same line handles 240002^{4000}24000, where 4000(0.30103)=1204.124000(0.30103) = 1204.124000(0.30103)=1204.12 tells you the answer has 120512051205 digits, without writing a single one of them.

That is the shape of every application in the next lessons. Whenever an unknown is stuck in an exponent, take a logarithm and the power rule drags it down to where ordinary algebra can reach it.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Splitting a sum. log⁡b(M+N)\log_b(M + N)logb​(M+N) is not log⁡bM+log⁡bN\log_b M + \log_b Nlogb​M+logb​N. The product rule is about a product inside the logarithm, never a sum. Test it: log⁡2(4+4)=log⁡28=3\log_2(4 + 4) = \log_2 8 = 3log2​(4+4)=log2​8=3, while log⁡24+log⁡24=2+2=4\log_2 4 + \log_2 4 = 2 + 2 = 4log2​4+log2​4=2+2=4.
  • Confusing a quotient of logs with the log of a quotient. log⁡bMlog⁡bN\dfrac{\log_b M}{\log_b N}logb​Nlogb​M​ is not log⁡bM−log⁡bN\log_b M - \log_b Nlogb​M−logb​N. The first is a change of base and equals log⁡NM\log_N MlogN​M; the second is the quotient rule. They measure different things and must not be substituted for each other.
  • Forgetting the domain conditions. log⁡b(MN)=log⁡bM+log⁡bN\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b Nlogb​(MN)=logb​M+logb​N needs M>0M > 0M>0 and N>0N > 0N>0, not just MN>0MN > 0MN>0: with M=N=−2M = N = -2M=N=−2 the left side is log⁡b4\log_b 4logb​4 but the right side does not exist. And log⁡b ⁣(x2)=2log⁡bx\log_b\!\left(x^2\right) = 2\log_b xlogb​(x2)=2logb​x is false for every x<0x < 0x<0, because the left side exists there but log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x does not; the identity valid for all x≠0x \ne 0x=0 is log⁡b ⁣(x2)=2log⁡b∣x∣\log_b\!\left(x^2\right) = 2\log_b |x|logb​(x2)=2logb​∣x∣.
  • Moving an exponent that is not inside. (log⁡bM)2(\log_b M)^2(logb​M)2 is not 2log⁡bM2\log_b M2logb​M. The power rule only applies when the exponent is on the argument, inside the logarithm. When in doubt, write the parentheses.
  • Condensing before clearing coefficients. 2log⁡bx+log⁡by2\log_b x + \log_b y2logb​x+logb​y is not log⁡b(2xy)\log_b(2xy)logb​(2xy). Send the 222 up as an exponent first, giving log⁡b ⁣(x2y)\log_b\!\left(x^2 y\right)logb​(x2y).

Key takeaways

  • A logarithm is an exponent, so every rule here is an exponent law read backwards: log⁡bM=x\log_b M = xlogb​M=x means bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M.
  • Product rule: log⁡b(MN)=log⁡bM+log⁡bN\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b Nlogb​(MN)=logb​M+logb​N, valid when M>0M > 0M>0 and N>0N > 0N>0.
  • Quotient rule: log⁡b ⁣(MN)=log⁡bM−log⁡bN\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b Nlogb​(NM​)=logb​M−logb​N, valid when M>0M > 0M>0 and N>0N > 0N>0. In particular log⁡b ⁣(1N)=−log⁡bN\log_b\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\right) = -\log_b Nlogb​(N1​)=−logb​N.
  • Power rule: log⁡b ⁣(Mp)=plog⁡bM\log_b\!\left(M^p\right) = p\log_b Mlogb​(Mp)=plogb​M for M>0M > 0M>0 and any real ppp. For x<0x < 0x<0 use log⁡b ⁣(x2)=2log⁡b∣x∣\log_b\!\left(x^2\right) = 2\log_b |x|logb​(x2)=2logb​∣x∣.
  • Change of base: log⁡bM=log⁡aMlog⁡ab\log_b M = \dfrac{\log_a M}{\log_a b}logb​M=loga​bloga​M​ for any valid base aaa. Dividing is safe because log⁡ab≠0\log_a b \ne 0loga​b=0 whenever b≠1b \ne 1b=1.
  • To expand, split quotients, then products, then bring exponents down. To condense, send coefficients up as exponents first, then combine.
  • The rules turn multiplication into addition and powers into multiplication, which is why log⁡10\log_{10}log10​ can count the digits of 2402^{40}240 without anyone multiplying it out.

Where the change-of-base formula comes from

The lesson states the formula and uses it; this is the derivation, which rests on nothing but the definition and the power rule you already have.

The change-of-base formula#

Let x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M, which exists because M>0M > 0M>0, and translate it with the definition: bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M.

Two equal positive numbers have equal logarithms in any valid base, so we may apply log⁡a\log_aloga​ to both sides of bx=Mb^x = Mbx=M:

log⁡a ⁣(bx)=log⁡aM.\log_a\!\left(b^x\right) = \log_a M.loga​(bx)=loga​M.

The power rule applies to the left side, since b>0b > 0b>0, and pulls the exponent to the front: xlog⁡ab=log⁡aMx \log_a b = \log_a Mxloga​b=loga​M.

To solve for xxx we want to divide by log⁡ab\log_a bloga​b, which is only legal if that number is not zero. It is not zero, and here is why: log⁡ab=0\log_a b = 0loga​b=0 would mean a0=ba^0 = ba0=b, that is b=1b = 1b=1, and b=1b = 1b=1 was excluded as a base from the start. So the division is safe, and

x=log⁡aMlog⁡ab,x = \frac{\log_a M}{\log_a b},x=loga​bloga​M​,

which, since x=log⁡bMx = \log_b Mx=logb​M, is the formula.

∎

Why swapping the base and the argument inverts the value

The change-of-base formula lets you pick any legal base for aaa. Nothing stops you picking MMM itself, the very number you are taking the logarithm of, provided M>0M > 0M>0 and M≠1M \ne 1M=1 so that MMM is a legal base. Since log⁡MM=1\log_M M = 1logM​M=1, the numerator collapses:

log⁡bM=log⁡MMlog⁡Mb=1log⁡Mb.\log_b M = \frac{\log_M M}{\log_M b} = \frac{1}{\log_M b}.logb​M=logM​blogM​M​=logM​b1​.

So log⁡bM\log_b Mlogb​M and log⁡Mb\log_M blogM​b are reciprocals of each other. Read it as a statement about exponents and it is almost obvious: if it takes the power 333 to get from 222 to 888, it must take the power 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ to get from 888 back to 222, because 81/3=28^{1/3} = 281/3=2. The identity is the same fact, written in logarithms.

A bit of history (optional)

This lesson handed you log⁡102≈0.3010\log_{10} 2 \approx 0.3010log10​2≈0.3010 and invited you to use it. Somebody had to compute that number first, and there was nowhere to look it up.

Henry Briggs, an English professor of geometry, set himself the whole table. He would calculate the base-10 logarithm of thirty thousand numbers, to fourteen decimal places. The result appeared in 1624. He had no calculator and no base-10 table to lean on. He had a single rule with any real leverage in it, and it is the power rule of this lesson.

Watch what he does with it, beginning from the one value he already knows, log⁡1010=1\log_{10} 10 = 1log10​10=1. Taking a square root halves a logarithm, so the square root of 101010 delivers a second entry immediately. The square root of that delivers a third. He continues for fifty-four rounds, each root halving the logarithm and dragging the number itself closer to 111. From that ladder of roots, recombined by the product rule, every remaining entry in the table could be assembled.

The arithmetic occupied him for years, all of it on paper, to more decimal places than your calculator will show you. His choice of base is a big reason a bare log key still means base 101010 today. And the numbers you have been quoting, the 0.30100.30100.3010 and the 0.47710.47710.4771, are the ones he computed.

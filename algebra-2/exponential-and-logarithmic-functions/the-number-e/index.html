The Natural Base e

Intuition

A bank paying 100%100\%100% once a year turns 1 dollar into 2 dollars. Suppose instead it pays half the interest at midyear, so the second half of the year earns interest on that interest as well. Now the dollar becomes 2.25 dollars. Pay quarterly and you do better still. So ask the greedy question: if the bank compounds every month, every day, every second, how rich does one dollar get? The answer is that it closes in on about 2.71828 dollars without ever reaching it, no matter how finely you slice the year. That ceiling is not a quirk of banking. It is a constant of arithmetic, written eee, and it turns up in any process, from populations to radioactivity, whose instantaneous rate of change is proportional to how much of it there already is, as long as that change happens continuously instead of in jumps.

Learning goals

  • Watch (1+1n)n\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{n}\right)^n(1+n1​)n climb toward a ceiling
  • Name that ceiling eee, an irrational number
  • Model continuous growth with A=PertA = Pe^{rt}A=Pert
  • Define ln⁡x\ln xlnx as log⁡ex\log_e xloge​x, with every property carried over
  • Solve for time by taking ln⁡\lnln of both sides

Compounding faster and faster

You already have the discrete compounding formula. An amount PPP, growing at annual rate rrr and compounded nnn times a year for ttt years, becomes

A=P(1+rn)nt.A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.A=P(1+nr​)nt.

Strip it down to the simplest possible case so nothing distracts from the one question we care about. Take one dollar of principal, P=1P = 1P=1; take an absurdly generous rate of 100%100\%100%, so r=1r = 1r=1; and take one year, t=1t = 1t=1. Everything collapses:

A=(1+1n)n.A = \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}.A=(1+n1​)n.

Only nnn is left, the number of times a year the interest is added. Each extra compounding cuts the interest into smaller pieces but puts every piece to work sooner, so it should be worth something. The question is how much. Compute the balance for a few values of nnn:

Two things jump out. Every row does beat the row above it, so compounding more often really does pay more. But look at the size of the gain. Going from yearly to twice a year adds 0.250.250.25 dollars, a quarter on the dollar. Going from hourly to every single second adds only 0.0001550.0001550.000155 dollars, under two hundredths of a cent, even though the number of compoundings grew by a factor of over three thousand. The column is not marching upward without end. It is pressing against a ceiling somewhere just past 2.7182.7182.718.

The number the column is pressing against

Two facts decide the story.

The values increase. Every row of the table beats the row above it, and that continues for every nnn, not just the seven values shown.

The values never reach 3. However large you make nnn, the balance stays below 333 dollars.

A column of numbers that keeps rising and never gets past 333 cannot run off to infinity, and it cannot jump around. Such a column has to close in on one single value, and it has to approach that value from below. That value is the number we want.

Now be clear about what has and has not been shown, because this is the honest part. The table is evidence, not proof. The first fact is at least believable, since every piece of interest starts earning sooner when you compound more often, so finer compounding cannot pay less. The second fact is not obvious at all, and neither one is proved in this lesson: both go beyond what the algebra here can settle. Even the claim just made, that a column which keeps rising and stays under a ceiling has to close in on one single number, is itself a theorem about the real numbers that this lesson has not proved. We are borrowing all of this, out in the open, and calculus is where the borrowing gets paid back. What follows is the definition it earns us.

The number that (1+1n)n\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}(1+n1​)n closes in on, as nnn grows without end, is called eee:

e=2.718281828459045…e = 2.718281828459045\ldotse=2.718281828459045…

Like π\piπ, it is irrational: it is not a ratio of whole numbers, and its decimal expansion never ends and never repeats. So 2.718282.718282.71828 is a rounding, not the number. (The block 182818281828 appearing twice in a row is a coincidence that makes eee easy to memorize; the pattern breaks apart immediately afterward at 459045459045459045.) That eee is irrational was proved in the 1730s, and that proof, like the convergence itself, waits for calculus.

The punchline of the table is worth stating on its own: compounding more often does not pay unboundedly more. Split the year into a million pieces, or a billion, and your dollar still comes back at about 2.718282.718282.71828 dollars, a hair under that ceiling and never over it. The infinitely greedy bank and the merely hourly bank owe you almost exactly the same money.

Where e enters the general formula

The table used a 100%100\%100% rate over one year because it made the algebra vanish. Real rates are not 100%100\%100%, so we have to put rrr and ttt back into the discrete formula:

A=P(1+rn)nt.A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.A=P(1+nr​)nt.

The surprise is that no new constant appears. Split the exponent as nt=nr⋅rtnt = \frac{n}{r} \cdot rtnt=rn​⋅rt, and regroup the formula around that split:

A=P[(1+rn)n/r]rt.A = P\left[\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{n/r}\right]^{rt}.A=P[(1+nr​)n/r]rt.

The bracketed piece has exactly the table’s shape: a growing count sitting both in the fraction and in the exponent, just relabeled by rrr. So as the compounding gets finer, that bracket closes in on the very same eee the table found, whatever rrr is, and what is left is raising that limit to the fixed outer power rtrtrt:

A=Pert.A = Pe^{rt}.A=Pert.

The full derivation relabels the compounding count so the formula matches the table’s shape exactly, then lets that relabeled count grow without bound. You can read every step in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full derivation

The formula A=PertA = Pe^{rt}A=Pert is called continuous compounding, or, away from money, the continuous growth model. There is no nnn in it at all: the interest is credited so often that the question “how often” has stopped mattering. Two notes on using it. The rate rrr goes in as a decimal (6%6\%6% means r=0.06r = 0.06r=0.06, never 666). Also, rrr and ttt must use the same unit of time, so a yearly rate demands ttt in years.

When the quantity shrinks instead of grows, the same formula is written N=N0ektN = N_{0}e^{kt}N=N0​ekt with kkk negative. This lesson does not repeat the derivation for a negative rate; here N=N0ektN = N_{0}e^{kt}N=N0​ekt with k<0k < 0k<0 is presented directly as the model of a quantity that continuously loses a fixed fraction of itself. Every method in the rest of this lesson, including solving for time with ln⁡\lnln, still applies to it unchanged.

The natural logarithm

Every exponential function has an inverse, and you already know what it is called: a logarithm. Apply that to base eee and you get the logarithm this chapter has been building toward.

For x>0x > 0x>0, the natural logarithm of xxx is the logarithm with base eee:

ln⁡x=log⁡ex.\ln x = \log_{e} x.lnx=loge​x.

It is read “ell en of xxx”. Unpacking the definition of a logarithm at this base gives the statement you will use constantly, for x>0x > 0x>0:

ln⁡x=yexactly wheney=x.\ln x = y \quad \text{exactly when} \quad e^{y} = x.lnx=yexactly wheney=x.

Both directions hold, and both are just the definition of log⁡e\log_eloge​ read aloud. If ln⁡x=y\ln x = ylnx=y, then yyy is the exponent that turns eee into xxx, so ey=xe^{y} = xey=x. And if ey=xe^{y} = xey=x, then yyy is that same exponent, so ln⁡x=y\ln x = ylnx=y. Feeding one statement into the other gives the two undoing rules:

eln⁡x=x    (x>0),ln⁡(ex)=x    for every x.e^{\ln x} = x \;\; (x > 0), \qquad \ln\left(e^{x}\right) = x \;\; \text{for every } x.elnx=x(x>0),ln(ex)=xfor every x.

Now the important structural point, and the reason this section is short: ln⁡\lnln is not a new kind of object. It is the same logarithm you met earlier in this chapter, with the base pinned at eee. Nothing about the properties depended on which base was used, so every one of them survives the substitution untouched. For positive xxx and yyy and any real kkk:

ln⁡(xy)=ln⁡x+ln⁡y,ln⁡ ⁣(xy)=ln⁡x−ln⁡y,ln⁡(xk)=kln⁡x.\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y, \qquad \ln\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \ln x - \ln y, \qquad \ln\left(x^{k}\right) = k\ln x.ln(xy)=lnx+lny,ln(yx​)=lnx−lny,ln(xk)=klnx. ln⁡1=0,ln⁡e=1,log⁡bx=ln⁡xln⁡b.\ln 1 = 0, \qquad \ln e = 1, \qquad \log_{b} x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln b}.ln1=0,lne=1,logb​x=lnblnx​.

The third of those is the change-of-base rule with ln⁡\lnln as the common base. That rule holds for any valid base bbb, meaning b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \neq 1b=1, so that ln⁡b\ln blnb is defined and is not zero. It is the reason a calculator needs only two logarithm keys to compute a logarithm in any base at all.

Why “natural”? For now, because it is the logarithm that the growth constant hands you. Any question about continuous growth reaches into the exponent of erte^{rt}ert, and ln⁡\lnln is the tool that reaches there. There is a deeper reason the name is deserved, and you will meet it in calculus.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Expecting (1+1n)n\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}(1+n1​)n to collapse to 111. The reasoning “the fraction shrinks to nothing, and 111 to any power is 111” ignores that the exponent is growing at the same time. The two effects pull against each other, and the tug of war settles at 2.71828…2.71828\ldots2.71828…, which is neither 111 nor infinity. Watching a base and an exponent move at once is the whole point of the table.
  • Believing that compounding more often keeps paying meaningfully more. It does pay more, and it never pays less, but the extra shrinks to nothing. Going from hourly to every-second compounding on a dollar at 100%100\%100% adds less than two hundredths of a cent.
  • Writing e=2.72e = 2.72e=2.72 as if it were exact. eee is irrational, so every decimal you write is a rounding. Carry full precision through a calculation and round only at the end, or your interest figures will drift by cents.
  • Confusing ln⁡\lnln with log⁡\loglog. They are different bases: log⁡100=2\log 100 = 2log100=2 but ln⁡100≈4.6052\ln 100 \approx 4.6052ln100≈4.6052. On most calculators the key marked log⁡\loglog is base 101010 and the key marked ln⁡\lnln is base eee.
  • Turning ln⁡(x+y)\ln(x + y)ln(x+y) into ln⁡x+ln⁡y\ln x + \ln ylnx+lny. The product rule converts a product into a sum. A sum inside a logarithm cannot be broken up at all, and no rule anywhere in this chapter will do it.
  • Feeding the percent, not the decimal, into erte^{rt}ert. A rate of 6%6\%6% means r=0.06r = 0.06r=0.06. Writing e6te^{6t}e6t instead of e0.06te^{0.06t}e0.06t inflates one year of growth from a factor of about 1.061.061.06 to a factor of over 400400400.

Key takeaways

  • Compounding 1 dollar at 100%100\%100% for one year, nnn times, gives (1+1n)n\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}(1+n1​)n. Those values increase with nnn but never reach 333, so they close in on a single number, and that number is e=2.718281828…e = 2.718281828\ldotse=2.718281828…, an irrational number.
  • The moral of that table: compounding more often always pays a little more, and never pays unboundedly more. The ceiling is eee.
  • Continuous growth model: A=PertA = Pe^{rt}A=Pert, with rrr written as a decimal and ttt in the same time unit as rrr. It is what P(1+rn)ntP\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}P(1+nr​)nt closes in on as the compounding gets finer.
  • The natural logarithm is ln⁡x=log⁡ex\ln x = \log_{e} xlnx=loge​x for x>0x > 0x>0, so ln⁡x=y\ln x = ylnx=y exactly when ey=xe^{y} = xey=x, and therefore eln⁡x=xe^{\ln x} = xelnx=x and ln⁡(ex)=x\ln\left(e^{x}\right) = xln(ex)=x.

Why P(1+r/n)ntP(1 + r/n)^{nt}P(1+r/n)nt turns into PertPe^{rt}Pert

The lesson states the result: as the compounding gets finer, P(1+rn)ntP\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}P(1+nr​)nt closes in on PertPe^{rt}Pert. Here is the algebra behind that, worked through in full.

Compounding ever more finely turns P(1+rn)ntP\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}P(1+nr​)nt into PertPe^{rt}Pert#

Start from the discrete formula, with PPP, rrr and ttt fixed and nnn free to grow:

A=P(1+rn)nt.A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.A=P(1+nr​)nt.

The only expression we know anything about is (1+1m)m\left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^{m}(1+m1​)m, whose distinguishing feature is that the same letter appears under the 111 and up in the exponent. So force the formula into that shape. Define

m=nr,so thatn=mr.m = \frac{n}{r}, \qquad \text{so that} \qquad n = mr.m=rn​,so thatn=mr.

Since rrr is a fixed positive number, mmm grows exactly when nnn does, so letting the compounding get finer is the same as letting mmm get large.

Substitute. The fraction inside the parentheses becomes

rn=rmr=1m,\frac{r}{n} = \frac{r}{mr} = \frac{1}{m},nr​=mrr​=m1​,

and the exponent becomes nt=(mr)t=mrtnt = (mr)t = mrtnt=(mr)t=mrt. The formula is now

A=P(1+1m)mrt.A = P\left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^{mrt}.A=P(1+m1​)mrt.

Read the power-of-a-power law (xm)rt=xmrt\left(x^{m}\right)^{rt} = x^{mrt}(xm)rt=xmrt from right to left to split that exponent, which peels the rtrtrt away from the part we recognize:

A=P[(1+1m)m]rt.A = P\left[\left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^{m}\right]^{rt}.A=P[(1+m1​)m]rt.

A bit of history (optional)

The constant arrived long before anyone could say what it was. In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli, a Swiss mathematician, asked the greedy question that opens this lesson. Can a lender grow endlessly rich by compounding his interest more and more finely?

He proved that the answer is no. Whatever the bank agrees to, one unit lent for a year at full interest always comes back below three units. Three is only an upper bound, not the exact ceiling: your table’s column stays under it while pressing toward a smaller number that took decades longer to pin down and name. Bernoulli fixed the bound more than three centuries ago without a name or a decimal for that smaller number. Nor did he connect any of it to logarithms, though their tables had been in print all his life.

Leonhard Euler is the one who turned a banking curiosity into an object. He wrote the letter eee for it in a manuscript of the late 1720s. He put it into print in 1736, and by 1748 he had computed it to eighteen decimal places.

Why that letter? Nobody knows. He was certainly not naming it after himself, whatever you may hear, because such vanity was foreign to him. The likelier answers are dull ones: the letter aaa was already busy elsewhere in his notation, or eee simply stood for exponential.

Euler also proved, in the 1730s, that eee is irrational. That is the borrowed fact behind the warning in this lesson: 2.718282.718282.71828 is a rounding, not the number. It is why your column of balances closes in forever without arriving.

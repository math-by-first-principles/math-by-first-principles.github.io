Exponential Functions and Graphs

Intuition

Every function you have graphed so far is built out of adding. A line adds the same amount for each step you take to the right. A parabola adds a little more each time, a cubic more still, and even a rational function is two adding machines divided by each other. Now change one word. Instead of adding the same amount at every step, multiply by the same amount at every step. Fold a sheet of paper and the thickness doubles; fold it again and it doubles again. Ten folds later it is about a thousand sheets thick, and twenty folds later about a million. Nothing about the input sped up, since each fold is still just one more fold. What changed is the kind of arithmetic hiding behind the steps. That single change produces the exponential function, and this lesson shows that it forces itself on us. Once you insist that equal steps multiply, and that the graph is one unbroken curve, the rule f(x)=abxf(x) = a b^xf(x)=abx is the only kind of function that can obey you.

Learning goals

  • Write f(x)=abxf(x) = a b^xf(x)=abx and recognize it by testing for equal ratios over equal steps
  • State the values b0b^0b0, b−nb^{-n}b−n, and bp/qb^{p/q}bp/q that the law of exponents forces
  • Require b>0b>0b>0 and b≠1b\ne1b=1, saying why, and describe the parent graph, asymptote and monotonicity
  • Use the one-to-one property to solve an equation when both sides share a base
  • Shift to abx−h+ka b^{x-h} + kabx−h+k and move the asymptote to y=ky = ky=k

Adding versus multiplying

Put two machines side by side. Both start at 333. The first adds 444 every time the input goes up by one; the second multiplies by 222 every time the input goes up by one.

The adding row is the linear function L(x)=3+4xL(x) = 3 + 4xL(x)=3+4x. Its differences are constant: 7−3=47 - 3 = 47−3=4, 11−7=411 - 7 = 411−7=4, 15−11=415 - 11 = 415−11=4. Its ratios are not: 73\tfrac{7}{3}37​, then 117\tfrac{11}{7}711​, then 1511\tfrac{15}{11}1115​, all different.

The multiplying row is the function E(x)=3⋅2xE(x) = 3 \cdot 2^xE(x)=3⋅2x. Now the tables trade places. Its differences are not constant (333, then 666, then 121212), but its ratios are: 63=2\tfrac{6}{3} = 236​=2, 126=2\tfrac{12}{6} = 2612​=2, 2412=2\tfrac{24}{12} = 21224​=2.

That is the whole idea, and it deserves a name.

An exponential function is a function of the form

f(x)=a⋅bx,a≠0,b>0,b≠1,f(x) = a \cdot b^{x}, \qquad a \neq 0, \quad b > 0, \quad b \neq 1,f(x)=a⋅bx,a=0,b>0,b=1,

where the variable sits in the exponent and the base bbb is a fixed number. (The restrictions on bbb look arbitrary right now. Two sections from now you will see that they are forced.) Compare it with x2x^2x2 or x1/3x^{1/3}x1/3, where the variable sits in the base and the exponent is fixed. Those are power functions, and they behave nothing like this.

Check the ratio for the general rule. Step the input up by one:

f(x+1)f(x)=a⋅bx+1a⋅bx=b.\frac{f(x+1)}{f(x)} = \frac{a \cdot b^{x+1}}{a \cdot b^{x}} = b.f(x)f(x+1)​=a⋅bxa⋅bx+1​=b.

The xxx cancels completely, so the answer does not depend on where you started. Take a step of size sss instead and the same cancellation gives f(x+s)f(x)=bs\tfrac{f(x+s)}{f(x)} = b^{s}f(x)f(x+s)​=bs. Equal steps in the input multiply the output by the same factor, everywhere along the curve. That property is the reason a bank account, a bacterial culture, and the gap between a cooling cup of coffee and the room around it all end up with the same kind of formula. That same property is the one we are going to take as the definition.

It also gives you a test with teeth. Faced with a table of equally spaced inputs, divide neighbouring outputs rather than subtracting them. If every quotient is the same positive number other than 111, the data fits an exponential rule, and that quotient is bsb^{s}bs, where sss is the spacing between the inputs. Only when the inputs step by one is the quotient the base itself, and the difference matters. If the inputs run 0,2,40, 2, 40,2,4 and the outputs are 3,12,483, 12, 483,12,48, the constant quotient 444 is b2b^{2}b2, so the base is 222, not 444. If instead every difference is the same, the data is linear.

The two excluded quotients are worth naming. A constant quotient of 111 repeats a single output forever, which is the constant function. And a constant negative quotient, as in 1,−2,4,−81, -2, 4, -81,−2,4,−8, flips the sign at every step, which no positive base can do.

Equal ratios force the law of exponents

So far bxb^xbx is a formula we wrote down. Now let us do it the other way around, and demand the behaviour we want before we know any formula at all.

Suppose fff is a function defined for every real number xxx, and we insist on exactly three things.

  1. It starts at 111, so f(0)=1f(0) = 1f(0)=1. (A general starting value aaa can be multiplied back in later.)
  2. A step multiplies. For each step size sss there is some factor m(s)m(s)m(s), depending on the step but never on where you started, with f(x+s)=m(s)⋅f(x)f(x + s) = m(s) \cdot f(x)f(x+s)=m(s)⋅f(x) for every input xxx.
  3. No jumps, and no doubling back. The graph is one unbroken curve, so nudging the input a little only nudges the output a little. And when the base turns out to exceed 111, a bigger input always gives a bigger output.

That is all. We have not said the word exponent. The first two demands already pin down the law that governs everything that follows, and they will carry us all the way through the fractions. The third demand looks too obvious to be worth writing down, and we will spend it exactly once, at the very end, where it turns out to be indispensable. Watch for it.

The first two demands are the law of exponents in disguise#

Take the second demand, f(x+s)=m(s)⋅f(x)f(x+s) = m(s) \cdot f(x)f(x+s)=m(s)⋅f(x), and try it at the one input whose value we already know, namely x=0x = 0x=0. It says f(0+s)=m(s)⋅f(0)f(0 + s) = m(s) \cdot f(0)f(0+s)=m(s)⋅f(0), and since f(0)=1f(0) = 1f(0)=1 by the first demand, this collapses to f(s)=m(s)f(s) = m(s)f(s)=m(s).

So the mystery multiplier was never a mystery. The factor by which a step of size sss multiplies the output is nothing other than the function’s own value at sss. Substituting m(s)=f(s)m(s) = f(s)m(s)=f(s) back into the second demand gives

f(x+s)=f(s)⋅f(x)for all real x and s.f(x + s) = f(s) \cdot f(x) \qquad \text{for all real } x \text{ and } s.f(x+s)=f(s)⋅f(x)for all real x and s.

Rename sss as yyy and the statement reads f(x+y)=f(x) f(y)f(x + y) = f(x)\,f(y)f(x+y)=f(x)f(y): adding inputs multiplies outputs. This is the law of exponents, and we did not assume it. It fell out of the single requirement that equal steps scale the output by the same factor.

∎

One more piece of notation and we are ready to build. The function’s value at x=1x = 1x=1 is the factor produced by a one-unit step, so it deserves the name base:

b=f(1).b = f(1).b=f(1).

The next section shows that bbb is not just an important value. It is the only input you get to choose. Every other value of fff is then forced.

Building the function one input at a time

We know two things about fff: its value at 000 is 111, and f(x+y)=f(x)f(y)f(x+y) = f(x) f(y)f(x+y)=f(x)f(y). That is barely two sentences of information, but pinning down every value of fff from it, one kind of input at a time, is worth seeing in full, and the complete argument lives in Go Deeper below. What survives it, and what you will use for the rest of this course, are five facts.

  • f(x)>0f(x) > 0f(x)>0 for every real xxx. Cutting any input in half writes f(x)f(x)f(x) as a square, f(x)=[f(x/2)]2f(x) = \left[f(x/2)\right]^{2}f(x)=[f(x/2)]2, and a square is never negative; fff cannot be 000 anywhere either, since that would force it to be 000 everywhere, contradicting f(0)=1f(0) = 1f(0)=1.
  • f(n)=bnf(n) = b^{n}f(n)=bn for a positive integer nnn, from applying the law n−1n - 1n−1 times: f(2)=f(1)f(1)=b2f(2) = f(1)f(1) = b^2f(2)=f(1)f(1)=b2, f(3)=f(2)f(1)=b3f(3) = f(2)f(1) = b^3f(3)=f(2)f(1)=b3, and so on.
  • b0=1b^{0} = 1b0=1 is forced, not chosen. Setting y=0y = 0y=0 in the law gives f(x)=f(x)⋅f(0)f(x) = f(x) \cdot f(0)f(x)=f(x)⋅f(0), and dividing by f(x)≠0f(x) \neq 0f(x)=0 leaves f(0)=1f(0) = 1f(0)=1.
  • b−n=1bnb^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{b^{n}}b−n=bn1​. Since xxx and −x-x−x add to 000, the law gives f(x)f(−x)=f(0)=1f(x) f(-x) = f(0) = 1f(x)f(−x)=f(0)=1.
  • bp/q=b pqb^{p/q} = \sqrt[q]{b^{\,p}}bp/q=qbp​. Stacking a 1q\tfrac1qq1​-step qqq times must return bbb, so f ⁣(1q)f\!\left(\tfrac1q\right)f(q1​) is the positive qqq-th root of bbb, and ppp of those steps give the rest.

The domain is every real number, not only the rationals. The rationals are dense, so at this point the graph is already a fine dust of points that only looks like a curve. The third demand, that the graph has no jumps, is what fuses that dust into one unbroken curve: it forces the value at an irrational input like 2\sqrt22​ to sit exactly where climbing rational powers on both sides squeeze it.

Every one of those five facts follows step by step from nothing but the two demands above; reaching the irrationals also needs the third demand, that the graph has no jumps. Both stages are spelled out in full, the same reasoning the rest of algebra’s exponent rules rest on.

Why the base must be positive and never one

The restrictions b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \neq 1b=1 were stated without justification earlier. Here is why each one is unavoidable.

A negative base breaks immediately. Suppose someone hands you b=−4b = -4b=−4 and asks for the function f(x)=(−4)xf(x) = (-4)^{x}f(x)=(−4)x. At whole-number inputs nothing looks wrong: f(2)=16f(2) = 16f(2)=16 and f(3)=−64f(3) = -64f(3)=−64. But ask for f ⁣(12)f\!\left(\tfrac12\right)f(21​). Squaring it would have to give f(1)=−4f(1) = -4f(1)=−4, and no real number squares to a negative. So the function has no value at x=12x = \tfrac12x=21​. The same failure repeats at x=14x = \tfrac14x=41​, at x=16x = \tfrac16x=61​, and at every fraction whose denominator is even once the fraction is written in lowest terms. So the graph is riddled with holes, and no unbroken curve exists to draw. (The lowest-terms wording is doing real work. Writing 12\tfrac1221​ as 24\tfrac2442​ does not rescue anything, but 22\tfrac2222​ is just the whole number 111 in disguise, and (−4)1(-4)^{1}(−4)1 is perfectly fine.) A negative base is not merely inconvenient. It makes the function impossible.

A base of zero breaks too. With b=0b = 0b=0 every positive input gives f(x)=0f(x) = 0f(x)=0, which contradicts what we proved: an fff obeying the law of exponents with f(0)=1f(0) = 1f(0)=1 is never zero. And f(−1)f(-1)f(−1) would ask for 10\tfrac{1}{0}01​.

A base of 111 is legal but empty. The function f(x)=1xf(x) = 1^{x}f(x)=1x does satisfy f(x+y)=f(x)f(y)f(x+y) = f(x)f(y)f(x+y)=f(x)f(y), but it is the constant function 111, a horizontal line. It never grows, never decays, takes the same output for every input, and so has no inverse to undo it. Nothing exponential happens, so we exclude it by convention rather than by necessity.

That leaves b>0b > 0b>0 with b≠1b \neq 1b=1, which splits into exactly two behaviours: b>1b > 1b>1 and 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1.

The graph and what it tells you

Plot y=2xy = 2^{x}y=2x and y=(12)xy = \left(\tfrac12\right)^{x}y=(21​)x on the same axes. They are not two unrelated shapes. Since (12)x=(2−1)x=2−x\left(\tfrac12\right)^{x} = \left(2^{-1}\right)^{x} = 2^{-x}(21​)x=(2−1)x=2−x, replacing xxx by −x-x−x turns one into the other, and replacing xxx by −x-x−x is precisely the reflection across the yyy-axis you learned in the transformations lesson.

Read the picture against everything proved so far. Every statement in this list is about the parent curve y=bxy = b^{x}y=bx; the coefficient aaa goes back in the next section, and it moves some of them.

  • Domain. Every real number, because we built a value at integer, rational, and then irrational inputs.
  • Range. All positive numbers. Nothing below the axis, since f(x)=[f(x/2)]2f(x) = [f(x/2)]^2f(x)=[f(x/2)]2 is never negative and never zero. And nothing positive is missed, because the curve is unbroken and climbs from values as close to 000 as you like up to values as large as you like.
  • The yyy-intercept is always (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1), whatever the base, because b0=1b^0 = 1b0=1 for every bbb. The point (1,b)(1, b)(1,b) is on the curve too, which is how you read the base off a graph.
  • Horizontal asymptote y=0y = 0y=0. For b>1b > 1b>1 the curve flattens toward the xxx-axis on the left; for 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1 it does so on the right. It never arrives. This is a one-sided asymptote, unlike the two-sided approach you saw with rational functions.
  • Monotonic. Under the standing assumptions (b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \neq 1b=1) the function is strictly increasing exactly when b>1b > 1b>1, and strictly decreasing exactly when 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1. There is no third case and no turning point anywhere.

That last bullet has a consequence worth its own display. A strictly increasing or strictly decreasing function never takes the same output twice, so an exponential function is one-to-one:

bu=bv⟺u=v(b>0,  b≠1).b^{u} = b^{v} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad u = v \qquad (b > 0,\; b \neq 1).bu=bv⟺u=v(b>0,b=1).

Read right to left it is obvious. Read left to right it is a tool: if you can write both sides of an equation as powers of the same base, you may simply equate the exponents.

Shifting and stretching

Everything you learned about transforming graphs applies here without change. The general transformed exponential is

g(x)=a⋅b x−h+k,g(x) = a \cdot b^{\,x-h} + k,g(x)=a⋅bx−h+k,

and each constant does its usual job: hhh shifts horizontally, kkk shifts vertically, and aaa stretches the curve vertically when ∣a∣>1|a| > 1∣a∣>1 and compresses it when 0<∣a∣<10 < |a| < 10<∣a∣<1 (flipping it upside down too when aaa is negative). The one thing to watch is the asymptote. The parent curve hugs y=0y = 0y=0, so adding kkk carries the asymptote with it, up to y=ky = ky=k. The range follows: all y>ky > ky>k when a>0a > 0a>0, and all y<ky < ky<k when a<0a < 0a<0.

There is one surprise, and it belongs to exponentials alone. Watch what a horizontal shift really does:

b x−h=bx⋅b−h.b^{\,x-h} = b^{x} \cdot b^{-h}.bx−h=bx⋅b−h.

Since b−hb^{-h}b−h is just a positive constant, sliding an exponential sideways is the same as stretching it vertically. Shifting y=2xy = 2^{x}y=2x three units right multiplies it by 2−3=182^{-3} = \tfrac182−3=81​, so the “shifted” curve is the original squashed to one eighth of its height. No parabola behaves like that: sliding y=x2y = x^2y=x2 sideways genuinely moves its vertex. And the correspondence runs both ways, because the range of bxb^xbx is all positive numbers, so every stretch factor c>0c > 0c>0 equals b−hb^{-h}b−h for exactly one hhh. Naming that hhh is a job we cannot yet do, and it is the reason the next lesson exists.

Nothing polynomial keeps up

A doubling function starts small. At x=3x = 3x=3, 2x=82^{x} = 82x=8 while x2=9x^{2} = 9x2=9, so the humble parabola is ahead. It stays ahead for a while, and then it loses forever.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing x2x^{2}x2 with 2x2^{x}2x. The variable’s seat decides the species. In x2x^2x2 the variable is the base and the exponent is fixed, so it is a power function. And there it stops. Every rational input now has a forced value, and the first two demands have nothing more to say. A bit of history (optional)

This lesson trapped 222^{\sqrt{2}}22​ between rational powers and squeezed it to 2.66512.66512.6651. Squeezing tells you where a number sits. It says almost nothing about what kind of number you have caught.

In 1900 David Hilbert, then the most influential mathematician alive, drew up a list of problems for the new century. The seventh was headed “irrationality and transcendence of certain numbers”. A power of exactly this shape was one of his examples. A transcendental number is one that no polynomial equation ever has as a root, whatever whole-number coefficients you allow. Nobody could prove that 222^{\sqrt{2}}22​ is such a number, and nobody could prove that it is not.

Hilbert expected a long wait. Lecturing some years later, he guessed that the famous open problems of arithmetic would fall first. Nobody then sitting in the room, he said, would live to see this small power settled.

He was wrong, and pleasantly so. Aleksandr Gelfond, a young Russian mathematician, cracked it in 1934, and Hilbert lived to hear the news.

So the number you squeezed between those rational powers is not merely irrational. Nothing you factored in this course could ever catch it. Gelfond’s theorem asks for more than an odd-looking input, though. The exponent must be irrational and still be a root of some polynomial, as 2\sqrt{2}2​ is. Plenty of irrational inputs miss that: there is some irrational input where 2x2^{x}2x lands exactly on the ordinary whole number 333, and that input passes tamely through Gelfond’s theorem without saying anything transcendental at all. The third demand of this lesson, that the graph run unbroken, is what strings numbers of both kinds onto one curve.

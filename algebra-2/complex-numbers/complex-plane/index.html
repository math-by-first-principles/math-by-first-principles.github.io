The Complex Plane and Modulus Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

Take 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i. It is built from two ordinary real numbers, 333 and 222, and a pair of real numbers is exactly what a point of a plane needs: an across-coordinate and an up-coordinate. Plot 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i at the point (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2), and in general plot a+bia + bia+bi at (a,b)(a, b)(a,b). Complex numbers stop being formal expressions and become geometry you can see.

That plane is genuinely needed, not just convenient. The first lesson of this chapter built iii because the real number line had no room for it: every real number squares to something at least 000, so no point on the line can be a square root of −1-1−1. If iii has nowhere to live on the line, neither does 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i, so the complex numbers need a second dimension. In that plane, addition turns into sliding and conjugation turns into a mirror reflection. Even the vague question “how big is a complex number” gets an honest answer: the distance from zero, measured by the Pythagorean theorem you have known since Pre-Algebra.

A number with two coordinates

A complex number is written z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi with aaa and bbb real; aaa is its real part and bbb its imaginary part. Notice that the imaginary part is the real number bbb, not the term bibibi. The plan is to treat the pair (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) as coordinates, and the plan works because the pair pins the number down with no ambiguity: two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts match and their imaginary parts match. So distinct numbers land on distinct pairs (a,b)(a, b)(a,b), and every pair of real numbers is claimed by exactly one complex number, a+bia + bia+bi. The matching between complex numbers and points of the plane is perfect in both directions.

That matching-both-directions claim has a short formal proof, resting on the fact that no real number squares to −1-1−1. Read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full argument

That perfect matching is the definition of the complex plane: draw the familiar coordinate plane, and let the point (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) be the number a+bia + bia+bi. The horizontal axis collects the numbers with imaginary part 000, which are exactly the real numbers, so it is called the real axis: your old number line, embedded unchanged inside the bigger picture. The vertical axis collects the numbers with real part 000, which are 000 and the pure imaginary numbers bibibi, so it is called the imaginary axis. The four quadrants are read the same way as for (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) points: 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i sits in the first quadrant, −2+3i-2 + 3i−2+3i in the second, and so on.

Addition slides points

The previous lesson established the arithmetic rule: to add complex numbers, add the parts separately,

(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d) i.(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)\,i.(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i.

Read that rule with coordinate eyes. The point for the sum has horizontal coordinate a+ca + ca+c and vertical coordinate b+db + db+d. So adding the fixed number w=c+diw = c + diw=c+di sends every point ccc units across and ddd units up (negative values meaning the opposite direction). Adding www is a rigid slide of the entire plane, the same shift applied to every point at once; no stretching, no turning.

There is a second picture of the same fact. Draw zzz not as a dot but as an arrow from 000 to its point. To form z+wz + wz+w, place a copy of www‘s arrow so its tail starts at zzz‘s tip; the copy’s tip lands on z+wz + wz+w. This is tip-to-tail addition, and it is what physics and later mathematics call adding vectors. Doing it in the other order, www first and then zzz, traces the other two sides of the same figure, so both routes end at the same corner. The four points 000, zzz, www, and z+wz + wz+w form a parallelogram, and the equation z+w=w+zz + w = w + zz+w=w+z becomes something you can see.

Subtraction has its own reading. The difference z−wz - wz−w is, by definition of subtraction, the number you add to www to reach zzz, since w+(z−w)=zw + (z - w) = zw+(z−w)=z. As an arrow, then, z−wz - wz−w is the trip from www to zzz, picked up and carried back so its tail sits at 000. That arrow is what will turn the modulus into a ruler for measuring the gap between any two complex numbers.

Conjugation reflects across the real axis

The previous lesson introduced the conjugate z‾=a−bi\overline{z} = a - biz=a−bi of z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi as the sign flip that makes multiplication produce something real. In the plane, conjugation sends the point (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) to the point (a,−b)(a, -b)(a,−b): the horizontal coordinate is untouched and the vertical coordinate changes sign. That is precisely a reflection across the real axis, the mirror image of the point in the horizontal line. A number in the upper half of the plane trades places with its twin in the lower half, directly above or below itself.

Two small facts fall out of the picture immediately, and the algebra confirms both. Reflecting twice puts every point back where it started, and indeed z‾‾=a−bi‾=a+bi=z\overline{\overline{z}} = \overline{a - bi} = a + bi = zz=a−bi​=a+bi=z. And the points that do not move are exactly the points on the mirror: solving z=z‾z = \overline{z}z=z means a+bi=a−bia + bi = a - bia+bi=a−bi, so 2bi=02bi = 02bi=0, so b=0b = 0b=0. The numbers equal to their own conjugate are exactly the real numbers. That gives a clean geometric test for realness: a number zzz is real precisely when its point sits on the real axis, which is precisely when conjugation fixes it.

The modulus is a distance

For a real number xxx, the first chapter defined the absolute value ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ as the distance from xxx to 000 on the number line. Complex numbers now live in a plane, but the same question still makes sense: how far is the point zzz from 000? That distance is called the modulus of zzz (also, still, its absolute value), and it is written with the same bars, ∣z∣|z|∣z∣.

The picture shows the whole idea. Drop straight down from z=3+4iz = 3 + 4iz=3+4i to the real axis, and the segment from 000 to zzz becomes the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 333 and 444. The Pythagorean theorem gives ∣z∣=32+42=25=5|z| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5∣z∣=32+42​=25​=5. The same triangle works for any point off both axes, which is what turns the picture into a formula.

The modulus formula, from the Pythagorean theorem#

Let z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi with a≠0a \ne 0a=0 and b≠0b \ne 0b=0, so the point sits off both axes, exactly like 3+4i3 + 4i3+4i above. Drop a segment from (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) straight down (or up) to the real axis; its foot is the point (a,0)(a, 0)(a,0). The three points 000, (a,0)(a, 0)(a,0), and (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) form a right triangle: the leg along the real axis has length ∣a∣|a|∣a∣, and the vertical leg has length ∣b∣|b|∣b∣. The right angle sits at the foot, because a vertical segment meets the horizontal axis squarely. The hypotenuse runs from 000 to the point, so its length is exactly the distance we want. The Pythagorean theorem gives

∣z∣2=∣a∣2+∣b∣2=a2+b2,|z|^2 = |a|^2 + |b|^2 = a^2 + b^2,∣z∣2=∣a∣2+∣b∣2=a2+b2,

where the bars vanish because squaring erases signs. Distances are never negative, so taking the non-negative square root of both sides yields the formula

∣a+bi∣=a2+b2.|a + bi| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}.∣a+bi∣=a2+b2​.

If a=0a = 0a=0 or b=0b = 0b=0 there is no triangle, but the formula still tells the truth: a point on an axis sits ∣a∣|a|∣a∣ or ∣b∣|b|∣b∣ from 000, exactly what the formula returns once the other coordinate squares to 000. So the formula computes the distance from 000 for every complex number without exception.

∎

For a real number z=az = az=a, the formula gives ∣a+0i∣=a2=∣a∣|a + 0i| = \sqrt{a^2} = |a|∣a+0i∣=a2​=∣a∣: on the real axis, the modulus is the old absolute value. The bars are not being recycled for something new; the same concept, distance from 000, has simply grown to cover the whole plane.

Three properties come straight from the geometry, and each is one line of algebra. First, ∣z∣≥0|z| \ge 0∣z∣≥0 always, and ∣z∣=0|z| = 0∣z∣=0 exactly when z=0z = 0z=0. A sum of two real squares a2+b2a^2 + b^2a2+b2 is zero only when a=b=0a = b = 0a=b=0, matching the fact that the only point at distance 000 from the origin is the origin itself. Second, ∣z‾∣=∣z∣|\overline{z}| = |z|∣z∣=∣z∣: reflection across the real axis does not change how far a point is from 000, and indeed a2+(−b)2=a2+b2\sqrt{a^2 + (-b)^2} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}a2+(−b)2​=a2+b2​. Third, ∣−z∣=∣z∣|-z| = |z|∣−z∣=∣z∣: the point (−a,−b)(-a, -b)(−a,−b) sits on the exact opposite side of 000 at the same distance. One warning while the bars are fresh: the symbols <<< and >>> do not compare complex numbers at all, so a statement like 2+3i<3+i2 + 3i < 3 + i2+3i<3+i is meaningless. What you can compare are the moduli themselves, since ∣2+3i∣|2 + 3i|∣2+3i∣ and ∣3+i∣|3 + i|∣3+i∣ are real numbers. Comparing them settles only which point sits farther from 000, nothing more: two different complex numbers can share the same modulus, so an equal modulus never means an equal number.

The conjugate identity

The modulus and the conjugate look like unrelated gadgets, one a distance and the other a reflection. They are joined by a single identity that powers most complex-number computations from here through the rest of the subject.

The identity z z‾=∣z∣2z\,\overline{z} = |z|^2zz=∣z∣2#

The previous lesson already proved that multiplying z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi by its conjugate erases iii entirely,

z z‾=a2+b2,z\,\overline{z} = a^2 + b^2,zz=a2+b2,

by expanding the product and watching the cross terms cancel. That real number is exactly the square of the modulus formula above, so for every complex number zzz,

z z‾=∣z∣2.z\,\overline{z} = |z|^2.zz=∣z∣2.

∎

Pause on what the identity says. The left side is a product of two complex numbers; the right side is a real number that is never negative. Multiplying any number by its conjugate always lands you on the non-negative real axis, and it lands you at a geometrically meaningful spot: the squared distance from 000. This is the secret behind the division method of the previous lesson. Multiplying a denominator www by w‾\overline{w}w produced the real number ww‾w\overline{w}ww, and now you know that mystery real number by name: it is ∣w∣2|w|^2∣w∣2.

The identity also unlocks the most useful multiplication fact about the modulus: for any two complex numbers,

∣zw∣=∣z∣ ∣w∣.|zw| = |z|\,|w|.∣zw∣=∣z∣∣w∣.

In words, the length of a product is the product of the lengths. Here is why. Conjugating a product multiplies the conjugates, zw‾=z‾ w‾\overline{zw} = \overline{z}\,\overline{w}zw=zw, so squaring ∣zw∣|zw|∣zw∣ and applying the conjugate identity three times gives

∣zw∣2=(zw) zw‾=(zw)(z‾ w‾)=(z z‾)(w w‾)=∣z∣2 ∣w∣2,|zw|^2 = (zw)\,\overline{zw} = (zw)\big(\overline{z}\,\overline{w}\big) = \big(z\,\overline{z}\big)\big(w\,\overline{w}\big) = |z|^2\,|w|^2,∣zw∣2=(zw)zw=(zw)(zw)=(zz)(ww)=∣z∣2∣w∣2,

using that complex multiplication can be reordered (commutativity) and regrouped (associativity) however is convenient. Both ∣zw∣|zw|∣zw∣ and ∣z∣ ∣w∣|z|\,|w|∣z∣∣w∣ are non-negative real numbers, and non-negative numbers with equal squares are equal, so ∣zw∣=∣z∣ ∣w∣|zw| = |z|\,|w|∣zw∣=∣z∣∣w∣.

The step zw‾=z‾ w‾\overline{zw} = \overline{z}\,\overline{w}zw=zw was used without proof above, though an earlier lesson already established it. Read the reminder in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See why that step holds

This is a genuine shortcut: it finds the modulus of a complicated product without ever expanding it. It is also a genuine surprise, since nothing about the multiplication rule (ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i(ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i makes it obvious at a glance.

Distance between points, and circles

The subtraction picture now pays off. The arrow from www to zzz is the number z−wz - wz−w, so the distance between two complex numbers is the length of that arrow:

distance from w to z  =  ∣z−w∣.\text{distance from } w \text{ to } z \;=\; |z - w|.distance from w to z=∣z−w∣.

The coordinates confirm that this is the honest geometric distance. With z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi and w=c+diw = c + diw=c+di, the difference is z−w=(a−c)+(b−d) iz - w = (a - c) + (b - d)\,iz−w=(a−c)+(b−d)i, so

∣z−w∣=(a−c)2+(b−d)2,|z - w| = \sqrt{(a - c)^2 + (b - d)^2},∣z−w∣=(a−c)2+(b−d)2​,

which is the Pythagorean theorem applied to the right triangle whose legs are the horizontal gap ∣a−c∣|a - c|∣a−c∣ and the vertical gap ∣b−d∣|b - d|∣b−d∣ between the two points. Distance from 000 was the special case w=0w = 0w=0 all along.

The triangle inequality

Walk from 000 to zzz, then from zzz onward to z+wz + wz+w. The first leg has length ∣z∣|z|∣z∣, and the second leg is a translated copy of www‘s arrow, so it has length ∣w∣|w|∣w∣. The straight-line distance from 000 to the final point is ∣z+w∣|z + w|∣z+w∣. A detour through zzz can never be shorter than the straight route, so the geometry announces the triangle inequality:

∣z+w∣  ≤  ∣z∣+∣w∣.|z + w| \;\le\; |z| + |w|.∣z+w∣≤∣z∣+∣w∣.

The name is apt: 000, zzz, and z+wz + wz+w are the corners of a triangle whose side lengths are ∣z∣|z|∣z∣, ∣w∣|w|∣w∣, and ∣z+w∣|z + w|∣z+w∣. And the inequality says one side of a triangle never exceeds the other two sides combined. Geometry makes it believable, and the conjugate identity turns it into a full algebraic proof.

The route picture above already shows why the inequality holds. The Go deeper section at the end of this lesson proves it directly from the conjugate identity, with no picture needed.

Equality happens exactly when the detour is no detour at all: the arrows for zzz and www point in the same direction (or one of them is 000), so the two-leg route is already a straight line. Any other pair of directions makes the direct route strictly shorter, so ∣z+w∣<∣z∣+∣w∣|z + w| < |z| + |w|∣z+w∣<∣z∣+∣w∣.

The inequality also has a useful reverse reading. Since z=(z+w)+(−w)z = (z + w) + (-w)z=(z+w)+(−w), applying the triangle inequality to the right side gives ∣z∣≤∣z+w∣+∣w∣|z| \le |z + w| + |w|∣z∣≤∣z+w∣+∣w∣, so ∣z+w∣≥∣z∣−∣w∣|z + w| \ge |z| - |w|∣z+w∣≥∣z∣−∣w∣, and swapping the roles of zzz and www gives ∣z+w∣≥∣w∣−∣z∣|z + w| \ge |w| - |z|∣z+w∣≥∣w∣−∣z∣ as well. A sum can never be shorter than the gap between the two lengths. Together the bounds pin ∣z+w∣|z + w|∣z+w∣ into a window. For example, if ∣z∣=3|z| = 3∣z∣=3 and ∣w∣=4|w| = 4∣w∣=4, then ∣z+w∣|z + w|∣z+w∣ can be as large as 777, as small as 111, and never anything outside [1,7][1, 7][1,7].

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the coordinates. The number 2+5i2 + 5i2+5i sits at (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5): real part across, imaginary part up. Plotting it at (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) draws a different number, 5+2i5 + 2i5+2i.
  • Calling bibibi the imaginary part. The imaginary part of a+bia + bia+bi is the real number bbb. The imaginary part of 7−3i7 - 3i7−3i is −3-3−3, not −3i-3i−3i; coordinates are always real numbers.
  • Shortcutting the modulus. ∣3+4i∣|3 + 4i|∣3+4i∣ is not 3+4=73 + 4 = 73+4=7 and not 9+16=259 + 16 = 259+16=25; it is 9+16=5\sqrt{9 + 16} = 59+16​=5. Square the parts, add, then take the root, always in that order.
  • Letting a sign survive the squaring. ∣−5+12i∣=25+144=13|-5 + 12i| = \sqrt{25 + 144} = 13∣−5+12i∣=25+144​=13, because (−5)2=25(-5)^2 = 25(−5)2=25. Minus signs never make a modulus smaller, and ∣a−bi∣=∣a+bi∣|a - bi| = |a + bi|∣a−bi∣=∣a+bi∣ always.
  • Confusing z2z^2z2 with zz‾=∣z∣2z\overline{z} = |z|^2zz=∣z∣2. They differ unless zzz is real: (3+4i)2=−7+24i(3 + 4i)^2 = -7 + 24i(3+4i)2=−7+24i, a complex number, while (3+4i)(3−4i)=25(3 + 4i)(3 - 4i) = 25(3+4i)(3−4i)=25, a non-negative real. Only the conjugate product measures length.
  • Writing z<wz < wz<w for complex numbers. The symbols <<< and >>> do not compare complex numbers; 2+3i<3+i2 + 3i < 3 + i2+3i<3+i is meaningless. You can compare the real numbers ∣z∣|z|∣z∣ and ∣w∣|w|∣w∣, but equal moduli never mean equal numbers: 3+5i3 + 5i3+5i and 5−3i5 - 3i5−3i both have modulus 34\sqrt{34}34​.
  • Replacing ∣z−w∣|z - w|∣z−w∣ by ∣z∣−∣w∣|z| - |w|∣z∣−∣w∣. The distance between points is ∣z−w∣|z - w|∣z−w∣. For z=3z = 3z=3 and w=3iw = 3iw=3i the distance is ∣3−3i∣=32|3 - 3i| = 3\sqrt{2}∣3−3i∣=32​, while ∣z∣−∣w∣=0|z| - |w| = 0∣z∣−∣w∣=0; the two are not even close.
  • Promoting the triangle inequality to an equation. ∣z+w∣=∣z∣+∣w∣|z + w| = |z| + |w|∣z+w∣=∣z∣+∣w∣ only when the arrows point the same way, or one of zzz, www is 000. In general only ≤\le≤ holds, with ∣z+w∣|z + w|∣z+w∣ possibly as small as the difference of the lengths.

Key takeaways

  • The complex number a+bia + bia+bi is the point (a,b)(a, b)(a,b): the matching is one-to-one because complex numbers are equal exactly when both parts match. The horizontal real axis is the old number line (b=0b = 0b=0); the vertical imaginary axis holds 000 and the pure imaginaries (a=0a = 0a=0).
  • Addition is a slide: adding c+dic + dic+di moves every point ccc across and ddd up, and z+wz + wz+w completes the parallelogram with vertices 000, zzz, www. The difference z−wz - wz−w is the arrow from www to zzz.
  • Conjugation is the mirror flip across the real axis: (a,b)↦(a,−b)(a, b) \mapsto (a, -b)(a,b)↦(a,−b). Its fixed points are exactly the real numbers, and z‾‾=z\overline{\overline{z}} = zz=z.
  • The modulus ∣a+bi∣=a2+b2|a + bi| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}∣a+bi∣=a2+b2​ is the distance from zzz to 000, by the Pythagorean theorem. It extends the real absolute value, ∣z∣≥0|z| \ge 0∣z∣≥0 always, ∣z∣=0|z| = 0∣z∣=0 only for z=0z = 0z=0, and ∣z‾∣=∣−z∣=∣z∣|\overline{z}| = |-z| = |z|∣z∣=∣−z∣=∣z∣.
  • The conjugate identity z z‾=∣z∣2z\,\overline{z} = |z|^2zz=∣z∣2 ties the two gadgets together, explains why dividing by www uses w‾\overline{w}w, and proves multiplicativity, ∣zw∣=∣z∣ ∣w∣|zw| = |z|\,|w|∣zw∣=∣z∣∣w∣.
  • Distance and circles: the distance between zzz and www is ∣z−w∣|z - w|∣z−w∣, and the equation ∣z−c∣=r|z - c| = r∣z−c∣=r (with r>0r > 0r>0) describes the circle of radius rrr centered at ccc. The inequality ∣z−c∣<r|z - c| < r∣z−c∣<r describes that circle’s inside.
  • Triangle inequality: ∣z+w∣≤∣z∣+∣w∣|z + w| \le |z| + |w|∣z+w∣≤∣z∣+∣w∣, with equality only when the arrows are aligned, or one of zzz, www is 000, and in reverse ∣z+w∣|z + w|∣z+w∣ is at least the difference of the two moduli.

Why (a, b) determines z uniquely

The Imaginary Unit and Complex Numbers lesson already proved this in full, by contradiction: assuming a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di with b≠db \ne db=d isolates iii as a ratio of two real numbers, forcing iii itself to be real, which is impossible since no real number squares to a negative one. That contradiction forces b=db = db=d, and the equation then collapses to a=ca = ca=c as well.

So the parts of a complex number are uniquely determined: two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts match and their imaginary parts match. Distinct numbers therefore land on distinct pairs (a,b)(a, b)(a,b), and conversely every pair (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) of real numbers is claimed by exactly one complex number, namely a+bia + bia+bi. The matching between complex numbers and points of the plane is perfect in both directions.

Why conjugation passes through a product

The Arithmetic of Complex Numbers lesson already proved this: writing z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi and w=c+diw = c + diw=c+di, both zw‾\overline{zw}zw and z‾ w‾\overline{z}\,\overline{w}zw expand to the same coordinates, (ac−bd)−(ad+bc) i(ac - bd) - (ad + bc)\,i(ac−bd)−(ad+bc)i, whichever order you conjugate and multiply in. So zw‾=z‾ w‾\overline{zw} = \overline{z}\,\overline{w}zw=zw: conjugating a product equals multiplying the conjugates.

The full algebraic proof of the triangle inequality

The triangle inequality ∣z+w∣≤∣z∣+∣w∣|z + w| \le |z| + |w|∣z+w∣≤∣z∣+∣w∣#

Two preliminary facts. First, conjugation respects addition: flipping the sign of the combined imaginary part gives z+w‾=(a+c)−(b+d)i=(a−bi)+(c−di)=z‾+w‾\overline{z + w} = (a + c) - (b + d)i = (a - bi) + (c - di) = \overline{z} + \overline{w}z+w​=(a+c)−(b+d)i=(a−bi)+(c−di)=z+w. Second, no complex number’s real part exceeds its modulus: if u=p+qiu = p + qiu=p+qi, then p≤∣p∣=p2≤p2+q2=∣u∣p \le |p| = \sqrt{p^2} \le \sqrt{p^2 + q^2} = |u|p≤∣p∣=p2​≤p2+q2​=∣u∣, and note also that u+u‾=2pu + \overline{u} = 2pu+u=2p, twice the real part.

Now expand the squared modulus of the sum with the conjugate identity:

The outer terms are ∣z∣2|z|^2∣z∣2 and ∣w∣2|w|^2∣w∣2. So the middle pair is a number plus its own conjugate, which is twice that number’s real part, and by the second preliminary fact,

zw‾+wz‾  ≤  2 ∣zw‾∣=2 ∣z∣ ∣w‾∣=2 ∣z∣ ∣w∣,z\overline{w} + w\overline{z} \;\le\; 2\,|z\overline{w}| = 2\,|z|\,|\overline{w}| = 2\,|z|\,|w|,zw+wz≤2∣zw∣=2∣z∣∣w∣=2∣z∣∣w∣,

where multiplicativity and ∣w‾∣=∣w∣|\overline{w}| = |w|∣w∣=∣w∣ finish the bound. Putting the pieces together,

∣z+w∣2  ≤  ∣z∣2+2∣z∣∣w∣+∣w∣2=(∣z∣+∣w∣)2,|z + w|^2 \;\le\; |z|^2 + 2|z||w| + |w|^2 = \big(|z| + |w|\big)^2,∣z+w∣2≤∣z∣2+2∣z∣∣w∣+∣w∣2=(∣z∣+∣w∣)2,

and since ∣z+w∣|z + w|∣z+w∣ and ∣z∣+∣w∣|z| + |w|∣z∣+∣w∣ are both non-negative, comparing squares compares the numbers: ∣z+w∣≤∣z∣+∣w∣|z + w| \le |z| + |w|∣z+w∣≤∣z∣+∣w∣.

A bit of history (optional)

For two and a half centuries a fair question had no answer. Everyone agreed on how to calculate with square roots of negatives. Nobody could say where such a number actually sat. An ordinary number has a position on the line, while these had none at all. That is most of the reason they were called impossible.

Wessel was not the first to gesture at an answer. John Wallis had sketched a related geometric picture in 1685, but it did not catch on. The first complete, correct treatment came from outside mathematics entirely. Caspar Wessel was a Norwegian surveyor who spent his life mapping coastline. A surveyor’s daily job is turning a heading and a length into a pair of numbers. So he already saw a quantity as an arrow that points, which was exactly what was needed. In 1797 his paper laying the whole idea out was presented to the Royal Danish Academy on his behalf, since he was not a member and was not there to read it himself. Measure one part along one axis and the other part at right angles, and the number becomes a place you can reach.

His account was complete and correct, and almost nobody read it. Wessel wrote in Danish, in a journal barely read abroad, and his paper was recovered only in the 1890s. So the picture carries the name of Jean-Robert Argand, a bookkeeper who printed the same idea independently in 1806.

The picture is the surveyor’s, however, and you have relied on it all lesson. The number a+bia + bia+bi sits at the point (a,b)(a, b)(a,b), and its distance from zero is its modulus.

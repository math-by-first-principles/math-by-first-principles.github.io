Complex Roots of Quadratics

Intuition

The Quadratic Formula lesson ended with an honest refusal. When the discriminant Δ=b2−4ac\Delta = b^2 - 4acΔ=b2−4ac came out negative, the parabola floated clear of the xxx-axis and the formula asked for the square root of a negative number. In that case the only correct answer available was that no real solution exists. That refusal was never a fact about the equation. It was a fact about the numbers we were allowed to use. This chapter built the missing numbers. You now own a number iii with i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, arithmetic for numbers of the form a+bia + bia+bi, and a conjugate operation that flips the sign of the imaginary part. This closing lesson brings those tools back to the question that motivated the whole chapter. Run the quadratic formula when Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 and do not stop. Every quadratic turns out to have exactly two roots after all, counting a repeated root twice. The pair the discriminant said were missing were never missing; they sit just off the real line, mirror images of each other across it.

Learning goals

  • Convert to iii-form, then run the quadratic formula through a negative discriminant
  • Prove the conjugate root theorem for real coefficients
  • Read the shared real part as the axis of symmetry
  • Rebuild the monic quadratic from one non-real root
  • Factor every quadratic over the complex numbers

Square roots of negative numbers, pinned down

The Imaginary Unit and Complex Numbers lesson already answered the question the real numbers could not: for any positive real kkk, the negative number −k-k−k has exactly two square roots, iki\sqrt{k}ik​ and −ik-i\sqrt{k}−ik​, and the radical symbol −k\sqrt{-k}−k​ names the choice iki\sqrt{k}ik​, the same convention that makes 9\sqrt{9}9​ mean 333 rather than −3-3−3. So −16=4i\sqrt{-16} = 4i−16​=4i, and the other root, −4i-4i−4i, is never lost: the ±\pm± in the quadratic formula supplies it, exactly as it supplied −3-3−3 alongside 333 in the real case.

One habit from that lesson is worth repeating here, because everything below leans on it: convert every −k\sqrt{-k}−k​ to iii-form before doing any radical arithmetic. The familiar rule a⋅b=ab\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​⋅b​=ab​ holds only for non-negative aaa and bbb, and multiplying negative radicals directly, without converting first, gives the wrong sign.

Two features of that answer deserve a hard look. First, the real part p=−b2ap = -\tfrac{b}{2a}p=−2ab​ is the axis of symmetry, the same axis the real roots straddled in Chapter 4, and it is exactly the 333 that Example 1 found. Second, the two roots have the same real part and opposite imaginary parts, so each is the conjugate of the other, as 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i and 3−2i3 - 2i3−2i were. That is not a small remark, and the next section proves it is no accident of the formula.

The left graph below and the right graph are two different pictures of the same equation, not one picture: the left one plots yyy against xxx, and no point on that curve has an imaginary part. The right one plots the complex plane, where the axes are the real and imaginary parts of a root, not xxx and yyy. The only thing the two pictures share is the number 333, the parabola’s axis of symmetry and the roots’ common real part.

For real coefficients, non-real roots always arrive in conjugate pairs

The formula handed us conjugate pairs, but that could in principle be a habit of the formula rather than a law of the equations. It is a law. Real coefficients alone force it, by an argument that never mentions the quadratic formula and will survive, word for word, for polynomials of every degree.

The conjugate root theorem for quadratics#

Let aaa, bbb, ccc be real with a≠0a \ne 0a=0, and suppose the complex number zzz satisfies az2+bz+c=0az^2 + bz + c = 0az2+bz+c=0. We claim z‾\overline{z}z satisfies it too.

An earlier lesson already proved that conjugation passes through both addition and multiplication, w+v‾=w‾+v‾\overline{w + v} = \overline{w} + \overline{v}w+v​=w+v and wv‾=w‾ v‾\overline{wv} = \overline{w}\,\overline{v}wv=wv, and that a real number is its own conjugate. Those two facts are all this proof needs, applied to z2=z⋅zz^2 = z \cdot zz2=z⋅z in particular.

Now conjugate the whole equation az2+bz+c=0az^2 + bz + c = 0az2+bz+c=0. The right side is real, so it conjugates to itself, 0‾=0\overline{0} = 00=0. The left side is a sum of products, so the conjugate passes through every join, and the real coefficients aaa, bbb, ccc come through unchanged:

0=az2+bz+c‾=a‾ z‾ 2+b‾ z‾+c‾=az‾ 2+bz‾+c.0 = \overline{az^2 + bz + c} = \overline{a}\,\overline{z}^{\,2} + \overline{b}\,\overline{z} + \overline{c} = a\overline{z}^{\,2} + b\overline{z} + c.0=az2+bz+c​=az2+bz+c=az2+bz+c.

That last expression is the original quadratic evaluated at z‾\overline{z}z, and it equals 000, so z‾\overline{z}z is a root.

∎

Read the theorem’s fine print, because both halves of it matter. When zzz is real, z‾=z\overline{z} = zz=z and the statement says nothing new; its full force is for non-real roots, which it forbids from ever appearing alone. A quadratic with real coefficients therefore has exactly three possible root inventories: two distinct real roots, one repeated real root, or a conjugate pair off the real line. “One real root and one non-real root” is not on the list, for any real coefficients whatsoever. Sum and product still tell the truth

Chapter 4 closed with the relations r1+r2=−bar_1 + r_2 = -\tfrac{b}{a}r1​+r2​=−ab​ and r1r2=car_1 r_2 = \tfrac{c}{a}r1​r2​=ac​, but the proof there leaned on a factorization over the reals, which assumed the roots were real. Do the relations survive the move off the real line? They do, and the cleanest way to see it is to compute both quantities straight from the pair p±qip \pm qip±qi. The sum is immediate, because the imaginary parts cancel:

(p+qi)+(p−qi)=2p=2(−b2a)=−ba.(p + qi) + (p - qi) = 2p = 2\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) = -\frac{b}{a}.(p+qi)+(p−qi)=2p=2(−2ab​)=−ab​.

The product is a conjugate product, the difference of squares in disguise:

(p+qi)(p−qi)=p2−(qi)2=p2−q2i2=p2+q2,(p + qi)(p - qi) = p^2 - (qi)^2 = p^2 - q^2 i^2 = p^2 + q^2,(p+qi)(p−qi)=p2−(qi)2=p2−q2i2=p2+q2,

and substituting the formulas for ppp and qqq collapses it to a single fraction:

p2+q2=b24a2+4ac−b24a2=4ac4a2=ca.p^2 + q^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} + \frac{4ac - b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{4ac}{4a^2} = \frac{c}{a}.p2+q2=4a2b2​+4a24ac−b2​=4a24ac​=ac​.

Both relations hold verbatim, so everything you learned to do with sums and products of roots still works when the roots are complex. The previous lesson measured a complex number’s distance from the origin by its modulus, with ∣z∣2=p2+q2|z|^2 = p^2 + q^2∣z∣2=p2+q2, so the product you just computed is exactly the squared modulus of either root:

r1r2=∣z∣2=ca.r_1 r_2 = |z|^2 = \frac{c}{a}.r1​r2​=∣z∣2=ac​.

Because p2+q2p^2 + q^2p2+q2 is a sum of real squares with q≠0q \ne 0q=0, it is strictly positive, and that forces a fact about the coefficients, plus a geometric reading of r1r2r_1 r_2r1​r2​ as a distance.

The practical payoff is reconstruction. One non-real root of a real quadratic determines everything, because the conjugate theorem hands you the other root, and then the sum and product hand you the coefficients. A monic quadratic with roots p±qip \pm qip±qi must be

Every quadratic factors completely now

The factoring lesson in Chapter 4 insisted that “factors” is meaningless until you name the number system. Over the reals a quadratic with Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 is genuinely irreducible: no real roots, so no real linear factors. Over the complex numbers the last holdouts fall.

For any two roots r1r_1r1​ and r2r_2r2​ of ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c, real or not, the sum and product relations turn a(x−r1)(x−r2)=a(x2−(r1+r2)x+r1r2)a(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = a\left(x^2 - (r_1 + r_2)x + r_1 r_2\right)a(x−r1​)(x−r2​)=a(x2−(r1​+r2​)x+r1​r2​) straight back into ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c, so a(x−r1)(x−r2)a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)a(x−r1​)(x−r2​) is always the factored form. Chapter 4 already showed this when the roots are real. When they are the non-real conjugate pair zzz and z‾\overline{z}z, it is the identity just proved:

a(x−z)(x−z‾)=a(x2−(z+z‾)x+zz‾)=a(x2−(−ba)x+ca)=ax2+bx+c.\begin{aligned} a(x - z)(x - \overline{z}) &= a\left(x^2 - \left(z + \overline{z}\right)x + z\overline{z}\right) \\ &= a\left(x^2 - \left(-\frac{b}{a}\right)x + \frac{c}{a}\right) = ax^2 + bx + c. \end{aligned}a(x−z)(x−z)​=a(x2−(z+z)x+zz)=a(x2−(−ab​)x+ac​)=ax2+bx+c.​

The simplest cases are the most striking: x2+25=x2−(5i)2=(x−5i)(x+5i)x^2 + 25 = x^2 - (5i)^2 = (x - 5i)(x + 5i)x2+25=x2−(5i)2=(x−5i)(x+5i) is a difference of squares in disguise, and x2−4x+5x^2 - 4x + 5x2−4x+5, irreducible over the reals, factors as (x−2−i)(x−2+i)(x - 2 - i)(x - 2 + i)(x−2−i)(x−2+i).

Step back and look at what happened to the discriminant’s trichotomy for a quadratic with real coefficients. Over the reals it counted roots: two, one, or none. Over the complex numbers the count is always the same, exactly two roots counted with multiplicity, and the discriminant’s job shrinks to saying where they live. If Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0 they sit at two points of the real line; if Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 they coincide at one real point, giving the repeated factor a(x−r)2a(x - r)^2a(x−r)2. If Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 they leave the line and stand as a conjugate pair, one above it and one below. Nothing is ever missing: this is the degree-two case of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, which a later chapter states for every degree.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying radicals before converting to iii-form. −4⋅−9\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9}−4​⋅−9​ is (2i)(3i)=−6(2i)(3i) = -6(2i)(3i)=−6, not 36=6\sqrt{36} = 636​=6. The rule ab=ab\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​b​=ab​ was proved for non-negative reals only. Rewrite every −k\sqrt{-k}−k​ as iki\sqrt{k}ik​ first, then multiply.

What the sum and product also tell you

The product r1r2=p2+q2r_1 r_2 = p^2 + q^2r1​r2​=p2+q2 is a sum of two real squares, and q≠0q \ne 0q=0 whenever the roots are non-real, so that sum is strictly positive. Two extra facts follow for free.

A bit of history (optional)

Some insults harden into ordinary technical terms. This is one of them.

By the 1630s algebra had noticed an odd gap in the counting of roots. A quadratic ought to deliver two, a cubic three, a quartic four. The count kept coming up short, because certain roots were no measured amount at all. Gerolamo Cardano had already met such a pair in 1545. He split 101010 into two parts whose product is 404040, then walked away calling the answer useless.

Rene Descartes, the philosopher who rebuilt geometry on coordinates, named that gap in 1637. An equation has as many roots as its degree, he wrote, but some are merely imagined. He meant it as an insult. A genuine root you can locate on a line, while an imagined one was, he supposed, a piece of bookkeeping that kept the count tidy.

The word survived, and it has misled students ever since. Nothing about 5+2i5 + 2i5+2i is imagined. It is as solid as 2\sqrt{2}2​, and you can add it, multiply it, and return it to the equation. The adjective carries an old opinion, not a property of the number.

What Descartes called imagined, you now know how to find, check, and put to use. It is as ordinary a number as any he trusted.

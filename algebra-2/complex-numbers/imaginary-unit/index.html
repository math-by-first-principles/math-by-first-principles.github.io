The Imaginary Unit and Complex Numbers

Intuition

The last chapter ended with a refusal. When the discriminant of a quadratic is negative, the verdict was “no real solutions,” and the proof was airtight. No real number has a negative square, so an equation like x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1 asks for something no real number can do. But refusals like this one have a history of not being final. Once, 3−53 - 53−5 had no answer, until negative numbers were admitted. Once, 2x=32x = 32x=3 had no answer, until fractions were. Once, x2=2x^2 = 2x2=2 had no answer, until irrational numbers were. Each time, mathematics did not bend the rules of arithmetic; it enlarged the supply of numbers until the unsolvable equation became solvable. In every one of those enlargements, the rules of arithmetic kept working exactly as before. This lesson makes that move one final time. We introduce a single new number, called iii, defined by the lone property i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, and from that one equation the whole system of complex numbers a+bia + bia+bi unfolds. A later chapter proves the remarkable payoff: this enlargement is the last one that polynomial equations will ever demand.

Learning goals

  • Define iii by i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, keeping the laws of arithmetic
  • Convert −k\sqrt{-k}−k​ to iki\sqrt kik​ before multiplying
  • Cycle the powers of iii with period four
  • Write a complex number as a+bia + bia+bi with real parts
  • Match parts to check whether two complex numbers are equal
  • Place this beside the earlier enlargements of the number system

The equation the discriminant refused

Take the simplest quadratic with no real roots:

x2+1=0.x^2 + 1 = 0.x2+1=0.

Here a=1a = 1a=1, b=0b = 0b=0, c=1c = 1c=1, so the discriminant is Δ=02−4(1)(1)=−4<0\Delta = 0^2 - 4(1)(1) = -4 < 0Δ=02−4(1)(1)=−4<0, and the previous chapter’s biconditional delivers its verdict: no real solutions. The parabola y=x2+1y = x^2 + 1y=x2+1 has its vertex at height 111 and floats entirely above the xxx-axis. But the discriminant is a summary of a deeper fact, and since this whole chapter is built on that fact, it deserves its own proof from first principles.

No real number has a negative square#

Let xxx be any real number. Exactly one of three things is true of it: xxx is positive, xxx is zero, or xxx is negative.

If xxx is positive, then x2=x⋅xx^2 = x \cdot xx2=x⋅x is a product of two positive numbers, which is positive. If xxx is zero, then x2=0x^2 = 0x2=0. If xxx is negative, then x2x^2x2 is a product of two negative numbers. In that case the sign rules of arithmetic make a negative times a negative positive, so x2x^2x2 is positive again.

In every case x2≥0x^2 \ge 0x2≥0. Consequently, for any positive number kkk, the equation x2=−kx^2 = -kx2=−k has no real solution at all: its left side is at least 000, while its right side sits strictly below 000. In particular nothing real solves x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1, which is the algebraic reason the parabola y=x2+1y = x^2 + 1y=x2+1 never touches the axis.

∎

The proof is airtight, so read its conclusion carefully. The word doing all the work is real. The argument used facts about real numbers, their signs and their order, and it rules out a real solution only. It leaves open a subtler question: could there be a larger supply of numbers, obeying the same laws of arithmetic, in which x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1 does have an answer?

Enlarging the number system, one more time

So we make the same move for x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1, and the entire construction is one sentence. The imaginary unit iii is a new number defined by the single property

 i2=−1 \boxed{\,i^2 = -1\,}i2=−1​

and we insist that the ordinary rules of algebra continue to apply to it. That is the complete definition. Every other fact in this chapter is deduced from that one equation plus the laws of arithmetic you already own.

If inventing a number by decree feels like cheating, notice that every extension before it was the same decree. Nobody can hold −2-2−2 apples either, yet negative numbers earned their place because they obey the rules and settle real questions. What makes the decree mathematics rather than wishful thinking is consistency: no contradiction ever falls out of computing with iii. And a later lesson in this chapter will even show you where these numbers live geometrically, which is when most people stop feeling uneasy about them.

The payoff is immediate. The equation x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1 now has a solution, namely iii, and in fact it has a second one: −i-i−i. To check it, square −i-i−i using the sign rules, which still apply:

(−i)2=(−i)(−i)=i⋅i=i2=−1.(-i)^2 = (-i)(-i) = i \cdot i = i^2 = -1.(−i)2=(−i)(−i)=i⋅i=i2=−1.

So x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1 has the two solutions x=±ix = \pm ix=±i, a mirror pair, exactly the way x2=2x^2 = 2x2=2 has the mirror pair ±2\pm\sqrt{2}±2​.

That is exactly the property that makes an enlargement legitimate mathematics rather than a convenient trick, since no contradiction ever falls out of it.

Square roots of negative numbers

With iii in hand, every negative number acquires a square root. For a positive real number kkk, we define

−k=ik.\sqrt{-k} = i\sqrt{k}.−k​=ik​.

The definition has to earn the radical sign, so check that the right side really does square to −k-k−k, using the commutative law to square each factor separately:

(ik)2=i2⋅(k)2=(−1)⋅k=−k.\left(i\sqrt{k}\right)^2 = i^2 \cdot \left(\sqrt{k}\right)^2 = (-1) \cdot k = -k.(ik​)2=i2⋅(k​)2=(−1)⋅k=−k.

It checks. So −25=i25=5i\sqrt{-25} = i\sqrt{25} = 5i−25​=i25​=5i, and −7=i7\sqrt{-7} = i\sqrt{7}−7​=i7​. Two habits of notation are worth adopting on day one. Write the iii in front of the radical, as in i7i\sqrt{7}i7​, or after a whole coefficient, as in 5i5i5i. Either placement keeps the iii from ever being misread as sitting underneath the radical. And remember that the radical symbol names a single number, just as it did for positive inputs: −25\sqrt{-25}−25​ is 5i5i5i, while the equation x2=−25x^2 = -25x2=−25 has the two solutions ±5i\pm 5i±5i.

One familiar rule does not survive the trip. For nonnegative aaa and bbb you proved a b=ab\sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​b​=ab​, and that hypothesis was not decoration. Feed the rule two negative inputs and it fails:

−4⋅−9=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6,but(−4)(−9)=36=6.\sqrt{-4} \cdot \sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6, \qquad \text{but} \qquad \sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6.−4​⋅−9​=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6,but(−4)(−9)​=36​=6.

The left computation is the correct one; the right one applies a theorem outside the territory it was proved on. The safe procedure is mechanical: convert every square root of a negative into its iii-form first, and only then multiply.

Equations with no real roots, solved

The whole chapter opened because x2+1=0x^2 + 1 = 0x2+1=0 had no real solution. Equations of that shape now surrender completely.

Worked example 2 Solve x2+49=0x^2 + 49 = 0x2+49=0

Isolate the square:

x2=−49.x^2 = -49.x2=−49.

The proof at the top of the lesson shows no real number can work, so look among the new numbers, guided by −49=7i\sqrt{-49} = 7i−49​=7i. Test 7i7i7i by squaring it:

(7i)2=72⋅i2=49⋅(−1)=−49.(7i)^2 = 7^2 \cdot i^2 = 49 \cdot (-1) = -49.(7i)2=72⋅i2=49⋅(−1)=−49.

It works. Test −7i-7i−7i as well: (−7i)2=(−7)2⋅i2=49⋅(−1)=−49(-7i)^2 = (-7)^2 \cdot i^2 = 49 \cdot (-1) = -49(−7i)2=(−7)2⋅i2=49⋅(−1)=−49, so it works too. The solutions are

x=±7i.x = \pm 7i.x=±7i.

Just as x2=49x^2 = 49x2=49 has the mirror pair ±7\pm 7±7, the equation x2=−49x^2 = -49x2=−49 has the mirror pair ±7i\pm 7i±7i. A quadratic never has more than two solutions, a fact the lesson on complex roots of quadratics will pin down in this new setting. So that mirror pair, ±7i\pm 7i±7i, is the complete answer.

The same two steps, isolate the square and attach ±i\pm i±i, solve every equation of the form x2=−kx^2 = -kx2=−k with kkk positive: the solutions are x=±ikx = \pm i\sqrt{k}x=±ik​. What this lesson does not yet do is solve a general quadratic whose discriminant is negative. That harvest, where the quadratic formula runs unchanged and simply outputs complex numbers, is the final lesson of this chapter.

The powers of i cycle

The definition hands you i2i^2i2. Multiplying by iii again and again hands you everything else, and a striking pattern appears within four steps:

i1=i,i2=−1,i3=i2⋅i=−i,i4=i3⋅i=−i⋅i=−i2=1.i^1 = i, \qquad i^2 = -1, \qquad i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i, \qquad i^4 = i^3 \cdot i = -i \cdot i = -i^2 = 1.i1=i,i2=−1,i3=i2⋅i=−i,i4=i3⋅i=−i⋅i=−i2=1.

The fourth power is 111, so the fifth power is i4⋅i=1⋅i=ii^4 \cdot i = 1 \cdot i = ii4⋅i=1⋅i=i and the tape starts over. The powers of iii march through the same four values forever: iii, −1-1−1, −i-i−i, 111, then again iii, −1-1−1, −i-i−i, 111, with no exceptions.

The cycle turns the scariest-looking problems into division with remainder.

Every power of iii is one of four values#

First, i4=1i^4 = 1i4=1, computed above from nothing but i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1. Now take any positive integer exponent nnn and divide it by 444 with remainder, writing n=4q+rn = 4q + rn=4q+r where qqq is the quotient and the remainder rrr is one of 0,1,2,30, 1, 2, 30,1,2,3. The exponent laws for positive whole-number exponents encode nothing but repeated multiplication together with the associative and commutative laws. So they hold for iii just as they hold for every other number. Therefore

in=i4q+r=(i4)q⋅ir=1q⋅ir=ir.i^n = i^{4q + r} = \left(i^4\right)^{q} \cdot i^r = 1^q \cdot i^r = i^r.in=i4q+r=(i4)q⋅ir=1q⋅ir=ir.

So the value of ini^nin depends only on the remainder rrr. A remainder of 111 gives iii, a remainder of 222 gives −1-1−1, a remainder of 333 gives −i-i−i, and a remainder of 000 means n=4qn = 4qn=4q exactly, so in=(i4)q=1i^n = \left(i^4\right)^q = 1in=(i4)q=1. Four remainders, four values, repeating forever with period four.

∎

Worked example 3 Compute i2026i^{2026}i2026

Divide the exponent by 444 and keep the remainder:

2026=4⋅506+2,2026 = 4 \cdot 506 + 2,2026=4⋅506+2,

so the remainder is 222. By the cycle,

i2026=(i4)506⋅i2=1⋅(−1)=−1.i^{2026} = \left(i^4\right)^{506} \cdot i^2 = 1 \cdot (-1) = -1.i2026=(i4)506⋅i2=1⋅(−1)=−1.

A shortcut makes this instant for big exponents: 100100100 is a multiple of 444. So all the digits except the last two contribute a multiple of 444, and only the final two digits matter. Here they are 262626, and 26=4⋅6+226 = 4 \cdot 6 + 226=4⋅6+2 leaves remainder 222, the same answer with less arithmetic.

Complex numbers and their two parts

Multiples of iii alone are not the end of the construction, because the rules of arithmetic let you add a real number to one of them. The result, something like 3+5i3 + 5i3+5i, cannot be compressed any further: it is not a real number, and it is not a plain multiple of iii. It is a genuinely two-part number, and numbers of this shape get the chapter’s name.

A complex number is a number of the form a+bia + bia+bi, where aaa and bbb are real numbers. The real number aaa is called its real part, the real number bbb is called its imaginary part, and the expression a+bia + bia+bi is called standard form. Note the fine print carefully: the imaginary part of 3+5i3 + 5i3+5i is 555, the real coefficient of iii, and not 5i5i5i. Signs ride along with the parts, so −2−i=−2+(−1)i-2 - i = -2 + (-1)i−2−i=−2+(−1)i has real part −2-2−2 and imaginary part −1-1−1.

The definition quietly swallows everything that came before it. Choose b=0b = 0b=0 and a+0ia + 0ia+0i is just the real number aaa: every real number is a complex number. So the reals sit inside the new system unchanged, exactly as the integers sit inside the rationals. Choose a=0a = 0a=0 with b≠0b \ne 0b=0 and you get numbers like 4i4i4i, called pure imaginary numbers. Choose both parts nonzero and you get the new two-part numbers like 3+5i3 + 5i3+5i. The name complex is the old sense of the word, a whole built out of parts, as in a building complex; it is a description of structure, not of difficulty.

Answer choices

Standard form puts the real part first, then the imaginary part as the coefficient of iii: real part −6-6−6 and imaginary part 222 give −6+2i-6 + 2i−6+2i.

The second option swaps which part is which. The third flips the sign of the imaginary part. The fourth flips the sign of the real part.

How to add, multiply, and divide these two-part numbers is the entire next lesson. This lesson’s job is to know what they are, and to settle the one operation that needs no new machinery at all: deciding when two of them are equal.

One real-number habit, though, does not survive the extension, and it is worth seeing why before it bites. Real numbers are ordered: any two of them can be compared with <<<. The previous chapters’ inequality rules relied on that, in particular the rule that multiplying an inequality by a positive number preserves it. Try to fit iii into such an ordering. If i>0i > 0i>0, then multiplying both sides of i>0i > 0i>0 by the positive number iii must preserve the inequality, giving i2>0i^2 > 0i2>0, that is, −1>0-1 > 0−1>0, which is false. If instead i<0i < 0i<0, then −i>0-i > 0−i>0, and multiplying −i>0-i > 0−i>0 by the positive number −i-i−i gives (−i)2>0(-i)^2 > 0(−i)2>0, which is again −1>0-1 > 0−1>0, false. And i=0i = 0i=0 fails immediately, since 02=0≠−10^2 = 0 \ne -102=0=−1. No placement is consistent, so no <<< or >>> ordering of the complex numbers can keep the familiar inequality rules working: a question like “is 2+3i2 + 3i2+3i bigger than 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i” has no meaning. Complex numbers support equality, not an order like that.

Equality matches the parts

Two complex numbers in standard form are equal exactly when they match part by part. This sounds too obvious to need proof, but it is a genuine theorem, not just a convenient habit.

Here is why a mismatch is impossible. Suppose someone claimed 3+2i=7+5i3 + 2i = 7 + 5i3+2i=7+5i. Subtract 777 and 2i2i2i from both sides:

3−7=(5−2) i,that is,−4=3i,soi=−43.3 - 7 = (5 - 2)\,i, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad -4 = 3i, \qquad \text{so} \qquad i = -\frac{4}{3}.3−7=(5−2)i,that is,−4=3i,soi=−34​.

That would make iii a real number, −43-\frac{4}{3}−34​. Check it by squaring: i2i^2i2 is supposed to be −1-1−1, but (−43)2=169\left(-\frac{4}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{16}{9}(−34​)2=916​, which is not −1-1−1. Something broke, and the culprit is the assumption that 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i and 7+5i7 + 5i7+5i could be equal while their imaginary coefficients, 222 and 555, disagree. Nothing about the numbers 333, 777, 222, and 555 was special; whenever two complex numbers in standard form have different imaginary coefficients, the same steps force iii to equal some real number, and the very first proof of this lesson already ruled that out: no real number squares to −1-1−1. So the imaginary coefficients must match, and once they do, the real parts are left equal too.

The paragraph above shows the reason for one pair of numbers. You can read the same argument written out for any a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di at once, in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

The theorem is a workhorse, because it means one complex equation silently carries the information of two real equations: match the real parts, then match the imaginary parts. Whole problems fall to that single move.

Worked example 4 One complex equation, two real unknowns

Find the real numbers xxx and yyy satisfying

(2x+3)+(y−1) i=9+4i.(2x + 3) + (y - 1)\,i = 9 + 4i.(2x+3)+(y−1)i=9+4i.

Both sides are in standard form, and xxx and yyy are real, so the parts on each side are real numbers and the equality theorem applies. Match the real parts:

2x+3=9⇒2x=6⇒x=3.2x + 3 = 9 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2x = 6 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 3.2x+3=9⇒2x=6⇒x=3.

Match the imaginary parts:

y−1=4⇒y=5.y - 1 = 4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = 5.y−1=4⇒y=5.

So x=3x = 3x=3 and y=5y = 5y=5, and the check confirms it: (2⋅3+3)+(5−1)i=9+4i(2 \cdot 3 + 3) + (5 - 1)i = 9 + 4i(2⋅3+3)+(5−1)i=9+4i. Notice the fine print earned its keep: the matching move is valid because the problem said xxx and yyy are real. That is what guarantees 2x+32x + 32x+3 and y−1y - 1y−1 really are the real and imaginary parts.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Hearing “imaginary” as “fake.” The name is a four-century-old taunt that stuck. The number iii is defined by i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 and obeys the same laws of arithmetic as every other number. So iii is exactly as legitimate as −2-2−2 and 2\sqrt{2}2​, both of which were also once refused as absurd.
  • Multiplying roots of negatives with the product rule. −4⋅−9\sqrt{-4} \cdot \sqrt{-9}−4​⋅−9​ is not 36=6\sqrt{36} = 636​=6. The rule a b=ab\sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​b​=ab​ was proved for nonnegative aaa and bbb only. Convert to iii-form first: −4⋅−9=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6\sqrt{-4} \cdot \sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6−4​⋅−9​=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6.
  • Calling bibibi the imaginary part. The imaginary part of a+bia + bia+bi is the real number bbb. For 3−5i3 - 5i3−5i it is −5-5−5: not −5i-5i−5i, and not 555.
  • Leaving i2i^2i2 standing in an answer. Every i2i^2i2 collapses to −1-1−1 on sight. (3i)2(3i)^2(3i)2 is 9i2=−99i^2 = -99i2=−9, a real number, not 9i29i^29i2 and certainly not 999.
  • Reporting “no solution” for x2=−25x^2 = -25x2=−25. It has no real solution. Among the complex numbers it has exactly two, x=±5ix = \pm 5ix=±5i. Always name the number system your answer lives in.
  • Comparing complex numbers with <<< or >>>. There is no consistent way to call iii positive or negative; either assumption forces −1>0-1 > 0−1>0. No <<< or >>> ordering of the complex numbers keeps the usual inequality rules working, so complex numbers are compared for equality, not size.
  • Using the wrong cycle for powers. ini^nin is decided by the remainder of nnn on division by 444, not by whether nnn is even or odd. An even exponent does not mean the answer is 111: the remainder of 666 is 222, so i6=−1i^6 = -1i6=−1. A remainder of 000 gives 111.

  • For k>0k > 0k>0, −k=ik\sqrt{-k} = i\sqrt{k}−k​=ik​, and (ik)2=−k\left(i\sqrt{k}\right)^2 = -k(ik​)2=−k. Why matching parts is the only way complex numbers can be equal

a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di exactly when a=ca = ca=c and b=db = db=d#

Throughout, aaa, bbb, ccc, ddd are real numbers. One direction needs no work: if a=ca = ca=c and b=db = db=d, the two expressions are the same number written twice.

For the other direction, suppose a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di. Subtract ccc and bibibi from both sides and factor the right side:

a−c=(d−b) i.a - c = (d - b)\,i.a−c=(d−b)i.

Now suppose, aiming for a contradiction, that d≠bd \ne bd=b. Then d−bd - bd−b is a nonzero real number, and dividing both sides by it is legal, because dividing by a nonzero real is just multiplying by another real. That isolates iii:

i=a−cd−b.i = \frac{a - c}{d - b}.i=d−ba−c​.

The right side is a quotient of real numbers, so it is itself a real number, and the equation says iii is that real number. Square both sides: −1=i2-1 = i^2−1=i2 would be the square of a real number. But the first proof of this lesson showed no real number has a negative square. Contradiction. So the supposition fails, and d=bd = bd=b after all. Feeding that back in gives a−c=0⋅i=0a - c = 0 \cdot i = 0a−c=0⋅i=0, so a=ca = ca=c as well.

Both directions hold, so the biconditional stands: equality of complex numbers is equality of real parts and equality of imaginary parts, simultaneously.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

For almost two hundred years these numbers had no symbol of their own. People simply wrote the square root of minus one, over and over, in the middle of enormous calculations. That looks harmless. It was not.

A radical sign carries habits along with it. The most dangerous is the familiar rule that a root times a root equals the root of the product. That rule is proved for positive numbers only, and it fails on negative ones. So the notation itself invited an error on every line, and it collected plenty.

Leonhard Euler, a Swiss mathematician of enormous output, gave the number its own symbol instead. In a paper of 1777 he handed the whole business to a single letter, iii, the number whose square is −1-1−1. A letter carries no borrowed habits the way a radical sign does: it promises nothing about roots, so it offers no familiar rule to misapply by accident.

Carl Friedrich Gauss later supplied the word for a number with two parts, and called it complex. He believed the older word, imaginary, had frightened people away from mathematics that was never seriously in doubt.

Your own definition needs neither the excuse nor the label. It is one letter and one equation, i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, and the whole of this lesson follows from that.

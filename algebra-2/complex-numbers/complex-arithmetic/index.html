Arithmetic of Complex Numbers

Intuition

The previous lesson introduced one new number, iii, with the single property i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, and called anything of the form a+bia + bia+bi, with aaa and bbb real, a complex number. Knowing what a number is only gets you halfway; you also need to calculate with it. This lesson supplies the arithmetic, and most of it is not new. You add and subtract complex numbers by combining like terms, exactly as with 3+5x3 + 5x3+5x. You multiply them by expanding, exactly as with binomials, then use the one new fact i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 wherever it shows up. Even division, which looks hardest, reuses a method you already know: the same conjugate trick that once cleared 2\sqrt{2}2​ out of a denominator, aimed at a new target.

Learning goals

  • Add and subtract complex numbers, real parts with real parts
  • Multiply by expanding, then exchange i2i^2i2 for −1-1−1
  • Find a conjugate, and use zz‾=a2+b2z\overline{z} = a^2 + b^2zz=a2+b2
  • Divide by rationalizing with the denominator's conjugate

One new symbol, no new laws

When the previous lesson called a+bia + bia+bi a number, that word carried a promise: the new numbers obey the same laws of algebra as the old ones. Addition is still commutative and associative, multiplication still distributes over addition, and subtraction and division still mean what they always meant, namely adding an opposite and multiplying by a reciprocal. The complex numbers add exactly one fact to that rulebook:

i2=−1.i^2 = -1.i2=−1.

Everything in this lesson is forced by that single stipulation. The working strategy throughout is to treat a+bia + bia+bi as a two-term expression in the symbol iii and run the ordinary algebra you have used for years. Then exchange i2i^2i2 for −1-1−1 the moment it appears. The question worth asking in advance is whether the answers stay inside the system. If you add, subtract, multiply, or divide two complex numbers, is the result again of the form (real)+(real) i(\text{real}) + (\text{real})\,i(real)+(real)i? Watch for the answer as each operation is built, because it will be yes all four times. That is what makes the complex numbers a self-contained place to do mathematics rather than a notational accident.

Addition and subtraction combine like terms

Add two complex numbers by regrouping, which the commutative and associative laws permit, and then factoring iii out of the two imaginary terms, which the distributive law permits:

(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(bi+di)=(a+c)+(b+d) i.(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (bi + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)\,i.(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(bi+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i.

Real parts add to real parts, and imaginary parts add to imaginary parts, precisely the way xxx-terms and constant terms keep to themselves when you simplify (3+5x)+(2+4x)(3 + 5x) + (2 + 4x)(3+5x)+(2+4x). Subtraction is the same computation after distributing the minus sign through both terms of the second number:

(a+bi)−(c+di)=(a−c)+(b−d) i.(a + bi) - (c + di) = (a - c) + (b - d)\,i.(a+bi)−(c+di)=(a−c)+(b−d)i.

Notice what the right sides look like. Since a+ca + ca+c and b+db + db+d are real numbers, each result is again (real)+(real) i(\text{real}) + (\text{real})\,i(real)+(real)i: adding and subtracting complex numbers can never produce anything but a complex number. Two operations down, two to go.

Multiplication is expansion plus one substitution

Multiply two complex numbers the way you multiply any two binomials: distribute every term of the first factor across every term of the second. Nothing about that step is new. The new step comes after, when an i2i^2i2 is sitting in the result and must be exchanged for −1-1−1:

(a+bi)(c+di)=ac+ad i+bc i+bd i2=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc) i.(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + ad\,i + bc\,i + bd\,i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\,i.(a+bi)(c+di)=ac+adi+bci+bdi2=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i.

Read the middle expression carefully. The four products are exactly the four you would write for (a+bx)(c+dx)(a + bx)(c + dx)(a+bx)(c+dx). The only difference is the last one, where bd i2=−bdbd\,i^2 = -bdbdi2=−bd abandons the imaginary column and joins the real part with its sign flipped. That is the whole novelty of complex multiplication. Do not memorize the boxed-looking formula (ac−bd)+(ad+bc) i(ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\,i(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i; it is faster and safer to expand and substitute each time, letting the formula rebuild itself. And once more the result is (real)+(real) i(\text{real}) + (\text{real})\,i(real)+(real)i, so multiplication also stays inside the system.

The conjugate makes products real

One special product deserves its own name, because it powers everything in the rest of the lesson. The conjugate of z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi is the number

z‾=a−bi,\overline{z} = a - bi,z=a−bi,

the same number with the sign of its imaginary part flipped. So 4+7i‾=4−7i\overline{4 + 7i} = 4 - 7i4+7i​=4−7i, the conjugate of −3−5i-3 - 5i−3−5i is −3+5i-3 + 5i−3+5i, and 6i‾=−6i\overline{6i} = -6i6i=−6i. Flipping the sign twice restores the original, so z‾‾=z\overline{\overline{z}} = zz=z: conjugation is its own undo. A real number is its own conjugate, since r=r+0ir = r + 0ir=r+0i and flipping the sign of 000 changes nothing. The converse also holds: z‾=z\overline{z} = zz=z means a−bi=a+bia - bi = a + bia−bi=a+bi, and matching the imaginary parts forces −b=b-b = b−b=b, so b=0b = 0b=0. A complex number equals its own conjugate exactly when it is real.

The product of conjugates is real and never negative#

Expand the product of z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi and z‾=a−bi\overline{z} = a - biz=a−bi like any other product:

(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2−ab i+ab i−b2i2=a2+b2.(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - ab\,i + ab\,i - b^2 i^2 = a^2 + b^2.(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2−abi+abi−b2i2=a2+b2.

The two middle terms are opposites and cancel, and the last term flips sign because i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1. Compare this with the difference-of-squares pattern (x+y)(x−y)=x2−y2(x + y)(x - y) = x^2 - y^2(x+y)(x−y)=x2−y2 that you know from factoring: the pattern fires here too, giving a2−(bi)2=a2−b2i2a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 - b^2 i^2a2−(bi)2=a2−b2i2. That last i2i^2i2 turns the familiar difference of squares into a sum of squares. So

z z‾=a2+b2,z\,\overline{z} = a^2 + b^2,zz=a2+b2,

a real number built from two squares of real numbers. Each square is at least 000, so z z‾≥0z\,\overline{z} \ge 0zz≥0 always. In fact it is never merely 000 unless zzz is: if a2+b2=0a^2 + b^2 = 0a2+b2=0 with a,ba, ba,b real, neither square can be positive without pushing the sum above 000, so both squares are 000 and a=0a = 0a=0, b=0b = 0b=0. Conversely z=0z = 0z=0 gives 02+02=00^2 + 0^2 = 002+02=0. So z z‾z\,\overline{z}zz is strictly positive for every complex number except z=0z = 0z=0 itself.

Conjugation has one more property, useful for the chapter’s final lesson: it does not care whether you conjugate before or after adding or multiplying.

Conjugating a sum gives the same answer as adding the conjugates, and conjugating a product gives the same answer as multiplying the conjugates. That fact is what will let the final lesson of this chapter show that complex roots of a quadratic with real coefficients always arrive in a conjugate pair, a preview worked out in this Dive Deeper item.

Division is rationalizing in disguise

You have solved this problem before, in a different costume. When a denominator held a radical, you multiplied by a well-chosen form of 111 to clear it:

13+2=13+2⋅3−23−2=3−29−2=3−27.\frac{1}{3 + \sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{3 + \sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{3 - \sqrt{2}}{3 - \sqrt{2}} = \frac{3 - \sqrt{2}}{9 - 2} = \frac{3 - \sqrt{2}}{7}.3+2​1​=3+2​1​⋅3−2​3−2​​=9−23−2​​=73−2​​.

The conjugate 3−23 - \sqrt{2}3−2​ was chosen because the difference-of-squares pattern makes the new denominator 32−(2)2=73^2 - (\sqrt{2})^2 = 732−(2​)2=7, a rational number. Division of complex numbers is the same trick with the same name. To divide by c+dic + dic+di, multiply numerator and denominator by its conjugate c−dic - dic−di, and the proof above guarantees the new denominator is the real number c2+d2c^2 + d^2c2+d2:

a+bic+di=a+bic+di⋅c−dic−di=(a+bi)(c−di)c2+d2.\frac{a + bi}{c + di} = \frac{a + bi}{c + di} \cdot \frac{c - di}{c - di} = \frac{(a + bi)(c - di)}{c^2 + d^2}.c+dia+bi​=c+dia+bi​⋅c−dic−di​=c2+d2(a+bi)(c−di)​.

Multiplying by c−dic−di\frac{c - di}{c - di}c−dic−di​ is multiplying by 111, so the value never changes; only its costume does. After this move, the numerator is one ordinary complex multiplication, and dividing a complex number by the positive real c2+d2c^2 + d^2c2+d2 just divides each part, since p+qim=pm+qm i\frac{p + qi}{m} = \frac{p}{m} + \frac{q}{m}\,imp+qi​=mp​+mq​i.

Pause on when this is allowed. The move needs c2+d2≠0c^2 + d^2 \ne 0c2+d2=0, and the conjugate proof showed c2+d2=0c^2 + d^2 = 0c2+d2=0 happens only for c+di=0c + di = 0c+di=0. So the only forbidden division is division by zero, the same single exception the real numbers always had. Every nonzero complex number can divide, and in particular every nonzero z=c+diz = c + diz=c+di has a reciprocal,

1c+di=c−dic2+d2,\frac{1}{c + di} = \frac{c - di}{c^2 + d^2},c+di1​=c2+d2c−di​,

again of the form (real)+(real) i(\text{real}) + (\text{real})\,i(real)+(real)i. That settles the closure question asked at the start: all four operations take complex numbers to complex numbers. The system that began as one symbol and one rule supports the full arithmetic that the rational and real numbers enjoy, with nothing missing and nothing extra needed.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Leaving an i2i^2i2 unspent. The expansion (2+3i)(4−5i)=8−10i+12i−15i2(2 + 3i)(4 - 5i) = 8 - 10i + 12i - 15i^2(2+3i)(4−5i)=8−10i+12i−15i2 is not finished: the last term must become +15+15+15 and join the real part. Treating i2i^2i2 as if it were 111, or dropping it, is the most common multiplication error, and it changes 23+2i23 + 2i23+2i into a wrong answer like −7+2i-7 + 2i−7+2i.
  • Subtracting only half of the second number. In (4+3i)−(6−8i)(4 + 3i) - (6 - 8i)(4+3i)−(6−8i), the minus sign hits both terms: 4+3i−6+8i4 + 3i - 6 + 8i4+3i−6+8i. Forgetting to flip the −8i-8i−8i gives −2−5i-2 - 5i−2−5i instead of the correct −2+11i-2 + 11i−2+11i.
  • Calling (a+bi)(a−bi)(a + bi)(a - bi)(a+bi)(a−bi) a difference of squares and stopping at a2−b2a^2 - b^2a2−b2. The pattern does fire, but it produces a2−b2i2a^2 - b^2 i^2a2−b2i2, and the i2i^2i2 flips the sign: the product of conjugates is the sum a2+b2a^2 + b^2a2+b2. That plus sign is the entire reason the conjugate clears denominators.
  • Multiplying only the denominator by the conjugate. To compute a+bic+di\frac{a + bi}{c + di}c+dia+bi​ you multiply by c−dic−di\frac{c - di}{c - di}c−dic−di​, a form of 111. Multiplying the bottom alone (or using the numerator’s conjugate) changes the value of the fraction, not just its form.
  • Flipping the wrong sign when conjugating. The conjugate changes only the imaginary part: the conjugate of −3−5i-3 - 5i−3−5i is −3+5i-3 + 5i−3+5i, not 3+5i3 + 5i3+5i. The real part never moves.
  • Stopping short of the form a+bia + bia+bi. An answer like 5+10i5\frac{5 + 10i}{5}55+10i​ or 8−10i+12i−15i28 - 10i + 12i - 15i^28−10i+12i−15i2 is unfinished work. Reduce every power of iii, combine like terms, and split any real denominator across both parts before calling a computation done.

Key takeaways

  • Complex numbers obey the ordinary laws of algebra plus the single rule i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1.
  • Add and subtract like terms: (a+bi)±(c+di)=(a±c)+(b±d) i(a + bi) \pm (c + di) = (a \pm c) + (b \pm d)\,i(a+bi)±(c+di)=(a±c)+(b±d)i.
  • Multiply by expanding, then exchange every i2i^2i2 for −1-1−1: (a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc) i(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\,i(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i. Re-derive this by expansion rather than memorizing it.
  • The conjugate of z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi is z‾=a−bi\overline{z} = a - biz=a−bi. Then z z‾=a2+b2z\,\overline{z} = a^2 + b^2zz=a2+b2, real and never negative, and 000 only for z=0z = 0z=0.
  • Divide by rationalizing: multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, so the denominator becomes the positive real c2+d2c^2 + d^2c2+d2; in particular 1c+di=c−dic2+d2\frac{1}{c + di} = \frac{c - di}{c^2 + d^2}c+di1​=c2+d2c−di​.
  • All four operations return answers of the form (real)+(real) i(\text{real}) + (\text{real})\,i(real)+(real)i, and only division by 000 is forbidden.

A bit of history (optional)

By the middle of the 1500s, square roots of negatives kept turning up inside perfectly normal problems. Nobody could ban them, since the algebra produced them without being asked. Nobody could use them with any confidence either. One writer had multiplied a pair of them in a single worked case. He set the answer aside as useless. What one such root times another meant in general, nobody had settled.

Rafael Bombelli was an engineer who drained marshes in central Italy for a living. He wrote his algebra book while one of those projects was held up, and it appeared in 1572. What he offered was not so much a discovery as a decision. He treated the impossible root as an object like any other, and built it the first systematic table of signs.

He had no symbol for it, so he used words. A positive multiple of the root he called “plus of minus”, and a negative one “minus of minus”. Then came the rule that opened everything after it: plus of minus, times plus of minus, makes minus. Take the words away and that line is i⋅i=−1i \cdot i = -1i⋅i=−1, two centuries before the letter arrived.

The rest followed from ordinary algebra, and Bombelli used it on cubics that had defeated everyone. His key point was a humble one. Once you can multiply, you can expand, and this lesson is that point carried through. You spread the product out as you would any binomial, then trade every i2i^2i2 for −1-1−1.

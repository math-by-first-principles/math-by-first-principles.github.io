Solving Quadratics by Factoring

Intuition

In Algebra I you learned how to factor: hunt for the pair of numbers, split the middle term, group. This lesson asks the question that course postponed. Why does rewriting x2−5x+6x^2 - 5x + 6x2−5x+6 as (x−2)(x−3)(x - 2)(x - 3)(x−2)(x−3) solve anything? A factorization is only a change of form; the equation has not changed at all. The answer is a single fact about the real numbers, called the Zero Product Property. It says that the only way a product of real numbers can equal zero is for one of the factors to be zero. That fact, not the factoring itself, is what turns one quadratic equation into two linear ones, and only when a zero sits on one side to begin with. The lesson is also honest about the method’s limit: factoring is a search, and a search can fail even when real solutions exist.

Learning goals

  • Factor a quadratic with a zero on one side, then apply the Zero Product Property
  • Recognize a zero, a root, and an xxx-intercept's xxx-coordinate as one number
  • Turn one known zero into a factor
  • Accept that an integer or rational factor search can fail
  • Explain why a repeated root touches the axis instead of crossing it

Factoring is a change of form

The last lesson introduced a quadratic function as one object wearing several normal forms, each of which answers one question for free. Standard form f(x)=ax2+bx+cf(x) = ax^2 + bx + cf(x)=ax2+bx+c hands you the yyy-intercept, since f(0)=cf(0) = cf(0)=c, and the end behavior, from the sign of aaa. Vertex form f(x)=a(x−h)2+kf(x) = a(x - h)^2 + kf(x)=a(x−h)2+k hands you the vertex and everything that flows from it. Standard form and vertex form exist for every quadratic. A third form,

f(x)=a(x−r1)(x−r2),f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2),f(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​),

is factored form, and where it exists over the real numbers, it answers for free the question this lesson lives on: where is fff equal to zero? Not every quadratic has one, since not every quadratic has a real zero to display; the section “One zero buys one factor” pins down exactly when it does. Factoring is not a new kind of problem. It is the change of form that starts at standard and ends at factored, done precisely because you want the zero question answered.

Half of the claim that “the zeros are visible” costs nothing to check. Substitute x=r1x = r_1x=r1​: the first factor becomes r1−r1=0r_1 - r_1 = 0r1​−r1​=0, so the whole product is f(r1)=a⋅0⋅(r1−r2)=0f(r_1) = a \cdot 0 \cdot (r_1 - r_2) = 0f(r1​)=a⋅0⋅(r1​−r2​)=0. The same happens at x=r2x = r_2x=r2​. So r1r_1r1​ and r2r_2r2​ are zeros of fff, by substitution alone.

But “visible” claims more than that. It claims r1r_1r1​ and r2r_2r2​ are the only zeros, that no third number sneaks in. Substitution cannot rule out numbers you have not tried. What closes the gap is the next section, and it is a fact about the real numbers themselves, not about polynomials.

The Zero Product Property

To solve (x−2)(x−3)=0(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0(x−2)(x−3)=0, read the left side as a product of the two real numbers x−2x - 2x−2 and x−3x - 3x−3. The product is zero, so at least one of those two numbers must itself be zero:

x−2=0orx−3=0,x - 2 = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x - 3 = 0,x−2=0orx−3=0,

and one quadratic equation has become two linear equations, which Chapter 1 taught you to solve. The two equations are joined by “or,” not “and”: either factor being zero is enough, so the solution set collects every number that solves either one, {2,3}\{2, 3\}{2,3}.

That move rested on one fact: a product of two real numbers is zero only when one of the numbers itself is zero. It is called the Zero Product Property, and it is the whole engine of this lesson. Here it is stated precisely, for two real numbers, and proved.

The Zero Product Property#

Suppose uuu and vvv are real numbers with uv=0uv = 0uv=0. We claim u=0u = 0u=0 or v=0v = 0v=0.

If u=0u = 0u=0, we are done. So suppose u≠0u \neq 0u=0. Every nonzero real number has a reciprocal, so 1u\tfrac{1}{u}u1​ exists. Multiply both sides of uv=0uv = 0uv=0 by it:

1u(uv)=1u⋅0.\tfrac{1}{u}(uv) = \tfrac{1}{u} \cdot 0.u1​(uv)=u1​⋅0.

The left side regroups as (1u u)v=1⋅v=v\left(\tfrac{1}{u}\, u\right) v = 1 \cdot v = v(u1​u)v=1⋅v=v, and the right side is 000. So v=0v = 0v=0. Either way, at least one factor is zero.

The converse direction is immediate: if a factor is 000, the product is 0⋅v=00 \cdot v = 00⋅v=0. So a product of two real numbers equals zero exactly when at least one of the factors equals zero.

∎

Notice what the proof leaned on: every nonzero real number has a reciprocal. That is a privilege of the real numbers, not a law of arithmetic in general. Build a toy arithmetic out of a clock face, where you count hours and every multiple of 121212 lands back at 000. In that system 3×4=12=03 \times 4 = 12 = 03×4=12=0: a product of two nonzero numbers that is zero. The property fails there, and it fails exactly where the proof would break: on the clock 333 has no reciprocal, since multiplying 333 by whole numbers only ever lands on 000, 333, 666, or 999, never on 111. Number systems in which nonzero factors can multiply to zero are said to have zero divisors; the real numbers have none. Every time you set a factor equal to zero this week, that is the license you are using.

The general factored form adds nothing new. If a≠0a \neq 0a=0 and a(x−r1)(x−r2)=0a(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = 0a(x−r1​)(x−r2​)=0, multiply by 1a\tfrac{1}{a}a1​ to get (x−r1)(x−r2)=0(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = 0(x−r1​)(x−r2​)=0, and then x=r1x = r_1x=r1​ or x=r2x = r_2x=r2​. So f(x)=a(x−r1)(x−r2)f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)f(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​) vanishes exactly at r1r_1r1​ and r2r_2r2​: the zeros on display in factored form are all the zeros there are. The gap left by the first section is closed, and the number system is what closed it.

The zero on one side is not optional

The property is about zero and about nothing else. Zero is the only number that pins down a factor: the only way to multiply two reals and get 000 is for one of them to be 000. Every other number factors in many ways. Six, for instance, is 1⋅61 \cdot 61⋅6, and 2⋅32 \cdot 32⋅3, and 12⋅12\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1221​⋅12, and (−4)(−32)(-4)\left(-\tfrac{3}{2}\right)(−4)(−23​), and infinitely many other products. Knowing that a product of two numbers equals 666 does not tell you what either number is. So if the right-hand side is not 000, the step “set each factor equal to the right-hand side” is not a small shortcut; nothing justifies it.

Worked example 2 The right-hand side must be zero

Solve (x−1)(x−2)=6(x - 1)(x - 2) = 6(x−1)(x−2)=6.

The tempting move is to copy the pattern of the last example and write ”x−1=6x - 1 = 6x−1=6 or x−2=6x - 2 = 6x−2=6,” giving x=7x = 7x=7 or x=8x = 8x=8. Test x=7x = 7x=7:

(7−1)(7−2)=6⋅5=30≠6.(7 - 1)(7 - 2) = 6 \cdot 5 = 30 \neq 6.(7−1)(7−2)=6⋅5=30=6.

The correct route puts a zero on one side first. Expand, then subtract 666:

x2−3x+2=6⟹x2−3x−4=0,x^2 - 3x + 2 = 6 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0,x2−3x+2=6⟹x2−3x−4=0,

Check both against the original equation: (4−1)(4−2)=3⋅2=6(4 - 1)(4 - 2) = 3 \cdot 2 = 6(4−1)(4−2)=3⋅2=6 and (−1−1)(−1−2)=(−2)(−3)=6(-1 - 1)(-1 - 2) = (-2)(-3) = 6(−1−1)(−1−2)=(−2)(−3)=6. The same discipline settles equations that arrive half-factored. Three names for one object

Three chapters of this course have each given a name to the same numbers, and it is worth pinning down how they relate. A zero of the function fff is a number rrr where f(r)=0f(r) = 0f(r)=0; that is Chapter 2 vocabulary. A root (or solution) of the equation ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 is a number that makes the equation true; that is equation-solving vocabulary. Substituting shows these are the same number: a zero of fff is exactly a root of f(x)=0f(x) = 0f(x)=0. The xxx-intercept of the parabola y=f(x)y = f(x)y=f(x) is different in kind: it is a point, not a number, the point where the graph meets the xxx-axis. A point sits on the xxx-axis exactly when its yyy-coordinate is 000, so the graph point (r,f(r))(r, f(r))(r,f(r)) lands on the axis exactly when f(r)=0f(r) = 0f(r)=0, that is, exactly when rrr is a zero. So every zero rrr gives the xxx-intercept point (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0), and every xxx-intercept point’s xxx-coordinate is a zero. Two names for one number, plus a point that number locates on the graph.

The figure shows one more coincidence that is not a coincidence. A parabola is a mirror shape: the two points where it meets the axis are reflections of each other across the axis of symmetry, so that axis has to run exactly halfway between them,

x=r1+r22,x = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2},x=2r1​+r2​​,

the midpoint of the two zeros. (Expanding a(x−r1)(x−r2)a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)a(x−r1​)(x−r2​) and comparing it to standard form gives the same fact algebraically, and turns into a systematic tool in the lesson on sums and products of roots.)

Factored form also controls the sign of fff everywhere, not just where it vanishes: each factor x−rx - rx−r is negative to the left of rrr and positive to the right of it, and a quadratic can switch sign only by passing through zero. For f(x)=(x+1)(x−3)f(x) = (x + 1)(x - 3)f(x)=(x+1)(x−3), both factors are negative left of −1-1−1 (product positive), the factors disagree between −1-1−1 and 333 (product negative), and both are positive right of 333 (product positive), which is exactly what the figure shows: the arch dips below the axis only between its two zeros. One zero buys one factor

Factored form displays its zeros. This section proves the reverse: any known zero can be cashed in for a factor, so the two currencies, zeros and factors, exchange in both directions.

A real zero always buys a real factor#

Let f(x)=ax2+bx+cf(x) = ax^2 + bx + cf(x)=ax2+bx+c with real coefficients and a≠0a \neq 0a=0, and suppose rrr is a real zero, so ar2+br+c=0ar^2 + br + c = 0ar2+br+c=0. We claim x−rx - rx−r divides fff exactly, with the leftover factor written down explicitly.

The x2x^2x2 coefficients already agree. Matching the xxx coefficients forces d−ar=bd - ar = bd−ar=b, so d=b+ard = b + ard=b+ar. Conversely, suppose fff factors as (px+q)(sx+t)(px + q)(sx + t)(px+q)(sx+t) with real coefficients, where p,s≠0p, s \neq 0p,s=0 because the leading coefficient ps=aps = aps=a is not zero. The identical argument runs word for word with “rational” in place of “real”. If aaa, bbb, ccc and the zero rrr are all rational, then d=b+ard = b + ard=b+ar is rational, so the factorization is rational as well. ∎

Keep the real and rational versions of this fact separate; they use different number systems, and mixing them up produces false statements. A quadratic can have real zeros and still refuse to factor over the rationals, as the next section shows. What can never happen is a real zero without a real factorization, or a real factorization without a real zero. The proof also hands you a working tool: find one zero by any means, even by guessing, and the factorization follows.

Factoring is a search, and the search can fail

Look honestly at what the factoring step is. To factor x2+bx+cx^2 + bx + cx2+bx+c over the integers, you hunt for a pair of integers ppp and qqq with

(x+p)(x+q)=x2+(p+q) x+pq,sop+q=b,pq=c.(x + p)(x + q) = x^2 + (p + q)\,x + pq, \qquad \text{so} \qquad p + q = b, \quad pq = c.(x+p)(x+q)=x2+(p+q)x+pq,sop+q=b,pq=c.

When c≠0c \neq 0c=0, the hunt is finite, since only finitely many integer pairs multiply to a fixed nonzero number, and that finiteness is why it feels mechanical. (When c=0c = 0c=0, factor out xxx first, as the checkpoint above did.) But it is still a search, and a search can come up empty. When it does, you have learned something about your candidate list, not necessarily about the equation.

Take x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2. The integer pairs with product −2-2−2 are 1,−21, -21,−2 and 2,−12, -12,−1, with sums −1-1−1 and 111: never 000. So the integer hunt fails, and no rational pair rescues it either. The reason is that a rational factorization would hand us a rational zero, by the last section, and the zeros of x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 are ±2\pm\sqrt{2}±2​, which are irrational. Yet over the real numbers the same polynomial factors on sight, as a difference of squares with k=2k = \sqrt{2}k=2​:

x2−2=(x−2 )(x+2 ).x^2 - 2 = \big(x - \sqrt{2}\,\big)\big(x + \sqrt{2}\,\big).x2−2=(x−2​)(x+2​).

So the honest sentence is never ”x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 does not factor.” It is ”x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 does not factor over the rationals, and does factor over the reals.” The claim has no meaning until you name the number system, because the supply of allowed coefficients is what the search draws on.

A second kind of failure cuts deeper: some quadratics do not factor even over the reals. By the previous section, that must mean those quadratics have no real zeros at all. Take x2+x+1x^2 + x + 1x2+x+1: add and subtract 14\tfrac1441​ to rewrite it as (x+12)2+34\left(x + \tfrac12\right)^2 + \tfrac34(x+21​)2+43​, a square, which is never negative, plus 34\tfrac3443​. So x2+x+1≥34x^2 + x + 1 \geq \tfrac34x2+x+1≥43​ for every real xxx: it can never reach 000, and the search for a real factorization was never going to succeed. It fails not for lack of cleverness, but because there is nothing to find.

The paragraph above already shows x2+x+1x^2 + x + 1x2+x+1 is always at least 34\tfrac3443​, so it never reaches zero. You can read a second, independent proof of that same fact in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

But f(0)=−4<0f(0) = -4 < 0f(0)=−4<0 while the parabola opens upward and climbs without bound on both sides, so the graph starts high, dips below the axis, and climbs high again: it meets the axis twice. Two real zeros exist, and they are simply invisible to an integer search because they are irrational.

This is the honest reason the chapter continues past this lesson. Factoring, when it works, is the fastest route to the zeros. But it is a search, and the integer or rational version of that search comes up empty whenever the zeros are irrational, or when there are no real zeros at all, and a failed search cannot tell you which of those two situations you are in. Completing the square, the next lesson, is not a search: it is a computation with no step that can fail, and the discriminant that comes out of it will announce in advance which of the three situations you are in, without a search.

Check your understanding

Answer choices

A failed integer search reports only on the searcher's finite candidate list, not on the equation itself. As the x2+2x−4x^2 + 2x - 4x2+2x−4 example above showed, an integer search can fail even when real zeros exist, simply because those zeros are irrational; nothing about a failed search rules that out. The graph of x2+3x−5x^2 + 3x - 5x2+3x−5 starts below the axis at f(0)=−5f(0) = -5f(0)=−5 and climbs without bound on both sides, so it must cross the axis twice, and those two real zeros are exactly the ones a finite integer hunt was never going to catch. Completing the square, the next lesson, settles which situation you are in without any search.

Old friends, read as factored forms

Your earlier course devoted a chapter to special factorizations. In this chapter’s language, each one is a factored form you should recognize on sight, because each instantly answers the zero question.

A difference of squares is the factored form with a symmetric pair of zeros:

x2−k2=(x−k)(x+k),zeros ±k.x^2 - k^2 = (x - k)(x + k), \qquad \text{zeros } \pm k.x2−k2=(x−k)(x+k),zeros ±k.

The symmetry is visible from standard form too: b=0b = 0b=0, so the axis of symmetry is x=0x = 0x=0, midway between −k-k−k and kkk, just as the midpoint rule promised.

A perfect square trinomial is the factored form with a repeated zero:

x2±2kx+k2=(x±k)2,x^2 \pm 2kx + k^2 = (x \pm k)^2,x2±2kx+k2=(x±k)2,

the case the next section examines. And a common factor is a factored form that is easy to walk past: 2x2−8x=2x(x−4)2x^2 - 8x = 2x(x - 4)2x2−8x=2x(x−4) is already a product. The factor xxx is just (x−0)(x - 0)(x−0) in everyday clothes, contributing the zero 000. That is the checkpoint above in disguise: dividing 2x2=8x2x^2 = 8x2x2=8x through by xxx throws that zero away, while moving everything to one side keeps it.

Solve 9x2−25=09x^2 - 25 = 09x2−25=0 and 4x2−12x+9=04x^2 - 12x + 9 = 04x2−12x+9=0 by recognizing forms rather than hunting.

The first is a difference of squares, (3x)2−52(3x)^2 - 5^2(3x)2−52:

A repeated root touches instead of crossing

When the two zeros coincide, the factored form collapses to

f(x)=a(x−r)2,f(x) = a(x - r)^2,f(x)=a(x−r)2,

and the solution set of f(x)=0f(x) = 0f(x)=0 is {r}\{r\}{r}: one element. A set does not count multiplicity, so nothing about the set remembers that the factor appeared twice. The graph remembers instead.

Take a>0a > 0a>0. A square is never negative, so a(x−r)2≥0a(x - r)^2 \geq 0a(x−r)2≥0 for every xxx, with equality only at x=rx = rx=r. The entire parabola lies on or above the xxx-axis and meets it at the single point (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0): the graph touches the axis and turns back without crossing. We say the parabola is tangent to the axis there. (If a<0a < 0a<0 instead, the same argument runs upside down: the parabola lies on or below the axis and is still tangent at (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0), just from underneath.) At a simple zero the sign of fff flips as you pass the zero; at a repeated zero the square locks the sign, so the graph stays on one side. And the point of tangency is no ordinary point: the value 000 at x=rx = rx=r is the minimum (or, when a<0a < 0a<0, the maximum), so the vertex is (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0), sitting on the axis itself. In this case factored form and vertex form are literally the same expression, a(x−r)2+0a(x - r)^2 + 0a(x−r)2+0, one more sign that the repeated root is the boundary case between two crossings and none. The discriminant, two lessons from now, will give that three-way split a single numerical name.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Splitting a product that is not zero. From (x−1)(x−2)=6(x - 1)(x - 2) = 6(x−1)(x−2)=6 you may not conclude x−1=6x - 1 = 6x−1=6 or x−2=6x - 2 = 6x−2=6. Six factors in infinitely many ways, so a product of 666 pins down neither factor; only zero does that. Expand, move the 666 across, and refactor.
  • Dividing by a factor that might be zero. Solving x2=5xx^2 = 5xx2=5x by dividing by xxx discards the solution x=0x = 0x=0; canceling (x−1)(x - 1)(x−1) from both sides of (x−1)(x−2)=(x−1)(x+4)(x - 1)(x - 2) = (x - 1)(x + 4)(x−1)(x−2)=(x−1)(x+4) discards x=1x = 1x=1. Move everything to one side and factor instead.
  • Saying “does not factor” without naming the number system. x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 does not factor over the rationals yet factors over the reals as (x−2)(x+2)\big(x - \sqrt{2}\big)\big(x + \sqrt{2}\big)(x−2​)(x+2​); x2+x+1x^2 + x + 1x2+x+1 factors over neither. The claim is meaningless until the system is named.
  • Concluding “no real solutions” from a failed integer hunt. No integer pair has product −4-4−4 and sum 222, yet x2+2x−4=0x^2 + 2x - 4 = 0x2+2x−4=0 has two real (irrational) solutions. A failed search reports on the searcher’s list, not on the equation.
  • Expecting the graph to cross at a repeated root. At x=rx = rx=r the graph of a(x−r)2a(x - r)^2a(x−r)2 touches the axis and turns back; the sign of fff does not change. Crossing happens at simple zeros only.
  • Reading “or” as “and.” The Zero Product Property concludes that at least one factor is zero, possibly both, not that both are.

Key takeaways

  • The procedure: move everything to one side so the other side is 000; factor completely; set each factor equal to 000; solve each; check in the original equation.
  • Factoring is the change of form from standard to factored, ax2+bx+c=a(x−r1)(x−r2)ax^2 + bx + c = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)ax2+bx+c=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​), and factored form answers the zero question for free.
  • The Zero Product Property (if uv=0uv = 0uv=0 then u=0u = 0u=0 or v=0v = 0v=0) is what makes factoring solve equations: it converts one quadratic equation into two linear ones. It holds because every nonzero real has a reciprocal, so it is a fact about the real numbers, and there are arithmetics (a clock face) where it fails.
  • The property needs a zero on one side. A product equal to 666 constrains neither factor; expand, move everything across, and refactor.
  • A zero of fff and a root of f(x)=0f(x) = 0f(x)=0 are the same number rrr; the xxx-intercept is the point (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0) that number locates on the graph. The axis of symmetry is the zeros’ midpoint, x=r1+r22x = \tfrac{r_1 + r_2}{2}x=2r1​+r2​​, and the zeros carve the axis into intervals where the sign of fff is constant.
  • One zero buys one factor: ax2+bx+c=(x−r)(ax+b+ar)ax^2 + bx + c = (x - r)(ax + b + ar)ax2+bx+c=(x−r)(ax+b+ar) whenever f(r)=0f(r) = 0f(r)=0. A quadratic with real coefficients factors over the reals exactly when it has a real zero, and the same sentence holds with “rational” in both slots.
  • Factoring over the integers is a finite search, and the search can fail: x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 factors only once irrational coefficients are allowed, and x2+x+1x^2 + x + 1x2+x+1 has no real factorization at all. Completing the square (next lesson) is a computation that never fails, and the quadratic formula is that computation done once in general.
  • A repeated root gives f(x)=a(x−r)2f(x) = a(x - r)^2f(x)=a(x−r)2, a one-element solution set, and a parabola tangent to the xxx-axis at its vertex (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0).

Why x2+x+1x^2 + x + 1x2+x+1 has no real zero#

We show x2+x+1>0x^2 + x + 1 > 0x2+x+1>0 for every real xxx a second way, splitting the line into three pieces.

If −1<x<0-1 < x < 0−1<x<0, then x+1>0x + 1 > 0x+1>0 and x2>0x^2 > 0x2>0, so x2+x+1=x2+(x+1)>0x^2 + x + 1 = x^2 + (x + 1) > 0x2+x+1=x2+(x+1)>0.

A bit of history (optional)

The plainest mark in this lesson is the one that took longest to arrive. It is the zero on the right.

For most of the history of algebra, an equation stated that two positive quantities matched. Nothing was not a quantity. So no equation was permitted to end in one, and a quadratic had no single shape to be poured into. It arrived instead in half a dozen separate kinds. They were sorted by which terms had been pushed to which side, and every kind carried its own solving recipe. Learning quadratics meant learning the list.

The habit that collapses the list reached print in 1631, in a book called Artis analyticae praxis. Colleagues assembled it from the papers of the English mathematician Thomas Harriot, ten years after he died. How much of the arrangement was his own is still argued. What the book does is carry every term of an equation to one side, leaving the other side reading zero. That is the arrangement this lesson insists on before you may split a product.

Notice what the innocent zero buys. A product equal to six pins down neither factor, as the second worked example showed. A product equal to zero forces one of them to vanish. So a whole shelf of recipes gave way to a single property of the real numbers. The price was agreeing to write a side that says nothing at all.

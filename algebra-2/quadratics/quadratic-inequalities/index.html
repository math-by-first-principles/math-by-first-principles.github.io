Quadratic Inequalities

Intuition

You can already find where a quadratic is zero. Factoring and the quadratic formula both answer the question ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0, the inputs where the parabola meets the xxx-axis. But most real questions are not about the single instant a quantity is zero. A ball is above ten feet for a stretch of time, not at one instant, and a business turns a profit over a range of prices, not at one price. Those are questions about where a quadratic is positive or negative, and that is a quadratic inequality.

The whole method rests on one fact: a quadratic can change sign only at a root. Take (x−3)(x+2)(x - 3)(x + 2)(x−3)(x+2). It is zero at x=−2x = -2x=−2 and x=3x = 3x=3, and those two roots cut the number line into three pieces. Test one point from each piece and the pattern is unmistakable: at x=−3x = -3x=−3 the product is (−6)(−1)=6(-6)(-1) = 6(−6)(−1)=6; at x=0x = 0x=0 it is (−3)(2)=−6(-3)(2) = -6(−3)(2)=−6; at x=4x = 4x=4 it is (1)(6)=6(1)(6) = 6(1)(6)=6. Positive, then negative, then positive again, and the sign flips only where a root sits. Once you trust that fact, the answer to an inequality is no longer a single number but a whole solution set, one piece of the number line or several joined together.

Learning goals

  • Argue that a quadratic changes sign only at a root
  • Split the line at the roots and read each sign
  • Record the answer in interval notation, joining with a union
  • Open or close endpoints for strict or non-strict symbols
  • Predict the shape of the answer from Δ\DeltaΔ, the sign of aaa, and the symbol

A quadratic changes sign only at a root

You just saw that happen for (x−3)(x+2)(x - 3)(x + 2)(x−3)(x+2): the sign flipped only at its two roots. That is worth stating as a general claim and proving, since everything in this lesson rests on it. A quadratic with two real roots factors, by the last few lessons, as f(x)=a(x−r1)(x−r2)f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)f(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​). The claim concerns the sign of such a product across the number line. The product is positive on some stretches and negative on others, but it can only switch between the two at r1r_1r1​ or r2r_2r2​.

Why a quadratic changes sign only at a root#

Write the quadratic in factored form, f(x)=a(x−r1)(x−r2)f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)f(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​) with a≠0a \ne 0a=0. Its sign at any input is the product of three signs: the sign of aaa, together with the sign of each linear factor. So control the factors and you control the sign of fff.

Each linear factor is positive on one side of its own root and negative on the other, and changes sign nowhere else: x−r1x - r_1x−r1​ flips only at x=r1x = r_1x=r1​, and x−r2x - r_2x−r2​ only at x=r2x = r_2x=r2​. So on any interval that contains neither root, both factors hold one fixed sign, a product of two fixed signs has a fixed sign, and multiplying by the constant aaa only reverses or preserves it. Therefore fff has one constant sign across the whole interval.

The only way fff can pass from positive to negative is for one of its factors to change sign, and that happens only at a root. So the roots are the only places the sign can turn over, not places it must. If the two roots coincide, as in f(x)=a(x−r)2f(x) = a(x - r)^2f(x)=a(x−r)2, the two factors are identical and never disagree, so the sign never turns over at all there, as the last worked example in this lesson shows.

∎

The consequence is the entire method. Two roots cut the number line into three pieces, the stretch left of both, the stretch between them, and the stretch right of both. On each piece the sign of the quadratic is one fixed thing. So solving a quadratic inequality is never about checking infinitely many points. It is about finding the roots, which split the line, and then labelling each piece positive or negative.

Reading the sign: test a point, or read the parabola

Once you know the sign is constant on each piece, you need the sign on each one, and there are two ways to get it.

The first is a test point. Pick any convenient number inside a piece, substitute it, and record whether the result comes out positive or negative. That one value settles the entire piece, because the sign cannot change again until the next root. Testing x=0x = 0x=0 in (x−3)(x+2)(x - 3)(x + 2)(x−3)(x+2) gives (−3)(2)=−6<0(-3)(2) = -6 < 0(−3)(2)=−6<0, so the quadratic is negative across the whole middle piece.

The second way is faster and harder to get wrong: read the parabola. You already know that the sign of aaa fixes which way a parabola opens. When a>0a > 0a>0 it opens upward, so it dips below the xxx-axis between its two roots and rises above the axis outside them. So with no substituting at all, an upward parabola is negative exactly on the open interval between the roots and positive outside them. When a<0a < 0a<0 it opens downward and the picture flips top to bottom, positive between the roots and negative outside. The graph route is quicker because it computes nothing: you just recall the shape and the two roots. Keep the test point as a check whenever you are unsure of the shape or the roots.

This is the same fact the discriminant already told you. For a>0a > 0a>0 with two real roots, the vertex sits at height −Δ4a-\dfrac{\Delta}{4a}−4aΔ​, which is negative, so the bottom of the parabola lies below the axis. That is precisely why an upward parabola with two roots is negative between them.

Whichever route you use, write the answer as a solution set, not as a loose phrase. The systems chapter made the point that the solution of an inequality is a set, an object you can name, draw, and combine. That solution is not merely a lone number to solve for. Here that set is a piece of the number line, written in interval notation, and when it arrives in two pieces you join them with a union ∪\cup∪. Shading it on a number line is just the one-dimensional version of shading a region in the plane.

Worked example 1 Solve x2−x−6≥0x^2 - x - 6 \ge 0x2−x−6≥0

The quadratic is already compared to zero, so factor it. Two numbers with product −6-6−6 and sum −1-1−1 are −3-3−3 and 222, giving

x2−x−6=(x−3)(x+2),x^2 - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2),x2−x−6=(x−3)(x+2),

with roots x=3x = 3x=3 and x=−2x = -2x=−2. These cut the line into three pieces.

The leading coefficient is a=1>0a = 1 > 0a=1>0, so the parabola opens upward: negative between the roots, positive outside them. We want where it is greater than or equal to zero, which is the two outside pieces together with the roots themselves. Those roots are included, since the inequality is non-strict and they make the quadratic exactly zero:

x≤−2orx≥3,that is(−∞,−2]∪[3,∞).x \le -2 \quad \text{or} \quad x \ge 3, \qquad \text{that is} \qquad (-\infty, -2] \cup [3, \infty).x≤−2orx≥3,that is(−∞,−2]∪[3,∞).

The union is not decoration. The solution is two separate rays, not a single interval, and writing it as one interval would be wrong.

Strict versus non-strict endpoints

The steps above decide which pieces of the line make up the answer. The inequality symbol decides only one more thing, what happens at the endpoints, which are the roots.

The roots are exactly the inputs where the quadratic equals zero. A strict symbol, >>> or <<<, asks for values that are honestly positive or honestly negative, so it excludes the roots. That means open circles on the number line, and parentheses in interval notation. A non-strict symbol, ≥\ge≥ or ≤\le≤, also accepts zero, so it includes the roots: closed circles, square brackets. For two distinct roots, the interior of the solution is identical either way, and only the endpoints change. So x2−x−6>0x^2 - x - 6 > 0x2−x−6>0 has solution (−∞,−2)∪(3,∞)(-\infty, -2) \cup (3, \infty)(−∞,−2)∪(3,∞), the same two rays as the worked example but with the roots now removed. A repeated root works a little differently, as the last worked example in this lesson shows.

When the leading coefficient is negative

A negative aaa flips the parabola upside down, and with it every sign in the argument. There are two safe ways to handle it, and one tempting way that goes wrong.

The safe habit is to make aaa positive first. Multiply both sides of the inequality by −1-1−1, and remember the rule from the linear inequalities lesson: multiplying an inequality by a negative number reverses its direction. Now you are back to an upward parabola and the reading you already trust. The other safe route is to read the downward parabola directly. With a<0a < 0a<0 the parabola opens down, so it is positive between its roots and negative outside them, the mirror image of the upward case.

The tempting error is to multiply or divide by the negative and leave the symbol pointing the same way. That silently swaps the solution set for its opposite. If you ever rewrite an inequality by a negative factor, the symbol must turn around.

Worked example 2 Solve −x2+2x+3≥0-x^2 + 2x + 3 \ge 0−x2+2x+3≥0

Here a=−1a = -1a=−1, so use both routes and watch them agree.

Making aaa positive, multiply through by −1-1−1 and reverse the symbol:

−x2+2x+3≥0⟺x2−2x−3≤0.-x^2 + 2x + 3 \ge 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^2 - 2x - 3 \le 0.−x2+2x+3≥0⟺x2−2x−3≤0.

Factor the upward quadratic: x2−2x−3=(x−3)(x+1)x^2 - 2x - 3 = (x - 3)(x + 1)x2−2x−3=(x−3)(x+1), with roots 333 and −1-1−1. An upward parabola is at or below zero on the closed interval between its roots, so

−1≤x≤3,that is[−1,3].-1 \le x \le 3, \qquad \text{that is} \qquad [-1, 3].−1≤x≤3,that is[−1,3].

Reading the downward parabola directly gives the same thing. The roots of −x2+2x+3-x^2 + 2x + 3−x2+2x+3 are again −1-1−1 and 333, and because a<0a < 0a<0 the parabola opens downward and is positive between its roots. Including the roots for the non-strict symbol, the solution set is once more [−1,3][-1, 3][−1,3]. The two routes must agree, because they describe the same object.

What the discriminant decides

The factored form a(x−r1)(x−r2)a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)a(x−r1​)(x−r2​) assumed two real roots. Whether a quadratic has them is settled by the discriminant Δ=b2−4ac\Delta = b^2 - 4acΔ=b2−4ac from the quadratic formula, and the picture below shows what each sign of Δ\DeltaΔ does to an upward parabola. Take a>0a > 0a>0 throughout, since you can always arrange that first.

Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0 gives two real roots and the dip you already know. Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 gives one repeated root, where the parabola only touches the axis. Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 gives no real roots at all, and the two extreme cases on the right and in the middle are where both students and authors slip.

When Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 there are no real roots, so the parabola never touches the xxx-axis. A curve that never touches the axis cannot cross from positive to negative without passing through zero somewhere, so it keeps one sign everywhere, and since an upward parabola climbs to infinity it must be that one sign is positive.

Worked example 3 Solve x2+x+1>0x^2 + x + 1 > 0x2+x+1>0, then x2+x+1<0x^2 + x + 1 < 0x2+x+1<0

Check the discriminant first: Δ=12−4(1)(1)=1−4=−3<0\Delta = 1^2 - 4(1)(1) = 1 - 4 = -3 < 0Δ=12−4(1)(1)=1−4=−3<0, so there are no real roots and no factoring over the real numbers. Complete the square instead:

x2+x+1=(x+12)2+34.x^2 + x + 1 = \left(x + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \tfrac{3}{4}.x2+x+1=(x+21​)2+43​.

The square is never negative, so the whole expression is at least 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​, which is positive, for every real xxx. Therefore x2+x+1>0x^2 + x + 1 > 0x2+x+1>0 is true for all inputs, and its solution set is all of R\mathbb{R}R. The opposite inequality x2+x+1<0x^2 + x + 1 < 0x2+x+1<0 is never true, so its solution set is the empty set ∅\varnothing∅. A negative discriminant is not a dead end. It is the case where the quadratic keeps one sign forever, and reading that sign answers the inequality immediately.

That argument used one specific quadratic. The same idea works for any quadratic with a>0a > 0a>0 and Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0: completing the square puts the vertex above the axis, so the whole parabola stays there too.

The paragraph above already shows the idea for one quadratic. You can read the same argument written for every quadratic with a>0a > 0a>0 and Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 at once, using vertex form, in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying by a negative and keeping the symbol. Rewriting −x2+2x+3≥0-x^2 + 2x + 3 \ge 0−x2+2x+3≥0 as x2−2x−3≥0x^2 - 2x - 3 \ge 0x2−2x−3≥0 hands back the opposite solution set. Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative must reverse the symbol; safest is to make aaa positive first.
  • Confusing strict and non-strict endpoints. For two distinct roots, >>>, <<< exclude them and ≥\ge≥, ≤\le≤ include them, with the same interval either way. A repeated root is different: switching the symbol can turn an empty set into a single included point, as in Worked Example 4.
  • Forcing the answer into a single interval. The “outside” inequality for two roots gives a union of two rays, such as (−∞,−2]∪[3,∞)(-\infty, -2] \cup [3, \infty)(−∞,−2]∪[3,∞). Collapsing that to [−2,3][-2, 3][−2,3] reports the exact opposite set.
  • Believing “empty means no real roots.” Emptiness depends on the sign of aaa, on Δ\DeltaΔ, and on the symbol together, never on Δ\DeltaΔ alone: (x−2)2<0(x - 2)^2 < 0(x−2)2<0 is empty even though 222 is a real root.
  • Treating a double root like a sign change. At a repeated root the quadratic touches zero without changing sign, so (x−3)2>0(x - 3)^2 > 0(x−3)2>0 removes only the point 333 and keeps both sides.
  • Testing a point that is actually a root. A test point sitting on a root gives zero, which decides nothing. Choose a point strictly inside a piece.

Key takeaways

  • The structural fact. A quadratic’s sign can turn over only at a root, so between consecutive roots the sign is constant.
  • Method. Move everything to one side, find the roots, and let them split the number line. Read the sign on each piece off the parabola, up for a>0a > 0a>0 and down for a<0a < 0a<0, or confirm with one interior test point.
  • Answer as a set. Record the solution in interval notation, joining separate pieces with a union ∪\cup∪.
  • Endpoints. For two distinct roots, strict >>>, <<< exclude them and non-strict ≥\ge≥, ≤\le≤ include them, with the same interval either way; a repeated root can instead turn an empty set into a single included point.
  • Δ\DeltaΔ, aaa, and the symbol together decide the shape. Δ\DeltaΔ picks the case: two roots, one, or none. Then aaa fixes which regions are positive, and the symbol picks which of those regions the answer keeps, whether that is one interval, a union of two rays, all of R\mathbb{R}R, R\mathbb{R}R missing a point, a single point, or ∅\varnothing∅.

Why a quadratic with no real roots keeps one sign#

Completing the square writes any quadratic in vertex form f(x)=a(x−h)2+kf(x) = a(x - h)^2 + kf(x)=a(x−h)2+k, where the vertex height is k=−Δ4ak = -\dfrac{\Delta}{4a}k=−4aΔ​. Suppose a>0a > 0a>0 and Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0. Then k=−Δ4ak = -\dfrac{\Delta}{4a}k=−4aΔ​ is a negative divided by a positive with an overall minus sign, so k>0k > 0k>0.

Now read off the sign. The square (x−h)2(x - h)^2(x−h)2 is never negative, and a>0a > 0a>0, so a(x−h)2≥0a(x - h)^2 \ge 0a(x−h)2≥0 for every xxx. Adding the positive number kkk,

f(x)=a(x−h)2+k≥k>0for every x.f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k \ge k > 0 \quad\text{for every } x.f(x)=a(x−h)2+k≥k>0for every x.

So fff is strictly positive everywhere. There is no arithmetic with roots and no sign chart, only the fact that a real square cannot be negative. It follows at once that f(x)>0f(x) > 0f(x)>0 has solution set all of R\mathbb{R}R, while f(x)<0f(x) < 0f(x)<0 has solution set ∅\varnothing∅, the empty set, because fff is never negative. With a<0a < 0a<0 the same argument gives k<0k < 0k<0 and f(x)<0f(x) < 0f(x)<0 everywhere, so the two answers swap.

A bit of history (optional)

The quantity b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac is a great deal older than its name.

Mathematicians worked with it for centuries without a word of their own for it. Every mention had to be spelled back out of the coefficients, or dressed in a borrowed name belonging to some other job, a real cost: a quantity nobody can say in one breath is hard to reason with, and harder still to teach.

In 1851 James Joseph Sylvester, an English mathematician with an appetite for inventing words, gave it the one we still use. He called it the discriminant, from a Latin verb meaning to separate, to tell one thing apart from another. Sylvester minted a great many terms in his career, and most of them were quietly dropped. This one survived because it says what the number is for.

Nowhere does it earn the name better than in this lesson. Set against an equation, the discriminant sorts three futures: two roots, one root, or none at all. Set against an inequality, that same sort still runs underneath, but now the sign of aaa and the inequality symbol join it to fix the final shape: a single interval, two rays joined by a union, the whole line, the line with one point cut out of it, one lonely point, or nothing whatever.

Completing the Square

Intuition

The last lesson solved quadratics by factoring, and factoring is a search: you hunt for two integers with the right sum and product, and sometimes there are none. Try x2−6x+7=0x^2 - 6x + 7 = 0x2−6x+7=0 and the hunt comes up empty, because no pair of rational numbers works, even though the equation does have real solutions, as you will confirm with the quadratic formula in the next lesson. Factoring cannot be the whole story. This lesson builds the method that never fails: it rewrites any quadratic in standard form, with no guessing, as a multiple of a perfect square plus a constant, which is exactly the vertex form from the start of the chapter. Written that way, it hands you the vertex without a graph, and it solves the equation without a factorization. The name comes from a picture, an actual square with a corner missing that you fill in, and that picture is where we begin.

Learning goals

  • Add (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 to complete the square
  • Factor aaa out first, then distribute it back
  • Derive general vertex form and read the vertex
  • Solve from (x+p)2=k(x + p)^2 = k(x+p)2=k with a ±\pm±
  • Use it when factoring is not apparent, since it never fails

The identity that builds a perfect square

Everything here runs on one line of algebra you already know, read in an unfamiliar direction. Squaring a binomial gives

(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2.(x + p)^2 = x^2 + 2px + p^2.(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2.

Read left to right, this expands a square. Read right to left, it is a recipe for recognizing one. A trinomial x2+2px+p2x^2 + 2px + p^2x2+2px+p2 is a perfect square, and the number multiplying xxx in the middle is exactly twice the number ppp that appears in the answer. That single observation tells you what constant a bare x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx is missing.

Try it on a number before doing it in general. Take x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x. If this is missing one constant ddd to become a perfect square (x+p)2(x + p)^2(x+p)2, matching the middle terms means 2p=62p = 62p=6, so p=3p = 3p=3, and matching the constants means d=p2=9d = p^2 = 9d=p2=9. Check it by expanding: (x+3)2=x2+6x+9(x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9(x+3)2=x2+6x+9, which is exactly x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x with d=9d = 9d=9 added. Nothing about that reasoning depended on 666 being the coefficient; the same matching works for any coefficient in place of 666, which is what the argument below shows.

The one constant that completes a perfect square#

Fix a number bbb. We ask which constant ddd makes x2+bx+dx^2 + bx + dx2+bx+d a perfect square, meaning equal to (x+p)2(x + p)^2(x+p)2 for some ppp.

Suppose x2+bx+d=(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2x^2 + bx + d = (x + p)^2 = x^2 + 2px + p^2x2+bx+d=(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2. Two polynomials are equal only when their matching coefficients agree, so the xxx terms give b=2pb = 2pb=2p and the constants give d=p2d = p^2d=p2. The first equation forces p=b2p = \dfrac{b}{2}p=2b​, and then the second forces

d=p2=(b2)2.d = p^2 = \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2.d=p2=(2b​)2.

Conversely, if d=(b2)2d = \left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2d=(2b​)2, the identity runs forward: x2+bx+(b2)2=(x+b2)2x^2 + bx + \left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+bx+(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2. So the constant that completes the square is not just a choice, it is the only choice, and it equals (b2)2\left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2.

∎

This is why the familiar instruction “take half the coefficient of xxx, then square it” is true. It is not a rule to memorize; it is a consequence of matching the middle term 2px=bx2px = bx2px=bx, which forces p=b2p = \tfrac{b}{2}p=2b​, and then reading off the constant p2p^2p2. Notice the added constant (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 is never negative, whatever the sign of bbb, because squaring kills the sign.

The added constant almost never happens to be sitting there already. So we add it and immediately subtract it, changing the expression’s appearance without changing its value:

x2+bx=x2+bx+(b2)2⏟a perfect square−(b2)2=(x+b2)2−(b2)2.x^2 + bx = \underbrace{x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2}_{\text{a perfect square}} - \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2.x2+bx=a perfect squarex2+bx+(2b​)2​​−(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2−(2b​)2.

That equation is the engine of the lesson. Every worked example below is this one move, applied and then cleaned up.

Completing the square, literally

The name is not a metaphor. Picture x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx as area. The x2x^2x2 is a square with side xxx. The bxbxbx is a rectangle with one side xxx and the other side bbb, so it is bbb tall and xxx wide, sitting next to the square. Now cut that rectangle down its length into two equal strips, each of width b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​ and length xxx. Glue one strip to the right edge of the square and the other to the bottom edge. The two strips fit flush, and what you have built is almost a bigger square of side x+b2x + \tfrac{b}{2}x+2b​, missing only the little corner where the two strips would meet. That corner is a square of side b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​, so its area is (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2. Fill it in and the figure is a complete square of side x+b2x + \tfrac{b}{2}x+2b​.

The picture draws both xxx and bbb as positive lengths, since an actual rectangle cannot have a negative side. But the identity it illustrates is pure algebra, and algebra does not care about the sign of xxx or bbb. The very next worked example runs this same move on x2−6xx^2 - 6xx2−6x, where b=−6b = -6b=−6: the picture stops making sense as a picture, but the equation x2+bx+(b2)2=(x+b2)2x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+bx+(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2 still holds, because it was already proved above for every real number bbb, whatever real number xxx eventually turns out to be too.

The area bookkeeping is the algebra. The original pieces have total area x2x^2x2 (the big square) plus 2⋅b2⋅x=bx2 \cdot \tfrac{b}{2} \cdot x = bx2⋅2b​⋅x=bx (the two strips), which is x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx, our starting expression. Fill in the corner of area (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 and the whole figure is a square of side x+b2x + \tfrac{b}{2}x+2b​:

x2+bx+(b2)2=(x+b2)2.x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2.x2+bx+(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2.

That is the identity from the last section, now seen rather than derived. The constant you add is the geometric corner that was missing, and “completing the square” describes precisely what your hand does to the picture.

The algorithm: standard form to a single square

To rewrite a whole quadratic, complete the square on its x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx part and carry the leftover constant along. Start with a monic quadratic, one whose leading coefficient is 111.

Worked example 1 A monic quadratic to vertex form

Rewrite x2−6x+7x^2 - 6x + 7x2−6x+7 as a perfect square plus a constant.

The variable part is x2−6xx^2 - 6xx2−6x, so here b=−6b = -6b=−6 and half of it is −3-3−3. The completing constant is (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9. Add it and subtract it so the value is untouched:

x2−6x+7=(x2−6x+9)−9+7.x^2 - 6x + 7 = \big(x^2 - 6x + 9\big) - 9 + 7.x2−6x+7=(x2−6x+9)−9+7.

The bracketed trinomial is the perfect square (x−3)2(x - 3)^2(x−3)2, and the loose constants combine to −9+7=−2-9 + 7 = -2−9+7=−2:

x2−6x+7=(x−3)2−2.x^2 - 6x + 7 = (x - 3)^2 - 2.x2−6x+7=(x−3)2−2.

This is vertex form a(x−h)2+ka(x - h)^2 + ka(x−h)2+k with a=1a = 1a=1, h=3h = 3h=3, k=−2k = -2k=−2. No factoring, no guessing, and it worked on the very quadratic that had no rational factorization.

When the leading coefficient is not 111, the square you are completing lives inside a factor of aaa, so pull aaa out of the x2x^2x2 and xxx terms first. This is the step most often botched, because the aaa has to come back out at the end.

Worked example 2 A leading coefficient, and reading the vertex

Write f(x)=2x2+12x+7f(x) = 2x^2 + 12x + 7f(x)=2x2+12x+7 in vertex form, then read off its vertex, its minimum value, and its range.

Factor 222 out of the first two terms only. The constant 777 stays outside, because it has no xxx in it:

2x2+12x+7=2(x2+6x)+7.2x^2 + 12x + 7 = 2\big(x^2 + 6x\big) + 7.2x2+12x+7=2(x2+6x)+7.

Complete the square inside the parentheses. The coefficient of xxx there is 666 (not the original 121212, since the 222 has already been factored out), so half of it is 333, and 32=93^2 = 932=9. Add and subtract 999 inside:

2(x2+6x+9−9)+7=2((x+3)2−9)+7.2\big(x^2 + 6x + 9 - 9\big) + 7 = 2\big((x + 3)^2 - 9\big) + 7.2(x2+6x+9−9)+7=2((x+3)2−9)+7.

Now distribute the 222 back across both pieces, the trap being to distribute it only to the square. The −9-9−9 is inside the parentheses, so it is multiplied by 222 as well:

2(x+3)2−18+7=2(x+3)2−11.2(x + 3)^2 - 18 + 7 = 2(x + 3)^2 - 11.2(x+3)2−18+7=2(x+3)2−11.

In vertex form f(x)=2(x+3)2−11f(x) = 2(x + 3)^2 - 11f(x)=2(x+3)2−11, so the vertex is (−3,−11)(-3, -11)(−3,−11). Because a=2>0a = 2 > 0a=2>0 the parabola opens upward, the vertex is the lowest point, the minimum value is −11-11−11, and the range is y≥−11y \ge -11y≥−11.

Two payoffs from one computation

Completing the square is the bridge from standard form to vertex form. The very same finished expression then answers two different questions depending on what you do with it next. Convert and read, and you get the vertex. Convert and solve, and you get the roots. One computation, two payoffs.

Payoff one: the vertex, the extremum, and the range

Run the algorithm on a completely general quadratic and the vertex appears in closed form.

Completing the square gives the vertex of every parabola#

Take f(x)=ax2+bx+cf(x) = ax^2 + bx + cf(x)=ax2+bx+c with a≠0a \ne 0a=0. Factor aaa from the first two terms:

f(x)=a(x2+bax)+c.f(x) = a\left(x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x\right) + c.f(x)=a(x2+ab​x)+c.

Inside, the coefficient of xxx is ba\tfrac{b}{a}ab​, so half of it is b2a\tfrac{b}{2a}2ab​ and the completing constant is (b2a)2=b24a2\left(\tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \tfrac{b^2}{4a^2}(2ab​)2=4a2b2​. Add and subtract it inside:

f(x)=a(x2+bax+b24a2−b24a2)+c=a((x+b2a)2−b24a2)+c.f(x) = a\left(x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{b^2}{4a^2}\right) + c = a\left(\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{b^2}{4a^2}\right) + c.f(x)=a(x2+ab​x+4a2b2​−4a2b2​)+c=a((x+2ab​)2−4a2b2​)+c.

Distribute aaa across both terms in the outer parentheses. The second term becomes a⋅b24a2=b24aa \cdot \tfrac{b^2}{4a^2} = \tfrac{b^2}{4a}a⋅4a2b2​=4ab2​:

f(x)=a(x+b2a)2−b24a+c.f(x) = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{b^2}{4a} + c.f(x)=a(x+2ab​)2−4ab2​+c.

This is vertex form a(x−h)2+ka(x - h)^2 + ka(x−h)2+k with h=−b2ah = -\dfrac{b}{2a}h=−2ab​ and k=c−b24ak = c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a}k=c−4ab2​, so the vertex is

(−b2a,  c−b24a).\left(-\frac{b}{2a},\ \, c - \frac{b^2}{4a}\right).(−2ab​, c−4ab2​).

∎

Look at the xxx-coordinate: −b2a-\dfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​ is exactly the axis of symmetry the chapter derived earlier from the parabola’s mirror symmetry. That lesson found the vertex’s height too, but only by a separate evaluation, plugging the axis back into fff. Completing the square reaches the same height, c−b24ac - \dfrac{b^2}{4a}c−4ab2​, which is exactly f ⁣(−b2a)f\!\left(-\tfrac{b}{2a}\right)f(−2ab​), without a separate evaluation step: both coordinates fall out of the one rewritten expression, x=hx = hx=h and y=ky = ky=k together. From the vertex form the extremum and range follow with no extra work: when a>0a > 0a>0 the vertex is the minimum and the range is y≥ky \ge ky≥k. When a<0a < 0a<0 the vertex is instead the maximum and the range is y≤ky \le ky≤k. That is the whole reason vertex form is worth reaching, and completing the square always reaches it.

Payoff two: solving by isolating the square

To solve instead of read, do not distribute anything: get the square by itself on one side, then undo it. Undoing a square is where a genuinely important subtlety lives, so look at a number before stating it in general.

Take x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9. Two numbers square to 999: 32=93^2 = 932=9 and (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9, so both x=3x = 3x=3 and x=−3x = -3x=−3 solve the equation. Yet the symbol 9\sqrt{9}9​ names only one number, 333, the nonnegative one. If you solved x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9 by writing x=9=3x = \sqrt{9} = 3x=9​=3, you would report a true solution but miss the other one, x=−3x = -3x=−3, which is exactly as valid. The general case, x2=kx^2 = kx2=k for any k≥0k \ge 0k≥0, works the same way, and now you can see why.

Why the square root carries a plus or minus#

The symbol k\sqrt{k}k​ denotes, by definition, the nonnegative number whose square is kkk (this needs k≥0k \ge 0k≥0). So k\sqrt{k}k​ is one specific number, never two. The equation x2=kx^2 = kx2=k is a different object: it asks for every xxx whose square is kkk.

For k>0k > 0k>0, move everything to one side and factor as a difference of squares:

x2=k  ⟺  x2−k=0  ⟺  (x−k)(x+k)=0.x^2 = k \iff x^2 - k = 0 \iff \big(x - \sqrt{k}\big)\big(x + \sqrt{k}\big) = 0.x2=k⟺x2−k=0⟺(x−k​)(x+k​)=0.

By the Zero Product Property a product is zero exactly when a factor is zero, so x=kx = \sqrt{k}x=k​ or x=−kx = -\sqrt{k}x=−k​, which we write together as x=±kx = \pm\sqrt{k}x=±k​. Both are genuine solutions, and there are no others. For k=0k = 0k=0 the two collapse into the single solution x=0x = 0x=0. For k<0k < 0k<0 there is no real solution at all, because a real square is never negative.

∎

So the ±\pm± is not decoration, it is the second root. Writing x=kx = \sqrt{k}x=k​ by itself silently throws away x=−kx = -\sqrt{k}x=−k​, and that lost solution is one of the most common errors in all of algebra. One boundary case is worth seeing directly: if the isolated square already equals 000, such as (x−4)2=0(x - 4)^2 = 0(x−4)2=0, then 4+04 + \sqrt{0}4+0​ and 4−04 - \sqrt{0}4−0​ are both just 444, so the two solutions collapse into one, x=4x = 4x=4. With that pinned down, solving is mechanical: complete the square, isolate it, and extract the root with a ±\pm±.

Worked example 3 Solving the quadratic that would not factor

Solve x2−6x+7=0x^2 - 6x + 7 = 0x2−6x+7=0, the equation from the opening that had no rational factorization.

x−3=±2.x - 3 = \pm\sqrt{2}.x−3=±2​.

There are the two roots, x=3+2x = 3 + \sqrt{2}x=3+2​ and x=3−2x = 3 - \sqrt{2}x=3−2​. They are irrational, which is exactly why the integer search of factoring could never have found them, and why a method that does not rely on guessing was needed.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Dropping the leading coefficient. For 2x2+12x+72x^2 + 12x + 72x2+12x+7 you must factor the 222 out first, 2(x2+6x)+72(x^2 + 6x) + 72(x2+6x)+7, and then multiply it back across both pieces when you finish. Completing the square on 2x2+12x2x^2 + 12x2x2+12x as if it read x2+12xx^2 + 12xx2+12x adds the wrong constant and gives the wrong vertex.
  • Distributing aaa to only the square. After 2((x+3)2−9)+72\big((x + 3)^2 - 9\big) + 72((x+3)2−9)+7, the −9-9−9 is inside the parentheses too, so it becomes −18-18−18, giving 2(x+3)2−112(x + 3)^2 - 112(x+3)2−11, not 2(x+3)2−22(x + 3)^2 - 22(x+3)2−2.
  • Dropping the ±\pm±. From (x−3)2=2(x - 3)^2 = 2(x−3)2=2 the solution is x=3±2x = 3 \pm \sqrt{2}x=3±2​, two values. Writing only x=3+2x = 3 + \sqrt{2}x=3+2​ throws away the root 3−23 - \sqrt{2}3−2​, because 2\sqrt{2}2​ by definition names only the nonnegative root.
  • Adding without subtracting. Completing the square must not change the value. Add (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 and subtract it in the same line. When you are solving an equation you may instead add it to both sides, but you cannot add it to one side and forget the other.
  • Calling a negative right side an error. If the isolated square equals a negative number, the work is correct and the conclusion is “no real solution,” not a mistake to hunt down. A real square is never negative.
  • Using half of bbb instead of half, squared. The completing constant is (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2, not b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​. For x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x you add 999, not 333.

Key takeaways

  • One identity drives everything: x2+bx+(b2)2=(x+b2)2x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+bx+(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2. The constant that completes the square is forced to be (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2, the “half, then square” rule, and it is the missing corner of the area picture.
  • The algorithm: to rewrite ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c, factor aaa from the x2x^2x2 and xxx terms, add and subtract (b2a)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2(2ab​)2 inside, then distribute aaa back. Unlike factoring, it never fails.
  • General vertex form: ax2+bx+c=a(x+b2a)2+(c−b24a)ax^2 + bx + c = a\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + \left(c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}\right)ax2+bx+c=a(x+2ab​)2+(c−4ab2​), so the vertex is (−b2a, c−b24a)\left(-\tfrac{b}{2a},\ c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}\right)(−2ab​, c−4ab2​). This confirms the axis of symmetry x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​ and supplies the vertex’s height.
  • Two payoffs, one computation. Read the vertex form for the vertex, extremum, and range; or isolate the square to solve.
  • Solving: from (x+p)2=k(x + p)^2 = k(x+p)2=k, there are two real solutions x=−p±kx = -p \pm \sqrt{k}x=−p±k​ when k>0k > 0k>0, exactly one when k=0k = 0k=0, and none when k<0k < 0k<0.
  • The ±\pm± is a root, not a flourish. k\sqrt{k}k​ is the nonnegative root only, so x2=kx^2 = kx2=k has solutions ±k\pm\sqrt{k}±k​; leaving off the minus loses a solution.

A bit of history (optional)

The name of this method is not a description of a result. It is an instruction to a hand.

“Complete” carries an older sense than the one you use for homework. It comes from the Latin complere, to fill up. The same root gives us “replete”, and “complement”, the piece that fills a gap. To complete a square is therefore to finish an unfinished one. The figure in this lesson is unfinished in a very literal way. The constant you add is the area of its missing corner, and nothing else.

Around the year 820, in Baghdad, al-Khwarizmi set the method down in the treatise that carried it for the next seven centuries. He had no symbols to argue with. So his geometry was not a picture hung beside a proof. The geometry was the proof, and a reader’s only reason to trust the recipe was that the finished figure was visibly whole.

That is also why the completing constant is forced rather than chosen. A gap of a given shape admits exactly one piece that fills it. Half of bbb, then squared, is not one option among several. It is the only quantity that closes the figure. That is precisely what the algebra reported when matching coefficients left a single value for ddd.

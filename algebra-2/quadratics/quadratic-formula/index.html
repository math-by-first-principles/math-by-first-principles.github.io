The Quadratic Formula and the Discriminant

Intuition

The previous lesson gave you a procedure that never fails: completing the square rewrites any quadratic in vertex form. Once the variable sits inside that single square, you can isolate it and solve. That procedure works on x2−6x+7x^2 - 6x + 7x2−6x+7 and on 3x2+2x−23x^2 + 2x - 23x2+2x−2 and on every other specific quadratic, which raises an efficient thought. Instead of running it again for each new set of numbers, run it once on the general quadratic ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0. Carry the letters aaa, bbb, ccc the whole way, and record the answer. That single computation is the quadratic formula. It is not a new idea and not a recipe handed down to be memorized; it is completing the square done once, in full generality, and kept forever. Paying that cost one time settles every quadratic there will ever be, either handing back its exact real solutions or certifying that none exist. This lesson derives it, and reads it back against the parabola from the first lesson of the chapter. The lesson then extracts from inside the formula the one number, the discriminant, that decides in advance how many real solutions you are about to find.

Learning goals

  • Use the quadratic formula to solve a quadratic equation, identifying aaa, bbb, and ccc
  • Predict the number of real roots a quadratic has from Δ=b2−4ac\Delta = b^2 - 4acΔ=b2−4ac
  • Test whether a quadratic with integer coefficients factors over the rationals, using whether Δ\DeltaΔ is a perfect square

From completing the square to a formula

Take the destination on faith for one example before deriving it. For any ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 with a≠0a \ne 0a=0 and b2−4ac≥0b^2 - 4ac \ge 0b2−4ac≥0, so that a real solution exists, the solutions turn out to be x=−b±b2−4ac2ax = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​. For x2+3x−4=0x^2 + 3x - 4 = 0x2+3x−4=0, that means a=1a = 1a=1, b=3b = 3b=3, c=−4c = -4c=−4, so

x=−3±32−4(1)(−4)2(1)=−3±252=−3±52,x = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{3^2 - 4(1)(-4)}}{2(1)} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{25}}{2} = \frac{-3 \pm 5}{2},x=2(1)−3±32−4(1)(−4)​​=2−3±25​​=2−3±5​,

giving x=1x = 1x=1 or x=−4x = -4x=−4. Check either one in the original equation and it holds. That is the whole workflow: identify aaa, bbb, ccc, substitute, simplify. What is not yet clear is why this always works whenever a real solution exists, and what the formula tells you on the quadratics where one does not. That is what the derivation below proves, once and for all.

Everything rests on one derivation, so watch every step. We start from the general quadratic equation and complete the square on it exactly as the previous lesson taught, the only difference being that the coefficients are letters.

The quadratic formula, derived by completing the square#

Begin with ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0, where a≠0a \ne 0a=0 so the equation is genuinely quadratic.

Divide through by aaa, which is legal precisely because a≠0a \ne 0a=0, to make the leading coefficient 111:

x2+ba x+ca=0.x^2 + \frac{b}{a}\,x + \frac{c}{a} = 0.x2+ab​x+ac​=0.

Move the constant to the right, then complete the square on the left. Half of the coefficient ba\tfrac{b}{a}ab​ is b2a\tfrac{b}{2a}2ab​, and its square is b24a2\tfrac{b^2}{4a^2}4a2b2​, so add that to both sides:

x2+ba x+b24a2=−ca+b24a2.x^2 + \frac{b}{a}\,x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} = -\frac{c}{a} + \frac{b^2}{4a^2}.x2+ab​x+4a2b2​=−ac​+4a2b2​.

The left side is now a perfect square by construction. Combine the right side over the common denominator 4a24a^24a2, writing ca=4ac4a2\tfrac{c}{a} = \tfrac{4ac}{4a^2}ac​=4a24ac​:

This one line already holds the whole story, but push on to the formula. Multiply both sides by 4a24a^24a2, which clears every denominator at once: the left side becomes (2ax+b)2(2ax + b)^2(2ax+b)2, because 4a2(x+b2a)2=(2a(x+b2a))2=(2ax+b)24a^2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \left(2a\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)\right)^2 = (2ax + b)^24a2(x+2ab​)2=(2a(x+2ab​))2=(2ax+b)2.

Now take square roots of both sides. This step needs b2−4ac≥0b^2 - 4ac \ge 0b2−4ac≥0, because a real square root of a negative number does not exist; the previous lesson fixed the rule that k\sqrt{k}k​ names the non-negative root, so undoing a square needs a ±\pm± to recover both possibilities:

2ax+b=±b2−4ac.2ax + b = \pm\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}.2ax+b=±b2−4ac​.

Finally subtract bbb and divide by 2a2a2a:

x=−b±b2−4ac2a.x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​.

That is the quadratic formula, and it carried its one hypothesis along the whole way: it needs b2−4ac≥0b^2 - 4ac \ge 0b2−4ac≥0. If b2−4ac<0b^2 - 4ac < 0b2−4ac<0, the squared quantity (2ax+b)2(2ax+b)^2(2ax+b)2 would have to equal a negative number, which no real square ever does, so the equation has no real solution at all. Hold that thought; it becomes the whole next half of the lesson.

∎

The formula deserves its place because of what the derivation just proved. It is not a pattern that happens to work on the examples in a textbook. It is the exact result of completing the square on every quadratic at once, so it inherits that guarantee for any a≠0a \ne 0a=0.

 x=−b±b2−4ac2a \boxed{\,x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\,}x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​​

The roots straddle the axis of symmetry

This section is about the case b2−4ac>0b^2 - 4ac > 0b2−4ac>0, where there are two distinct real roots. (If b2−4ac=0b^2 - 4ac = 0b2−4ac=0 there is only one root, sitting on the axis itself, so nothing straddles anything; if b2−4ac<0b^2 - 4ac < 0b2−4ac<0 there is no real root to place on the graph at all. Both of those cases get their own treatment soon.) Read the formula slowly, because it is telling you something the first lesson of this chapter promised. Split it into its two pieces:

x=−b2a⏟the axis  ±  b2−4ac2a⏟the offset.x = \underbrace{-\frac{b}{2a}}_{\text{the axis}} \;\pm\; \underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}}_{\text{the offset}}.x=the axis−2ab​​​±the offset2ab2−4ac​​​​.

The first piece, −b2a-\tfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​, is exactly the axis of symmetry that Quadratic Functions and Parabolas dug out of ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c using symmetry alone. The second piece is a single signed offset added on one side and subtracted on the other. So the formula says the two roots sit the same distance to either side of the line x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, one to its left and one to its right. That common distance is the magnitude b2−4ac2∣a∣\tfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2\lvert a\rvert}2∣a∣b2−4ac​​. The roots are a mirror pair about the axis, the same fact the parabola showed you before any algebra. The two xxx-intercepts are reflections of each other, so their midpoint lands on the axis. The graph knew where the roots were, up to that one offset, and the formula supplies it as ±b2−4ac2a\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}±2ab2−4ac​​.

This also explains, with no extra work, why the roots average to −b2a-\tfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​. Add the two values from the formula and the ±\pm± pieces cancel:

−b+b2−4ac2a+−b−b2−4ac2a=−2b2a=−ba,\frac{-b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} + \frac{-b - \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} = \frac{-2b}{2a} = -\frac{b}{a},2a−b+b2−4ac​​+2a−b−b2−4ac​​=2a−2b​=−ab​,

so their average is −b2a-\tfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​, the axis, precisely as symmetry demanded.

The discriminant and the vertex height

The quantity under the square root did all the deciding in the derivation, so it earns a name. The discriminant of ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c is

Δ=b2−4ac.\Delta = b^2 - 4ac.Δ=b2−4ac.

The Greek letter Δ\DeltaΔ (capital delta) is standard for it. Everything interesting about the number of real roots is packed into the sign of Δ\DeltaΔ, and the reason is not a coincidence of algebra. The vertex sits at x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, a fact the first lesson of this chapter already found; its height, the yyy-coordinate, turns out to be a fixed multiple of Δ\DeltaΔ, once aaa is fixed too.

The vertex height is −Δ4a-\dfrac{\Delta}{4a}−4aΔ​#

The first lesson of this chapter located the vertex of f(x)=ax2+bx+cf(x) = ax^2 + bx + cf(x)=ax2+bx+c at x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​. Evaluate fff there to find its height:

f ⁣(−b2a)=a(b24a2)+b(−b2a)+c=b24a−b22a+c.f\!\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) = a\left(\frac{b^2}{4a^2}\right) + b\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) + c = \frac{b^2}{4a} - \frac{b^2}{2a} + c.f(−2ab​)=a(4a2b2​)+b(−2ab​)+c=4ab2​−2ab2​+c.

Put the first two terms over 4a4a4a, using b22a=2b24a\tfrac{b^2}{2a} = \tfrac{2b^2}{4a}2ab2​=4a2b2​, and combine everything over 4a4a4a:

f ⁣(−b2a)=b2−2b24a+c=−b2+4ac4a=−b2−4ac4a=−Δ4a.f\!\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) = \frac{b^2 - 2b^2}{4a} + c = \frac{-b^2 + 4ac}{4a} = -\frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a} = -\frac{\Delta}{4a}.f(−2ab​)=4ab2−2b2​+c=4a−b2+4ac​=−4ab2−4ac​=−4aΔ​.

So the yyy-coordinate of the vertex is exactly −Δ4a-\tfrac{\Delta}{4a}−4aΔ​.

∎

This little identity turns the sign of Δ\DeltaΔ into a statement about the picture, once you also know the sign of aaa. Take a parabola that opens upward, so a>0a > 0a>0. Then −Δ4a-\tfrac{\Delta}{4a}−4aΔ​ has the opposite sign to Δ\DeltaΔ: when Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0 the vertex sits below the xxx-axis, so the upward arms must cross the axis twice. When Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 the vertex sits on the axis, so the parabola just touches it. When Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 the vertex sits above the axis, so the whole upward parabola floats clear of it and never meets it. For a parabola that opens downward, a<0a < 0a<0, every “above” and “below” swaps: the same positive Δ\DeltaΔ now puts the vertex above the axis instead of below it, because flipping the sign of aaa flips the sign of −Δ4a-\tfrac{\Delta}{4a}−4aΔ​ too. What never changes is whether the axis gets crossed, which is why the root-count table below depends on Δ\DeltaΔ alone, with no mention of aaa‘s sign at all.

Three signs, three outcomes, proved both ways

The picture makes the three cases believable. The completed-square line from the derivation makes them certain, and it even runs backward: each root count comes from exactly one sign of Δ\DeltaΔ, never two. This is the most careful passage in the lesson, so read the logic, not just the conclusions.

The discriminant counts the real roots#

Return to the line the derivation reached before any square roots:

(x+b2a)2=Δ4a2.\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{\Delta}{4a^2}.(x+2ab​)2=4a2Δ​.

The denominator 4a24a^24a2 is positive, since a≠0a \ne 0a=0, so the right side has the same sign as Δ\DeltaΔ. A real number xxx solves the equation exactly when the left side, a real square, can equal the right side. Every real number Δ\DeltaΔ is positive, zero, or negative, and nothing else, so those are the only three cases to check.

If Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0, the right side is a positive number, and a positive number has two distinct real square roots, so x+b2a=±Δ2ax + \tfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}x+2ab​=±2aΔ​​ gives two distinct values of xxx.

If Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0, the right side is 000, so the equation is (x+b2a)2=0\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = 0(x+2ab​)2=0, which holds only for x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, and holds there: one root, sitting on the axis where the parabola is tangent to it.

If Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0, the right side is negative, but the left side is a square and no real square is negative, so no real xxx works.

Now run it backward, which is the point people skip. The three signs of Δ\DeltaΔ cover every possibility with none left over, and the three outcomes, two roots, one root, no roots, can never overlap. So each outcome could only have come from its one matching sign: two distinct real roots only from Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0, since the other two signs give exactly one root or none, and likewise for the other two cases. When a quadratic factors, and over what

The factoring lesson left a question open on purpose. It showed that x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 has real zeros yet refuses to factor over the rationals, and it warned that “factors” is meaningless until you name the number system. The discriminant closes that question, but only if you keep two claims apart, because they are genuinely different and blurring them is the classic error at this exact spot.

The first claim is a real test with a real hypothesis.

The perfect-square test (for rational coefficients only)#

Let ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c have rational coefficients (integers are the common case), with a≠0a \ne 0a=0. The roots are −b±Δ2a\tfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}2a−b±Δ​​, and since aaa and bbb are rational, these roots are rational exactly when Δ\sqrt{\Delta}Δ​ is rational. A factoring lesson result says a quadratic with rational coefficients factors over the rationals exactly when it has a rational root. Chaining the two,

factors over Q  ⟺  rational root  ⟺  Δ rational  ⟺  Δ is the square of a rational number.\begin{aligned} \text{factors over } \mathbb{Q} &\iff \text{rational root} \\ &\iff \sqrt{\Delta}\ \text{rational} \\ &\iff \Delta\ \text{is the square of a rational number}. \end{aligned}factors over Q​⟺rational root⟺Δ​ rational⟺Δ is the square of a rational number.​

For integer coefficients Δ\DeltaΔ is an integer. If Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 there is no real square root at all, so no real (and hence no rational) root either. If Δ≥0\Delta \ge 0Δ≥0, then Δ\sqrt{\Delta}Δ​ is rational exactly when Δ\DeltaΔ is a perfect square; a non-negative integer that is not a perfect square has an irrational square root. So a quadratic with integer coefficients factors over the rationals if and only if its discriminant is a non-negative perfect square. That is the version you will use almost every time, since most coefficients you meet are integers; the “square of a rational number” version above is what actually holds when the coefficients are rational but not whole.

∎

The hypothesis is not decoration. The test speaks only about quadratics whose coefficients are rational, and it says nothing about a quadratic with irrational coefficients. State it with its hypothesis attached every single time.

The second claim is the trap, and it is false: a non-negative discriminant does not mean a quadratic “factors nicely.” Having real roots and factoring over the rationals are different properties. A quadratic with Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0 has two real zeros, and each real zero buys a real factor. So such a quadratic always factors over the reals, but with integer coefficients it factors over the rationals only when that Δ\DeltaΔ is a perfect square on top of being positive. The next example holds the two apart.

Worked example 3 Real roots, yet no rational factorization

Does 3x2+5x−13x^2 + 5x - 13x2+5x−1 factor over the rationals? Does 3x2+5x−23x^2 + 5x - 23x2+5x−2?

This is the honest retirement of the factoring lesson’s open question: “factors” always needs a number system named, and the discriminant is what names it.

Designing with the discriminant, and choosing a method

Because Δ\DeltaΔ controls the root count, you can run the logic backwards: fix the number of roots you want and solve for the coefficient that delivers it. The condition Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 in particular is the exact knife edge between two roots and none. So problems that ask for “exactly one solution” or a graph “tangent to the axis” are really asking you to set the discriminant to zero.

Set it to zero: k2−36=0k^2 - 36 = 0k2−36=0, so k2=36k^2 = 36k2=36 and k=±6k = \pm 6k=±6. Both work, and they are geometrically the two ways to slide the parabola until its vertex just kisses the axis. At k=6k = 6k=6 the equation is x2+6x+9=(x+3)2=0x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2 = 0x2+6x+9=(x+3)2=0 with double root −3-3−3; at k=−6k = -6k=−6 it is (x−3)2=0(x - 3)^2 = 0(x−3)2=0 with double root 333. A single condition on the discriminant pinned down both designs.

The formula always works, which tempts you to reach for it every time, but “always works” is not the same as “always fastest.” Match the method to the equation. If a quadratic factors on sight, like x2−3x−10=(x−5)(x+2)x^2 - 3x - 10 = (x - 5)(x + 2)x2−3x−10=(x−5)(x+2), factoring reads off the roots in seconds and the formula is slower busy work. If the equation is already a square, or one step from being one, like x2=2x^2 = 2x2=2 or (x−3)2=5(x - 3)^2 = 5(x−3)2=5, taking a square root directly beats expanding into aaa, bbb, ccc and running the full formula. Reach for the formula once neither shortcut is available, which tends to happen when the coefficients are awkward or the roots turn out irrational. A quick glance at the discriminant tells you which world you are in before you commit. With integer coefficients, a perfect-square discriminant means the quadratic factors over the rationals, so a factoring search will succeed; with rational, non-integer coefficients the matching condition is that Δ\DeltaΔ is the square of a rational number. Either way, any other value means factoring over the rationals is hopeless, and the formula is your reliable route.

One caution belongs with any formula that produces radicals. The quadratic formula is a statement about exact arithmetic. The value 3+23 + \sqrt{2}3+2​ is an exact root of x2−6x+7x^2 - 6x + 7x2−6x+7; the decimal 4.4144.4144.414 is only an approximation. Rounding too early can quietly corrupt an answer, especially when the formula subtracts two nearly equal numbers. Keep radicals in exact form as long as you can, and round only at the very end if a decimal is what the problem wants.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading "Δ≥0\Delta \ge 0Δ≥0" as “factors nicely.” A non-negative discriminant means real roots, nothing more. x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 has Δ=0−4(1)(−2)=8>0\Delta = 0 - 4(1)(-2) = 8 > 0Δ=0−4(1)(−2)=8>0 and two real roots ±2\pm\sqrt{2}±2​. That quadratic factors over the reals as (x−2)(x+2)\big(x - \sqrt{2}\big)\big(x + \sqrt{2}\big)(x−2​)(x+2​), yet it does not factor over the rationals, because 888 is not a perfect square. Real roots and a rational factorization are different things.
  • Dropping the hypothesis from the perfect-square test. “Factors over the rationals if and only if Δ\DeltaΔ is a perfect square” needs integer coefficients (or “the square of a rational number” for rational coefficients more generally). With irrational coefficients the test says nothing, so state the hypothesis every time you use it.
  • Forgetting the 2a2a2a under the whole numerator. The formula is −b±b2−4ac2a\dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}2a−b±b2−4ac​​: the denominator sits under both −b-b−b and the radical. Writing −b±b2−4ac2a-b \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}−b±2ab2−4ac​​ divides only the second piece and is wrong.
  • Sign slips in −b-b−b and in b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac. For b=−6b = -6b=−6, the term −b-b−b is +6+6+6 and b2=36b^2 = 36b2=36, not −36-36−36. Substitute with parentheses, as in (−6)2−4(1)(7)(-6)^2 - 4(1)(7)(−6)2−4(1)(7), so the squares and double negatives take care of themselves.
  • Trying to take the root of a negative discriminant. If b2−4ac<0b^2 - 4ac < 0b2−4ac<0, stop and report “no real solutions.” Do not invent a real square root of a negative number; there is none until the number system is enlarged in Chapter 5.
  • Not simplifying the radical or the fraction. 6±82\dfrac{6 \pm \sqrt{8}}{2}26±8​​ is not yet finished: 8=22\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}8​=22​, and the common factor of 222 cancels to give 3±23 \pm \sqrt{2}3±2​. Leaving it unsimplified hides the symmetry about the axis.
  • Applying the formula when a=0a = 0a=0. With a=0a = 0a=0 the equation is linear, the 2a2a2a denominator is zero, and the formula is meaningless. Check that the x2x^2x2 term is really present first.

Key takeaways

  • The quadratic formula x=−b±b2−4ac2ax = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​ is completing the square carried out once on the general ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0, so it solves every quadratic with a≠0a \ne 0a=0 whenever b2−4ac≥0b^2 - 4ac \ge 0b2−4ac≥0.
  • When Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0, the formula splits as −b2a±b2−4ac2a-\dfrac{b}{2a} \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}−2ab​±2ab2−4ac​​: the two roots sit the same distance on either side of the axis of symmetry x=−b2ax = -\dfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, a mirror pair whose average is that axis (their sum is −ba-\dfrac{b}{a}−ab​), exactly as the parabola’s symmetry predicts. When Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 that distance is 000, so the single root sits on the axis; when Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 there is no real root to place.
  • The discriminant is Δ=b2−4ac\Delta = b^2 - 4acΔ=b2−4ac, and the vertex height is −Δ4a-\dfrac{\Delta}{4a}−4aΔ​: for a fixed aaa, the sign of Δ\DeltaΔ tells you which side of the xxx-axis the vertex sits on (which side flips if aaa‘s sign flips), and that is why Δ\DeltaΔ‘s sign alone still fixes the real-root count.
  • Three cases over the reals, for any real a≠0a \ne 0a=0, bbb, ccc: Δ>0\Delta > 0Δ>0 iff two distinct real roots, Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 iff one repeated real root (parabola tangent to the axis), and Δ<0\Delta < 0Δ<0 iff no real roots.
  • Perfect-square test, rational coefficients only: with integer coefficients, a quadratic factors over the rationals if and only if Δ\DeltaΔ is a perfect square; with rational coefficients more generally, replace “perfect square” with “the square of a rational number.” Drop the hypothesis and the test is false.
  • Δ≥0\Delta \ge 0Δ≥0 means real roots, not a rational factorization. x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 has Δ=8\Delta = 8Δ=8 and two real roots but no rational factorization; “factors” is meaningless until you name the number system.
  • Set Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0 to force a single (double) root, and choose your method by the equation. Factor when it splits on sight, complete the square when it is nearly a square, and use the formula whenever those would be slower, keeping radicals exact until the end.

A bit of history (optional)

People were solving quadratics for three thousand years before anyone wrote a single formula that covers every one of them.

Old Babylonian scribes, in what is now Iraq, left clay tablets from about 1800 BCE recording numerical procedures for this kind of problem. They set the same puzzle over and over. A number and its partner have a known sum and a known product; find them. The procedure pressed into the clay is the one you ran in the last lesson. It goes move for move: halve, square, subtract, take the root. What the scribes recorded was a method that worked on specific numbers, worked out fresh each time; what they lacked was a way to write “for every aaa, bbb and ccc” and be done with every case at once.

Stating the rule in general also meant accepting answers people did not want. Around the year 628 the astronomer Brahmagupta, working in India, wrote out a verbal rule for the quadratic. It covers the equation almost completely. His arithmetic elsewhere let a negative quantity stand as a number in its own right, which was rare. Most mathematicians before him treated a negative answer as not a real solution at all, and many continued to for a long time afterward. They reported one solution where there were two.

Both of those inheritances sit inside the single line you proved today. The ±\pm± is the scribes’ pair of numbers. Its minus branch is the kind of quantity Brahmagupta’s arithmetic already admitted, even where nobody yet wrote a rule that returned both roots at once. One derivation, carried out once in letters instead of worked fresh on every new set of numbers, closes three thousand years of solving the same kind of problem again and again by hand.

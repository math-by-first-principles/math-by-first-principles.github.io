Vieta's Formulas Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra II. You can skip it.

Intuition

In the quadratics chapter you learned two facts about ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c that probably felt like lucky accidents: the two roots add up to −ba-\frac{b}{a}−ab​, and they multiply to ca\frac{c}{a}ac​. Why does one of them carry a minus sign and the other one not? Why should the coefficients know anything about the roots without being asked? Those two formulas were not accidents, and they were not the end of the list. They are the first two members of a family that keeps going for every degree, and the whole family drops out of one honest computation you can already do. Here is that computation: take the complete factorization the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra hands you, multiply it out, and compare it with the coefficients you started with. Everything else in this lesson is bookkeeping on that single idea.

Learning goals

  • Expand an(x−r1)⋯(x−rn)a_n(x - r_1)\cdots(x - r_n)an​(x−r1​)⋯(x−rn​) and match coefficients
  • Write ek=(−1)kan−kane_k = (-1)^k \tfrac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}ek​=(−1)kan​an−k​​ for every kkk
  • Trace the sign to the kkk minus signs a term drags
  • Recover the quadratic sum and product as the n=2n = 2n=2 case
  • Include every root, repeats and nonreal ones alike
  • Compute a symmetric function without finding the roots

Two ways to write the same polynomial

Take any polynomial of degree nnn with a nonzero leading coefficient,

p(x)=anxn+an−1xn−1+⋯+a1x+a0,an≠0.p(x) = a_nx^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x + a_0, \qquad a_n \ne 0.p(x)=an​xn+an−1​xn−1+⋯+a1​x+a0​,an​=0.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra says ppp has nnn roots over the complex numbers once they are counted with multiplicity. The factor theorem then turns each of those roots into a factor, so ppp splits completely:

p(x)=an(x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn).p(x) = a_n(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n).p(x)=an​(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​).

Those two lines describe the same object in two different languages. The first one displays the coefficients and hides the roots. The second one displays the roots and hides the coefficients. They are equal, so multiplying out the second and comparing it against the first cannot fail to tell you how the roots and the coefficients are tied to each other. That comparison is the whole content of this lesson.

Start with the degree you have already seen. Expanding two factors is one application of the distributive law:

(x−r1)(x−r2)=x2−r2x−r1x+r1r2=x2−(r1+r2)x+r1r2.(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = x^2 - r_2x - r_1x + r_1r_2 = x^2 - (r_1 + r_2)x + r_1r_2.(x−r1​)(x−r2​)=x2−r2​x−r1​x+r1​r2​=x2−(r1​+r2​)x+r1​r2​.

Divide ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c by aaa so that both sides are monic (leading coefficient 111), and set them side by side:

x2+bax+ca=x2−(r1+r2)x+r1r2.x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{c}{a} = x^2 - (r_1 + r_2)x + r_1r_2.x2+ab​x+ac​=x2−(r1​+r2​)x+r1​r2​.

Reading off the coefficient of xxx gives r1+r2=−bar_1 + r_2 = -\frac{b}{a}r1​+r2​=−ab​, and reading off the constant term gives r1r2=car_1r_2 = \frac{c}{a}r1​r2​=ac​. There is the quadratics chapter, recovered in three lines. Now run exactly the same argument one degree higher and watch a formula appear that has no quadratic counterpart at all.

The cubic, expanded slowly

Multiply the three factors in two stages. Stage one is the product you just did, and stage two multiplies it by (x−r3)(x - r_3)(x−r3​), distributing across all three terms:

[x2−(r1+r2)x+r1r2](x−r3)=x3−(r1+r2)x2+r1r2 x−r3x2+(r1+r2)r3 x−r1r2r3.\begin{aligned} \bigl[x^2 - (r_1 + r_2)x + r_1r_2\bigr](x - r_3) &= x^3 - (r_1 + r_2)x^2 + r_1r_2\,x \\[2pt] &\quad - r_3x^2 + (r_1 + r_2)r_3\,x - r_1r_2r_3. \end{aligned}[x2−(r1​+r2​)x+r1​r2​](x−r3​)​=x3−(r1​+r2​)x2+r1​r2​x−r3​x2+(r1​+r2​)r3​x−r1​r2​r3​.​

Now collect like powers. The two x2x^2x2 terms merge into −(r1+r2+r3)x2-(r_1 + r_2 + r_3)x^2−(r1​+r2​+r3​)x2. The two xxx terms merge into (r1r2+r1r3+r2r3)x(r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3)x(r1​r2​+r1​r3​+r2​r3​)x, because (r1+r2)r3=r1r3+r2r3(r_1 + r_2)r_3 = r_1r_3 + r_2r_3(r1​+r2​)r3​=r1​r3​+r2​r3​:

(x−r1)(x−r2)(x−r3)=x3−(r1+r2+r3)x2+(r1r2+r1r3+r2r3)x−r1r2r3.\begin{aligned} (x - r_1)(x - r_2)(x - r_3) &= x^3 - (r_1 + r_2 + r_3)x^2 \\ &\quad + (r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3)x - r_1r_2r_3. \end{aligned}(x−r1​)(x−r2​)(x−r3​)​=x3−(r1​+r2​+r3​)x2+(r1​r2​+r1​r3​+r2​r3​)x−r1​r2​r3​.​

Stop and look at what the coefficients turned out to be, because the whole pattern is already visible. The coefficient of x2x^2x2 collects the roots one at a time. The coefficient of xxx collects them two at a time, every possible pair exactly once. The constant term collects all three at once. And the signs alternate: minus, plus, minus.

Matching against a general cubic is now mechanical. Divide ax3+bx2+cx+dax^3 + bx^2 + cx + dax3+bx2+cx+d by aaa and compare:

x3+bax2+cax+da=x3−(r1+r2+r3)x2+(r1r2+r1r3+r2r3)x−r1r2r3.x^3 + \frac{b}{a}x^2 + \frac{c}{a}x + \frac{d}{a} = x^3 - (r_1 + r_2 + r_3)x^2 + (r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3)x - r_1r_2r_3.x3+ab​x2+ac​x+ad​=x3−(r1​+r2​+r3​)x2+(r1​r2​+r1​r3​+r2​r3​)x−r1​r2​r3​.

Equal polynomials have equal coefficients, so

r1+r2+r3=−ba,r1r2+r1r3+r2r3=ca,r1r2r3=−da.r_1 + r_2 + r_3 = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3 = \frac{c}{a}, \qquad r_1r_2r_3 = -\frac{d}{a}.r1​+r2​+r3​=−ab​,r1​r2​+r1​r3​+r2​r3​=ac​,r1​r2​r3​=−ad​.

The first of those is the old sum formula wearing a third root. The last of those is the old product formula, though it has picked up a minus sign the quadratic version did not have. The middle one is new. A quadratic has only one pair of roots, so its “sum of the products of two roots” is just r1r2r_1r_2r1​r2​, easy to mistake for the product itself. A cubic has three pairs, so that same sum finally has several terms and stands on its own. The list of formulas grew by one, and it will grow by one more at every degree from here on.

The sum of the roots is −ba-\frac{b}{a}−ab​:

r1+r2+r3=−52.r_1 + r_2 + r_3 = -\frac{5}{2}.r1​+r2​+r3​=−25​.

The sum of the products of pairs is ca\frac{c}{a}ac​:

Every formula divides by the leading coefficient a=2a = 2a=2, which is the step most often dropped. If the cubic had been monic, the three answers would have been the coefficients themselves with their signs flipped or kept. But this cubic is not monic, so each of the three answers is halved.

Where the minus signs come from

The alternating sign is the part most students copy down without believing, so watch it being born.

Each factor of the product (x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn)(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n)(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​) offers exactly two things: the xxx, or the −ri-r_i−ri​. Expanding the product means choosing one of those two from every factor, multiplying the nnn choices together, and adding up the result over all 2n2^n2n ways to choose. A product of binomials contains nothing else.

Suppose one particular choice takes the root from kkk of the factors and the xxx from the other n−kn - kn−k. The factors that gave an xxx contribute xn−kx^{n-k}xn−k. The factors that gave a root do not contribute bare roots: each one arrives with the minus sign that sits in front of it inside its own factor. So kkk roots drag exactly kkk minus signs along with them, and those multiply to

(−1)(−1)⋯(−1)⏟k of them=(−1)k.\underbrace{(-1)(-1)\cdots(-1)}_{k \text{ of them}} = (-1)^k.k of them(−1)(−1)⋯(−1)​​=(−1)k.

A term that uses kkk roots therefore carries the sign (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k and the power xn−kx^{n-k}xn−k: negative when kkk is odd, positive when kkk is even. The sign has nothing to do with whether you happen to call the result a “sum” or a “product”. It counts how many roots the term used, and that is all it does.

The whole family at once

Give these sums a name so the general result fits on one line. For roots r1,r2,…,rnr_1, r_2, \ldots, r_nr1​,r2​,…,rn​, define

e1=r1+r2+⋯+rn,e2=r1r2+r1r3+⋯+rn−1rn,e3=r1r2r3+r1r2r4+⋯+rn−2rn−1rn,      ⋮en=r1r2⋯rn.\begin{aligned} e_1 &= r_1 + r_2 + \cdots + r_n, \\[2pt] e_2 &= r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + \cdots + r_{n-1}r_n, \\[2pt] e_3 &= r_1r_2r_3 + r_1r_2r_4 + \cdots + r_{n-2}r_{n-1}r_n, \\[2pt] &\;\;\;\vdots \\[2pt] e_n &= r_1r_2\cdots r_n. \end{aligned}e1​e2​e3​en​​=r1​+r2​+⋯+rn​,=r1​r2​+r1​r3​+⋯+rn−1​rn​,=r1​r2​r3​+r1​r2​r4​+⋯+rn−2​rn−1​rn​,⋮=r1​r2​⋯rn​.​

In words: eke_kek​ adds up every product of kkk of the roots, where each product uses kkk different entries of the list r1,…,rnr_1, \ldots, r_nr1​,…,rn​. “Different entries” is not the same as “different values”: if a root appears twice in the list, both copies are available. Those two copies are treated as two separate roots that happen to be equal. These sums are the elementary symmetric functions of the roots. Symmetric, because swapping any two roots leaves every eke_kek​ unchanged: you get back the same collection of products, merely written in a different order.

With that name in hand, the expansion argument of the last section says exactly this.

Vieta's formulas for a polynomial of degree nnn#

Let p(x)=anxn+an−1xn−1+⋯+a1x+a0p(x) = a_nx^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x + a_0p(x)=an​xn+an−1​xn−1+⋯+a1​x+a0​ with an≠0a_n \ne 0an​=0, and let r1,…,rnr_1, \ldots, r_nr1​,…,rn​ be its roots over the complex numbers, each listed as many times as its multiplicity. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, applied repeatedly with the factor theorem, gives the complete factorization p(x)=an(x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn)p(x) = a_n(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n)p(x)=an​(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​). Dividing by ana_nan​ makes both sides monic:

xn+an−1anxn−1+⋯+a1anx+a0an=(x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn).x^n + \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n}x^{n-1} + \cdots + \frac{a_1}{a_n}x + \frac{a_0}{a_n} = (x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n).xn+an​an−1​​xn−1+⋯+an​a1​​x+an​a0​​=(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​).

Expand the right side by the distributive law. The last section already showed what one choice from the nnn factors contributes. Taking the root from kkk of them and the xxx from the rest gives (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k times a product of kkk of the roots, times xn−kx^{n-k}xn−k. Now group the choices by the value of kkk. Every way of taking kkk of the roots occurs exactly once among the 2n2^n2n choices, and each one produces the same power xn−kx^{n-k}xn−k and the same sign (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k. So the full coefficient of xn−kx^{n-k}xn−k is (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k times the sum of all products of kkk roots, which is (−1)kek(-1)^ke_k(−1)kek​ by definition. Letting kkk run from 000 (take no roots at all, which leaves the leading xnx^nxn) up to nnn (take every root, which leaves no xxx at all),

(x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn)=xn−e1xn−1+e2xn−2−⋯+(−1)nen.(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n) = x^n - e_1x^{n-1} + e_2x^{n-2} - \cdots + (-1)^ne_n.(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​)=xn−e1​xn−1+e2​xn−2−⋯+(−1)nen​.

One step is left. Two equal polynomials must have identical coefficients at every power of xxx. A nonzero polynomial of degree ddd has at most ddd roots, so two polynomials agreeing at infinitely many xxx-values must be the same polynomial, coefficient for coefficient. So compare the coefficient of xn−kx^{n-k}xn−k on the two sides of the monic identity. On the left it is an−kan\frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}an​an−k​​; on the right it is (−1)kek(-1)^ke_k(−1)kek​. Hence (−1)kek=an−kan(-1)^ke_k = \frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}(−1)kek​=an​an−k​​, and multiplying both sides by (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k, which is legal because (−1)k(−1)k=1(-1)^k(-1)^k = 1(−1)k(−1)k=1, gives

ek=(−1)k an−kan,k=1,2,…,n.e_k = (-1)^k\,\frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}, \qquad k = 1, 2, \ldots, n.ek​=(−1)kan​an−k​​,k=1,2,…,n.

That one line is the entire family, and every member of it was already sitting inside the factorization waiting to be multiplied out.

For monic polynomials the pattern is bare enough to memorize by looking at it:

(x−r1)(x−r2)=x2−e1x+e2,(x−r1)(x−r2)(x−r3)=x3−e1x2+e2x−e3,(x−r1)(x−r2)(x−r3)(x−r4)=x4−e1x3+e2x2−e3x+e4.\begin{aligned} (x - r_1)(x - r_2) &= x^2 - e_1x + e_2, \\[2pt] (x - r_1)(x - r_2)(x - r_3) &= x^3 - e_1x^2 + e_2x - e_3, \\[2pt] (x - r_1)(x - r_2)(x - r_3)(x - r_4) &= x^4 - e_1x^3 + e_2x^2 - e_3x + e_4. \end{aligned}(x−r1​)(x−r2​)(x−r1​)(x−r2​)(x−r3​)(x−r1​)(x−r2​)(x−r3​)(x−r4​)​=x2−e1​x+e2​,=x3−e1​x2+e2​x−e3​,=x4−e1​x3+e2​x2−e3​x+e4​.​

The signs march minus, plus, minus, plus across every row, and the sign on the constant term depends on the degree. That term is −e3-e_3−e3​ for the cubic but +e4+e_4+e4​ for the quartic, because (−1)3=−1(-1)^3 = -1(−1)3=−1 while (−1)4=+1(-1)^4 = +1(−1)4=+1. That single flip is the most common place to go wrong. So read the parity of kkk every time rather than trusting a memory of “the product of the roots is negative”.

The quadratic case, explained

Now set n=2n = 2n=2 in the general formula and write the coefficients the way the quadratics chapter did, with a2=aa_2 = aa2​=a, a1=ba_1 = ba1​=b, and a0=ca_0 = ca0​=c:

e1=r1+r2=(−1)1a1a2=−ba,e2=r1r2=(−1)2a0a2=ca.e_1 = r_1 + r_2 = (-1)^1\frac{a_1}{a_2} = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad e_2 = r_1r_2 = (-1)^2\frac{a_0}{a_2} = \frac{c}{a}.e1​=r1​+r2​=(−1)1a2​a1​​=−ab​,e2​=r1​r2​=(−1)2a2​a0​​=ac​.

Those are the two formulas from the quadratics chapter, no longer two facts to memorize but one rule applied twice. That also answers the opening callout’s puzzle. The sum uses one root per term, so it drags one minus sign and comes out negative; the product uses two roots, so its two minus signs cancel. The asymmetry was never sums versus products. It was kkk odd versus kkk even, and a quadratic is simply too short for the pattern to show itself.

Every root belongs on the list

Vieta’s formulas are statements about the complete list of roots that the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra provides: nnn of them, counted with multiplicity, taken over the complex numbers. Leave any one out and the formulas are not approximately true, they are false. Two cubics that differ only in their constant term make the point.

The first is x3−5x2+8x−4x^3 - 5x^2 + 8x - 4x3−5x2+8x−4, which factors as (x−1)(x−2)2(x - 1)(x - 2)^2(x−1)(x−2)2. Its roots are 111, 222, and 222: the double root is two entries in the list, not one. Vieta says the roots sum to −−51=5-\frac{-5}{1} = 5−1−5​=5, and indeed 1+2+2=51 + 2 + 2 = 51+2+2=5. Had you listed only the distinct values 111 and 222, you would have got 333 and concluded the formula was broken. Multiplicity is not bookkeeping pedantry; the formula is simply false without it. The other two check out as well: 1⋅2+1⋅2+2⋅2=8=ca1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2 = 8 = \frac{c}{a}1⋅2+1⋅2+2⋅2=8=ac​, using both copies of the root 222, and 1⋅2⋅2=4=−da1 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 4 = -\frac{d}{a}1⋅2⋅2=4=−ad​.

The second cubic, x3−5x2+8x−6x^3 - 5x^2 + 8x - 6x3−5x2+8x−6, differs only in its constant term, and it has a single real root. That does not mean it has a single root.

Worked example 2 Verify Vieta's formulas for x3−5x2+8x−6x^3 - 5x^2 + 8x - 6x3−5x2+8x−6, whose roots are 333, 1+i1 + i1+i, 1−i1 - i1−i

Dividing the cubic by x−3x - 3x−3 leaves the quadratic factor x2−2x+2x^2 - 2x + 2x2−2x+2, whose discriminant is 4−8=−44 - 8 = -44−8=−4, so its two roots are the complex conjugates 1+i1 + i1+i and 1−i1 - i1−i. The complete list of roots is therefore 333, 1+i1 + i1+i, 1−i1 - i1−i, and Vieta’s formulas describe that list, not just its real member.

The sum of the roots should be −ba=5-\frac{b}{a} = 5−ab​=5. The two imaginary parts cancel:

3+(1+i)+(1−i)=3+2=5.3 + (1 + i) + (1 - i) = 3 + 2 = 5.3+(1+i)+(1−i)=3+2=5.

The sum of the products of pairs should be ca=8\frac{c}{a} = 8ac​=8. Use i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 on the last product:

3(1+i)+3(1−i)+(1+i)(1−i)=(3+3i)+(3−3i)+(1−i2)=6+2=8.\begin{aligned} 3(1 + i) + 3(1 - i) + (1 + i)(1 - i) &= (3 + 3i) + (3 - 3i) + (1 - i^2) \\ &= 6 + 2 = 8. \end{aligned}3(1+i)+3(1−i)+(1+i)(1−i)​=(3+3i)+(3−3i)+(1−i2)=6+2=8.​

The product of all three should be −da=6-\frac{d}{a} = 6−ad​=6:

3(1+i)(1−i)=3⋅2=6.3(1 + i)(1 - i) = 3 \cdot 2 = 6.3(1+i)(1−i)=3⋅2=6.

All three agree. Now try the same three checks with only the real root 333 on the list: the sum is 333, not 555, and the product is 333, not 666. Every formula fails at once. This is the single most common way Vieta’s formulas are misapplied, and note why the answers came out real despite the complex roots. For a polynomial with real coefficients the non-real roots arrive in conjugate pairs, and a conjugate pair has a real sum (222 here) and a real product (222 here).

Reading the formulas backwards

The formulas run in both directions. Given the coefficients they hand you the symmetric sums, and given the roots they hand you the coefficients. The second direction is how a polynomial gets built from a prescribed set of roots.

Worked example 3 Build the monic cubic whose roots are 222, 1+i1 + i1+i, and 1−i1 - i1−i

A monic cubic is x3−e1x2+e2x−e3x^3 - e_1x^2 + e_2x - e_3x3−e1​x2+e2​x−e3​, so all three symmetric sums are needed. Compute them directly from the roots.

The sum of the roots:

e1=2+(1+i)+(1−i)=4.e_1 = 2 + (1 + i) + (1 - i) = 4.e1​=2+(1+i)+(1−i)=4.

The sum of the products of pairs, using (1+i)(1−i)=1−i2=2(1 + i)(1 - i) = 1 - i^2 = 2(1+i)(1−i)=1−i2=2:

e2=2(1+i)+2(1−i)+(1+i)(1−i)=(2+2i)+(2−2i)+2=6.e_2 = 2(1 + i) + 2(1 - i) + (1 + i)(1 - i) = (2 + 2i) + (2 - 2i) + 2 = 6.e2​=2(1+i)+2(1−i)+(1+i)(1−i)=(2+2i)+(2−2i)+2=6.

The product of all three roots:

e3=2(1+i)(1−i)=2⋅2=4.e_3 = 2(1 + i)(1 - i) = 2 \cdot 2 = 4.e3​=2(1+i)(1−i)=2⋅2=4.

Substituting into the monic template, with the signs alternating minus, plus, minus:

p(x)=x3−4x2+6x−4.p(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + 6x - 4.p(x)=x3−4x2+6x−4.

Check it the long way if you like: (x−2)(x2−2x+2)=x3−4x2+6x−4(x - 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2) = x^3 - 4x^2 + 6x - 4(x−2)(x2−2x+2)=x3−4x2+6x−4, the same polynomial. Now try dropping 1−i1 - i1−i and keeping only 1+i1 + i1+i. No polynomial with real coefficients can have that root set at all, because a lone non-real root has no partner to cancel its imaginary part.

Symmetric facts without the roots

Here is what the formulas are actually for. Many useful expressions built from the roots do not depend on which root you call r1r_1r1​ and which you call r2r_2r2​. Any such expression can be rewritten in terms of e1,e2,…,ene_1, e_2, \ldots, e_ne1​,e2​,…,en​, and Vieta hands you those for free, straight off the coefficients. So you can compute the expression for a polynomial whose roots you have no hope of finding.

The classic example is the sum of the squares of the roots. Square e1e_1e1​ and see what appears:

(r1+r2+r3)2=r12+r22+r32+2(r1r2+r1r3+r2r3).(r_1 + r_2 + r_3)^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2 + 2(r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3).(r1​+r2​+r3​)2=r12​+r22​+r32​+2(r1​r2​+r1​r3​+r2​r3​).

The cross terms are exactly 2e22e_22e2​, so subtracting them off isolates what you want:

r12+r22+r32=e12−2e2.r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2 = e_1^2 - 2e_2.r12​+r22​+r32​=e12​−2e2​.

Nothing in that derivation cared that there were three roots. Squaring a sum always produces the squares plus twice every distinct pair, so the identity holds at any degree, which is why it is worth learning once.

Worked example 4 Find r12+r22+r32r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2r12​+r22​+r32​ for 2x3−6x2+x−52x^3 - 6x^2 + x - 52x3−6x2+x−5 without finding a single root

First check that the roots really are out of reach. The rational root theorem allows only ±1,±5,±12,±52\pm 1, \pm 5, \pm\frac{1}{2}, \pm\frac{5}{2}±1,±5,±21​,±25​ as rational candidates, and substituting each one gives a nonzero value, so this cubic has no rational root at all. Finding its roots by hand is not on the table.

The symmetric sums, on the other hand, are two lines of arithmetic. With a=2a = 2a=2, b=−6b = -6b=−6, c=1c = 1c=1:

e1=−ba=−−62=3,e2=ca=12.e_1 = -\frac{b}{a} = -\frac{-6}{2} = 3, \qquad e_2 = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{1}{2}.e1​=−ab​=−2−6​=3,e2​=ac​=21​.

Now apply the identity:

r12+r22+r32=e12−2e2=32−2⋅12=9−1=8.r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2 = e_1^2 - 2e_2 = 3^2 - 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 9 - 1 = 8.r12​+r22​+r32​=e12​−2e2​=32−2⋅21​=9−1=8.

The three roots are unknown, quite possibly ugly, and two of them may not even be real, and yet the sum of their squares is exactly 888. That is the whole point of the method: symmetric information about the roots is available without the roots.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to divide by the leading coefficient. Every formula has ana_nan​ underneath it. For 2x3−6x2+x−52x^3 - 6x^2 + x - 52x3−6x2+x−5 the roots sum to −−62=3-\frac{-6}{2} = 3−2−6​=3, not 666.
  • Losing the alternating sign. The sign is (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k, decided by how many roots the term collects. The product of the roots of a cubic is −da-\frac{d}{a}−ad​, but the product of the roots of a quartic is +a0a4+\frac{a_0}{a_4}+a4​a0​​. Check the parity of kkk every single time.
  • Listing only the distinct roots. A double root is two entries. The roots of x3−5x2+8x−4=(x−1)(x−2)2x^3 - 5x^2 + 8x - 4 = (x - 1)(x - 2)^2x3−5x2+8x−4=(x−1)(x−2)2 are 1,2,21, 2, 21,2,2 and they sum to 555, not 333.
  • Leaving the non-real roots out. The formulas describe the complete list over the complex numbers. The roots of x3−5x2+8x−6x^3 - 5x^2 + 8x - 6x3−5x2+8x−6 are 3,1+i,1−i3, 1 + i, 1 - i3,1+i,1−i; using 333 alone makes every formula fail.
  • Treating a missing term as a missing formula. No x2x^2x2 term means a2=0a_2 = 0a2​=0, so the matching symmetric sum is 000. That is information, not an absence of it.
  • Mistaking e2e_2e2​ for a sum of squares. e2e_2e2​ collects the products of two different entries of the root list (r1r2+r1r3+r2r3r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3r1​r2​+r1​r3​+r2​r3​), a different expression from r12+r22+r32r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2r12​+r22​+r32​ that need not equal it. Two entries may still hold the same value, so a repeated root does contribute its own square: for the roots 1,2,21, 2, 21,2,2 the term 2⋅22 \cdot 22⋅2 belongs in e2e_2e2​. The sum of squares is instead the derived quantity e12−2e2e_1^2 - 2e_2e12​−2e2​.

  • The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra gives p(x)=an(x−r1)⋯(x−rn)p(x) = a_n(x - r_1)\cdots(x - r_n)p(x)=an​(x−r1​)⋯(x−rn​) with all nnn roots listed with multiplicity over the complex numbers. Expanding that product and matching coefficients is Vieta’s formulas. There is no other ingredient.
  • The coefficient of xn−kx^{n-k}xn−k collects every product of kkk of the roots, with the sign (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k, which is the single formula ek=(−1)kan−kane_k = (-1)^k \frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}ek​=(−1)kan​an−k​​ for every kkk from 111 to nnn.
  • The sign is born in the factor (x−ri)(x - r_i)(x−ri​). A term that uses kkk roots drags kkk minus signs along, and they multiply to (−1)k(-1)^k(−1)k: nothing to do with sums versus products.
  • Monic cubic: x3−e1x2+e2x−e3x^3 - e_1x^2 + e_2x - e_3x3−e1​x2+e2​x−e3​. Monic quadratic: x2−e1x+e2x^2 - e_1x + e_2x2−e1​x+e2​, which is the quadratics chapter’s r1+r2=−bar_1 + r_2 = -\frac{b}{a}r1​+r2​=−ab​ and r1r2=car_1r_2 = \frac{c}{a}r1​r2​=ac​, now explained rather than asserted.
  • Every root must be on the list: repeats repeated, non-real roots included. Drop one and the formulas are false, not approximate.

A bit of history (optional)

These formulas carry the name of a man who would have thrown out half of your answers.

François Viète was a French lawyer who did mathematics in the hours the courts left him. He did write relations between the roots of an equation and its coefficients. He also refused most of the roots. A negative solution he declined to count at all, and an imaginary one was worse. So his relations held for some equations and quietly failed for others, with nothing in them to say which.

The repair came from Albert Girard, a Frenchman living in the Netherlands, in a short book of 1629. His move was simple to state: count every root, including the ones everybody else dismissed as impossible. Girard then stated the relations for every degree, not merely for quadratics, and at last they held with no exceptions. The same book claims that an equation of degree nnn has exactly nnn solutions once they are counted that way. That claim later became the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, the result this whole lesson stands on.

So the rule about counting every root is not a piece of bookkeeping fussiness. Girard’s stubbornness was not bolted onto Vieta’s formulas afterwards. It is the thing that made them true.

  1. Problem 1 Reading roots from a factorization

    The polynomial p(x)=4(x+3)(x−2)2(x+1)3p(x)=4(x+3)(x-2)^2(x+1)^3p(x)=4(x+3)(x−2)2(x+1)3 is already a complete factorization over the complex numbers. State every root of ppp and its multiplicity, then give the number of distinct roots and the number of roots counted with multiplicity.

    1. Hint 1

      Each linear factor names one root, and a repeated factor raises that root's multiplicity.

    2. Hint 2

      Add the multiplicities together and compare the total to the degree.

    Answer

    −3-3−3: multiplicity 111; 222: multiplicity 222; −1-1−1: multiplicity 333; 333 distinct roots; 666 roots counted with multiplicity.

    Full solution

    Each linear factor contributes one root, repeated as many times as its exponent shows.

    p(x)=4(x+3)(x−2)2(x+1)3p(x)=4(x+3)(x-2)^2(x+1)^3p(x)=4(x+3)(x−2)2(x+1)3

    So −3-3−3 has multiplicity 111, 222 has multiplicity 222, and −1-1−1 has multiplicity 333.

    Those are three different numbers, so ppp has 333 distinct roots.

    Counted with multiplicity, the roots number 1+2+3=61+2+3=61+2+3=6, which matches the degree of ppp.

    Answer

    −3-3−3: multiplicity 111; 222: multiplicity 222; −1-1−1: multiplicity 333; 333 distinct roots; 666 roots counted with multiplicity.

    Key idea

    A complete factorization reads off every root and its multiplicity directly, and the multiplicities always sum to the degree.

  2. Problem 2 A division record

    Dividing p(x)p(x)p(x) by x+4x+4x+4 gives quotient x2+5x+4x^2+5x+4x2+5x+4 and remainder 000. Determine the multiplicity of −4-4−4 in ppp.

    1. Hint 1

      A zero remainder starts the count but does not finish it.

    2. Hint 2

      Divide the quotient by the same linear expression, then test what remains.

    Answer

    −4-4−4 has multiplicity 222.

    Full solution

    The first division gives one copy of x+4x+4x+4.

    Dividing its quotient gives

    x2+5x+4=(x+4)(x+1)x^2+5x+4=(x+4)(x+1)x2+5x+4=(x+4)(x+1)

    A further division of x+1x+1x+1 by x+4x+4x+4 has remainder −3-3−3.

    Thus

    p(x)=(x+4)2(x+1)p(x)=(x+4)^2(x+1)p(x)=(x+4)2(x+1)

    The remaining factor is nonzero at −4-4−4, so the multiplicity is exactly 222.

    Answer

    −4-4−4 has multiplicity 222.

    Key idea

    The first nonzero remainder ends the count of repeated linear factors.

  4. Problem 4 Zeros at two inputs

    For p(x)=x5+2x4+x3p(x)=x^5+2x^4+x^3p(x)=x5+2x4+x3, establish the multiplicities of 000 and −1-1−1 by repeated division, then give the distinct-root count and the count with multiplicity.

    1. Hint 1

      Start with the root whose factor can be removed without synthetic division.

    2. Hint 2

      After removing the powers of xxx, divide the remaining quadratic by x+1x+1x+1 until a nonzero remainder appears.

    Answer

    000: multiplicity 333; −1-1−1: multiplicity 222; 222 distinct roots and 555 with multiplicity.

    Full solution

    Dividing by xxx successively gives x4+2x3+x2x^4+2x^3+x^2x4+2x3+x2, then x3+2x2+xx^3+2x^2+xx3+2x2+x, then x2+2x+1x^2+2x+1x2+2x+1.

    Each division has remainder 000.

    The last quotient has value 111 at 000, so the multiplicity of 000 is exactly 333.

    The remaining quadratic divides by x+1x+1x+1 to give x+1x+1x+1, and again to give 111.

    A further division has nonzero remainder 111.

    Thus

    p(x)=x3(x+1)2p(x)=x^3(x+1)^2p(x)=x3(x+1)2

    At −1-1−1, the other factor is (−1)3=−1(-1)^3=-1(−1)3=−1, which is nonzero.

    The root multiplicities are therefore 333 and 222.

    Their sum 555 matches the degree, and their two distinct values exhaust the root list.

    Answer

    000: multiplicity 333; −1-1−1: multiplicity 222; 222 distinct roots and 555 with multiplicity.

    Key idea

    An exhausted factorization checks both multiplicities and completeness of the root list.

  5. Problem 5 A pair of unknown counts

    A degree 777 polynomial with complex coefficients has exactly two distinct complex roots, aaa and bbb. The multiplicity of aaa is one greater than the multiplicity of bbb. Find both multiplicities and write every possible complete factorization.

    1. Hint 1

      Every factor must belong to one of the two stated roots.

    2. Hint 2

      Let the smaller multiplicity be mmm and make the multiplicities add to the degree.

    Answer

    aaa: multiplicity 444; bbb: multiplicity 333; c(x−a)4(x−b)3c(x-a)^4(x-b)^3c(x−a)4(x−b)3, where ccc may be any nonzero complex number.

    Full solution

    Let mmm be the multiplicity of bbb.

    The full root count gives

    m+(m+1)=7m+(m+1)=7m+(m+1)=7 2m=62m=62m=6 m=3m=3m=3

    So aaa has multiplicity 444.

    With nonzero leading coefficient ccc, the factorization is

    p(x)=c(x−a)4(x−b)3p(x)=c(x-a)^4(x-b)^3p(x)=c(x−a)4(x−b)3

    The distinctness of aaa and bbb ensures that no extra copy is hidden in the other factor.

    Answer

    aaa: multiplicity 444; bbb: multiplicity 333; c(x−a)4(x−b)3c(x-a)^4(x-b)^3c(x−a)4(x−b)3, where ccc may be any nonzero complex number.

    Key idea

    The degree is the sum of the multiplicities of all distinct complex roots.

  9. Problem 9 Finding the non-algebraic step

    A student sketches a proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra in three steps. Step 1: on a circle of very large radius, the polynomial's leading term dominates its other terms in size, so the image of that circle loops around the origin. Step 2: as the radius shrinks to 000, the image shrinks to the single point p(0)p(0)p(0), which sits away from the origin whenever p(0)≠0p(0)\ne0p(0)=0. Step 3: a loop cannot stop enclosing a point without sweeping across it, so some circle in between has an image passing through the origin, which is exactly where a root of ppp sits. One of these three steps depends on a property beyond ordinary algebra: that a continuously changing quantity cannot skip past a value without passing through it. Which step is that? Then state the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.

    1. Hint 1

      Two of the three steps are settled by comparing sizes of numbers or by a direct substitution; the remaining step describes something moving continuously and concludes it must pass through a particular value.

    2. Hint 2

      A statement built from inequalities between absolute values can be checked by arithmetic directly; a statement that a path cannot skip over a point needs the idea that change happens continuously.

    Answer

    Step 3 depends on continuity: a continuously changing quantity cannot skip past a value without passing through it. Every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root.

    Full solution

    Steps 1 and 2 rest on comparing the sizes of specific terms and on evaluating ppp directly at a particular radius; each conclusion follows from an ordinary absolute-value inequality or a substitution.

    Step 3 is different.

    Saying a loop "cannot stop enclosing a point without sweeping across it" is a claim about continuous motion: a quantity that changes without a jump cannot skip past a value without passing through it exactly.

    That continuity property holds for the real and complex numbers but fails for the rational numbers, where a continuously moving quantity can slip past a target value through a gap with no rational number sitting in it.

    Arithmetic rules alone, without continuity, cannot supply step 3.

    The correct statement is the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: every polynomial of degree at least 111 with complex coefficients has at least one complex root.

    Answer

    Step 3 depends on continuity: a continuously changing quantity cannot skip past a value without passing through it. Every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root.

    Key idea

    The one step a proof of this theorem cannot get from algebra alone is the continuity step, which is exactly why no purely algebraic proof exists.

  10. Problem 10 Two polynomial stages

    q(w)=0q(w)=0q(w)=0

    The theorem therefore gives a complex zzz with

    p(z)=wp(z)=wp(z)=w

    Answer

    Yes, at least one complex solution is guaranteed.

    Key idea

    A polynomial equation with complex coefficients does not require roots outside the complex numbers.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Intuition

You can already turn a root into a factor and a factor into a root. What you cannot yet do is promise that a root is there to be found at all. Every widening of the number system so far, from rationals to reals to complex numbers, was forced by a polynomial whose root the smaller system could not hold. So does it ever stop? The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra answers that question for good: allow complex numbers, and every non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a root, always. No such polynomial equation, however you write its coefficients, will force a fourth kind of number into existence.

Learning goals

  • State the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra and why its proof needs ideas beyond algebra
  • Explain how removing one root at a time factors a polynomial into nnn linear factors
  • Define multiplicity and find it by dividing by (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) repeatedly
  • Count roots with multiplicity, and distinguish that count from the number of distinct roots
  • Argue why the complex numbers need no further enlargement

The gap in your toolkit

The Factor Theorem says p(r)=0p(r) = 0p(r)=0 if and only if (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) divides p(x)p(x)p(x), so a root and a linear factor are two views of the same thing. The Rational Root Theorem then narrows the search for a rational root to a finite candidate list. Synthetic division tests each candidate and hands you the quotient when one works. None of that promises a root exists. It only promises that if a rational root is there, it is on the list.

A root can escape that search in two different ways. Take p(x)=x2−2p(x) = x^2 - 2p(x)=x2−2. Its rational candidates are ±1,±2\pm 1, \pm 2±1,±2, and not one of them is a root, yet ppp does have roots, 2\sqrt{2}2​ and −2-\sqrt{2}−2​. The Rational Root Theorem was simply never built to see an irrational one. Or take x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1: since x2≥0x^2 \ge 0x2≥0 for every real xxx, the value x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1 is never smaller than 111, so no real number is a root at all. Its roots are iii and −i-i−i. Each time the search failed, the fix was to widen the number system, from the rationals to the reals, then from the reals to the complex numbers. So the honest question is this: does it ever end? Is there some polynomial whose roots escape the complex numbers too and force a fourth kind of number into existence?

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

The answer is no, and the reason is the theorem this lesson is named for.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. Every polynomial of degree at least 111 whose coefficients are complex numbers has at least one complex root.

Four parts of that statement are each doing real work.

Degree at least 111. The hypothesis is not decoration. The constant polynomial p(x)=7p(x) = 7p(x)=7 has no root, since 7=07 = 07=0 is never true, and a theorem that claimed otherwise would be false on the first line. Non-constant is exactly the condition.

Complex coefficients. The coefficients themselves are allowed to be complex, not merely real. The proof further down needs that room.

At least one complex root. A real number is a complex number whose imaginary part is 000, so a real root is one of the possible outcomes. What the theorem refuses to promise is that the root is real, or rational, or anything you could guess. It promises only that some complex number satisfies the polynomial.

Has. The theorem only asserts that a root exists. It does not hand you a formula. It does not even guarantee that the Rational Root Theorem, synthetic division, and the quadratic formula will find that root. Those tools succeed only on polynomials built to have accessible roots. What changes is that you now know a root is genuinely there, whether or not those particular tools happen to find it.

Check your understanding

Exactly one of these statements is true. Which one?

Answer choices

The first fails on x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1, which has real coefficients and no real root.

The second fails because degree at least 111 is part of the hypothesis. A constant polynomial like p(x)=7p(x) = 7p(x)=7 is never 000, so it has no root at all.

The fourth fails because the theorem is an existence statement only. It promises a root is there without saying what it is.

The third statement is the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra itself.

Why algebra alone cannot prove it

The name is misleading, and pleasantly so. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra is a theorem about algebra that cannot be proved by algebra, and there is a sharp reason why.

Imagine a proof that uses nothing but the ordinary rules of arithmetic: adding, multiplying, dividing coefficients, rearranging, comparing degrees. An argument like that never asks what kind of numbers the coefficients are, so it would run word for word inside the rational numbers too. But the theorem is false there. The polynomial x2−2x^2 - 2x2−2 has rational coefficients and no rational root. A proof built only from those rules would therefore prove something false, so no such proof can exist. Every standard proof of this theorem instead leans on continuity, the fact that the real and complex numbers have no gaps to fall through, which the rationals do not have.

The standard proof watches what the polynomial does to a shrinking circle in the complex plane. On a large circle the polynomial loops around the origin; on a tiny circle its values huddle near a single point away from the origin. Continuity is what lets you say a loop cannot stop enclosing a point without sweeping across it, and that crossing is where the root sits.

Why continuity forces a root

That is as far as this course goes. The theorem is true, and turning that picture into a full proof is the work of a later course in analysis. What you can do today is take the theorem as given and see how much it buys you, which turns out to be a great deal.

From one root to all of them

One root does not sound like much, but the Factor Theorem turns it into a smaller problem of the same kind.

Feed the Fundamental Theorem a polynomial and it returns a root rrr. Feed that root to the Factor Theorem and it returns a factor (x−r)(x - r)(x−r). Divide that factor out and you hold a polynomial of degree one lower, which the Fundamental Theorem applies to again, just as well as it did the first time. Repeat, and each step removes exactly one more root and lowers the degree by exactly one. Starting from degree nnn, that process stops after nnn steps, when the last quotient is just the leading coefficient aaa left over. Collecting every factor pulled out along the way gives the complete factorization

p(x)=a(x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn),p(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n),p(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​),

where r1,…,rnr_1, \ldots, r_nr1​,…,rn​ are the nnn roots, repeated if the same root came up more than once.

The full proof establishes that “repeat until nothing is left” really does stop, after exactly nnn steps, and never leaves a root unaccounted for. It turns that repetition into a proof using mathematical induction.

Why repeated factoring finds every root

That repeated argument is where “complex coefficients” in the theorem earns its keep. Even when ppp starts out with real coefficients, the quotient after one division usually does not. Divide the perfectly real x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1 by the factor (x−i)(x - i)(x−i) and you get x+ix + ix+i, whose constant term is not real. If the Fundamental Theorem only promised roots for polynomials with real coefficients, this argument would stall on its very first quotient. Stating the theorem for complex coefficients is what lets it apply to its own leftovers, step after step.

The factorization also settles a question the theorem never mentioned: are there any other roots hiding somewhere? Suppose ccc is any complex number with p(c)=0p(c) = 0p(c)=0. Substituting into the factorization gives a(c−r1)(c−r2)⋯(c−rn)=0a(c - r_1)(c - r_2)\cdots(c - r_n) = 0a(c−r1​)(c−r2​)⋯(c−rn​)=0, and a product of complex numbers is zero only when one of its factors is. The leading coefficient aaa is never zero, so one of the remaining factors must vanish: c−ri=0c - r_i = 0c−ri​=0 for some iii, that is, c=ric = r_ic=ri​. Every root of ppp is therefore one of the nnn numbers r1,…,rnr_1, \ldots, r_nr1​,…,rn​, and by the factorization each of those numbers really is a root. The list is complete: no root of ppp can hide anywhere else.

Multiplicity, and the honest way to count

The factorization is allowed to repeat a factor, and that repetition is the single most useful piece of bookkeeping in this chapter.

Look at what repeats. The factor (x−1)(x - 1)(x−1) appears twice in that factorization, while (x−2)(x - 2)(x−2) and (x+3)(x + 3)(x+3) each appear once. That count of copies is called the root’s multiplicity: the root 111 has multiplicity 222, and the roots 222 and −3-3−3 each have multiplicity 111. A root of multiplicity 111 is called simple, and a root of multiplicity 222 or more is called repeated.

Definition. Let ppp be a polynomial that is not the zero polynomial, and let rrr be a root of ppp. The multiplicity of rrr in ppp is the positive integer mmm for which

p(x)=(x−r)m q(x),q(r)≠0p(x) = (x - r)^m\,q(x), \qquad q(r) \ne 0p(x)=(x−r)mq(x),q(r)=0

for some polynomial qqq. Concretely, mmm counts how many times you can divide ppp by (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) before the division stops coming out even. That matches counting copies of (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) in the complete factorization: however you count, the same mmm comes out.

Now the counting can be stated without a lie in it. Let the distinct roots of ppp be s1,s2,…,sks_1, s_2, \ldots, s_ks1​,s2​,…,sk​, with multiplicities m1,m2,…,mkm_1, m_2, \ldots, m_km1​,m2​,…,mk​. Every one of the nnn linear factors carries exactly one of those roots, so

m1+m2+⋯+mk=n.m_1 + m_2 + \cdots + m_k = n.m1​+m2​+⋯+mk​=n.

Root Counting Theorem. A polynomial of degree n≥1n \ge 1n≥1 with complex coefficients has exactly nnn roots counted with multiplicity, and at most nnn distinct roots. It has exactly nnn distinct roots if and only if every root is simple.

Both halves of that last sentence are worth checking. If there are nnn distinct roots, then nnn multiplicities, each at least 111, add up to nnn, which forces every one of them to equal 111. And if every multiplicity is 111, the sum of the kkk multiplicities is just kkk, so k=nk = nk=n.

A degree-222 polynomial can behave in exactly three ways, and each one is a pattern that reappears at every higher degree too, just combined with others:

Complete factorization in practice

Nothing about the theorem changes your method. You still hunt for a root with the Rational Root Theorem, divide it out, and keep going until what remains is small enough to finish with the quadratic formula. What has changed is that you now know a complete factorization genuinely exists, even for a polynomial whose roots these particular tools cannot reach.

Worked example 1 Factor x3−4x2+9x−10x^3 - 4x^2 + 9x - 10x3−4x2+9x−10 completely

This one works out cleanly because a rational root is on the candidate list. The leading coefficient is 111 and the constant term is −10-10−10, so the Rational Root Theorem offers the candidates ±1,±2,±5,±10\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 5, \pm 10±1,±2,±5,±10. Test the small ones:

p(1)=1−4+9−10=−4,p(2)=8−16+18−10=0.p(1) = 1 - 4 + 9 - 10 = -4, \qquad p(2) = 8 - 16 + 18 - 10 = 0.p(1)=1−4+9−10=−4,p(2)=8−16+18−10=0.

So 222 is a root, and by the Factor Theorem (x−2)(x - 2)(x−2) divides ppp. Synthetic division with 222 on the coefficients 1,−4,9,−101, -4, 9, -101,−4,9,−10 brings down 111, then produces −4+2=−2-4 + 2 = -2−4+2=−2, then 9−4=59 - 4 = 59−4=5, then −10+10=0-10 + 10 = 0−10+10=0. The zero remainder confirms the root and the quotient is

p(x)=(x−2)3(x+1).p(x) = (x - 2)^3(x + 1).p(x)=(x−2)3(x+1).

The root 222 has multiplicity 333 and the root −1-1−1 has multiplicity 111. Their multiplicities add to 3+1=43 + 1 = 43+1=4, which is the degree, exactly as the Root Counting Theorem demands. The polynomial has four roots counted with multiplicity and only two distinct ones.

The factorization runs backwards too: a root of multiplicity mmm contributes (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) repeated mmm times. Building one from the roots 333 (multiplicity 222) and −i-i−i shows a coefficient side effect worth seeing, a non-real root that does not drag its conjugate along.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Saying “a degree nnn polynomial has nnn roots” and stopping there. Over the reals the claim is false, since x2+1x^2 + 1x2+1 has no real root, and counting distinct roots it is false too, since x2−2x+1=(x−1)2x^2 - 2x + 1 = (x-1)^2x2−2x+1=(x−1)2 has only the root 111. The true sentence is “exactly nnn roots, counted with multiplicity, over the complex numbers.”
  • Expecting the theorem to hand you the roots. It is an existence statement. It never produces a root. It never even guarantees that the Rational Root Theorem and the quadratic formula will land on one, since those tools only reach polynomials built to have accessible roots.
  • Guessing a multiplicity instead of testing it. One successful division by (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) proves the multiplicity is at least 111 and nothing more. Keep dividing, and stop only when a remainder comes out nonzero.
  • Applying the theorem to a constant. Degree at least 111 is a hypothesis, not decoration. The polynomial p(x)=7p(x) = 7p(x)=7 has no roots whatsoever, and the theorem never claimed it did.

Key takeaways

  • The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. Every polynomial of degree at least 111 with complex coefficients has at least one complex root.
  • Complete factorization. Removing one root and one factor at a time gives p(x)=a(x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn)p(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n)p(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​), where aaa is the leading coefficient, and those nnn numbers are all of the roots there are.
  • Multiplicity. The root rrr has multiplicity mmm when p(x)=(x−r)mq(x)p(x) = (x - r)^m q(x)p(x)=(x−r)mq(x) with q(r)≠0q(r) \ne 0q(r)=0. It counts how many times (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) appears as a factor, and you find it by dividing repeatedly until the remainder is nonzero.
  • Counting. The multiplicities of the distinct roots sum to nnn. A degree-nnn polynomial has exactly nnn roots counted with multiplicity and at most nnn distinct roots, with equality exactly when every root is simple.
  • The complex numbers are enough. No polynomial equation with complex coefficients, however unpleasant, can force you to invent a new kind of number.

A sketch of what the proof actually uses

The core lesson shows that no proof built only from algebra’s ordinary rules can work: such a proof would run inside the rational numbers too, and the theorem is false there. Here is a sketch of what a real proof leans on instead. It is not a full proof, since it depends on ideas about continuous change that belong to a later course in analysis, but it shows where those ideas do their work.

Write a monic polynomial of degree n≥1n \ge 1n≥1, which costs nothing since dividing by the leading coefficient changes no roots. Call the variable zzz rather than xxx, as a reminder that zzz now ranges over the whole complex plane, not just the real line:

p(z)=zn+an−1zn−1+⋯+a1z+a0.p(z) = z^n + a_{n-1}z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 z + a_0.p(z)=zn+an−1​zn−1+⋯+a1​z+a0​.

Set S=∣an−1∣+⋯+∣a1∣+∣a0∣S = |a_{n-1}| + \cdots + |a_1| + |a_0|S=∣an−1​∣+⋯+∣a1​∣+∣a0​∣, and let zzz be any complex number on the circle ∣z∣=R|z| = R∣z∣=R, where RRR is chosen larger than both 111 and SSS. The modulus of a sum is never more than the sum of the moduli, because laying the arrows end to end can never carry you further from the start than the total of their lengths. So the tail of the polynomial obeys

∣an−1zn−1+⋯+a0∣  ≤  ∣an−1∣Rn−1+⋯+∣a1∣R+∣a0∣  ≤  S Rn−1  <  R⋅Rn−1=Rn.\begin{aligned} |a_{n-1}z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0| &\;\le\; |a_{n-1}|R^{n-1} + \cdots + |a_1|R + |a_0| \\ &\;\le\; S\,R^{n-1} \;<\; R \cdot R^{n-1} = R^n. \end{aligned}∣an−1​zn−1+⋯+a0​∣​≤∣an−1​∣Rn−1+⋯+∣a1​∣R+∣a0​∣≤SRn−1<R⋅Rn−1=Rn.​

The middle step uses R>1R > 1R>1, which makes every power RkR^kRk with k≤n−1k \le n-1k≤n−1 no bigger than Rn−1R^{n-1}Rn−1; the last step uses R>SR > SR>S. And RnR^nRn is precisely ∣zn∣|z^n|∣zn∣. On a large enough circle, then, the leading term znz^nzn is strictly bigger in size than the whole rest of the polynomial put together. The tail can nudge p(z)p(z)p(z) away from znz^nzn, but it can never nudge it as far as the origin.

Now watch where ppp sends that circle. As zzz travels once around ∣z∣=R|z| = R∣z∣=R, the point znz^nzn travels around the origin nnn times. Multiplying by iii is a quarter turn, i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 is a half turn, and raising to the nnnth power multiplies the turning by nnn. So the image of the big circle is a closed loop that wraps nnn times around the origin. By the size bound above, the tail is far too small to drag that loop across the origin and cancel a lap.

Now shrink RRR toward 000. The loop deforms continuously as it goes. When RRR finally reaches 000 the circle has collapsed to the single point z=0z = 0z=0, so its image has collapsed to the single point p(0)=a0p(0) = a_0p(0)=a0​. If a0=0a_0 = 0a0​=0, then 000 is a root and there is nothing left to prove. Otherwise the image is now a point sitting away from the origin, enclosing nothing. So the loop begins wrapped around the origin and ends clear of it, and a loop cannot stop enclosing a point without crossing it. At the radius where the crossing happens, some zzz satisfies p(z)=0p(z) = 0p(z)=0. That zzz is the root the theorem promises.

Every word of that sketch depends on ideas about continuous change: that a loop can deform gradually, and that it cannot stop enclosing a point without sweeping across it. Turning those words into a proof is the work of analysis and topology, courses still ahead of you.

The complete factorization proof, written out by induction

The core lesson explains the complete factorization by repeating “find a root, divide it out” until nothing is left. Turning “repeat” into a proof takes mathematical induction, proving a claim first for the smallest case and then showing it survives one more step, forever.

A polynomial of degree n≥1n \ge 1n≥1 splits into exactly nnn linear factors over the complex numbers#

Let ppp have degree n≥1n \ge 1n≥1, complex coefficients, and leading coefficient aaa. We prove by induction on nnn that there are complex numbers r1,r2,…,rnr_1, r_2, \ldots, r_nr1​,r2​,…,rn​, not necessarily different from each other, with p(x)=a(x−r1)(x−r2)⋯(x−rn)p(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n)p(x)=a(x−r1​)(x−r2​)⋯(x−rn​).

For the base case, take n=1n = 1n=1, so p(x)=ax+bp(x) = ax + bp(x)=ax+b with a≠0a \ne 0a=0. Factoring the leading coefficient out gives p(x)=a(x+ba)p(x) = a\left(x + \frac{b}{a}\right)p(x)=a(x+ab​), which is a(x−(−ba))a\bigl(x - (-\tfrac{b}{a})\bigr)a(x−(−ab​)). That is the required shape, with the single root r1=−bar_1 = -\frac{b}{a}r1​=−ab​.

Now suppose the claim holds for every polynomial of degree n−1n - 1n−1 with complex coefficients, and let ppp have degree n≥2n \ge 2n≥2 and leading coefficient aaa. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, ppp has at least one complex root; call it rnr_nrn​. By the Factor Theorem, (x−rn)(x - r_n)(x−rn​) divides ppp, so there is a polynomial qqq with complex coefficients satisfying p(x)=(x−rn) q(x)p(x) = (x - r_n)\,q(x)p(x)=(x−rn​)q(x).

Building a polynomial backwards from its roots

The factorization runs backwards as well as forwards. Given the roots and their multiplicities, you can write down a polynomial that has exactly those roots, and no others, by writing one linear factor per copy.

Worked example 3 Build the polynomial of degree 333 with leading coefficient 222 whose roots are 333 (multiplicity 222) and −i-i−i

Run the factorization backwards. Each root of multiplicity mmm contributes the factor (x−r)(x - r)(x−r) repeated mmm times, and the leading coefficient goes out front:

p(x)=2(x−3)2(x−(−i))=2(x−3)2(x+i).p(x) = 2(x - 3)^2\bigl(x - (-i)\bigr) = 2(x - 3)^2(x + i).p(x)=2(x−3)2(x−(−i))=2(x−3)2(x+i).

Check the count before expanding. Two factors of (x−3)(x - 3)(x−3) plus one factor of (x+i)(x + i)(x+i) is three linear factors, so the degree is 333, and 2+1=32 + 1 = 32+1=3 multiplicities as required.

Expanding, (x−3)2=x2−6x+9(x - 3)^2 = x^2 - 6x + 9(x−3)2=x2−6x+9, and multiplying by (x+i)(x + i)(x+i) gives

Multiplying through by the leading coefficient 222,

p(x)=2x3+(2i−12)x2+(18−12i)x+18i.p(x) = 2x^3 + (2i - 12)x^2 + (18 - 12i)x + 18i.p(x)=2x3+(2i−12)x2+(18−12i)x+18i.

Look at what came out. The coefficients are not real, and that is allowed: the Fundamental Theorem and the Root Counting Theorem never asked for real coefficients. Notice too that −i-i−i is a root while iii is not. A non-real root does not automatically drag its conjugate along; that only happens under an extra hypothesis, which is exactly what the next lesson is about.

A bit of history (optional)

Everybody believed this theorem long before anybody could prove it, and the failures are the interesting part.

The eighteenth century is a graveyard of attempted proofs. Pierre-Simon Laplace, a French scientist better known for his work on the planets, published one in 1795. Several others tried in the same decades. Every one of them failed, and they tended to fail in the same place.

The arguments began by supposing that the roots existed somewhere, in some larger system nobody had bothered to describe. From there they showed that such roots had to be complex. Read that twice. Whether the roots exist at all was the entire question, and each proof had quietly helped itself to the answer.

Carl Friedrich Gauss, a German student of twenty-two, made exactly that objection in his doctoral thesis of 1799, and supplied a proof of his own. The irony is that his argument had a hole in it too. At one step he assumed that two curves drawn a certain way must cross, which is obvious to look at and genuinely hard to establish. More than a century passed before anybody closed the gap.

Notice what all this labour bought, and what it did not: a guarantee that the roots exist, with no way to find a single one of them. Existence is one question, and finding an actual root is quite another.

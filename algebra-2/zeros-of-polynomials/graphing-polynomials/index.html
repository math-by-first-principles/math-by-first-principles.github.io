Graphing Polynomial Functions

Intuition

You already know how to describe the two ends of a polynomial’s graph, from its degree and leading coefficient. What you could not describe was the middle, where all the interesting behavior happens. The last lesson used a complete root list to read off coefficient information. This one turns the same list, and each root’s multiplicity, toward a different question: what does the curve itself look like? Once you know the roots and how many times each one repeats, the middle stops being a mystery. Each real zero pins the curve to the x-axis, and the multiplicity of that zero decides whether the curve crosses straight through or bounces back off it. The factored form is not just a solved equation. It is a blueprint of the picture.

Learning goals

  • Read the blueprint off factored form
  • Change sign only at a real zero, and not always there
  • Cross at odd multiplicity and touch at even
  • Settle a whole interval with one test point
  • Note that nonreal roots give no intercepts
  • Leave the turning points to calculus rather than faking them

The blueprint hidden in the factored form

Write a polynomial in factored form and read what it says:

P(x)=2(x+3)(x−1)2(x−4)3.P(x) = 2(x+3)(x-1)^2(x-4)^3.P(x)=2(x+3)(x−1)2(x−4)3.

The degree is 1+2+3=61 + 2 + 3 = 61+2+3=6, because multiplying out takes one xxx from the first factor, two from the second, and three from the third. The leading coefficient is 222, the number in front. The real zeros are −3-3−3, 111, and 444, with multiplicities 111, 222, and 333. Every fact you need is sitting right there in the notation, and a sketch is just those facts drawn.

A sketch has to answer four questions, and the factored form answers all four:

  1. Where do the two arms go? The degree and the leading coefficient settle the end behavior, which you already know how to read.
  2. Where does the curve meet the x-axis? At the real zeros, and nowhere else.
  3. How does it meet the axis at each zero? That is what the multiplicity is for, and it is the one genuinely new idea in this lesson.
  4. Which side of the axis is the curve on between consecutive zeros? One test point per interval settles it.

There is also a free fifth anchor: the y-intercept, P(0)P(0)P(0), which is just the constant term of the expanded polynomial. In the example above, P(0)=2(3)(−1)2(−4)3=2⋅3⋅1⋅(−64)=−384P(0) = 2(3)(-1)^2(-4)^3 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 \cdot (-64) = -384P(0)=2(3)(−1)2(−4)3=2⋅3⋅1⋅(−64)=−384, so the curve passes through (0,−384)(0, -384)(0,−384).

Questions 2, 3, and 4 all rest on a single fact from the start of the last chapter: the graph of a polynomial is one unbroken curve. No jumps, no holes, no gaps. Everything below is squeezed out of that one sentence.

Where a polynomial is allowed to change sign

A polynomial changes sign only at a real zero#

Suppose a<ba < ba<b and that P(a)P(a)P(a) and P(b)P(b)P(b) have opposite signs, say P(a)<0P(a) < 0P(a)<0 and P(b)>0P(b) > 0P(b)>0. The point (a,P(a))(a, P(a))(a,P(a)) lies below the x-axis and the point (b,P(b))(b, P(b))(b,P(b)) lies above it. Since the graph of a polynomial is a single unbroken curve, the piece of the curve joining those two points cannot leap across the axis. Instead that connecting piece of the polynomial’s curve has to meet the axis somewhere between those two points. A point where the curve meets the axis is a point where the height is zero, so there is some ccc with a<c<ba < c < ba<c<b and P(c)=0P(c) = 0P(c)=0. That ccc is a real zero.

Now read the statement backwards. If an interval contains no real zero of PPP, then no two points of that interval can give values of opposite sign. The reason is that a sign change would force a real zero of PPP to sit between those two points. So on an interval free of real zeros, PPP keeps one and the same sign at every single point.

∎

This is the engine of the whole lesson, so it is worth being precise about what it does and does not say. It says a sign change requires a real zero. It does not say that a real zero forces a sign change. The converse is false, and the next section is about exactly the zeros where the sign refuses to flip.

The immediate payoff is practical. The real zeros cut the number line into intervals. Inside one of those intervals there is no zero, so PPP holds one sign the whole way across. Test one point and you have settled the entire interval. Pick whatever number is easiest to evaluate, and never pick a zero itself, since that returns 000 and tells you nothing.

How the curve meets the axis at a zero

Recall what multiplicity means. Saying that rrr is a zero of multiplicity mmm means

P(x)=(x−r)m G(x),G(r)≠0,P(x) = (x - r)^m \, G(x), \qquad G(r) \neq 0,P(x)=(x−r)mG(x),G(r)=0,

where GGG is a polynomial: the factor (x−r)(x-r)(x−r) divides PPP exactly mmm times and no more. The whole question of how the curve behaves at rrr comes down to the sign of that product just to the left and just to the right of rrr.

See the pattern first on the simplest possible case, r=2r = 2r=2. Compare y=(x−2)2y = (x-2)^2y=(x−2)2 with y=(x−2)3y = (x-2)^3y=(x−2)3 at a point just left of 222 and a point just right of it:

(1.9−2)2=0.01,(2.1−2)2=0.01,(1.9−2)3=−0.001,(2.1−2)3=0.001.\begin{aligned} (1.9 - 2)^2 &= 0.01, \qquad (2.1-2)^2 = 0.01, \\ (1.9-2)^3 &= -0.001, \qquad (2.1-2)^3 = 0.001. \end{aligned}(1.9−2)2(1.9−2)3​=0.01,(2.1−2)2=0.01,=−0.001,(2.1−2)3=0.001.​

The even power gives the same sign, positive, on both sides: the curve stays above the axis and only touches it at 222. The odd power flips sign, negative then positive: the curve is below the axis on one side and above it on the other, so it has to cross. That flip-or-hold pattern is the whole proof. The argument below just checks that multiplying by G(x)G(x)G(x), which keeps one sign near rrr, cannot undo it.

Odd multiplicity crosses, even multiplicity touches#

Let rrr be a real zero of PPP with multiplicity mmm, and write P(x)=(x−r)mG(x)P(x) = (x-r)^m G(x)P(x)=(x−r)mG(x) with G(r)≠0G(r) \neq 0G(r)=0.

First, pin down GGG near rrr. A polynomial of degree kkk has at most kkk real zeros, so GGG has only finitely many, and rrr is not one of them. Choose a distance ddd smaller than the gap from rrr to the nearest real zero of GGG. If GGG has no real zeros at all, then the distance ddd can be anything you like. On the interval r−d<x<r+dr - d < x < r + dr−d<x<r+d the polynomial GGG has no zero, so by the result above it holds a single sign there. And since rrr sits inside that interval, the single sign the polynomial GGG holds there is the sign of G(r)G(r)G(r).

Next, the sign of the other factor. For x>rx > rx>r the quantity x−rx - rx−r is positive, so (x−r)m(x-r)^m(x−r)m is positive no matter what mmm is. For x<rx < rx<r the quantity x−rx - rx−r is negative, and a negative number raised to the power mmm is positive when mmm is even and negative when mmm is odd.

Multiply the two signs together on our small interval, where GGG never changes sign.

If mmm is odd, then just to the right of rrr the product P(x)P(x)P(x) carries the sign of G(r)G(r)G(r), and just to the left it carries the opposite sign. The curve is on one side of the axis before rrr and on the other side after it, and it is zero at rrr itself. It crosses.

If mmm is even, then (x−r)m(x-r)^m(x−r)m is positive on both sides, so P(x)P(x)P(x) carries the sign of G(r)G(r)G(r) on both sides of rrr. The curve approaches the axis, reaches it at rrr, and leaves on the side it came from. It touches and turns back. In fact P(r)=0P(r) = 0P(r)=0 while PPP has one constant sign nearby, so rrr is a genuine turning point of the graph. That turning point is a local minimum if the constant sign is positive, and a local maximum if that sign is negative.

At a real zero there are only two possibilities, crossing or touching, and there are only two possibilities for mmm, odd or even. The two implications above therefore run both ways: the graph crosses the x-axis at a real zero rrr if and only if the multiplicity of rrr is odd. And the graph touches without crossing at that same zero rrr if and only if the multiplicity of rrr is even.

∎

Read the scope of that theorem carefully. It is a statement about a real zero of PPP, and it describes only what happens at the axis. It says nothing whatever about turning points elsewhere: a graph can perfectly well turn around at a point high above the axis. At such a point no zero and no multiplicity is involved at all.

The proof also hands you a mental picture. Near rrr, the factor G(x)G(x)G(x) barely moves, sitting close to the fixed number G(r)G(r)G(r), while (x−r)m(x-r)^m(x−r)m does all the work. So near a zero of multiplicity mmm, the curve looks like the power curve y=G(r)(x−r)my = G(r)(x-r)^my=G(r)(x−r)m, shifted to sit at rrr:

Why a higher multiplicity flattens the curve

Look again at ∣P(x)∣=∣x−r∣m⋅∣G(x)∣|P(x)| = |x-r|^m \cdot |G(x)|∣P(x)∣=∣x−r∣m⋅∣G(x)∣. Now watch what the exponent does to a small distance. At d=0.2d = 0.2d=0.2 away from the zero,

d1=0.2,d2=0.04,d3=0.008,d5=0.000 32.d^1 = 0.2, \qquad d^2 = 0.04, \qquad d^3 = 0.008, \qquad d^5 = 0.000\,32 .d1=0.2,d2=0.04,d3=0.008,d5=0.00032.

Each extra power multiplies the height by another factor of 0.20.20.2. Raising the multiplicity from 111 to 555 therefore pulls the curve 0.24=1/6250.2^4 = 1/6250.24=1/625 of the way toward the axis at that same distance. The parity still decides whether the curve crosses or rebounds, and the size of mmm only decides how flattened it looks on the way.

The sign chart

Put the two ideas together. The real zeros chop the number line into intervals; on each interval PPP holds one sign. So one test point per interval reveals the entire picture of which side of the axis the curve travels on. Take

Q(x)=(x+1)(x−2)2.Q(x) = (x+1)(x-2)^2 .Q(x)=(x+1)(x−2)2.

The real zeros are −1-1−1 and 222, which leave three intervals to test. Choose easy numbers inside each:

Q(−2)=(−1)(−4)2=−16,Q(0)=(1)(−2)2=4,Q(3)=(4)(1)2=4.Q(-2) = (-1)(-4)^2 = -16, \qquad Q(0) = (1)(-2)^2 = 4, \qquad Q(3) = (4)(1)^2 = 4 .Q(−2)=(−1)(−4)2=−16,Q(0)=(1)(−2)2=4,Q(3)=(4)(1)2=4.

So QQQ is negative to the left of −1-1−1, positive between −1-1−1 and 222, and positive again to the right of 222.

The sign chart and the multiplicity rule are two views of the same fact, and each checks the other. The zero at −1-1−1 has multiplicity 111, which is odd, so the curve crosses, and sure enough the sign flips from negative to positive across it. The zero at 222 has multiplicity 222, which is even, so the curve touches, and sure enough the sign stays positive on both sides of it. If your test points ever contradict your multiplicities, you have made an arithmetic slip, and you should find it before you draw anything.

Non-real roots leave no x-intercept

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra promises a degree nnn polynomial exactly nnn roots, counted with multiplicity. It does not promise you nnn x-intercepts, and here is the reason. The graph lives in the real plane, and an x-intercept is a real number xxx where the height P(x)P(x)P(x) is zero. A non-real root such as 2+3i2 + 3i2+3i is not a location on the x-axis at all, so it adds no point where the curve meets the axis. Only real zeros produce x-intercepts. The factor that root came from is still there, still shaping the curve’s exact height everywhere else. It just never gets to touch the axis.

So the counting works like this. Suppose the real zeros of PPP have multiplicities adding up to RRR. The remaining n−Rn - Rn−R roots are non-real, and the last lesson already established why they cannot stand alone: a real polynomial’s non-real roots always pair off with their conjugates. That pairing is why the non-real count is always even, which forces RRR and nnn to have the same parity.

That single observation pays a dividend. If nnn is odd, RRR cannot be zero (zero is even), so an odd-degree polynomial always has at least one real zero. More than that: the real multiplicities cannot all be even, or they would sum to an even RRR, so at least one real zero has odd multiplicity. By the theorem above, the graph must cross the axis somewhere. That agrees exactly with the end behavior you already know. An odd-degree graph sends one arm up and the other down, so it has to travel from below the axis to above it. Any journey from one side of the axis to the other has to cross. Two completely different arguments, the same conclusion.

Getting the zeros when the polynomial is not factored

A polynomial rarely arrives already factored. The route to its real zeros is the one this chapter and the last one built:

  1. The Rational Root Theorem lists the candidate rational roots p/qp/qp/q, where ppp divides the constant term and qqq divides the leading coefficient.
  2. Synthetic division tests a candidate. A remainder of 000 confirms a root and hands you the depressed polynomial, one degree lower, at the same time.
  3. Divide by the same root again. If the remainder is 000 a second time, the root has multiplicity at least 222, and you keep going until the remainder is nonzero. Repeated synthetic division is how you measure multiplicity in practice.
  4. Once you are down to a quadratic, factor it or use the quadratic formula. A positive discriminant gives two real zeros, which may be irrational, and irrational zeros are still perfectly good x-intercepts. A negative discriminant gives a conjugate pair of non-real roots, which contribute no intercept at all.

Keep one honest limitation in view. The Rational Root Theorem only ever proposes rational candidates. A polynomial such as (x2−2)(x2+1)(x^2 - 2)(x^2 + 1)(x2−2)(x2+1) has the real zeros 2\sqrt{2}2​ and −2-\sqrt{2}−2​, which are genuine x-intercepts, and no rational candidate will ever find them. They surface only when the quadratic factor is solved.

Putting it together

Here is the routine, in the order that makes each step cheap.

  1. Ends. Read the degree and the leading coefficient, and fix the two arms.
  2. Real zeros. Factor as far as you can, and mark each real zero on the axis.
  3. Multiplicity. At each real zero, odd means cross, even means touch and turn back. A large multiplicity flattens the curve there.
  4. Sign. Test one point in each interval between consecutive zeros, and one in each unbounded interval beyond the outermost zeros. Check the outer signs against the end behavior from step 1.
  5. Anchor. Plot P(0)P(0)P(0), and any other point you would like, for extra accuracy.

What algebra cannot tell you

Look at the sketch in Worked Example 1 again and ask a natural question: how deep is the dip between −2-2−2 and 111, and where exactly is the bottom of it? Nothing in this lesson can answer that. The tools of this chapter locate the curve’s intercepts and its sign, and that is all. They say nothing about how high a hump climbs or where a valley bottoms out.

It is tempting to guess that the bottom sits halfway between the two zeros, at x=−0.5x = -0.5x=−0.5. That guess is false, and you can refute it with arithmetic alone. Compare the value there with the value at x=−1x = -1x=−1:

P(−0.5)=(1.5)(2.25)(−3.5)=−11.8125,P(−1)=(1)(4)(−4)=−16.P(-0.5) = (1.5)(2.25)(-3.5) = -11.8125, \qquad P(-1) = (1)(4)(-4) = -16 .P(−0.5)=(1.5)(2.25)(−3.5)=−11.8125,P(−1)=(1)(4)(−4)=−16.

The curve is already lower at x=−1x = -1x=−1 than at the midpoint, so the midpoint is certainly not the lowest point. The symmetry that puts a parabola’s vertex halfway between its roots is special to degree 222, and it does not survive to higher degrees.

What we can prove is weaker and still useful. Between two consecutive real zeros the curve leaves the axis and comes back to it, so somewhere in between it has to turn around. There is at least one turning point in each gap between consecutive real zeros. And you already know the ceiling on the count, at most n−1n - 1n−1 turning points for a degree nnn polynomial.

Two kinds of turning point come with exactly known coordinates from algebra alone, and both are worth naming. A parabola’s vertex sits halfway between its two roots, by the symmetry argument from the quadratics chapter. And a real zero rrr of even multiplicity is itself a turning point, at the exactly known coordinates (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0), which is how Worked Example 1 could name (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0). A turning point that is not one of those two is generally out of algebra’s reach. Where it sits, and how high or low it goes, is a question about the slope of the curve. And finding a point where the slope is zero is the job of the derivative, a calculus tool you have not met.

So be honest about what your sketch claims. It claims the correct ends, the correct intercepts, the correct behavior at each intercept, and the correct side of the axis on every interval. It does not claim the exact height of any hump. If you want a more accurate picture, there is one legitimate way to get it: plot more points by evaluating PPP. Anything else is decoration.

Reading a graph backwards

Run the machine in reverse and you can recover a great deal of a polynomial from its picture, as long as you also say clearly what you cannot recover. Suppose you are shown a graph and told that every x-intercept is visible in the window.

What the picture gives you. Each x-intercept is a real zero. A crossing means odd multiplicity, a touch means even multiplicity. The two arms give you the sign of the leading coefficient and the parity of the degree. Adding one to the count for each crossing and two for each touch gives the least possible degree. The true degree can exceed that minimum only by an even number, since any extra multiplicity comes in steps of two and any non-real roots come in pairs.

What the picture hides. It cannot tell you a crossing’s multiplicity is 111 rather than 333 or 555 (a flattened crossing is a hint, never a proof). And it cannot show you a single non-real root. It also cannot give you the leading coefficient itself, only its sign, until you are handed one more point.

Worked example 4 Build a cubic from its graph

A cubic crosses the x-axis at x=−2x = -2x=−2, touches the axis at x=1x = 1x=1 without crossing, and passes through (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6). Find it.

Turn each feature into a factor. Crossing at −2-2−2 means a zero of odd multiplicity there; touching at 111 means a zero of even multiplicity. The smallest choices are 111 and 222, and they use up 1+2=31 + 2 = 31+2=3 degrees, which is the whole cubic. Nothing is left over, so those are the only options:

P(x)=a(x+2)(x−1)2.P(x) = a(x+2)(x-1)^2 .P(x)=a(x+2)(x−1)2.

Use the extra point to pin aaa. Substituting x=0x = 0x=0 and P(0)=6P(0) = 6P(0)=6,

a(2)(−1)2=6⟹2a=6⟹a=3.a(2)(-1)^2 = 6 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 2a = 6 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a = 3 .a(2)(−1)2=6⟹2a=6⟹a=3.

So P(x)=3(x+2)(x−1)2P(x) = 3(x+2)(x-1)^2P(x)=3(x+2)(x−1)2, which expands to 3x3−9x+63x^3 - 9x + 63x3−9x+6.

Check the picture. The degree is odd and a=3>0a = 3 > 0a=3>0, so the left arm goes down and the right arm goes up. Testing P(−3)=3(−1)(16)=−48P(-3) = 3(-1)(16) = -48P(−3)=3(−1)(16)=−48 and P(0)=6P(0) = 6P(0)=6 and P(2)=3(4)(1)=12P(2) = 3(4)(1) = 12P(2)=3(4)(1)=12 gives the signs negative, positive, positive: the curve crosses at −2-2−2 and only touches at 111, exactly as described. Since the curve is positive on both sides of x=1x = 1x=1 and zero there, the point (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) is a local minimum.

One caution on the word “find”. This is the only cubic with those features, because a cubic has just three degrees to spend. If the problem had said “a polynomial” instead of “a cubic”, there would be infinitely many answers. That is because we could raise the multiplicity at −2-2−2 to 333, giving P(x)=a(x+2)3(x−1)2P(x) = a(x+2)^3(x-1)^2P(x)=a(x+2)3(x−1)2. Or we could multiply by any quadratic with a positive leading coefficient and no real roots, and then rescale aaa so the curve still passes through (0,6)(0,6)(0,6). Each alternative would still cross at −2-2−2, touch at 111, and pass through (0,6)(0,6)(0,6), the three facts the picture actually shows. Only the shape in between those facts, which the picture never revealed, would be different.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating every zero as a crossing. A zero of even multiplicity does not cross. Reading P(x)=(x−3)2(x+1)P(x) = (x-3)^2(x+1)P(x)=(x−3)2(x+1) as “crosses at 333 and at −1-1−1” throws away the multiplicity, which is the one piece of information this chapter was built to give you.
  • Counting non-real roots as x-intercepts. A degree 555 polynomial with one real zero has exactly one x-intercept, not five. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra counts roots in the complex numbers; the x-axis only ever shows you the real ones.
  • Testing a point that is itself a zero. A test point must lie strictly inside the interval. Feeding a zero into PPP returns 000, which reports no sign at all.
  • Believing a hump peaks halfway between two zeros. True for a parabola, false in general. For P(x)=(x+2)(x−1)2(x−3)P(x) = (x+2)(x-1)^2(x-3)P(x)=(x+2)(x−1)2(x−3) the value at the midpoint x=−0.5x = -0.5x=−0.5 is −11.8125-11.8125−11.8125, while P(−1)=−16P(-1) = -16P(−1)=−16 is lower still, so the bottom is not at the midpoint.
  • Inventing a maximum. Drawing a hump is fine; labelling its peak with coordinates you never computed is not. Without calculus, the only heights you may claim are the ones you actually evaluated.
  • Reading an exact degree off a picture. A graph with two crossings and one touch has degree at least 444, and could be degree 666, 888, or more. The visible features fix a minimum and a parity, never the exact value.
  • Forgetting to reconcile the signs with the ends. If your sign chart says PPP is negative far to the right while your leading term says the right arm rises, one of the two is wrong. Find the error before you draw.

Key takeaways

  • The factored form is a blueprint: it shows the degree, the leading coefficient, every real zero, and every multiplicity, which is everything a sketch needs.
  • A polynomial’s graph is unbroken, so the sign of PPP can change only at a real zero. The converse fails, and multiplicity is what decides.
  • At a real zero rrr of multiplicity mmm, write P(x)=(x−r)mG(x)P(x) = (x-r)^m G(x)P(x)=(x−r)mG(x) with G(r)≠0G(r) \neq 0G(r)=0. The graph crosses at rrr if and only if mmm is odd, and touches and turns back if and only if mmm is even. A larger mmm flattens the curve against the axis either way.
  • The real zeros cut the line into intervals, and PPP holds one sign on each. One test point settles an entire interval.
  • Non-real roots produce no x-intercepts. They come in conjugate pairs, so the multiplicities of the real zeros always add up to a number with the same parity as the degree. That parity is why every odd-degree polynomial must cross the axis at least once.
  • An honest sketch gets the ends, the intercepts, the behavior at each intercept, and the sign on every interval right. A parabola’s vertex and the turning points that sit on the axis at even zeros are within algebra’s reach. Beyond those two, where the curve turns and how high the humps climb is generally a question for calculus, and it should not be faked.

A bit of history (optional)

Sketching a curve from its equation was once a research problem, and a hard one.

By the end of the seventeenth century the parabola was well understood. The cubic was not. Nobody had a list of the shapes a cubic curve can take, and nobody had an obvious method for producing one. The trouble is exactly the one you have been living with. Everything except the humps follows from the algebra, and the humps do not.

Isaac Newton, the English mathematician who had just invented the calculus, thought the question worth several years of his attention. He published his answer in 1704, as an appendix to a book about light. It sorts every cubic curve into seventy-two species, each one described and each one drawn. What made the classification possible was his machinery for locating the exact point where a curve turns.

Two footnotes are worth having. Later mathematicians found species that Newton had missed, so even the inventor did not finish the job by hand. And notice which half of the work required that method at all.

Your sketch pins down the ends, the intercepts, the behavior at each intercept, and the sign on every interval, all of it from the factored form. Where the humps peak belongs to the next subject. It occupied the person who invented that subject for years. Stopping there is not laziness. It is an honest boundary.

Geometric Sequences and Series

Intuition

An arithmetic sequence takes the same step every time: add ddd, add ddd, add ddd. A geometric sequence takes the same stretch every time: multiply by rrr, multiply by rrr, multiply by rrr. Swapping “add” for “multiply” changes the entire character of the sequence. Adding 333 over and over climbs slowly and evenly; multiplying by 333 over and over grows to three times the start, then nine times the start, then races off the page. This pattern drives compound interest, a population that grows by a fixed percentage each year, and a drug that clears a fixed fraction of itself from the blood each hour. Every other quantity that changes by a factor rather than by an amount follows the same pattern. Adding up a run of such terms looks hopeless, until you see a two-line trick that collapses the whole sum at once.

Learning goals

  • Divide consecutive terms for a constant ratio
  • Apply an=a1rn−1a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}an​=a1​rn−1, with n−1n - 1n−1 multiplications
  • Prove the sum by shifting and subtracting
  • Exclude r=1r = 1r=1, where the sum is nanana instead
  • Separate growth in size from growth in value
  • Reach for logarithms when nnn sits in the exponent

What makes a sequence geometric

A sequence is geometric when each term is the same fixed multiple of the term before it:

an+1=r⋅an.a_{n+1} = r \cdot a_n .an+1​=r⋅an​.

The number rrr is the common ratio. Dividing both sides by ana_nan​ shows where the name comes from:

r=an+1an,r = \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n},r=an​an+1​​,

and this ratio is the same everywhere in the sequence. Arithmetic sequences keep a constant difference; geometric sequences keep a constant ratio. That is the whole distinction.

Two conditions ride along with the definition, and neither is a technicality. A geometric sequence is defined to have a1≠0a_1 \neq 0a1​=0 and r≠0r \neq 0r=0, precisely so the ratio r=an+1/anr = a_{n+1}/a_nr=an+1​/an​ always makes sense. If either one were 000, then every term from some point on would be 000, and that quotient would be a division by zero. Requiring a1≠0a_1 \neq 0a1​=0 and r≠0r \neq 0r=0 keeps every term nonzero and every ratio defined.

To test a sequence, divide each term by the one before it. Every quotient the same means geometric; one mismatch means not. In 2,6,18,552, 6, 18, 552,6,18,55 the first two quotients are 6/2=36/2 = 36/2=3 and 18/6=318/6 = 318/6=3, but 55/1855/1855/18 is not 333, so the sequence is not geometric. Note also that 3,6,9,123, 6, 9, 123,6,9,12 fails the test even though it looks orderly: 6/3=26/3 = 26/3=2 but 9/6=1.59/6 = 1.59/6=1.5. It is arithmetic (constant difference 333), not geometric.

The nth term, and the off-by-one hiding in it

The nnnth term of a geometric sequence is an=a1r n−1a_n = a_1 r^{\,n-1}an​=a1​rn−1#

Start at a1a_1a1​ and walk forward. The definition says each step multiplies by rrr, so

a2=a1r,a3=a2r=(a1r)r=a1r2,a4=a3r=a1r3.a_2 = a_1 r, \qquad a_3 = a_2 r = (a_1 r) r = a_1 r^2, \qquad a_4 = a_3 r = a_1 r^3 .a2​=a1​r,a3​=a2​r=(a1​r)r=a1​r2,a4​=a3​r=a1​r3.

The exponent is not the term number. It is one less, and counting the steps instead of the terms shows why. Between a1a_1a1​ and ana_nan​ there are n−1n - 1n−1 gaps. Term 111 to term 222 is the first multiplication, term 222 to term 333 is the second, and the last one, term n−1n-1n−1 to term nnn, is the (n−1)(n-1)(n−1)th. So travelling from a1a_1a1​ to ana_nan​ multiplies by rrr exactly n−1n - 1n−1 times, not nnn times:

an=a1⋅r⋅r⋯r⏟n−1 factors=a1r n−1.a_n = a_1 \underbrace{\cdot r \cdot r \cdots r}_{n-1 \text{ factors}} = a_1 r^{\,n-1} .an​=a1​n−1 factors⋅r⋅r⋯r​​=a1​rn−1.

Check the formula at the near end, where mistakes show up fastest. At n=1n = 1n=1 it gives a1r0=a1a_1 r^0 = a_1a1​r0=a1​, which is exactly right: you have not moved yet, so you have multiplied by nothing. An exponent of nnn instead would report the first term as a1ra_1 ra1​r, which is the second term.

∎

This is the same off-by-one you met in arithmetic sequences, where an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d for precisely the same reason: nnn terms have only n−1n - 1n−1 gaps between them. Only the operation changed, from n−1n-1n−1 additions of ddd to n−1n-1n−1 multiplications by rrr.

Summing a finite geometric series

A geometric series is what you get when you add up the terms of a geometric sequence. Writing aaa for the first term, the sum of the first nnn terms is

Sn=a+ar+ar2+⋯+ar n−1.S_n = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{\,n-1} .Sn​=a+ar+ar2+⋯+arn−1.

The same off-by-one appears again: there are nnn terms, and the last exponent is n−1n - 1n−1. You could add them one at a time, but there is a much faster way, and it is worth seeing in full.

For r≠1r \neq 1r=1, the sum of the first nnn terms is Sn=a(1−rn)1−rS_n = \dfrac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}Sn​=1−ra(1−rn)​#

Write the sum, and underneath it write rrr times the same sum. Multiplying by rrr turns each term into the next one, so the second line is the first line shifted one place to the right:

Sn  =  a+ar+ar2+⋯+ar n−1rSn  =  a+ar+ar2+⋯+ar n−1+ar n\begin{aligned} S_n \;&=\; a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{\,n-1} \\[2pt] rS_n \;&=\; \phantom{a + {}} ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{\,n-1} + ar^{\,n} \end{aligned}Sn​rSn​​=a+ar+ar2+⋯+arn−1=a+ar+ar2+⋯+arn−1+arn​

Now subtract the second line from the first. Every term of SnS_nSn​ except the leading aaa also appears in rSnrS_nrSn​, and every term of rSnrS_nrSn​ except the trailing ar nar^{\,n}arn also appears in SnS_nSn​. Those shared terms, from ararar all the way up to ar n−1ar^{\,n-1}arn−1, cancel in pairs, and only the two ends survive:

Sn−rSn=a−ar n.S_n - rS_n = a - ar^{\,n} .Sn​−rSn​=a−arn.

Factor each side:

Sn(1−r)=a(1−r n).S_n(1 - r) = a(1 - r^{\,n}) .Sn​(1−r)=a(1−rn).

This equation is true for every ratio rrr, with no exceptions. The condition enters only at the final step. To isolate SnS_nSn​ you divide both sides by 1−r1 - r1−r, and division is legal only when 1−r≠01 - r \neq 01−r=0, that is, only when r≠1r \neq 1r=1. Assuming r≠1r \neq 1r=1:

Sn=a(1−r n)1−r.S_n = \frac{a(1 - r^{\,n})}{1 - r} .Sn​=1−ra(1−rn)​.

Test it on a sum you can check by hand. With a=3a = 3a=3, r=2r = 2r=2, and n=4n = 4n=4 the formula gives 3(1−16)/(1−2)=3(−15)/(−1)=453(1 - 16)/(1 - 2) = 3(-15)/(-1) = 453(1−16)/(1−2)=3(−15)/(−1)=45, and indeed 3+6+12+24=453 + 6 + 12 + 24 = 453+6+12+24=45.

∎

Multiplying the numerator and denominator by −1-1−1 gives the same number in a second common form:

Sn=a(1−r n)1−r=a(r n−1)r−1.S_n = \frac{a(1 - r^{\,n})}{1 - r} = \frac{a(r^{\,n} - 1)}{r - 1} .Sn​=1−ra(1−rn)​=r−1a(rn−1)​.

They are equal, so use whichever keeps the minus signs out of your way. When r>1r > 1r>1 the right-hand form is tidier, because the denominator r−1r - 1r−1 and the factor r n−1r^{\,n} - 1rn−1 are both positive there. Under that same condition the left-hand form makes both of those pieces negative, and relies on the two minus signs cancelling. In summation notation the result reads

∑k=1nar k−1=a(1−r n)1−r,r≠1.\sum_{k=1}^{n} a r^{\,k-1} = \frac{a(1 - r^{\,n})}{1 - r}, \qquad r \neq 1 .k=1∑n​ark−1=1−ra(1−rn)​,r=1.

When the ratio is 1

The sum formula has a hole in it, and it is worth seeing exactly where.

Suppose r=1r = 1r=1. Then every term is a⋅1 n−1=aa \cdot 1^{\,n-1} = aa⋅1n−1=a, so the sequence is a,a,a,…a, a, a, \ldotsa,a,a,…, which is genuinely geometric: the ratio really is constant. Adding nnn copies of aaa needs no clever trick at all:

Sn=a+a+⋯+a⏟n terms=na.S_n = \underbrace{a + a + \cdots + a}_{n \text{ terms}} = na .Sn​=n termsa+a+⋯+a​​=na.

Now try the formula on it. It reads

Sn=a(1−1 n)1−1=a⋅00,S_n = \frac{a(1 - 1^{\,n})}{1 - 1} = \frac{a \cdot 0}{0},Sn​=1−1a(1−1n)​=0a⋅0​,

which is undefined. Nothing went wrong in the derivation; it simply stops one step short. The identity the derivation proved,

Sn(1−r)=a(1−r n),S_n(1 - r) = a(1 - r^{\,n}),Sn​(1−r)=a(1−rn),

is still perfectly true at r=1r = 1r=1, where it says Sn⋅0=0S_n \cdot 0 = 0Sn​⋅0=0. The trouble is that this statement is true for every value of SnS_nSn​, so it pins down nothing at all. Multiplying by 1−r1 - r1−r threw the information away, and no rearranging can bring it back. To get the sum you must return to the definition, which hands it over immediately: Sn=naS_n = naSn​=na.

So the geometric sum formula carries a side condition, and it is not fine print.

Sn={a(1−r n)1−r,r≠1,na,r=1.S_n = \begin{cases} \dfrac{a(1 - r^{\,n})}{1 - r}, & r \neq 1, \\[10pt] na, & r = 1. \end{cases}Sn​=⎩⎨⎧​1−ra(1−rn)​,na,​r=1,r=1.​

Only r=1r = 1r=1 is excluded. The neighbouring case r=−1r = -1r=−1 is perfectly fine, because the denominator becomes 1−(−1)=21 - (-1) = 21−(−1)=2, which is nowhere near zero. For instance 5−5+5−55 - 5 + 5 - 55−5+5−5 has a=5a = 5a=5, r=−1r = -1r=−1, n=4n = 4n=4, and the formula gives 5(1−1)/2=05(1 - 1)/2 = 05(1−1)/2=0, which is right: the four terms cancel in pairs.

Growth, decay, and the curve behind the sequence

Take absolute values of the defining rule an+1=rana_{n+1} = r a_nan+1​=ran​ and you get ∣an+1∣=∣r∣⋅∣an∣|a_{n+1}| = |r| \cdot |a_n|∣an+1​∣=∣r∣⋅∣an​∣, so the sizes of the terms are themselves a geometric sequence, with ratio ∣r∣|r|∣r∣. That single line settles the growth question. If ∣r∣>1|r| > 1∣r∣>1, each term is larger in size than the last, and the sizes grow without bound. If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, each term is smaller in size than the last, and the sizes decay. If ∣r∣=1|r| = 1∣r∣=1, the size never changes at all: the terms stay put when r=1r = 1r=1 and flip sign forever when r=−1r = -1r=−1.

Read every word of that as a statement about size, not about which term is greater. With a1=−8a_1 = -8a1​=−8 and r=12r = \tfrac{1}{2}r=21​ the terms run −8,−4,−2,−1-8, -4, -2, -1−8,−4,−2,−1, and each one is greater than the last while steadily shrinking. The sign pattern is a separate question, decided by the signs of a1a_1a1​ and rrr. A positive ratio rrr keeps every term on the same side of zero as a1a_1a1​, while a negative ratio rrr makes the terms alternate.

There is a familiar function hiding behind all this. Since r≠0r \neq 0r=0 you may write

an=a1r n−1=a1r⋅r n=Cr n,C=a1r,a_n = a_1 r^{\,n-1} = \frac{a_1}{r} \cdot r^{\,n} = C r^{\,n}, \qquad C = \frac{a_1}{r},an​=a1​rn−1=ra1​​⋅rn=Crn,C=ra1​​,

and when r>0r > 0r>0 and r≠1r \neq 1r=1 that is precisely the exponential function f(x)=Cr xf(x) = C r^{\,x}f(x)=Crx, evaluated at x=1,2,3,…x = 1, 2, 3, \ldotsx=1,2,3,… instead of at every real number. A geometric sequence with r>0r > 0r>0 and r≠1r \neq 1r=1 is an exponential function sampled at the integers. Both exclusions are real, and they fail for different reasons. At r=1r = 1r=1 the rule C⋅1 xC \cdot 1^{\,x}C⋅1x is a constant function, and an exponential base is never allowed to be 111. At r<0r < 0r<0 the power r xr^{\,x}rx has no real value at most exponents (there is no real (−2)1/2(-2)^{1/2}(−2)1/2), so no real exponential curve passes through the terms at all. With a negative ratio the terms simply alternate in sign, while their sizes march along the curve belonging to ∣r∣|r|∣r∣. Everything you know about exponential growth and decay transfers straight across: a quantity growing by a fixed percentage each period has ratio r=1+p100r = 1 + \frac{p}{100}r=1+100p​ for a ppp percent rise. Reading the value of that quantity once per period produces a geometric sequence.

Finding the number of terms with logarithms

In an=a1r n−1a_n = a_1 r^{\,n-1}an​=a1​rn−1 the unknown nnn sits in the exponent, which is exactly the situation logarithms were built for. Divide by a1a_1a1​, take the logarithm of both sides, and bring the exponent down:

r n−1=ana1⟹(n−1)log⁡r=log⁡ ⁣(ana1)⟹n=1+log⁡(an/a1)log⁡r,r^{\,n-1} = \frac{a_n}{a_1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (n-1)\log r = \log\!\left(\frac{a_n}{a_1}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = 1 + \frac{\log(a_n / a_1)}{\log r},rn−1=a1​an​​⟹(n−1)logr=log(a1​an​​)⟹n=1+logrlog(an​/a1​)​,

valid whenever r>0r > 0r>0 and r≠1r \neq 1r=1, so that log⁡r\log rlogr exists and is not zero. When the target is a nice power (2 n−1=1282^{\,n-1} = 1282n−1=128, say) you can read the exponent off by inspection. When it is not, the logarithm does the work.

Note that this eighth hour corresponds to the ninth term of the sequence, which is exactly the trap.

What happens if you never stop adding, and let the terms of a decaying geometric sequence pile up forever, is a genuinely different question. That question is the subject of the next lesson.

A bit of history (optional)

How would you prove something about a sum whose length nobody has told you? Modern algebra writes the letter nnn and moves on. Greek mathematics had no symbol like nnn or SnS_nSn​ to write instead.

Euclid, who taught in Alexandria, a port city in Egypt, around 300 BCE, proved it regardless. Proposition 35 of Book IX of his Elements states the sum of a geometric progression, wholly in the language of ratios. His argument reaches the same result as the shift and subtract you carried out earlier, though he never writes a shifted row of terms; he weighs the amount by which the second term exceeds the first against the amount by which the last term exceeds the first. Both comparisons produce the same ratio.

Then he does something odd with it. The very next proposition concerns perfect numbers, meaning numbers like 666 and 282828 that equal the total of their own smaller divisors. Euclid adds up 1+2+4+⋯1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots1+2+4+⋯, and proves that whenever the running total is prime, that total times the last term is perfect. Try it. Here 1+2+4=71 + 2 + 4 = 71+2+4=7 is prime, and 7×4=287 \times 4 = 287×4=28.

Nobody has ever found an odd perfect number, and nobody has shown that none exists. So a sum of powers opened a question in 300 BCE that remains open today. The instrument that opened it is the formula you obtained by shifting the sum and subtracting.

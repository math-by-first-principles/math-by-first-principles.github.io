Infinite Geometric Series

Intuition

Cut a cake in half. Cut the leftover half in half. Cut that leftover in half, and keep going forever. The pieces you take are 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, then 14\tfrac{1}{4}41​, then 18\tfrac{1}{8}81​, then 116\tfrac{1}{16}161​, and it looks like they should add up to the whole cake. But addition only ever combines two numbers at a time. Three numbers are fine, since you add two of them and then add the third, and any finite list works the same way. An endless list of cuts has no last step, so “add them all up” has no meaning yet. Before any formula, this lesson has to say what an infinite sum even means.

Learning goals

  • Define an infinite sum through its partial sums
  • Decide whether a geometric series with a≠0a \ne 0a=0 converges, using ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1
  • Find its sum with S=a1−rS = \tfrac{a}{1-r}S=1−ra​
  • Distinguish two ways a series can diverge, running away and never settling
  • Turn any repeating decimal into an exact fraction

An infinite sum needs a definition

You cannot perform infinitely many additions. What you can do, for any nnn you like, is stop early and add the first nnn terms. That is an ordinary finite sum, and it has a name: the nnn-th partial sum, written SnS_nSn​.

For the cake, the partial sums are

S1=12,S2=12+14=34,S3=78,S4=1516,S10=10231024.S_1 = \tfrac12, \qquad S_2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34, \qquad S_3 = \tfrac78, \qquad S_4 = \tfrac{15}{16}, \qquad S_{10} = \tfrac{1023}{1024}.S1​=21​,S2​=21​+41​=43​,S3​=87​,S4​=1615​,S10​=10241023​.

Each of these is a perfectly ordinary number, computed with finitely many additions. Nothing infinite has happened. But look at what the list is doing: the partial sums are marching toward 111. The gap between SnS_nSn​ and 111 is exactly (12)n\left(\tfrac12\right)^n(21​)n, the size of the piece you just took. The cake still on the plate always matches the last slice you cut, and that leftover shrinks as fast as you please.

That observation is what we promote into a definition.

Definition. Given an endless list of terms, form its partial sums S1,S2,S3,…S_1, S_2, S_3, \ldotsS1​,S2​,S3​,… . Suppose those partial sums close in on a single number SSS, meaning they get and then stay as near to SSS as you care to demand. Then we say the series converges, and we define its sum to be SSS. If the partial sums do not close in on any single number, the series diverges and it has no sum at all.

This is a definition, not a fact to prove: we are deciding what “infinite sum” will mean, and choosing it so that it agrees with ordinary addition on any finite list. The phrase “close in on” stands in for the precise idea of a limit, which calculus will define carefully. Here we use it in the everyday sense: gets close, and stays close.

There is a notation for the series itself. The endless sum a+ar+ar2+⋯a + ar + ar^2 + \cdotsa+ar+ar2+⋯ is written

∑n=1∞a r n−1.\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a\,r^{\,n-1}.n=1∑∞​arn−1.

The ∞\infty∞ on top is not a number, and nothing is ever substituted for it. The whole symbol names the series itself, the endless list of terms being added. When its partial sums close in on a single number, that same symbol also stands for that number, the sum. When they do not, the series diverges, and the symbol still names the series, just one with no sum.

The partial sums of a geometric series

A geometric series is one whose terms have a constant ratio rrr:

a+ar+ar2+ar3+⋯ ,n-th term ar n−1.a + ar + ar^2 + ar^3 + \cdots, \qquad \text{$n$-th term } a r^{\,n-1}.a+ar+ar2+ar3+⋯,n-th term arn−1.

The previous lesson already did the hard finite work. For r≠1r \neq 1r=1,

Sn  =  a+ar+⋯+ar n−1  =  a(1−r n)1−r.S_n \;=\; a + ar + \cdots + ar^{\,n-1} \;=\; \frac{a\left(1 - r^{\,n}\right)}{1 - r}.Sn​=a+ar+⋯+arn−1=1−ra(1−rn)​.

That formula is exact for every nnn, and it is the whole reason geometric series are the ones we can handle. Now split the single fraction into two pieces, one of which contains nnn and one of which does not:

Sn  =  a1−r  −  a1−r⋅r n.S_n \;=\; \frac{a}{1-r} \;-\; \frac{a}{1-r}\cdot r^{\,n}.Sn​=1−ra​−1−ra​⋅rn.

Read that carefully, because the rest of the lesson is already visible in it. The first piece, a1−r\tfrac{a}{1-r}1−ra​, is a fixed number: it is the same for S1S_1S1​ and for S1,000,000S_{1{,}000{,}000}S1,000,000​. The second piece is that same fixed number multiplied by r nr^{\,n}rn. So the question “what do the partial sums do?” has collapsed into a single much smaller question: what does r nr^{\,n}rn do as nnn grows?

The one fact we borrow from calculus

Everything now rests on the behavior of the powers of rrr, and here we have to be honest with you.

The Powers Fact. Let rrr be a real number.

  • If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, the powers r nr^{\,n}rn close in on 000 as nnn grows.
  • If ∣r∣>1|r| > 1∣r∣>1, the sizes ∣r n∣\left|r^{\,n}\right|∣rn∣ pass every bound as nnn grows.

Watch it happen. Multiplying repeatedly by a number smaller than 111 in size drives the size down; multiplying repeatedly by a number larger than 111 in size drives it up. The values below are rounded.

A table is not an argument, and it is easy to over-claim from one. Shrinking is not enough on its own: the numbers 1+1n1 + \tfrac1n1+n1​ shrink at every step too, and they close in on 111, not on 000. What is special about r nr^{\,n}rn when ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 is that it shrinks by the same factor every step, which is strong enough to beat any tolerance you name, however small. A negative ratio needs no extra care: ∣r n∣=∣r∣ n\left|r^{\,n}\right| = |r|^{\,n}∣rn∣=∣r∣n, so only the size of the power is ever in question, and the sign just flips back and forth.

The table is evidence, not proof. “Closes in on 000” has not been given a precise meaning anywhere in this course; supplying that meaning is the definition of a limit, and proving the Powers Fact from it is a calculus result you will meet later. We are borrowing the fact here, out in the open, and calculus is where the borrowing gets paid back.

The sum formula

Grant the Powers Fact, and the rest is short. Recall the split from before:

Sn=a1−r−c r n,where c=a1−r.S_n = \frac{a}{1-r} - c\,r^{\,n}, \qquad \text{where } c = \frac{a}{1-r}.Sn​=1−ra​−crn,where c=1−ra​.

The first piece never changes with nnn. The second piece is that same fixed number ccc times r nr^{\,n}rn, and the Powers Fact says r nr^{\,n}rn closes in on 000 whenever ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1. A fixed number times something closing in on 000 also closes in on 000, so the whole second piece vanishes and the partial sums close in on a1−r−0=a1−r\tfrac{a}{1-r} - 0 = \tfrac{a}{1-r}1−ra​−0=1−ra​. By the definition of an infinite sum, that is the series’ sum.

The step above skips over exactly one thing: showing that ”ccc times something closing in on 000” really does close in on 000, no matter how large ccc is, and handling the case a=0a = 0a=0 separately. The Go deeper section proves both, using the tolerance idea the Powers Fact itself borrows from calculus.

Convergence formula. If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, then

a+ar+ar2+⋯=a1−r.a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1-r}.a+ar+ar2+⋯=1−ra​.

One more thing falls out of the split for free. Writing S=a1−rS = \tfrac{a}{1-r}S=1−ra​, the split above says Sn=S(1−r n)S_n = S\left(1 - r^{\,n}\right)Sn​=S(1−rn), so stopping after nnn terms leaves an error of exactly

S−Sn=S r n.S - S_n = S\,r^{\,n}.S−Sn​=Srn.

That leftover is not merely small: it is a known number, and its size, ∣S−Sn∣=∣S∣ ∣r∣ n|S - S_n| = |S|\,|r|^{\,n}∣S−Sn​∣=∣S∣∣r∣n, shrinks by a factor of ∣r∣|r|∣r∣ with every extra term. It is the same thing you saw in the cake, where S=1S = 1S=1 and r=12r = \tfrac12r=21​: after nnn cuts the uneaten leftover is (12)n\left(\tfrac12\right)^n(21​)n, exactly the size of the piece you just took.

Because the error after nnn terms is exactly S r nS\,r^{\,n}Srn, logarithms can answer a question no table could: how many terms of a specific series are needed to land within a stated distance of its sum? The Go deeper section works a complete example.

Exactly when does a geometric series converge?

The formula needed ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1. What happens when that fails? The series has no sum, but it is worth seeing how the partial sums fail, because they fail in two genuinely different ways.

Case r=1r = 1r=1. Every term equals aaa, so Sn=naS_n = naSn​=na. (The partial-sum formula is not even available here: it divides by 1−r=01 - r = 01−r=0.) If a≠0a \neq 0a=0, the sizes ∣Sn∣=n∣a∣|S_n| = n|a|∣Sn​∣=n∣a∣ pass every bound. The partial sums run away.

Case r=−1r = -1r=−1. The terms are a,−a,a,−a,…a, -a, a, -a, \ldotsa,−a,a,−a,…, so the partial sums are a,0,a,0,…a, 0, a, 0, \ldotsa,0,a,0,… . If a≠0a \neq 0a=0 those are two different numbers, and the partial sums hop between them forever. Nothing runs away here; every partial sum is aaa or 000, so they stay in a bounded pen. They simply never settle on one number, and the definition demands one number. Diverging does not mean blowing up. It means not settling, and this is the case that proves the two are different.

Case ∣r∣>1|r| > 1∣r∣>1. Split the partial sum again, Sn=a1−r−c r nS_n = \tfrac{a}{1-r} - c\,r^{\,n}Sn​=1−ra​−crn with c=a1−rc = \tfrac{a}{1-r}c=1−ra​. The first piece is fixed. If a≠0a \neq 0a=0 then c≠0c \neq 0c=0, and by the Powers Fact the sizes ∣r n∣\left|r^{\,n}\right|∣rn∣ pass every bound, so the sizes ∣c r n∣=∣c∣∣r n∣\left|c\,r^{\,n}\right| = |c|\left|r^{\,n}\right|∣crn∣=∣c∣∣rn∣ do too. A fixed number minus something unbounded is unbounded, so, when a≠0a \neq 0a=0, the partial sums run away again.

The degenerate case a=0a = 0a=0. The previous lesson required a1≠0a_1 \neq 0a1​=0 for a geometric sequence, so the all-zeros list 0,0,0,…0, 0, 0, \ldots0,0,0,… was never one of those. But the series a+ar+ar2+⋯a + ar + ar^2 + \cdotsa+ar+ar2+⋯ is just an algebraic expression, defined for any real aaa and rrr, and setting a=0a = 0a=0 is a legal thing to do to that expression even though it produces no genuine geometric sequence underneath. Every term is 0⋅r n−1=00 \cdot r^{\,n-1} = 00⋅rn−1=0, every partial sum is 000, and the partial sums close in on 000 about as convincingly as anything ever has. The all-zeros series converges to 000 no matter what rrr is, even r=10r = 10r=10.

That last case is exactly why you must not repeat the slogan you will hear everywhere, that “a geometric series converges exactly when ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1.” As stated, it is false. The forward half holds: ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 does force convergence, for every aaa. The other half fails, and the all-zeros series with r=10r = 10r=10 is the counterexample: it converges while ∣r∣≥1|r| \geq 1∣r∣≥1. One hypothesis repairs the statement.

Convergence criterion. Let a≠0a \neq 0a=0. The infinite geometric series a+ar+ar2+⋯a + ar + ar^2 + \cdotsa+ar+ar2+⋯ converges if and only if ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, and in that case its sum is a1−r\dfrac{a}{1-r}1−ra​.

Both directions are now in hand. If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, the theorem above builds the sum. If ∣r∣≥1|r| \geq 1∣r∣≥1, one of the three cases above applies (r=1r = 1r=1, r=−1r = -1r=−1, or ∣r∣>1|r| > 1∣r∣>1), and in each of them the partial sums fail to settle, so the series diverges. The hypothesis a≠0a \neq 0a=0 is not decoration: it is the whole difference between a true biconditional and a false one.

One harmless edge deserves a mention. The previous lesson also required r≠0r \neq 0r=0 for a genuine geometric sequence, for the same reason as a1≠0a_1 \neq 0a1​=0: it keeps the ratio an+1/ana_{n+1}/a_nan+1​/an​ defined. Plug r=0r = 0r=0 into the series expression anyway and it reads a+0+0+⋯a + 0 + 0 + \cdotsa+0+0+⋯, whose sum is plainly aaa, and the formula agrees: a1−0=a\tfrac{a}{1-0} = a1−0a​=a. No special case is needed.

Every repeating decimal is a geometric series

Decimal notation is an infinite sum in disguise. Writing 0.4444…0.4444\ldots0.4444… means

410+4100+41000+⋯ ,\frac{4}{10} + \frac{4}{100} + \frac{4}{1000} + \cdots,104​+1004​+10004​+⋯,

which is geometric with a=410a = \tfrac{4}{10}a=104​ and r=110r = \tfrac{1}{10}r=101​. Since ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 the series converges, and the sum is 4/109/10=49\tfrac{4/10}{9/10} = \tfrac499/104/10​=94​. So a repeating decimal is a fraction, always, and now you know why: repetition is exactly what makes the digit blocks form a geometric series.

The famous case is 0.999…0.999\ldots0.999… . By the same reading,

0.999…=910+9100+91000+⋯=9101−110=910910=1.0.999\ldots = \frac{9}{10} + \frac{9}{100} + \frac{9}{1000} + \cdots = \frac{\tfrac{9}{10}}{1 - \tfrac{1}{10}} = \frac{\tfrac{9}{10}}{\tfrac{9}{10}} = 1 .0.999…=109​+1009​+10009​+⋯=1−101​109​​=109​109​​=1.

Every partial sum here, 0.90.90.9, 0.990.990.99, 0.9990.9990.999, is less than 111. Every single one. And the sum is exactly 111, computed directly from the formula above. That can look like a contradiction: how can a sum equal 111 when no partial sum ever does? It is not one, because the sum was never defined to be one of the partial sums. It is defined to be the number they close in on, and the gap between SnS_nSn​ and 111 is exactly (110)n\left(\tfrac{1}{10}\right)^n(101​)n, a gap that shrinks past any positive amount you could name. There is no room left between 0.999…0.999\ldots0.999… and 111: they are two names for one number.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using a1−r\tfrac{a}{1-r}1−ra​ when ∣r∣≥1|r| \geq 1∣r∣≥1. For 1+2+4+8+⋯1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots1+2+4+8+⋯ the formula would return 11−2=−1\tfrac{1}{1-2} = -11−21​=−1, and a sum of positive numbers is not negative. The formula is a statement about convergent series only, so check the ratio before you write it down.
  • Reading “diverges” as “blows up”. The partial sums of 1−1+1−1+⋯1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots1−1+1−1+⋯ are 1,0,1,0,…1, 0, 1, 0, \ldots1,0,1,0,…, which stay bounded and still never settle, so the series diverges. Feeding r=−1r = -1r=−1 into the formula produces 12\tfrac1221​, a number that is the sum of nothing here.
  • Taking the ratio upside down. It is a term divided by the one before it, r=a2a1r = \tfrac{a_2}{a_1}r=a1​a2​​. In 8+2+12+⋯8 + 2 + \tfrac12 + \cdots8+2+21​+⋯ the ratio is 14\tfrac1441​, not 444.
  • The sign slip with a negative ratio. With r=−13r = -\tfrac13r=−31​ the denominator is 1−(−13)=431 - \left(-\tfrac13\right) = \tfrac431−(−31​)=34​, never 23\tfrac2332​. Subtracting a negative adds.
  • Using the wrong first term. In a1−r\tfrac{a}{1-r}1−ra​, the letter aaa means the first term of the series you are actually summing. If a problem starts at ar3ar^3ar3, then ar3ar^3ar3 is the first term for that sum.
  • Confusing a partial sum with the sum. Every partial sum of 0.9+0.09+0.009+⋯0.9 + 0.09 + 0.009 + \cdots0.9+0.09+0.009+⋯ is less than 111, and the sum is exactly 111. There is no last term and no leftover gap.
  • Assuming shrinking terms are always enough. For a geometric series, ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 does force convergence, but do not carry that slogan to other series: 1+12+13+14+⋯1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \cdots1+21​+31​+41​+⋯ has terms shrinking to 000, yet its partial sums pass every bound. Why that happens is a calculus topic; here, just do not assume it.

Key takeaways

  • An infinite sum is defined, not discovered. Form the partial sums SnS_nSn​. If they close in on a single number SSS, the series converges and its sum is defined to be SSS; if they do not, it diverges and has no sum.
  • For a geometric series with r≠1r \neq 1r=1, the exact partial sum Sn=a(1−r n)1−rS_n = \dfrac{a\left(1-r^{\,n}\right)}{1-r}Sn​=1−ra(1−rn)​ splits as a1−r−a1−r r n\dfrac{a}{1-r} - \dfrac{a}{1-r}\,r^{\,n}1−ra​−1−ra​rn, so everything depends on the powers r nr^{\,n}rn.
  • The borrowed fact. If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 the powers r nr^{\,n}rn close in on 000; if ∣r∣>1|r| > 1∣r∣>1 their sizes pass every bound. Calculus supplies the proof.
  • The sum. If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, then a+ar+ar2+⋯=a1−ra + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \dfrac{a}{1-r}a+ar+ar2+⋯=1−ra​, and stopping after nnn terms leaves an error of exactly S r nS\,r^{\,n}Srn.
  • The criterion, stated correctly. For a≠0a \neq 0a=0, the series converges if and only if ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1. The hypothesis a≠0a \neq 0a=0 is load-bearing: the all-zeros series converges for every rrr.
  • Divergence comes in two forms, running away (r=1r = 1r=1 or ∣r∣>1|r| > 1∣r∣>1) and never settling (r=−1r = -1r=−1).
  • Every repeating decimal is a geometric series, which is why every repeating decimal equals a fraction, and why 0.999…=10.999\ldots = 10.999…=1 exactly.

Why a fixed number times a vanishing power still vanishes

The lesson states the result: once you grant the Powers Fact, the second piece of the split partial sum closes in on 000. Here is that step made precise.

If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, then a+ar+ar2+⋯=a1−ra + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \dfrac{a}{1-r}a+ar+ar2+⋯=1−ra​#

If a=0a = 0a=0 every term is 000, every partial sum is 000, and the formula returns 01−r=0\tfrac{0}{1-r} = 01−r0​=0 as well, so that case is settled. Take a≠0a \neq 0a=0 from here on.

Because ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 we certainly have r≠1r \neq 1r=1, so the partial-sum formula applies to every nnn, and splitting it as above gives

Sn=a1−r−c r n,where c=a1−r≠0.S_n = \frac{a}{1-r} - c\,r^{\,n}, \qquad \text{where } c = \frac{a}{1-r} \neq 0 .Sn​=1−ra​−crn,where c=1−ra​=0.

The first piece never changes with nnn. The second piece is the fixed number ccc times r nr^{\,n}rn, and by the Powers Fact r nr^{\,n}rn closes in on 000. Multiplying by ccc does not spoil that. To force ∣c r n∣\left|c\,r^{\,n}\right|∣crn∣ below any tolerance you name, force ∣r n∣\left|r^{\,n}\right|∣rn∣ below that tolerance divided by ∣c∣|c|∣c∣, which the Powers Fact says you can do. So the second piece closes in on 000 too, and the partial sums close in on a1−r−0\tfrac{a}{1-r} - 01−ra​−0.

By the definition of an infinite sum, that settles it. The series converges, and its sum is a1−r\tfrac{a}{1-r}1−ra​.

How many terms are enough to land within a target distance of the sum

Because the error after nnn terms is exactly S r nS\,r^{\,n}Srn, you can answer a question no table could: how many terms are enough? Logarithms turn the demand into an inequality about nnn, and you solve it.

Worked example How many terms of 5+4+3.2+⋯5 + 4 + 3.2 + \cdots5+4+3.2+⋯ land within 0.010.010.01 of the sum?

The ratio is r=45=0.8r = \tfrac{4}{5} = 0.8r=54​=0.8 and the first term is a=5a = 5a=5, so the series converges and

S=51−0.8=50.2=25.S = \frac{5}{1 - 0.8} = \frac{5}{0.2} = 25 .S=1−0.85​=0.25​=25.

25(0.8)n<0.01⟺(0.8)n<0.0004.25(0.8)^n < 0.01 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad (0.8)^n < 0.0004 .25(0.8)n<0.01⟺(0.8)n<0.0004.

A bit of history (optional)

Zeno of Elea, a Greek thinker of the fifth century BCE, maintained that you cannot walk across a room. First you must arrive at the halfway point. From there you must arrive at half of what remains, and after that at half of the remainder. The list of errands never finishes, so a journey with no final step can never be completed. Motion, he concluded, is an illusion.

The distances Zeno listed are precisely this lesson’s cake, 12+14+18+⋯\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots21​+41​+81​+⋯. The modern reply is not that his list secretly stops, because it does not. The reply is that adding infinitely many numbers is not an act carried out one step after another. It is a definition about partial sums, and under that definition the distances total exactly one room.

Two thousand years later the definition still had not been written, and one series made the cost obvious. In 1703 Guido Grandi, an Italian priest and mathematician, asked what 1−1+1−1+⋯1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots1−1+1−1+⋯ amounts to. Bracket the terms one way and the answer is 000. Bracket them the other way and it is 111. Substitute r=−1r = -1r=−1 into the geometric formula and it is 12\tfrac1221​. All three answers had defenders, and the quarrel continued for the better part of a century. Nobody involved had made an arithmetic mistake; the flaw was assuming an endless sum could be regrouped like a finite one.

None of the three answers is actually the series’ sum. A series has a sum only when its partial sums settle on one number, and Grandi’s hop between 111 and 000 forever. That is the second kind of failure you met above, the one that never settles. It is why the definition had to arrive before any formula.

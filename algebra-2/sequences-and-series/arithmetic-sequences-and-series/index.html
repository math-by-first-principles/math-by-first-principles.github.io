Arithmetic Sequences and Series

Intuition

Count by sevens and you get 7,14,21,287, 14, 21, 287,14,21,28. Add three each time and you get 5,8,11,145, 8, 11, 145,8,11,14. Build a theater where each row holds two more seats than the row in front of it and you get 18,20,22,2418, 20, 22, 2418,20,22,24. The numbers are different, but the rule behind all three is the same one: from each term to the next you take the same fixed step. Sequences built this way are called arithmetic. Almost every question anyone asks about such a sequence is one of two: what is the hundredth term, and what do the first hundred terms add up to? Both have short answers, and neither one requires you to write out a hundred numbers.

Learning goals

  • Test for a constant difference ddd throughout
  • Apply an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d, counting steps not terms
  • Recover ddd from two terms by dividing rise by steps
  • Count terms with n=L−a1d+1n = \tfrac{L - a_1}{d} + 1n=dL−a1​​+1
  • Prove Sn=n2(a1+an)S_n = \tfrac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)Sn​=2n​(a1​+an​) by reversing and adding
  • Read a quadratic SnS_nSn​ backwards to recover ddd

What makes a sequence arithmetic

A sequence is a function whose domain is a run of consecutive integers, so ana_nan​ is the output at input nnn. That function can do anything at all. An arithmetic sequence is one that does the simplest possible thing: it adds the same number every time you advance the input by one.

A sequence a1,a2,a3,…a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldotsa1​,a2​,a3​,… is arithmetic when there is a fixed number ddd, the common difference, with

an+1−an=dfor every n≥1,a_{n+1} - a_n = d \qquad \text{for every } n \ge 1,an+1​−an​=dfor every n≥1,

which is the same recurrence as an+1=an+da_{n+1} = a_n + dan+1​=an​+d; paired with a seed a1a_1a1​, that recurrence becomes a complete recursive rule for the sequence. The word common is doing real work here: the difference must be the same for every pair of neighbours, not just the first pair you happen to check.

Three quick examples and two non-examples make the definition concrete:

  • 4,9,14,19,…4, 9, 14, 19, \ldots4,9,14,19,… is arithmetic with d=5d = 5d=5 (each term is five more than the one before).
  • 20,13,6,−1,…20, 13, 6, -1, \ldots20,13,6,−1,… is arithmetic with d=−7d = -7d=−7. A negative ddd is perfectly legal; the sequence simply runs downhill.
  • 3,3,3,3,…3, 3, 3, 3, \ldots3,3,3,3,… is arithmetic with d=0d = 0d=0. It is a dull sequence, but it satisfies the definition, and the formulas below for the nnnth term and for the sum will handle it without complaint. (One later formula, the one that counts terms, will need d≠0d \neq 0d=0, and the reason is worth seeing when you get there.)
  • 1,2,4,8,…1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots1,2,4,8,… is not arithmetic. Its differences are 111, then 222, then 444, and they never settle down.
  • 1,4,9,16,…1, 4, 9, 16, \ldots1,4,9,16,… (the squares) is not arithmetic either. Its differences are 333, then 555, then 777. They change by a constant amount, which is interesting, but they are not constant.

To test a sequence, subtract each term from the one after it and compare all the results. Two matching differences prove nothing: 2,5,8,132, 5, 8, 132,5,8,13 starts with 333 and 333 and then jumps by 555.

The nth term, by counting steps

You know a1a_1a1​ and you know ddd. How do you reach a50a_{50}a50​ without writing down forty-nine additions?

Every move to the next term costs one ddd. So the question is not “how many terms are there” but “how many steps do I take”, and those are two different counts.

Five terms sit on that line, and four arrows join them. The terms are the places; ddd is the toll you pay to move between places. Nobody pays a toll to arrive at the place they are already standing on, so the first term costs nothing. Because that first place is free, the count of tolls is one less than the count of places.

The nth term is an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d#

Take any n≥2n \ge 2n≥2 (the case n=1n = 1n=1 just says a1=a1a_1 = a_1a1​=a1​ and needs no argument).

The definition hands you one equation per step. Stepping from term 111 to term 222 gives a2−a1=da_2 - a_1 = da2​−a1​=d, stepping from term 222 to term 333 gives a3−a2=da_3 - a_2 = da3​−a2​=d, and so on. The steps run from 111 to 222, from 222 to 333, all the way up to the step from n−1n-1n−1 to nnn. So there are exactly n−1n - 1n−1 of them, one for each starting index between 111 and n−1n-1n−1.

Add all n−1n - 1n−1 of those equations together. Every right side is ddd, so the right sides total (n−1)d(n-1)d(n−1)d:

∑k=1n−1(ak+1−ak)=∑k=1n−1d=(n−1)d.\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \big(a_{k+1} - a_k\big) = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} d = (n-1)d.k=1∑n−1​(ak+1​−ak​)=k=1∑n−1​d=(n−1)d.

The left side collapses. Written out, it is (a2−a1)+(a3−a2)+(a4−a3)+⋯+(an−an−1)(a_2 - a_1) + (a_3 - a_2) + (a_4 - a_3) + \cdots + (a_n - a_{n-1})(a2​−a1​)+(a3​−a2​)+(a4​−a3​)+⋯+(an​−an−1​), and every interior term appears twice, once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign. Those pairs cancel out and only the two ends survive, so the left side is an−a1a_n - a_1an​−a1​. Therefore

an−a1=(n−1)d,soan=a1+(n−1)d.a_n - a_1 = (n-1)d, \qquad \text{so} \qquad a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d.an​−a1​=(n−1)d,soan​=a1​+(n−1)d.

The whole content of the formula is that count n−1n - 1n−1. It is the number of steps, and the number of steps between nnn terms is always one fewer than nnn. Fence posts show the same pattern: five posts have four gaps between them.

∎

Two rearrangements of that formula earn their keep. Solving for ddd gives

d=an−a1n−1,d = \frac{a_n - a_1}{n - 1},d=n−1an​−a1​​,

and the same argument run between any two terms (there are m−km - km−k steps from term kkk to term mmm) gives the more flexible version

am=ak+(m−k)d,sod=am−akm−k.a_m = a_k + (m - k)d, \qquad \text{so} \qquad d = \frac{a_m - a_k}{m - k}.am​=ak​+(m−k)d,sod=m−kam​−ak​​.

Both denominators count steps, never terms. From a3a_3a3​ to a11a_{11}a11​ there are 888 steps, so you divide by 888, not by 111111 and not by 999.

The fence-post count is the kind of thing that stays slippery until you have done it with your hands, so the figure below hands you one step at a time. You set the number the step starts from and the number added to it, and the arrow shows that single step and where it lands.

Set the start to −4-4−4 and the number added to 222. The arrow lands on −2-2−2, so a sequence with a1=−4a_1 = -4a1​=−4 and d=2d = 2d=2 has a2=−2a_2 = -2a2​=−2. Move the start to −2-2−2, where you just landed, and take the same step again to reach 000, which is a3a_3a3​. Carry on from 000 and then from 222. You end on 444, and 444 is a5a_5a5​. Now count what you did: five terms named, four steps taken, which is the fence-post rule above with the posts in your hands. That gap of one is the whole of n−1n - 1n−1, and it is not a convention anyone chose. The first term is where you begin, so it costs nothing, and only the terms after it have to be walked to. Set the number added to −2-2−2 and run the route backwards from 444 to confirm it from the other side. You take four steps again, and land back on −4-4−4, because a negative common difference changes the direction of the walk and nothing at all about the counting.

An arithmetic sequence is a linear function of n

Expand the formula and something familiar appears:

an=a1+(n−1)d=dn+(a1−d).a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d = dn + (a_1 - d).an​=a1​+(n−1)d=dn+(a1​−d).

The right side is dn+bdn + bdn+b with b=a1−db = a_1 - db=a1​−d, so ana_nan​ is a linear function of nnn with slope ddd. The converse is true as well. If a sequence obeys an=mn+ba_n = mn + ban​=mn+b for every nnn, then

an+1−an=(m(n+1)+b)−(mn+b)=m,a_{n+1} - a_n = \big(m(n+1) + b\big) - \big(mn + b\big) = m,an+1​−an​=(m(n+1)+b)−(mn+b)=m,

a constant, so the sequence is arithmetic with d=md = md=m. Both directions hold, so “arithmetic” and “linear in nnn” are two names for the same family of sequences. That is a genuinely useful equivalence: whenever you meet a rule like ak=4k+1a_k = 4k + 1ak​=4k+1, you may read off d=4d = 4d=4 on sight, with no subtraction at all.

It also tells you what these sequences look like. Plot the points (n,an)(n, a_n)(n,an​) and they land on a straight line of slope ddd, evenly spaced, one point per positive integer. The intercept a1−da_1 - da1​−d is the “zeroth term”, the value the line would take at n=0n = 0n=0, a term the sequence itself never has.

How many terms are there

Turn the formula around once more. If a sequence starts at a1a_1a1​, steps by ddd, and its last term is LLL, then L=a1+(n−1)dL = a_1 + (n-1)dL=a1​+(n−1)d, so

n=L−a1d+1.n = \frac{L - a_1}{d} + 1.n=dL−a1​​+1.

The division tells you how many steps separate the first term from the last, and then the +1+1+1 converts steps back into terms. It is the fence-post count again, run backwards, and the missing +1+1+1 is one of the most common errors in this entire topic.

That formula also assumes LLL really is a term, not just some number you are curious about. Take 6,10,14,…6, 10, 14, \ldots6,10,14,… and ask how many terms run up to 313131. The division gives (31−6)÷4=6.25(31 - 6) \div 4 = 6.25(31−6)÷4=6.25, and a fractional step count is the formula telling you 313131 is never landed on; it falls between two terms. A question like “how many terms up to the first one past 313131” is not asking for that division at all. It is asking where the sequence first clears 313131, so solve the inequality instead: 6+4(n−1)>316 + 4(n - 1) > 316+4(n−1)>31 gives n>7.25n > 7.25n>7.25, so the first term past 313131 is the eighth one, a8=34a_8 = 34a8​=34.

This is the one formula in the lesson that demands d≠0d \neq 0d=0, since it divides by ddd. The restriction is not a flaw in the formula but a fact about the question: if d=0d = 0d=0 then every term equals LLL. In that case 3,3,33, 3, 33,3,3 and 3,3,3,3,33, 3, 3, 3, 33,3,3,3,3 look identical from their values alone, so no rule could recover the count from them. The nnnth-term and sum formulas never divide by ddd, so they stay valid even in that constant case.

Worked example 1 Find the 30th term of 4,11,18,25,…4, 11, 18, 25, \ldots4,11,18,25,…

The differences are 11−4=711 - 4 = 711−4=7, 18−11=718 - 11 = 718−11=7, and 25−18=725 - 18 = 725−18=7, so the sequence is arithmetic with a1=4a_1 = 4a1​=4 and d=7d = 7d=7.

Getting from the 1st term to the 30th takes 292929 steps, not 303030:

a30=a1+(30−1)d=4+29⋅7=4+203=207.a_{30} = a_1 + (30 - 1)d = 4 + 29 \cdot 7 = 4 + 203 = 207.a30​=a1​+(30−1)d=4+29⋅7=4+203=207.

Had you multiplied by 303030 instead, you would have got 4+210=2144 + 210 = 2144+210=214, which is the 31st term. The formula never says “multiply ddd by the term number”; it says “multiply ddd by the number of steps”.

Worked example 2 An arithmetic sequence has a6=23a_6 = 23a6​=23 and a14=55a_{14} = 55a14​=55. Find a1a_1a1​ and a formula for ana_nan​.

From term 666 to term 141414 is 14−6=814 - 6 = 814−6=8 steps, and those steps carry the value from 232323 up to 555555, a rise of 323232. Each step is worth the same amount, so

d=a14−a614−6=55−238=328=4.d = \frac{a_{14} - a_6}{14 - 6} = \frac{55 - 23}{8} = \frac{32}{8} = 4.d=14−6a14​−a6​​=855−23​=832​=4.

Now walk backwards from a6a_6a6​ to a1a_1a1​, which is 555 steps down:

a1=a6−5d=23−5⋅4=3.a_1 = a_6 - 5d = 23 - 5 \cdot 4 = 3.a1​=a6​−5d=23−5⋅4=3.

That gives the explicit rule, which you can simplify:

an=3+(n−1)4=4n−1.a_n = 3 + (n-1)4 = 4n - 1.an​=3+(n−1)4=4n−1.

Check it against the data you were given: a6=24−1=23a_6 = 24 - 1 = 23a6​=24−1=23 and a14=56−1=55a_{14} = 56 - 1 = 55a14​=56−1=55. Both match. Notice the check is worth doing, because dividing by 999 (the number of terms from the 6th to the 14th) instead of 888 would have produced d=329d = \tfrac{32}{9}d=932​. Starting from a6=23a_6 = 23a6​=23 and taking eight steps of 329\tfrac{32}{9}932​ does not land back on 555555, so that value of ddd fails the data you were actually given. A fraction is not what gives a wrong ddd away (an arithmetic sequence is allowed to have a fractional common difference); landing on the wrong term is.

Worked example 3 How many terms are in 8,15,22,…,1488, 15, 22, \ldots, 1488,15,22,…,148?

The common difference is d=7d = 7d=7, and the last term is L=148L = 148L=148.

First count the steps from the first term to the last. Each step adds 777, and the total climb is 148−8=140148 - 8 = 140148−8=140, so

L−a1d=148−87=1407=20 steps.\frac{L - a_1}{d} = \frac{148 - 8}{7} = \frac{140}{7} = 20 \text{ steps}.dL−a1​​=7148−8​=7140​=20 steps.

Twenty steps land you on the last term, but you were standing on a term before you took any step at all. So the number of terms is

n=20+1=21.n = 20 + 1 = 21.n=20+1=21.

Adding the terms, by writing the sum twice

A series is what you get when you add up the terms of a sequence. Write SnS_nSn​ for the sum of the first nnn terms, which in the sigma notation from the last lesson is

Sn=∑k=1nak=a1+a2+⋯+an.S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n.Sn​=k=1∑n​ak​=a1​+a2​+⋯+an​.

Adding a hundred terms one at a time is hopeless, and the trick that rescues you is one of the most famous in mathematics. Write the sum out forwards. Then, underneath it, write the very same sum backwards:

Sn=a1+(a1+d)+(a1+2d)+⋯+an,Sn=an+(an−d)+(an−2d)+⋯+a1.\begin{aligned} S_n &= a_1 + (a_1 + d) + (a_1 + 2d) + \cdots + a_n, \\ S_n &= a_n + (a_n - d) + (a_n - 2d) + \cdots + a_1. \end{aligned}Sn​Sn​​=a1​+(a1​+d)+(a1​+2d)+⋯+an​,=an​+(an​−d)+(an​−2d)+⋯+a1​.​

Now add the two lines column by column. The first column gives a1+ana_1 + a_na1​+an​. The second gives (a1+d)+(an−d)(a_1 + d) + (a_n - d)(a1​+d)+(an​−d), and the ddd‘s cancel, so it is a1+ana_1 + a_na1​+an​ again. The third gives (a1+2d)+(an−2d)=a1+an(a_1 + 2d) + (a_n - 2d) = a_1 + a_n(a1​+2d)+(an​−2d)=a1​+an​. Every column produces the same total, and the reason is easiest to see by walking along the two lines. As you walk right along the top line you gain ddd at each step, while the bottom line loses ddd at each step, and the two motions cancel exactly.

The sum of the first n terms is Sn=n2(a1+an)S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)Sn​=2n​(a1​+an​)#

Let the sequence be arithmetic with common difference ddd. The kkkth column of the layout above pairs the kkkth term counted from the front with the kkkth term counted from the back. In symbols, that column pairs aka_kak​ with an+1−ka_{n+1-k}an+1−k​, since counting back k−1k - 1k−1 steps from ana_nan​ lands on index n+1−kn+1-kn+1−k. Use the formula for the nnnth term on both:

ak+an+1−k=(a1+(k−1)d)+(a1+(n−k)d)=2a1+(n−1)d.a_k + a_{n+1-k} = \big(a_1 + (k-1)d\big) + \big(a_1 + (n-k)d\big) = 2a_1 + (n-1)d.ak​+an+1−k​=(a1​+(k−1)d)+(a1​+(n−k)d)=2a1​+(n−1)d.

The kkk has vanished, because (k−1)+(n−k)=n−1(k-1) + (n-k) = n-1(k−1)+(n−k)=n−1 no matter what kkk is. So every column has the same total, and since an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d, that common total is exactly

2a1+(n−1)d=a1+an.2a_1 + (n-1)d = a_1 + a_n.2a1​+(n−1)d=a1​+an​.

There are nnn columns, one for each term, so adding the two copies of the sum gives

2Sn=n(a1+an),henceSn=n2 (a1+an).2S_n = n(a_1 + a_n), \qquad \text{hence} \qquad S_n = \frac{n}{2}\,(a_1 + a_n).2Sn​=n(a1​+an​),henceSn​=2n​(a1​+an​).

The doubling is what makes the argument airtight. Suppose a student instead tries to fold the sum in half and pair the terms off physically, first with last, second with second-last. That student immediately has to ask whether nnn is even (so the pairs come out whole) or odd (so one lonely middle term is stranded). Writing a second, reversed copy of the whole sum never asks that question: every one of the nnn terms gets a partner, whether nnn is 100100100 or 101101101. When nnn is odd the middle term is simply paired with itself, and that is not a special case at all. That is because am+am=a1+ana_m + a_m = a_1 + a_nam​+am​=a1​+an​ holds for the middle index mmm, just as the column identity above says it must. No case split, no leftovers.

∎

The same formula, wearing different clothes

The formula Sn=n2(a1+an)S_n = \tfrac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)Sn​=2n​(a1​+an​) wants the last term. Often you know ddd instead, and substituting an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d turns it into

Sn=n2(a1+a1+(n−1)d)=n2(2a1+(n−1)d).S_n = \frac{n}{2}\Big(a_1 + a_1 + (n-1)d\Big) = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a_1 + (n-1)d\big).Sn​=2n​(a1​+a1​+(n−1)d)=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d).

These are not two formulas to memorize. They are one statement written with different inputs: use the first when the last term is handed to you, the second when only a1a_1a1​ and ddd are. Both come from the single fact 2Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)2Sn​=n(a1​+an​).

Read n2(a1+an)\tfrac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)2n​(a1​+an​) once more as n⋅a1+an2n \cdot \tfrac{a_1 + a_n}{2}n⋅2a1​+an​​, and the sum tells you something you can feel. An arithmetic series is just its number of terms times the average of its first and last term. The terms are evenly spread, so the small ones at one end compensate exactly for the large ones at the other. When nnn is odd the middle term is that average, since the middle index is m=n+12m = \tfrac{n+1}{2}m=2n+1​ and

am=a1+n−12 d=a1+an2,a_m = a_1 + \frac{n-1}{2}\,d = \frac{a_1 + a_n}{2},am​=a1​+2n−1​d=2a1​+an​​,

so a sum of 999 terms is 999 times its 5th term, and a sum of 111111 terms is 111111 times its 6th. That shortcut works only because the sequence is arithmetic. It is not a fact about sums in general.

One last observation, which the quadratics chapter has already prepared you for. Expanding the second form gives

Sn=d2n2+(a1−d2)n,S_n = \frac{d}{2}n^2 + \Big(a_1 - \frac{d}{2}\Big)n,Sn​=2d​n2+(a1​−2d​)n,

a quadratic in nnn with no constant term.

The converse holds too, and it costs one line, so do not take it on trust. Suppose all you are told is that some sequence has running total Sn=An2+BnS_n = An^2 + BnSn​=An2+Bn for every nnn. Recover its nnnth term as the jump the total makes when that term arrives:

an=Sn−Sn−1=(An2+Bn)−(A(n−1)2+B(n−1))=2An+(B−A).a_n = S_n - S_{n-1} = \big(An^2 + Bn\big) - \big(A(n-1)^2 + B(n-1)\big) = 2An + (B - A).an​=Sn​−Sn−1​=(An2+Bn)−(A(n−1)2+B(n−1))=2An+(B−A).

That is linear in nnn, and a sequence linear in nnn is arithmetic, by the equivalence proved earlier. So the sequence is arithmetic with d=2Ad = 2Ad=2A, whatever AAA and BBB are. If a problem hands you Sn=2n2+3nS_n = 2n^2 + 3nSn​=2n2+3n, you may read off d=4d = 4d=4 and an=4n+1a_n = 4n + 1an​=4n+1 without ever seeing a single term of the sequence.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying ddd by nnn instead of by n−1n - 1n−1. This is the single most common error in the topic. Getting from a1a_1a1​ to ana_nan​ takes n−1n - 1n−1 steps, not nnn, so a10=a1+9da_{10} = a_1 + 9da10​=a1​+9d. Writing a1+10da_1 + 10da1​+10d silently produces the eleventh term.
  • Forgetting the +1+1+1 when counting terms. The division (L−a1)÷d(L - a_1) \div d(L−a1​)÷d counts steps. The number of terms is one more. From 333 to 434343 by fours is 101010 steps but 111111 terms.
  • Dividing by the wrong count when recovering ddd from two terms. Between a4a_4a4​ and a9a_9a9​ there are 9−4=59 - 4 = 59−4=5 steps, so divide the rise by 555, never by 999 and never by 666.
  • Testing only one difference. A sequence is arithmetic only if every consecutive difference agrees. In 2,5,8,132, 5, 8, 132,5,8,13 the first two differences match and the sequence still is not arithmetic.
  • Dropping the half in the sum. The doubling produces 2Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)2Sn​=n(a1​+an​), so n(a1+an)n(a_1 + a_n)n(a1​+an​) is twice the answer. The n2\tfrac{n}{2}2n​ is the whole point of the trick.
  • Using the sum formula on a sequence that is not arithmetic. For 1,2,4,81, 2, 4, 81,2,4,8 the formula would claim 42(1+8)=18\tfrac{4}{2}(1 + 8) = 1824​(1+8)=18, while the true sum is 151515. The columns are only constant when the differences are.
  • Blending the two forms. n2(a1+an)\tfrac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)2n​(a1​+an​) needs the last term; n2(2a1+(n−1)d)\tfrac{n}{2}(2a_1 + (n-1)d)2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d) needs the common difference. Something like n2(a1+d)\tfrac{n}{2}(a_1 + d)2n​(a1​+d) is neither of them.
  • Losing the sign of a negative ddd. In 20,17,14,…20, 17, 14, \ldots20,17,14,… the common difference is −3-3−3, so a12=20+11(−3)=−13a_{12} = 20 + 11(-3) = -13a12​=20+11(−3)=−13. Subtracting ddd “because the sequence goes down” double-counts the sign.

Key takeaways

  • A sequence is arithmetic when an+1−an=da_{n+1} - a_n = dan+1​−an​=d for every nnn, with the same ddd throughout. A negative ddd runs the sequence downhill; d=0d = 0d=0 makes it constant.
  • The nth term: an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d. The n−1n - 1n−1 counts the steps from the first term to the nnnth, and a step is what costs you a ddd.
  • Between any two terms, am=ak+(m−k)da_m = a_k + (m-k)dam​=ak​+(m−k)d, so d=am−akm−kd = \dfrac{a_m - a_k}{m - k}d=m−kam​−ak​​: divide the rise by the number of steps.
  • Counting terms: n=L−a1d+1n = \dfrac{L - a_1}{d} + 1n=dL−a1​​+1. The division gives steps, and the +1+1+1 turns steps back into terms. This is the one formula that needs d≠0d \neq 0d=0, because it divides by ddd.
  • The sum (arithmetic series): write it forwards and backwards and add columnwise. Every column totals a1+ana_1 + a_na1​+an​ and there are nnn of them, so 2Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)2Sn​=n(a1​+an​) and Sn=n2(a1+an)=n2(2a1+(n−1)d)S_n = \dfrac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n) = \dfrac{n}{2}\big(2a_1 + (n-1)d\big)Sn​=2n​(a1​+an​)=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d).
  • The doubling works for odd and even nnn alike, because a reversed copy gives every term a partner, including the middle one, which partners itself.
  • A sum is the number of terms times the average of the first and last term. The terms are linear in nnn; their running total SnS_nSn​ is quadratic in nnn with no constant term. That runs both ways: a sequence with Sn=An2+BnS_n = An^2 + BnSn​=An2+Bn has an=2An+(B−A)a_n = 2An + (B - A)an​=2An+(B−A), so it is arithmetic with d=2Ad = 2Ad=2A.

A bit of history (optional)

Adding a run of evenly spaced numbers is the easy direction. Reversing it is not. Suppose you are handed the total, the first term and the step, and asked how many terms produced that total. The question is much harder, and its answer is fifteen centuries old.

It sits in the Aryabhatiya, a compact book of astronomy composed in verse in the year 499. Its author was Aryabhata, an astronomer working in India. One of his rules adds a progression, which was well known even then. The rule directly after it runs the other way, getting the number of terms back from the total, and that one is a real find.

Consider what such a rule must contain. A total that climbs by a fixed step is quadratic in the number of terms. That is exactly the shape you derived for SnS_nSn​ above. So getting the count back means solving a quadratic. Sure enough, the rule carries a square root inside it, stated in verse, centuries before anyone wrote a quadratic formula down.

The forward direction is older still, and it began as a picture. The Pythagoreans, a school of scholars in ancient Greece, arranged pebbles into triangles and studied 1+2+3+⋯1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots1+2+3+⋯ as a shape rather than a sum.

Both directions belong to the single identity you proved by writing the series above its own reversal.

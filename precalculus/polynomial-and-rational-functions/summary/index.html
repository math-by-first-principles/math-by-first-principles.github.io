Polynomial and Rational Functions: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Power function
One rule f(x)=axrf(x)=ax^rf(x)=axr, with fixed nonzero real aaa and fixed exponent rrr. Integer and rational exponents can give different real domains. For r=0r=0r=0, use the constant rule f(x)=af(x)=af(x)=a on R\mathbb RR, without evaluating 000^000.
Polynomial function
A finite sum of nonnegative integer powers with real coefficients, naturally defined on R\mathbb RR. Irrational coefficients are allowed; fractional or negative variable exponents are not.
Degree and leading term
After combining like terms, the largest surviving exponent is the degree; its coefficient is the leading coefficient. A nonzero constant has degree 000. The zero polynomial has no degree and vanishes everywhere.
Zero and multiplicity
A zero rrr satisfies P(r)=0P(r)=0P(r)=0. Its multiplicity counts all copies of x−rx-rx−r in the factorization. A real zero gives the real-graph intercept (r,0)(r,0)(r,0); repeated copies do not create distinct intercepts.
Turning point
A point where rising changes to falling or falling changes to rising. A local maximum or minimum may occur away from the axis.
First and second differences
First differences subtract consecutive outputs; second differences subtract consecutive first differences. Ordinary degree-pattern comparisons require equal input steps.
Concavity and inflection
Concave up means increasing secant slopes through successive intervals from left to right; concave down means decreasing slopes. An inflection point changes concavity. A straight line has neither strict bending pattern.
Complex number and complex plane
a+bia+bia+bi, with real a,ba,ba,b and i2=−1i^2=-1i2=−1, is represented by (a,b)(a,b)(a,b). The vertical coordinate is an imaginary part, not a polynomial output. Nonreal means b≠0b\ne0b=0.
Complex conjugate
The conjugate of a+bia+bia+bi is a−bia-bia−bi.
Residual
The output remaining after a proposed zero is substituted into the original polynomial.
Rational function
A quotient P/DP/DP/D of polynomials with DDD not the zero polynomial. Its natural real domain excludes every real zero of the original denominator; a constructed function may inherit further restrictions.
Hole and pole
A hole is a missing point with a finite reduced height. A pole is an excluded input with unbounded nearby outputs; its input gives a vertical asymptote.
Horizontal and slant asymptotes
Lines whose vertical gap from the graph becomes and stays smaller than any chosen positive distance sufficiently far away. A slant line has nonzero slope. The convention reserves asymptotes for approach, excluding a graph coinciding with that line throughout its domain.

  • Even and odd symmetry

    Even: f(−x)=f(x)Odd: f(−x)=−f(x)\begin{gathered} \text{Even: }f(-x)=f(x)\\ \text{Odd: }f(-x)=-f(x) \end{gathered}Even: f(−x)=f(x)Odd: f(−x)=−f(x)​

    Use when Opposite inputs must both belong to the domain, and the identity must hold everywhere there. For polynomials on R\mathbb RR, only even powers give even symmetry; only odd powers give odd symmetry. Nonzero constants are even; zero is both.

  • Polynomial end behavior and turning bound

    Even degree: the two ends agree. Odd degree: the ends oppose. A positive leading coefficient makes the far right rise without bound; a negative one makes it fall. At most n−1n-1n−1 turning points occur.

    Use when A real polynomial of degree n≥1n\ge1n≥1. Use the highest surviving term, regardless of coefficient size. Constants stay at fixed heights. The turning bound is a ceiling, and matching ends do not establish symmetry.

  • Quadratic zeros over the complex numbers

  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

    Every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex zero. A degree-nnn polynomial has exactly nnn complex zeros counted with multiplicity.

    Use when n≥1n\ge1n≥1 and the leading coefficient is nonzero. Repeated copies count separately; real zeros are included. Exact verified factors and a complete degree count can certify that no zero was missed.

    Use when Conjugate pairing requires all polynomial coefficients real, real a,ba,ba,b, and b≠0b\ne0b=0. The two zeros have equal multiplicities, so the total nonreal count with multiplicity is even. Only real zeros give intercepts of the real function graph.

  • Rational graph features

    Record original exclusions, then cancel completely. At an excluded input, a nonzero reduced denominator gives a hole; a zero reduced denominator gives a pole. Allowed reduced numerator zeros give x-intercepts.

Problem types, step by step

Classify a rule and describe change

  1. Reduce rational exponents and find the real domain; combine polynomial terms before reading degree and leading coefficient.
  2. Test opposite inputs for symmetry, then use the leading term separately for far ends.
  3. Calculate endpoint rates or equal-step differences. Distinguish slope sign, concavity, and a genuine concavity change.

Sketch or compare polynomial graphs

  1. Find all real zeros and exact multiplicities; combine factors when roots merge.
  2. Test interval signs, calculate the y-intercept, and reconcile contacts with end behavior and the turning bound.
  3. Apply parameter changes to their actual factors or outputs. Nonzero scaling preserves multiplicities; adding a constant can move zeros. Do not invent exact extrema.

Reconstruct a polynomial from features

  1. Translate contacts into multiplicity parities and use the exact degree to test possible factor lists.
  2. Use a leading coefficient or value away from every zero to determine the multiplier; solve remaining location conditions.
  3. Check every supplied feature and decide whether any undetermined choices remain.

e.g. Construct a cubic with double zero −8-8−8, simple zero 666, and P(0)=−768P(0)=-768P(0)=−768: P(x)=2(x+8)2(x−6)P(x)=2(x+8)^2(x-6)P(x)=2(x+8)2(x−6).

Divide and verify polynomials

  1. Order powers and insert missing zero coefficients. Choose long division or a suitable adjusted synthetic calculation.
  2. In long division, divide leading terms, multiply by the whole divisor, and subtract the whole product. In synthetic division, bring down, multiply by the corner, and add.
  3. Stop at the remainder degree condition; expand DQ+RDQ+RDQ+R to check every coefficient. Retain exclusions in a fractional answer.

e.g. Divide 2x3+7x2+4x+92x^3+7x^2+4x+92x3+7x2+4x+9 by x+3x+3x+3: Q=2x2+x+1Q=2x^2+x+1Q=2x2+x+1, R=6R=6R=6.

Recover coefficients from remainders

  1. Translate each linear-divisor remainder into a value at that divisor's zero.
  2. Substitute the unknown-coefficient rule and solve the resulting independent equations.
  3. Check every original evaluation; a factor condition requires remainder zero.

Find every exact polynomial zero

  1. Remove common powers of xxx, then generate and exactly test rational candidates or use a supplied verified zero.
  2. Divide out verified factors, retest for repetition, and solve the remaining factors, using complex arithmetic when needed.
  3. Include forced conjugates, state multiplicities, and account for the degree. Identify the real intercepts separately.

e.g. Find all zeros of (5x−8)(x2+6x+17)(5x-8)(x^2+6x+17)(5x−8)(x2+6x+17): 8/58/58/5 and −3±2i2-3\pm2i\sqrt2−3±2i2​, all simple.

Report a numerical zero

  1. Choose a window containing the feature and inspect both sides; evaluate the original polynomial at proposed bracket endpoints.
  2. Reliable opposite signs bracket at least one real zero. Check that the entire bracket supports the requested rounding.
  3. Use ≈\approx≈, calculate the residual, and supply a separate argument for uniqueness or completeness if claimed.

Analyze or reconstruct a rational graph

  1. Retain every original or inherited exclusion. Cancel completely and locate holes, poles, and allowed intercepts.
  2. Use signs immediately on each side of a pole to determine branch directions.
  3. Compare degrees or divide for far-away behavior. Solve the difference from an asymptote for possible meetings, retain allowed inputs, and test signs for crossings.
  4. For a specified family, translate the features into parameter equations and verify the recovered formula.

Solve a polynomial or rational inequality

  1. Move all terms to one side and combine into a single expression compared with zero; record original exclusions.
  2. Factor and order every real numerator zero and exclusion. Test each intervening interval, including intervals split by holes.
  3. Select the required signs; include allowed zeros only for non-strict inequalities. Exclusions always stay open, and isolated allowed zeros may survive.

e.g. Solve (x−9)(x−14)(x−9)(x−4)≤0\frac{(x-9)(x-14)}{(x-9)(x-4)}\le0(x−9)(x−4)(x−9)(x−14)​≤0: (4,9)∪(9,14](4,9)\cup(9,14](4,9)∪(9,14].

Check a model and its feasible answers

  1. State quantities, units, algebraic restrictions, and physical or operating restrictions, including integer counts.
  2. Check the proposed formula against given measurements or conditions; solve the requested comparison.
  3. Intersect answers with the feasible domain. Qualify unmeasured predictions, especially beyond the tested range.

Exam traps

  • Trap Reading a negative exponent as a negative output.

    Fix It requests a reciprocal. Determine the output sign from the base, powers, and coefficient on the correct domain.

  • Trap Calling a falling graph concave down automatically.

    Fix Negative slopes describe falling; slopes becoming less negative describe concave up. An inflection need not be a turn or a zero.

  • Trap Treating finite samples as a global rule.

    Fix Samples establish their values and endpoint rates. They alone prove neither a formula, intervening concavity, exact extrema, nor far-away behavior.

  • Trap Reading exact multiplicities from a sketch.

    Fix Crossing and touching give parity. Use factors, repeated division, and degree information for exact counts. If the multiplier becomes zero, isolated-zero multiplicities cease to apply.

  • Trap Accepting a division identity with an oversized remainder degree.

    Fix Continue dividing until R=0R=0R=0 or its degree is below the divisor's. Numerical remainder size and sign do not decide completion.

  • Trap Rejecting every zero after a failed rational search.

    Fix A complete failed candidate search excludes rational zeros only. Irrational real and nonreal zeros may remain.

  • Trap Treating a small residual as exact equality or an input-error bound.

    Fix A nonzero residual is not zero and alone need not mean a nearby input. Use exact factors or reliable brackets for stronger claims.

  • Trap Restoring an input after cancellation or reciprocation.

    Fix Preserve inherited exclusions. Taking 1/f1/f1/f also excludes allowed inputs where f=0f=0f=0; a reduced numerator zero may still be a hole.

  • Trap Calling every canceled factor a hole.

    Fix Complete cancellation first. If a denominator copy survives at that real input, it is a pole. Never connect branches across an undefined input.

  • Trap Treating an asymptote as an uncrossable barrier.

    Fix Horizontal and slant asymptotes describe far-away gaps. Check meetings algebraically and remove excluded inputs; a finite window alone does not establish an asymptote.

  • Trap Multiplying an inequality by an unknown-sign denominator.

    Fix Use a sign chart or separate positive and negative denominator cases. Non-strict equality never admits a hole or pole.

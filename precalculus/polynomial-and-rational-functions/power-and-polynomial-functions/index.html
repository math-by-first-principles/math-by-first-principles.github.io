Power and Polynomial Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Double every length of a box. Its surface area becomes four times as large, but its volume becomes eight times as large. The input doubled in both calculations; the powers made the outputs respond differently. A formula can reveal these patterns, including changes a short table might miss. We will connect powers with whole graphs, then ask how much a pattern in sampled values really tells us.

Learning goals

  • Classify power and polynomial functions and determine their natural real domains
  • Read a polynomial's degree and leading coefficient after combining like terms
  • Use symmetry and leading terms to describe polynomial graphs and end behavior
  • Compare equal-step differences and average rates for linear and quadratic rules
  • Interpret concavity changes and qualify conclusions drawn from finite samples

One power, several possible domains

A power describes how an output responds to scaling its input. For f(x)=5x2f(x)=5x^2f(x)=5x2, input 222 gives 202020 and input 444 gives 808080. Doubling the input multiplied the output by four, since the factor 222 was squared.

A power function has the form f(x)=axrf(x)=ax^rf(x)=axr, with a fixed nonzero real coefficient aaa and a fixed exponent rrr. This lesson uses integer and rational exponents. The variable is the base; a rule with a variable exponent belongs to a different family.

The exponent also determines which real inputs work. For example, x1/2=xx^{1/2}=\sqrt{x}x1/2=x​ accepts 000 but no negative input, while x−1/2=1/xx^{-1/2}=1/\sqrt{x}x−1/2=1/x​ also excludes 000. The minus sign in the exponent requests a reciprocal, not a negative output.

Write a nonzero rational exponent as p/qp/qp/q in lowest terms, with positive integer qqq. Use the real root first:

xp/q=(xq)p.x^{p/q}=\bigl(\sqrt[q]{x}\bigr)^p.xp/q=(qx​)p.

An odd root accepts every real input. An even root requires a nonnegative input and returns the nonnegative root. A negative ppp requires that root to be nonzero as well. Reducing the exponent first matters: x2/6x^{2/6}x2/6 means x1/3x^{1/3}x1/3, so it accepts negative inputs.

Adding powers makes a polynomial

A polynomial combines whole-number powers of the variable. The rule 3x4−2x+63x^4-2x+63x4−2x+6 adds three such terms. In contrast, 3x4/33x^{4/3}3x4/3 uses one power but cannot be a polynomial because its exponent is not a nonnegative integer.

A polynomial function is a finite sum of terms with real coefficients and nonnegative integer exponents. Its general form is

P(x)=anxn+⋯+a1x+a0.P(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0.P(x)=an​xn+⋯+a1​x+a0​.

For a nonzero polynomial in this form, an≠0a_n\ne0an​=0. The degree is the largest exponent with a nonzero coefficient, nnn. The leading coefficient is ana_nan​, and anxna_nx^nan​xn is the leading term. Combine like terms before reading them. The last term is the constant a0a_0a0​, defined at 000 without writing 000^000.

Nonzero constants have degree 000. The zero polynomial has no degree under our convention, since no nonzero leading term remains. Polynomial rules have natural domain R\mathbb RR; a situation can impose a smaller domain, as Domain and Range explains.

Symmetry connects opposite inputs

Opposite inputs can produce equal heights or opposite heights. For squaring, (−2)2=22=4(-2)^2=2^2=4(−2)2=22=4. For cubing, (−2)3=−8(-2)^3=-8(−2)3=−8, the opposite of 23=82^3=823=8. Pairing negative signs explains the difference: an even count pairs them all, while an odd count leaves one negative factor.

Graphs and Transformations calls a function even when f(−x)=f(x)f(-x)=f(x)f(−x)=f(x) and odd when f(−x)=−f(x)f(-x)=-f(x)f(−x)=−f(x) everywhere on its domain. Either classification requires a domain symmetric about zero, so opposite inputs are both allowed.

The square’s graph has yyy-axis symmetry; the cube’s has origin symmetry. Nonzero integer powers of even exponent are even functions, and those of odd exponent are odd, including negative exponents on domains excluding 000. For rational powers, test the rule and domain rather than inspecting only the numerator: x2/3x^{2/3}x2/3 is even, while x1/2x^{1/2}x1/2 is neither because its domain contains 111 but not −1-1−1.

For polynomials, every even-power term preserves its value at −x-x−x, while every odd-power term reverses sign. Thus 4x6−x2+84x^6-x^2+84x6−x2+8 is even and 2x5−7x2x^5-7x2x5−7x is odd. A mix can be neither: x2+xx^2+xx2+x has values 222 and 000 at inputs 111 and −1-1−1. Nonzero constant functions are even; the zero function on R\mathbb RR is both even and odd.

The leading term controls the far ends

The largest coefficient need not control a graph far from zero. For

R(x)=−2x6+9x2+4,R(x)=-2x^6+9x^2+4,R(x)=−2x6+9x2+4,

input 111 gives 111111, but input 101010 gives −1,999,096-1{,}999{,}096−1,999,096. The sixth power eventually outweighs the smaller powers. For nonzero xxx, rewrite the rule as

R(x)=x6(−2+9x4+4x6).R(x)=x^6\left(-2+\frac9{x^4}+\frac4{x^6}\right).R(x)=x6(−2+x49​+x64​).

As the size of xxx grows, the two fractions become small compared with 222. The bracket stays negative far enough from zero, and the magnitude of x6x^6x6 keeps growing. Thus the outputs fall below any fixed negative bound on both ends. This is what we mean by both ends falling.

The same comparison works for any nonconstant polynomial: after taking out its highest power, every lower term contributes a fraction with a growing denominator. The end behavior follows the leading term, though the lower terms still shape the middle. For positive degree nnn, use its parity and the sign of ana_nan​:

Equal steps reveal rate patterns

For two allowed, distinct inputs, Functions and Function Notation calls f(b)−f(a)b−a\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}b−af(b)−f(a)​ the average rate of change. It is the slope of the secant line through the two endpoint points.

Differences compare successive outputs. For the linear rule L(x)=3x+1L(x)=3x+1L(x)=3x+1 at inputs 0,2,4,60,2,4,60,2,4,6, the outputs are 1,7,13,191,7,13,191,7,13,19: every two-unit step adds 666. Dividing each increase by 222 gives the same average rate, 333.

Concavity describes how slopes change

A graph can keep rising while its average rates fall. For f(x)=xf(x)=\sqrt{x}f(x)=x​ on [0,∞)[0,\infty)[0,∞), consecutive one-unit intervals beginning at 000, 111, and 222 have average rates approximately 111, 0.4140.4140.414, and 0.3180.3180.318. The outputs rise, but each interval gains less height.

A graph is concave up on an interval if, for every choice of inputs p<q<rp<q<rp<q<r in that interval, the average rate on [p,q][p,q][p,q] is less than the average rate on [q,r][q,r][q,r]. It is concave down if the first rate is greater than the second for every such choice. These adjacent subintervals may have different widths; equal widths make a sampled comparison convenient. The condition must hold for every adjacent pair, so a finite list of sampled slopes alone does not establish concavity throughout an interval. A straight line has constant slopes and neither strict bending pattern.

For x2x^2x2, consecutive unit intervals from −2-2−2 to 222 give slopes −3,−1,1,3-3,-1,1,3−3,−1,1,3. They increase even on the left, where the function decreases. Concavity describes change in slopes, not the sign of the slopes.

For x3x^3x3, unit intervals from −3-3−3 to 333 give slopes 19,7,1,1,7,1919,7,1,1,7,1919,7,1,1,7,19. They suggest a change in bending near zero. To check every adjacent pair, factor the difference of cubes and divide by each interval width. For any p<q<rp<q<rp<q<r, the two average rates are

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Power versus polynomial: a single rational power need not be a polynomial, while a sum of integer powers need not be one power function.
  • Domain restrictions: reduce a rational exponent first; an even root excludes negatives and a negative exponent also excludes zero.
  • Degree and symmetry: combine like terms before reading degree; matching far ends alone does not establish even symmetry.
  • Difference comparisons: use equal input steps for ordinary finite differences. Divide output change by input change to obtain a rate.
  • Concavity and evidence: increasing slopes can be negative. A few sampled slopes suggest bending but do not prove all intervening behavior.

Key takeaways

  • Power functions use one fixed exponent; polynomials use finite sums of nonnegative integer powers with real coefficients.
  • Degree is the largest surviving exponent of a nonzero polynomial; the zero polynomial has no degree here.
  • Symmetry concerns opposite inputs across a symmetric domain. A nonconstant polynomial’s leading term determines its far ends.
  • Equal-step differences connect linear and quadratic rules with constant rates and constant changes in rates.
  • Concavity describes increasing or decreasing secant slopes. An inflection changes concavity; finite samples alone do not establish an entire graph.

A bit of history (optional)

Before electronic calculators, engineers and scientists relied on printed mathematical tables. Errors could enter both when a person calculated an entry and when someone copied it for printing. Charles Babbage wanted a machine that could calculate a table and print the results itself.

His proposed Difference Engine used a useful mechanical shortcut. Once the starting values and differences for a polynomial were loaded, adding differences could produce the next entry without repeated multiplication. Turning gearwheels to add was easier to arrange than making them multiply.

Babbage built only a small section of his first engine. Other inventors, including Georg and Edvard Scheutz in Sweden, did build difference engines, though these machines never became widely used. The ambitious plan had made a pattern in number tables into a design for machinery.

Sources: the Computer History Museum’s history of Babbage’s engines and the Computer History Museum’s explanation of the mechanism.

Zeros of Polynomial Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A real graph of x3−1x^3-1x3−1 crosses the horizontal axis only once. Yet a cubic has room for three linear factors. Where are the other zeros, and how can we tell when a search is finished? We will connect a shortlist of possible rational zeros, exact factorization, and a picture that can hold nonreal numbers.

Learning goals

  • Generate rational candidates and test which ones actually make an integer polynomial zero
  • Factor after a known zero and solve the remaining quadratic exactly
  • Locate complex zeros and identify which ones appear on a real graph
  • Account for every zero using multiplicity, degree, and real coefficient conjugate pairs
  • Distinguish exact root certificates from numerical estimates and limited graph evidence

A shortlist is a starting point

The first and last coefficients can rule out most rational numbers before we substitute anything. For example, expanding (3x+2)(x2−x−1)(3x+2)(x^2-x-1)(3x+2)(x2−x−1) gives E(x)=3x3−x2−5x−2E(x)=3x^3-x^2-5x-2E(x)=3x3−x2−5x−2. Its zero −2/3-2/3−2/3 has numerator −2-2−2, a divisor of the constant −2-2−2, and denominator 333, a divisor of the leading coefficient 333.

Run the reasoning on that zero. Substituting −2/3-2/3−2/3 into EEE and multiplying by 272727 clears every denominator:

−24−12+90−54=0.-24-12+90-54=0.−24−12+90−54=0.

The first three terms are even, so −54=(−2)⋅27-54=(-2)\cdot27−54=(−2)⋅27 must be even too. Since 222 and 272727 share no prime factor, that factor 222 must come from the constant −2-2−2. The last three terms are multiples of 333, so −24=3⋅(−8)-24=3\cdot(-8)−24=3⋅(−8) must be a multiple of 333; since 333 and 888 share no prime factor, the factor 333 must come from the leading coefficient 333.

For a reduced rational zero of any integer-coefficient polynomial, clearing denominators gives the same pattern. All but the constant-end term contain the numerator; all but the leading-end term contain the denominator. Their lack of shared prime factors forces the numerator to divide the constant and the denominator to divide the leading coefficient.

The Rational Root Theorem, also called the rational zero theorem, states that for

When a0≠0a_0\ne0a0​=0, form all signed quotients of positive divisors of ∣a0∣|a_0|∣a0​∣ by positive divisors of ∣an∣|a_n|∣an​∣, reduce them, and remove duplicates.

These are candidates, possible values requiring a test. For instance, 111 belongs on EEE‘s candidate list, but E(1)=−5E(1)=-5E(1)=−5, so it is not a zero. If the constant is zero, first factor out the largest power of xxx; that records zero and its multiplicity and leaves a nonzero constant for the finite list. Rational coefficients can be made integers by multiplying the whole polynomial by a common nonzero denominator, which does not change its zeros.

Numbers beyond the real line

A quadratic can leave us with a negative square. The equation x2+9=0x^2+9=0x2+9=0 requires x2=−9x^2=-9x2=−9, which no real number satisfies. Define iii by i2=−1i^2=-1i2=−1. Then (3i)2=−9(3i)^2=-9(3i)2=−9, so the zeros are 3i3i3i and −3i-3i−3i.

A complex number has the form a+bia+bia+bi, where a,ba,ba,b are real. Its real part is aaa, and its imaginary part is the real coefficient bbb of iii. Add by combining real and imaginary parts; multiply by distributing and replacing i2i^2i2 with −1-1−1. For example, (2+i)(2−i)=4−i2=5(2+i)(2-i)=4-i^2=5(2+i)(2−i)=4−i2=5. Real numbers are included by taking b=0b=0b=0; a number is nonreal when b≠0b\ne0b=0.

In the complex plane, a+bia+bia+bi is the point (a,b)(a,b)(a,b). The horizontal coordinate is its real part; the vertical coordinate is its imaginary part. Thus 2−3i2-3i2−3i is plotted at (2,−3)(2,-3)(2,−3). This is a picture of numbers themselves, unlike a real function graph, whose coordinates are input and output.

Count factors, including repeated ones

A count by degree includes repetitions. For instance, (x+4)2(x2−2x+10)(x+4)^2(x^2-2x+10)(x+4)2(x2−2x+10) has the real zero −4-4−4 twice and the nonreal zeros 1+3i,1−3i1+3i,1-3i1+3i,1−3i once each. That is three distinct numbers but four zeros counted with multiplicity.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra says every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex zero. Its counting form says a degree-nnn polynomial, for n≥1n\ge1n≥1, has exactly nnn complex zeros counted with multiplicity, or equivalently nnn linear factors including repeats and a nonzero leading constant. This theorem is stated here; a full proof belongs to later mathematics.

The factor connection explains the counting: each removed linear factor reduces the degree by one, and the theorem continues to guarantee a zero in any nonconstant quotient. Recall from Graphs of Polynomial Functions that multiplicity mmm means P(x)=(x−r)mQ(x)P(x)=(x-r)^mQ(x)P(x)=(x−r)mQ(x) with Q(r)≠0Q(r)\ne0Q(r)=0. For a real zero, odd multiplicity gives a crossing and even multiplicity a touch.

A complete exact factorization supplies more than a count. At any number different from all listed zeros, every linear factor is nonzero, so their product is nonzero. This certifies that none was missed. One successful division only supplies one factor; test the same zero in the quotient if repetition is possible.

Nonzero constants have no zeros, while the zero polynomial vanishes at every input and has no degree under our convention. Neither is covered by the nonconstant theorem.

Real coefficients force a partner

Real coefficients make the nonreal zeros occur in reflected pairs. In the cubic anchor, (−1+i3)/2(-1+i\sqrt3)/2(−1+i3​)/2 and (−1−i3)/2(-1-i\sqrt3)/2(−1−i3​)/2 have the same real part and opposite imaginary parts.

The complex conjugate of a+bia+bia+bi is a−bia-bia−bi. For z=a+biz=a+biz=a+bi, write z‾=a−bi\overline z=a-biz=a−bi; the bar means take the complex conjugate. Conjugating a sum or a product gives the same result as conjugating its ingredients first: distributing the products and using i2=−1i^2=-1i2=−1 checks this. Conjugation also leaves every real coefficient and zero unchanged. Therefore, if substituting a number gives zero in a real-coefficient polynomial, substituting its conjugate also gives zero.

The conjugate zero theorem says that for a polynomial with real coefficients, every nonreal zero a+bia+bia+bi has the partner a−bia-bia−bi, with the same multiplicity. The equal multiplicities follow by conjugating the repeated factors too. Without the real-coefficient condition, pairing is not guaranteed.

The conjugate of -1+4i is -1-4i, with the same multiplicity 1. Together with the three copies at 7, the count is 3+1+1=5. A further zero or extra copy would exceed the degree.

Exact certificates and numerical evidence

A decimal near a crossing is an estimate until something certifies exact equality. For H(x)=x3+x−4H(x)=x^3+x-4H(x)=x3+x−4, every rational candidate ±1,±2,±4\pm1,\pm2,\pm4±1,±2,±4 fails. This proves there is no rational zero. It does not mean there is no real zero.

When using a graphing tool, choose a window that includes the expected crossing, zoom to separate nearby features, and evaluate the proposed input in the original polynomial. Report the requested precision with ≈\approx≈. Exact factors, a verified count, or a separate argument about all inputs must supply any completeness claim.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Candidates as answers: the divisibility conditions are necessary; substitution or a zero remainder must verify a candidate.
  • A failed rational search: it rules out rational zeros, while irrational or nonreal zeros may remain.
  • Degree as an intercept count: the degree counts complex zeros with repetition, not distinct real intercepts.
  • Conjugates without a condition: pairing requires real coefficients and flips only the imaginary part.
  • Rounded equality: write an estimated zero with ≈\approx≈ and retain the distinction between a small residual and zero.

Key takeaways

  • Generate reduced rational candidates from integer endpoint coefficients; factor powers of xxx first if the constant is zero.
  • A verified zero gives a linear factor. Solve the quotient and check repetitions before declaring a search complete.
  • Plot a+bia+bia+bi at (a,b)(a,b)(a,b); only zeros on the real axis give intercepts of the real function graph.
  • Degree counts every complex zero with multiplicity. Real coefficients force nonreal conjugate pairs with matching multiplicities.
  • Numerical tables and graphs support estimates; exact factors and justified counts certify completeness.

A bit of history (optional)

Rafael Bombelli worked as an engineer on a project to drain marshland in Italy. When the work stopped in the 1550s, he began writing an algebra book. He wanted a careful account that readers could follow without already knowing the advanced mathematics in other books.

His book faced an awkward feature of cubic equations. A formula could produce square roots of negative numbers even when the final answer was real. Bombelli developed consistent rules for calculating with those expressions and showed how they could lead back to correct real solutions. The first three books of his Algebra appeared in 1572. He died soon afterward, leaving its planned geometry volumes unfinished.

Graphs of Polynomial Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A table can miss the place where a curve just touches zero and returns to the same side. A factored polynomial can reveal that contact even when none of the table’s inputs lands on it. Reading a graph from its factors helps you decide which features a plot must show, and which details still need evidence.

Learning goals

  • Relate zeros and multiplicities to crossings, touches, and signs between intercepts
  • Combine intercepts, end behavior, and turning bounds to guide a polynomial sketch
  • Compare the effects of root location, multiplicity, and nonzero scale parameters
  • Construct polynomial rules from sufficient features and assess a model's domain assumptions

From factors to contacts with the axis

A repeated factor can make a graph reach the axis without crossing it. Start with

P(x)=(x−1)2(x+2).P(x)=(x-1)^2(x+2).P(x)=(x−1)2(x+2).

At x=−2x=-2x=−2 the last factor is zero; at x=1x=1x=1 the squared factor is zero. These inputs are zeros of PPP, meaning inputs with output zero, so the x-intercepts are (−2,0)(-2,0)(−2,0) and (1,0)(1,0)(1,0). A product is zero only when at least one of its factors is zero, so this formula has no other real zeros.

Near −2-2−2, the square is positive while x+2x+2x+2 changes from negative to positive. The product changes sign, so the graph crosses. Near 111, x+2x+2x+2 stays positive and the square is positive on both sides. The graph reaches zero, then returns above the axis.

The multiplicity of a zero counts the number of copies of its factor. A simple zero has multiplicity 111; a double zero has multiplicity 222. Here −2-2−2 is simple and 111 is double. In general, a real zero rrr has multiplicity mmm when

P(x)=(x−r)mQ(x),Q(r)≠0,P(x)=(x-r)^m Q(x),\qquad Q(r)\ne0,P(x)=(x−r)mQ(x),Q(r)=0,

where mmm is a positive integer and QQQ is a polynomial. The nonzero condition says that every copy of x−rx-rx−r has been counted.

Since Q(r)Q(r)Q(r) is nonzero, QQQ keeps that sign sufficiently close to rrr. An odd power of x−rx-rx−r changes sign across rrr, while an even power keeps the same sign. Therefore an odd multiplicity gives a crossing; an even multiplicity gives a touch without crossing. An odd multiplicity above 111 can give a flattened crossing, so looking flat does not mean touching.

For F(x)=(x+5)3(x−4)2F(x)=(x+5)^3(x-4)^2F(x)=(x+5)3(x−4)2, what happens at its two real zeros?

Answer choices

The factor x+5x+5x+5 appears three times, giving odd multiplicity at −5-5−5. The factor x−4x-4x−4 appears twice, giving even multiplicity at 444. The cube changes sign across its zero; the square does not. Thus the graph crosses at −5-5−5 and touches at 444.

Signs, intercepts, and an honest sketch

Between zeros, a polynomial stays on one side of the axis. Its graph is continuous, meaning it has no jumps or holes, so it cannot change from a negative output to a positive one without passing through zero. Once all real zeros are known, one test input in each open interval determines that interval’s sign.

Worked example 1 Read the anchor graph from its factors

Problem. For P(x)=(x−1)2(x+2)P(x)=(x-1)^2(x+2)P(x)=(x−1)2(x+2) on all real inputs, find its intercepts, sign intervals, and crossing or touching behavior. State its end behavior and the maximum number of turning points. Use those facts to describe a sketch.

Solution. The zeros are −2-2−2 and 111, with multiplicities 111 and 222. The graph crosses at −2-2−2 and touches at 111.

The y-intercept uses input zero:

P(0)=(−1)2(2)=2.P(0)=(-1)^2(2)=2.P(0)=(−1)2(2)=2.

This gives the point (0,2)(0,2)(0,2). Choose one test input in each interval cut out by the zeros.

Thus the sketch is below the axis before −2-2−2, crosses upward there, passes through (0,2)(0,2)(0,2), and touches from above at (1,0)(1,0)(1,0). Separate the two positive intervals because P(1)=0P(1)=0P(1)=0, not a positive value.

The leading term is x3x^3x3. As recalled in Power and Polynomial Functions, its positive odd degree makes the far left fall and the far right rise.

For a nonconstant polynomial of degree nnn, the graph has at most n−1n-1n−1 turning points, where rising changes to falling or falling changes to rising. Here the maximum is 222; this bound is a stated result, whose general proof uses calculus.

The turning point at (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) is a local minimum because nearby outputs are positive. The other turn is above the axis between the two zeros; its exact coordinates are not supplied by the sign chart.

A turning bound is a ceiling, not a quota. For example, x3+2xx^3+2xx3+2x is strictly increasing: increasing xxx increases both x3x^3x3 and 2x2x2x. It has no turning point despite having degree 333. A constant polynomial also has no turning points; do not apply the expression n−1n-1n−1 with n=0n=0n=0.

Check your understanding

A polynomial has degree 555. Its graph has two turning points in the displayed window. Which conclusion follows?

Answer choices

For degree 555, the bound is 5−1=45-1=45−1=4 turning points over the entire graph. The two visible turns do not force the remaining allowance to be used, and a turning point need not have output zero.

Change one feature at a time

A parameter can move an intercept while preserving how the curve meets it. Compare

P(x)=(x−1)2(x+2)P(x)=(x-1)^2(x+2)P(x)=(x−1)2(x+2)

with

U(x)=(x−1)2(x+1).U(x)=(x-1)^2(x+1).U(x)=(x−1)2(x+1).

Only the simple zero moves, from −2-2−2 to −1-1−1. The double zero stays at 111. Both polynomials have positive leading term x3x^3x3, but their signs differ between the moved crossings.

Now keep the zero locations but add another copy of x−1x-1x−1:

R(x)=(x−1)3(x+2)=(x−1)P(x).R(x)=(x-1)^3(x+2)=(x-1)P(x).R(x)=(x−1)3(x+2)=(x−1)P(x).

Before looking at the next panel, predict the sign of RRR between −2-2−2 and 111. On that interval PPP is positive but x−1x-1x−1 is negative, so RRR is negative. To the right of 111, both factors are positive. The zero at 111 now crosses. Adding the factor also changes the degree to 444, so both far ends rise.

Let G(x)=a(x−6)2(x+3)G(x)=a(x-6)^2(x+3)G(x)=a(x−6)2(x+3) with a≠0a\ne0a=0. Changing aaa from 222 to −4-4−4 does what?

The new value is −2-2−2 times the old value at every input. Its magnitude doubles and its sign reverses. The nonzero multiplier does not change the factors that produce zeros, so both multiplicities and locations stay fixed.

Build a rule from enough information

Features determine a unique polynomial only when they leave no undetermined choice. Knowing zeros alone usually leaves both their exact multiplicities and a height scale open. An exact degree can fix multiplicities when the required factors account for that whole degree; an additional value away from the zeros can then fix the scale.

Worked example 3 Recover an unknown intercept

Problem. A cubic has leading coefficient 222, crosses at x=−6x=-6x=−6, and touches without crossing at an unknown positive zero rrr. Its y-intercept is (0,300)(0,300)(0,300). Find rrr and the polynomial, and explain why these facts determine it uniquely.

Solution. The crossing needs odd multiplicity at least 111; the touch needs even multiplicity at least 222. Those minimum counts already total 333, the whole degree. Its form is therefore

F(x)=2(x+6)(x−r)2.F(x)=2(x+6)(x-r)^2.F(x)=2(x+6)(x−r)2.

At input zero, the height condition gives

300=12r2.300=12r^2.300=12r2.

Thus r2=25r^2=25r2=25. Because the unknown zero is positive, r=5r=5r=5, giving

F(x)=2(x+6)(x−5)2.F(x)=2(x+6)(x-5)^2.F(x)=2(x+6)(x−5)2.

The factors give the required crossing and touch, and F(0)=300F(0)=300F(0)=300. The degree fixes both multiplicities, the leading coefficient fixes the multiplier, and the positivity condition selects just one value of rrr.

Without the positivity condition, r=−5r=-5r=−5 would give another cubic with the same leading coefficient and y-intercept. Likewise, end directions alone determine the sign of a leading coefficient, not its numerical value. Check which information is exact before treating a schematic picture as a unique rule.

A fitted curve is a model with assumptions

Matching a table is evidence for a model, not a reason that nature must follow its whole graph. A polynomial is algebraically defined at every real input, but a model may be supported only on a tested range or a physically feasible domain.

Problem. A compression sensor is tested at travel distances t=0,1,2t=0,1,2t=0,1,2 cm, giving force readings 8,9,128,9,128,9,12 newtons. Consider

Check your understanding

Two polynomial models agree at every measured input from 000 to 222, but disagree at input 555. Which claim is justified by those measurements alone?

Answer choices

A finite table checks only its recorded inputs. A degree assumption or a physical law could support a choice, but neither follows just from agreement at the measured inputs. Extrapolating to 555 asks for behavior beyond the measured range.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Every zero is a crossing: an even multiplicity keeps the same nearby sign and gives a touch.
  • Every flat contact is a touch: an odd multiplicity greater than one can cross while flattening near the axis.
  • The turning bound is an exact count: degree n≥1n\ge1n≥1 permits at most n−1n-1n−1 turns; it need not use them all.
  • A sketch fixes every coefficient: parity and end directions leave choices unless sufficient exact data are supplied.
  • A fitted graph is a physical law: state the supported input range and the assumptions behind unmeasured predictions.

Key takeaways

  • Real zeros give x-intercepts. Their multiplicities determine crossing or touching through a nearby sign change.
  • Test one point in each interval between known real zeros; use P(0)P(0)P(0) to locate the y-intercept.
  • Combine sign intervals with end behavior and the turning bound to guide a sketch without inventing exact extrema.
  • A nonzero height multiplier preserves zero locations and multiplicities; a negative one reverses signs.
  • Recover a unique rule only from sufficient features, and distinguish the polynomial’s algebraic domain from a model’s supported domain.

A bit of history (optional)

René Descartes published a book about a method for finding reliable knowledge in 1637. Its mathematical work appeared in an appendix, La Géométrie, alongside separate appendices about light and weather.

Descartes wanted geometry and algebra to help each other. A geometric problem could supply unknown lengths and relations between them; symbols could keep track of those relations without carrying the entire picture in memory. In La Géométrie he explained how to translate a problem into equations and then return to the geometry of its solutions.

The appendix was written for expert mathematicians rather than as a beginner’s textbook. The now familiar act of studying a curve through an equation was part of a much larger effort to change how mathematical problems were solved.

Sources: MacTutor’s biography of Descartes and translated extracts from La Géométrie.

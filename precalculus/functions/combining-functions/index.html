Combining and Composing Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A store offers a fixed discount and then adds tax. Another applies the tax first and takes the same discount afterward. The ingredients look identical, but each step acts on the amount handed to it. To compare the policies, we need to keep track of that handoff.

Learning goals

  • Form arithmetic combinations and retain every restriction from the original functions
  • Evaluate compositions from tables and formulas by following the inside function first
  • Compare composition orders and explain how intermediate units connect a model
  • Determine a composite domain by checking the input and the intermediate output
  • Decompose a formula and verify both its rule and its allowed inputs

A simpler formula keeps its original restrictions

Simplifying a quotient changes its written form. It does not supply an output where the original quotient was undefined.

Worked example 2 A canceled factor leaves an excluded input

Problem. Let a(x)=x2−64a(x)=x^2-64a(x)=x2−64 and b(x)=x−8b(x)=x-8b(x)=x−8, both on all real inputs. Define Q=a/bQ=a/bQ=a/b.

Passing one output into another function

Arithmetic uses two outputs at the same input. Composition instead uses one output as the next input. If g(−2)=4g(-2)=4g(−2)=4 and f(4)=13f(4)=13f(4)=13, then starting at −2-2−2, applying ggg, and applying fff gives 131313.

We write this as

(f∘g)(−2)=f(g(−2))=13.(f\circ g)(-2)=f(g(-2))=13.(f∘g)(−2)=f(g(−2))=13.

The general notation is (f∘g)(x)=f(g(x))(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)), read “f composed with g.” The inner function ggg acts first. The symbol ∘\circ∘ does not mean multiplication.

For example, let f(u)=3u+1f(u)=3u+1f(u)=3u+1 and g(x)=x2g(x)=x^2g(x)=x2, both on all real inputs. Replace the entire input of fff with x2x^2x2:

(f∘g)(x)=3x2+1.(f\circ g)(x)=3x^2+1.(f∘g)(x)=3x2+1.

In the other order, the whole output 3x+13x+13x+1 becomes the input to the squaring rule:

(g∘f)(x)=(3x+1)2.(g\circ f)(x)=(3x+1)^2.(g∘f)(x)=(3x+1)2.

At input −2-2−2, the first order gives 131313, while the second gives 252525. Some particular pairs of functions do give the same composite in both orders, but there is no general permission to swap them.

For a table, perform the same two lookups: first find g(x)g(x)g(x) in the inner table, then look for that value as an input in the outer table. Do not read both tables at the original input.

Order and units belong to the model

Splitting a formula into stages

To decompose a function is to write it as a composition of simpler functions. Choose an intermediate quantity, name the rule that produces it, and describe what is done to it next. Then substitute back and check the domain.

Worked example 5 Two useful ways to split one rule

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Arithmetic versus composition: f(x)g(x)f(x)g(x)f(x)g(x) multiplies two outputs; f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)) passes one output into another rule.
  • Canceled factors: simplification preserves every input excluded by the original functions and operation.
  • Order: identify the first action before writing the inner function, and keep its whole output together.
  • Composite domains: test the inner output against the outer domain, rather than intersecting two original-input sets.
  • Model units: the quantity produced by one stage must match the quantity expected by the next.

Key takeaways

  • Arithmetic combinations need a shared input; a quotient also needs a nonzero denominator output.
  • A simpler formula keeps the domain of the function it came from.
  • In f∘gf\circ gf∘g, apply ggg first and fff second. Changing the order can change the result.
  • A composite input is allowed exactly when both stages accept the values handed to them.
  • Decomposition identifies useful stages. Verify the formula and the domain after joining them again.

A bit of history (optional)

Arthur Cayley spent fourteen years working as a lawyer while continuing to publish mathematics. During that period he produced about 250 mathematical papers. One, published in 1854, explored an unusual kind of algebra: its symbols could stand for operations, such as rearranging objects, rather than ordinary numbers.

Writing two operation symbols together meant performing one operation after the other. Cayley carefully specified the order and noted that reversing it could change the result. His paper helped begin the study of abstract groups, although it attracted little attention at first. In 1863, he left law to become a professor of pure mathematics at Cambridge, accepting a substantial drop in income to make mathematics his profession.

Source: Janet Heine Barnett’s historical study, including Cayley’s original text, especially pages 48 to 50.

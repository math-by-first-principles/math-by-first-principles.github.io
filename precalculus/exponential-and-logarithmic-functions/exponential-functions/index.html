Exponential Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Two balances start at 100 dollars. One adds 5 dollars each year; the other gains five percent of its current balance. Both reach 105 dollars after one year. After that, the percent plan earns interest on earlier interest. A single matching measurement hides two different ways quantities can change.

Learning goals

  • Compare linear and exponential change using differences and ratios over equal input steps
  • Explain positive base restrictions and evaluate exponential functions at real inputs
  • Interpret growth factors and percent rates for specified time intervals
  • Describe transformed exponential graphs using points, direction, asymptotes, domain, and range
  • Calculate compounded balances and distinguish scheduled credits from continuous exponential models

Equal additions and equal factors

A fixed addition gives the same change at each step. A fixed percentage gives the same multiplier.

A linear function has equal output differences over equal input steps. An exponential function of the form f(x)=abxf(x)=ab^xf(x)=abx has equal output ratios over equal input steps, where a≠0a\ne0a=0, b>0b>0b>0, and b≠1b\ne1b=1.

A finite table can agree with a linear or exponential rule without proving the rule holds between or beyond the measurements. We need a stated model or a reason the same change continues.

Real inputs and positive bases

Half a step must contribute a factor which, used twice, makes one full step. For base 444, that factor is 222, since 2⋅2=42\cdot2=42⋅2=4. Thus 41/2=24^{1/2}=241/2=2. A backward step undoes the multiplication, so 4−1=1/44^{-1}=1/44−1=1/4.

For a positive base, these meanings extend the familiar exponent rules:

b0=1,b−u=1bu,bm/n=bmn.b^0=1,\qquad b^{-u}=\frac1{b^u},\qquad b^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{b^m}.b0=1,b−u=bu1​,bm/n=nbm​.

Here uuu is real, mmm is an integer, and nnn is a positive integer. Rational powers use the positive root. Real powers also include irrational inputs such as 2\sqrt22​. They give the continuous exponential curve, with no gaps or jumps; their formal construction is left to later work with limits. For example, since 1<2<21<\sqrt2<21<2​<2, the increasing base-444 curve gives 4<42<164<4^{\sqrt2}<164<42​<16.

A positive base keeps bxb^xbx real and positive for every real input xxx. Base −4-4−4 fails at x=1/2x=1/2x=1/2, because a real square cannot be negative. Base 000 fails at x=−1x=-1x=−1, because it would require division by zero. Those failures explain why a negative coefficient, as in −4x-4^x−4x, is permitted while the negative base in (−4)x(-4)^x(−4)x is not permitted for an exponential on all real inputs.

Step size, factors, and rates

The multiplier belongs to an interval, so label that interval before calling a number a rate. For f(x)=3⋅2xf(x)=3\cdot2^xf(x)=3⋅2x, going from x=1x=1x=1 to x=3x=3x=3 changes the output from 666 to 242424. The two-unit factor is 24/6=424/6=424/6=4, which is 222^222, rather than 222.

For any nonzero aaa, positive bbb, and real step sss, exponent laws give

f(x+s)f(x)=abx+sabx=bs.\frac{f(x+s)}{f(x)}=\frac{ab^{x+s}}{ab^x}=b^s.f(x)f(x+s)​=abxabx+s​=bs.

The common factor is the base only for a unit step. For a positive amount, a per-period factor bbb corresponds to an effective rate r=b−1r=b-1r=b−1: the fraction of the old amount gained or lost during that period. A factor of 1.071.071.07 means a 7%7\%7% increase; a factor of 0.820.820.82 means an 18%18\%18% decrease. In P(1+r)tP(1+r)^tP(1+r)t, require P>0P>0P>0, r>−1r>-1r>−1, and measure ttt in the periods used by rrr.

Transformations and asymptotes

The parent y=2xy=2^xy=2x passes through (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and (1,2)(1,2)(1,2). Moving right by 111 changes those points to (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) and (2,2)(2,2)(2,2). Multiplying the outputs by −3-3−3, then adding 666, changes them to (1,3)(1,3)(1,3) and (2,0)(2,0)(2,0).

The parent bxb^xbx has domain R\mathbb RR and range (0,∞)(0,\infty)(0,∞). It increases for b>1b>1b>1 and decreases for 0<b<10<b<10<b<1. On one end it approaches 000 without reaching it; on the other it grows without bound. For b>1b>1b>1 the approach to 000 occurs toward the left; for 0<b<10<b<10<b<1 it occurs toward the right.

The transformed family is

g(x)=abx−h+k,g(x)=ab^{x-h}+k,g(x)=abx−h+k,

where a≠0a\ne0a=0, b>0b>0b>0, b≠1b\ne1b=1, and h,kh,kh,k are real. Each parent point (u,v)(u,v)(u,v) becomes (u+h,av+k)(u+h,av+k)(u+h,av+k). A positive aaa preserves direction; a negative aaa reverses it. The domain remains all real numbers, and the horizontal asymptote becomes y=ky=ky=k. The range is (k,∞)(k,\infty)(k,∞) for a>0a>0a>0, or (−∞,k)(-\infty,k)(−∞,k) for a<0a<0a<0, since the exponential term has one strict sign and takes every positive magnitude.

A vertical shift changes which quantities have equal ratios. For p(x)=3⋅2x+5p(x)=3\cdot2^x+5p(x)=3⋅2x+5, the outputs at 000 and 111 are 888 and 111111; their ratio is not 222. Subtract the reference height 555, and the distances are 333 and 666, which do have ratio 222. The shift adds the same reference value to each output, so remove it before comparing exponential factors. In general,

g(x+s)−kg(x)−k=bs.\frac{g(x+s)-k}{g(x)-k}=b^s.g(x)−kg(x+s)−k​=bs.

The denominator is nonzero because a≠0a\ne0a=0 and the power is positive.

Unlike some rational graphs from the previous chapter, these transformed exponentials never meet their horizontal asymptote: abx−hab^{x-h}abx−h cannot be zero.

Scheduled compounding and continuous models

Scheduled interest is credited at particular times. Suppose an account starts with 50 dollars and credits 50%50\%50% interest once a year, with no other transactions. Immediately after successive annual postings, the balances are 505050, 757575, 112.50112.50112.50, and 168.75168.75168.75 dollars. Between postings, the credited balance stays fixed.

A nominal annual rate is a quoted rate split among the posting periods. At 12%12\%12% nominal annually, compounded quarterly, each quarter receives 0.12/4=0.030.12/4=0.030.12/4=0.03, so each posting multiplies the balance by 1.031.031.03.

Worked example 3 Counting credited periods

Problem. Deposit 800 dollars at a nominal annual rate of 12%12\%12%, compounded quarterly. With no fees, deposits, or withdrawals, find the balance just after the posting at 1.51.51.5 years, to the nearest cent. Find the exact effective one-year rate.

Solution. There are four quarters per year, so 1.51.51.5 years contains 4(1.5)=64(1.5)=64(1.5)=6 completed quarters.

A=800(1.03)6≈955.24 dollars.A=800(1.03)^6\approx955.24\text{ dollars}.A=800(1.03)6≈955.24 dollars.

A continuous exponential model is A(t)=PektA(t)=Pe^{kt}A(t)=Pekt, with P>0P>0P>0 and real coefficient kkk measured per time unit. Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring step size. A ratio over three hours is a three-hour factor. A one-hour factor must be applied three times to produce it.
  • Confusing a rate with a factor. A 16%16\%16% loss keeps 84%84\%84%, giving factor 0.840.840.84, not 0.160.160.16.
  • Reading the coefficient as the initial value. For abx−h+kab^{x-h}+kabx−h+k, substitute x=0x=0x=0; the result is ab−h+kab^{-h}+kab−h+k.
  • Taking raw ratios after a vertical shift. For abx+kab^x+kabx+k, equal steps give equal ratios of the signed distances g(x)−kg(x)-kg(x)−k, rather than of g(x)g(x)g(x) itself.
  • Mixing time and rate conventions. Count completed posting periods for a credited balance. For PektPe^{kt}Pekt, match the unit of ttt to the unit used by kkk.

Key takeaways

  • Linear and exponential change: equal input steps give equal differences for linear rules and equal ratios for abxab^xabx.
  • Real exponential powers: use b>0b>0b>0 and b≠1b\ne1b=1; the domain of abxab^xabx is all real numbers when a≠0a\ne0a=0.
  • Factors and rates: r=b−1r=b-1r=b−1 for the specified period; a step of size sss has factor bsb^sbs.
  • Transformations: abx−h+kab^{x-h}+kabx−h+k has horizontal asymptote y=ky=ky=k and stays above it for a>0a>0a>0, below it for a<0a<0a<0.
  • Compounding: scheduled balances count posting periods; PektPe^{kt}Pekt is a continuous model with effective unit-period rate ek−1e^k-1ek−1.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1683, the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli investigated how often interest could be compounded. Breaking a year into more payment periods made a deposit earn interest on interest sooner. He studied the amounts now written as (1+1/n)n(1+1/n)^n(1+1/n)n and established bounds between 222 and 333 for the number behind continuous compounding.

The familiar letter came later. Leibniz used bbb for this number in a 1690 letter to Huygens; Euler used eee in a letter to Goldbach in 1731. The reason Euler chose that particular letter is uncertain. MacTutor’s history of the number e follows the trail through these letters and earlier calculations.

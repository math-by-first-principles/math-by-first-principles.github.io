Modeling with Exponential and Logarithmic Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A hot mug may lose half its excess heat in a few minutes without losing half its temperature. A growing population may double twice in the observations without doubling forever. A model connects measurements to a rule, but choosing what to measure and deciding where that rule remains useful are part of the mathematics.

Learning goals

  • Fit exponential and logarithmic models from measurements with clearly stated units
  • Convert between an effective period rate and a continuous exponential coefficient
  • Calculate doubling times, half-lives, and thresholds in cooling models with ambient offsets
  • Interpret logarithmic scales and residuals when comparing candidate models with data
  • Judge interpolation and extrapolation using the physical domain and model assumptions

Fit the change between measurements

An exponential model describes a constant factor over equal time intervals. Suppose a quantity changes from 181818 units to 727272 units over two hours. The two-hour factor is 72/18=472/18=472/18=4. If the same factor applies in each hour, the hourly factor must be the positive square root, 222, because 2⋅2=42\cdot2=42⋅2=4.

Two readings at distinct times determine a positive exponential model once we assume this behavior. For positive readings y1,y2y_1,y_2y1​,y2​ at times t1<t2t_1<t_2t1​<t2​, dividing cancels the unknown starting amount:

bt2−t1=y2y1.b^{t_2-t_1}=\frac{y_2}{y_1}.bt2​−t1​=y1​y2​​.

Thus the factor per time unit and a convenient model are

b=(y2y1)1/(t2−t1),b=\left(\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right)^{1/(t_2-t_1)},b=(y1​y2​​)1/(t2​−t1​), y(t)=y1bt−t1.y(t)=y_1b^{t-t_1}.y(t)=y1​bt−t1​.

The exponent measures elapsed time since the first reading. Equal readings give b=1b=1b=1, a constant model. A fit through two points alone does not establish what happens at other times.

Solution. Four hours multiply the mass by 180/45=4180/45=4180/45=4, so b4=4b^4=4b4=4. The positive hourly factor is b=2b=\sqrt2b=2​.

Use the first measured time as the reference:

At t=2t=2t=2 the exponent is zero and the model returns 454545. At t=6t=6t=6 it is two and returns 180180180.

At 555 hours the model predicts

M(5)=902≈127.3 mg.M(5)=90\sqrt2\approx127.3\text{ mg}.M(5)=902​≈127.3 mg.

At 444 hours it predicts 909090 mg. The observation is 92−90=292-90=292−90=2 mg above the prediction. This third reading tests the fit instead of merely repeating one of the readings used to construct it.

The formula has algebraic domain all real ttt, but elapsed experimental time has physical domain t≥0t\ge0t≥0. Even there, the model assumes the culture’s conditions remain suitable for the same growth factor.

Rates and characteristic times

A period rate and a coefficient in an exponent describe the same model in different ways. For example, 50(1.12)t50(1.12)^t50(1.12)t multiplies by 1.121.121.12 each year, so its effective yearly rate is 12%12\%12%, the actual fraction gained over one year.

To write that very same curve as 50ekt50e^{kt}50ekt, require ek=1.12e^k=1.12ek=1.12. Taking natural logarithms gives k=ln⁡1.12≈0.1133k=\ln1.12\approx0.1133k=ln1.12≈0.1133 per year. The coefficient is not the exact yearly percentage gain.

In general, with ttt measured in the stated period and P>0P>0P>0,

P(1+r)t=Pekt,k=ln⁡(1+r),P(1+r)^t=Pe^{kt},\qquad k=\ln(1+r),P(1+r)t=Pekt,k=ln(1+r), r=ek−1.r=e^k-1.r=ek−1.

Here r>−1r>-1r>−1, so the period factor is positive. The continuous coefficient kkk has reciprocal time units; it changes if you switch from years to months. These equal formulas describe a continuous curve. A process updated only at whole periods may use only its whole-period values.

Cooling toward an ambient temperature

Cooling can preserve a constant factor in the difference from the surroundings. In a room at 22∘C22^\circ\text{C}22∘C, an object at 86∘C86^\circ\text{C}86∘C is 64∘C64^\circ\text{C}64∘C above room temperature. At 54∘C54^\circ\text{C}54∘C its excess is 32∘C32^\circ\text{C}32∘C, half as large. The absolute temperature has not halved.

If ambient temperature TaT_aTa​ stays constant and the excess follows exponential decay, use

T(t)=Ta+(T0−Ta)ekt,k<0.T(t)=T_a+(T_0-T_a)e^{kt},\qquad k<0.T(t)=Ta​+(T0​−Ta​)ekt,k<0.

For cooling from above ambient, T0>TaT_0>T_aT0​>Ta​. Subtract TaT_aTa​ before forming ratios or taking logarithms. The model stays above ambient for every finite t≥0t\ge0t≥0; it does not predict crossing below it. Changing room temperature or moving air may require a different model.

Worked example 3 Fit a cooling model and find a threshold

Problem. A drink cools in a room held at 22∘C22^\circ\text{C}22∘C. It starts at 86∘C86^\circ\text{C}86∘C and reaches 54∘C54^\circ\text{C}54∘C after 444 minutes. Assume its excess temperature decays exponentially. Find a model for t≥0t\ge0t≥0 and the time at which it reaches 30∘C30^\circ\text{C}30∘C.

Solution. The excess drops from 646464 to 323232 degrees in 444 minutes, so its half-life is 444 minutes:

T(t)=22+64(1/2)t/4.T(t)=22+64(1/2)^{t/4}.T(t)=22+64(1/2)t/4.

At the target, subtract the ambient 222222 first:

(1/2)t/4=(1/2)3.(1/2)^{t/4}=(1/2)^3.(1/2)t/4=(1/2)3.

Thus t/4=3t/4=3t/4=3 and t=12t=12t=12 minutes. Checking gives 22+64/8=3022+64/8=3022+64/8=30 degrees.

Logarithmic models and logarithmic scales

A logarithmic model makes equal output changes correspond to equal input factors. Suppose inputs 1,3,91,3,91,3,9 give scores 4,7,104,7,104,7,10. Each tripling raises the score by three. Since log⁡31=0\log_3 1=0log3​1=0, log⁡33=1\log_3 3=1log3​3=1, and log⁡39=2\log_3 9=2log3​9=2, the rule 4+3log⁡3x4+3\log_3 x4+3log3​x fits the pattern.

More generally, y=A+Bln⁡xy=A+B\ln xy=A+Blnx has domain x>0x>0x>0. Multiplying input by c>0c>0c>0 adds Bln⁡cB\ln cBlnc to the output, because ln⁡(cx)=ln⁡c+ln⁡x\ln(cx)=\ln c+\ln xln(cx)=lnc+lnx. This is why a logarithmic response can rise while its gains over equal additive input steps get smaller.

A logarithmic scale places equal ratios at equal distances. On a base-ten vertical scale, 2,20,200,20002,20,200,20002,20,200,2000 are equally spaced because each is ten times the last. Zero and negative values cannot appear on a logarithmic axis.

A semi-log plot has one ordinary axis and one logarithmic axis. To test y=Pbxy=Pb^xy=Pbx with P>0P>0P>0, keep xxx ordinary and make the positive yyy axis logarithmic:

Compare predictions with data

The residual is the observed value minus the model’s predicted value. If a model predicts 141414 units and you observe 151515, the residual is +1+1+1 unit. A positive residual means the point lies above the model; a negative one means it lies below.

With many noisy readings, graphing software can fit parameters by minimizing such a total, a method called least squares. State which quantities it compares. Fitting a line to ln⁡y\ln ylny minimizes squared misses in log values, which need not choose the same parameters as minimizing squared misses in yyy.

Look for size and pattern together. Residuals that curve or stay mostly on one side suggest a systematic miss. Small residuals scattered around zero support a model, but do not establish its physical assumptions.

Both candidates’ nonzero residuals in the table stay on one side of zero: negative for LLL and positive for EEE. In these observations, LLL systematically overpredicts where it misses, while EEE systematically underpredicts. The smaller squared total for LLL does not remove that pattern. Four readings alone do not show whether it would persist in further noisy data.

Interpolation predicts between measured inputs. Extrapolation predicts outside their range. A value at t=1.5t=1.5t=1.5 days interpolates the table; one at t=30t=30t=30 days extrapolates. Neither is guaranteed, and extrapolation needs particular care because growth may slow, resources may run out, or the environment may change. A larger-looking graph window cannot supply missing evidence.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring elapsed time. A factor measured over four hours is not automatically an hourly factor.
  • Confusing rate conventions. In PektPe^{kt}Pekt, the effective period rate is ek−1e^k-1ek−1 for the same time unit.
  • Halving absolute temperature. In cooling, apply ratios to the excess above a constant ambient temperature.
  • Logging the wrong axis. Ordinary xxx and logarithmic yyy test exponential behavior; logarithmic xxx and ordinary yyy test logarithmic behavior.
  • Treating fit as proof. Check residuals, units, physical domain, and assumptions before extending a model.

Key takeaways

  • Fit an exponential factor from the output ratio and the time gap, then test unused measurements.
  • Convert equivalent period and continuous forms with k=ln⁡(1+r)k=\ln(1+r)k=ln(1+r) and r=ek−1r=e^k-1r=ek−1.
  • Doubling and half-life concern multiplicative change; cooling applies that change to the excess above ambient.
  • Read axis scales before interpreting straightness, and define residuals as observed minus predicted.
  • Compare candidate fits and distinguish interpolation from extrapolation; finite data cannot prove the chosen rule.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1946, chemist Willard Libby proposed estimating the age of once-living objects by measuring their carbon-14. The isotope decays after death, so the remaining amount could serve as a clock. The difficulty was detecting such a weak signal among the background radiation around the apparatus.

Libby’s group developed counters and shielding sensitive enough for the task. They then tested the clock on objects whose ages were already known, including wood dated by tree rings and timber from an Egyptian funerary boat. In 1949, Libby and James Arnold published a comparison called the “Curve of Knowns,” placing the radiocarbon estimates alongside the independent ages. The agreement helped establish a new way to investigate the past.

The American Chemical Society’s account of radiocarbon dating describes both the instrument problem and those early tests.

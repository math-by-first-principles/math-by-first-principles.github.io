Logarithmic Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

What exponent turns 222 into 888? Since 23=82^3=823=8, the answer is 333. An exponential takes an exponent and produces a positive number. A logarithm runs that journey backward: given 888 and base 222, it returns the exponent 333. Following the journey in either direction helps explain its notation, its domain, and its graph.

Learning goals

  • Translate between logarithmic and exponential statements while preserving the three numerical roles
  • Evaluate exact logarithms, including fractional values and common and natural logarithms
  • Check that logarithm bases and whole arguments meet their real-number restrictions
  • Apply both inverse identities while retaining the domains of the original expressions
  • Read logarithmic graphs from exchanged coordinates, domains, ranges, and asymptotes

Keep the base, recover the exponent

A logarithm names an exponent. For example, 23=82^3=823=8 can also be written log⁡28=3\log_2 8=3log2​8=3, read as “log base two of eight equals three.” The base stays 222; the positive result 888 becomes the input; the exponent 333 becomes the output.

The previous lesson established that btb^tbt is one-to-one and has range (0,∞)(0,\infty)(0,∞) when its base is positive and different from 111. Every positive output therefore identifies exactly one real exponent. This is why the reversed question defines a function.

For b>0b>0b>0 with b≠1b\ne1b=1 and u>0u>0u>0, the logarithm log⁡bu\log_b ulogb​u is the unique real exponent on bbb that produces uuu. Its input uuu is called its argument. Thus

y=log⁡bu⟺by=u.y=\log_b u\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^y=u.y=logb​u⟺by=u.

This works in both directions because the two statements describe the same power. The restriction is on the whole argument. For log⁡3(2−x)\log_3(2-x)log3​(2−x), it is 2−x2-x2−x that must be positive, allowing negative xxx as well as some positive xxx.

The excluded bases have concrete problems. At base 111, every exponent gives 111, so an output cannot identify a unique exponent. At base 000, negative exponents fail. A negative base such as −9-9−9 has no real value at exponent 1/21/21/2. These bases cannot supply the real exponential function we need to reverse. An argument of zero or a negative number also fails, because a power of a positive base never produces either.

4−3/2=1(4)3=18.4^{-3/2}=\frac{1}{(\sqrt4)^3}=\frac18.4−3/2=(4​)31​=81​.

Therefore log⁡4(1/8)=−3/2\log_4(1/8)=-3/2log4​(1/8)=−3/2.

Two logarithms with short names

Some bases are used often enough to have their own notation. Because 10−2=0.0110^{-2}=0.0110−2=0.01, the common logarithm, written log⁡\loglog with no base, gives log⁡0.01=−2\log 0.01=-2log0.01=−2. In this course, a missing base means 101010.

The previous lesson used the number e≈2.71828e\approx2.71828e≈2.71828 as an exponential base. The natural logarithm, written ln⁡\lnln, has base eee. Since e1/2=ee^{1/2}=\sqrt ee1/2=e​, we have ln⁡(e)=1/2\ln(\sqrt e)=1/2ln(e​)=1/2. “Natural” names the base; it does not change the inverse question.

Worked example 2 Match the notation to the base

Undo both journeys on the right domains

Going forward and then backward returns the exponent. For example, (1/3)−2=9(1/3)^{-2}=9(1/3)−2=9 and log⁡1/39=−2\log_{1/3}9=-2log1/3​9=−2. Going backward and then forward returns a positive argument: log⁡28=3\log_2 8=3log2​8=3 and 23=82^3=823=8.

In general, for a positive base b≠1b\ne1b=1, the two inverse identities are

log⁡b(bt)=tfor every real t,\log_b(b^t)=t\quad\text{for every real }t,logb​(bt)=tfor every real t,

and

blog⁡bu=ufor u>0.b^{\log_b u}=u\quad\text{for }u>0.blogb​u=ufor u>0.

The domains differ because btb^tbt is positive for every real ttt, while the logarithm in the second identity must receive a positive input before anything can undo it. Matching bases matter too: ln⁡(10t)\ln(10^t)ln(10t) does not directly undo a base-101010 power.

Exchange points and the graph’s features

An inverse graph exchanges inputs and outputs. Since 2−1=1/22^{-1}=1/22−1=1/2, the exponential point (−1,1/2)(-1,1/2)(−1,1/2) becomes the logarithmic point (1/2,−1)(1/2,-1)(1/2,−1). Likewise, (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) becomes (1,0)(1,0)(1,0), and (2,4)(2,4)(2,4) becomes (4,2)(4,2)(4,2). Swapping coordinates reflects the graph across y=xy=xy=x, with equal unit scales on the axes.

Choose base 222, then base 1/21/21/2. Both curves rise in the first case and fall in the second. Try base 111 too: the exponential becomes the horizontal line y=1y=1y=1, and the logarithm disappears. The coordinate exchange requires a one-to-one original function.

The same exchange explains the allowed inputs, possible outputs, and asymptotes for every valid base:

The exponential’s positive outputs become the logarithm’s positive inputs. Its horizontal asymptote reflects to a vertical asymptote, so the logarithm never reaches the yyy-axis. The direction stays the same: reading a strictly increasing or decreasing input-output pairing backward preserves that direction.

For a decreasing base, the direction near the vertical asymptote deserves a separate look. Powers (1/3)1=1/3(1/3)^1=1/3(1/3)1=1/3, (1/3)2=1/9(1/3)^2=1/9(1/3)2=1/9, and (1/3)3=1/27(1/3)^3=1/27(1/3)3=1/27 give logarithm points (1/3,1)(1/3,1)(1/3,1), (1/9,2)(1/9,2)(1/9,2), and (1/27,3)(1/27,3)(1/27,3). Smaller positive inputs have larger outputs.

When b>1b>1b>1, log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x falls without bound as xxx approaches zero from the right and rises without bound as xxx grows. When 0<b<10<b<10<b<1, it rises without bound near zero from the right and falls without bound as xxx grows. In both cases, its range includes every real number. A negative output is allowed; a nonpositive argument is not.

Change the argument, then check the graph

A transformed argument changes which original inputs the logarithm sees. For h(x)=log⁡2(4−x)h(x)=\log_2(4-x)h(x)=log2​(4−x), input 333 sends argument 111 to the logarithm, giving h(3)=0h(3)=0h(3)=0. Input 222 sends 222, giving h(2)=1h(2)=1h(2)=1. Following the argument prevents a shift from being mistaken for a new domain rule.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reversed roles: in log⁡bu=y\log_b u=ylogb​u=y, the base remains bbb and the exponent is yyy, giving by=ub^y=uby=u.
  • Argument versus output: logarithms need positive arguments but can return negative numbers or zero.
  • Invalid bases: a real logarithm requires a positive base other than 111.
  • Lost domain: undoing a logarithm does not create values where the original logarithm was undefined.
  • Near-zero direction: a base below 111 gives a decreasing logarithm, rising without bound near zero from the right.

Key takeaways

  • Translate y=log⁡buy=\log_b uy=logb​u to by=ub^y=uby=u, with b>0b>0b>0, b≠1b\ne1b=1, and u>0u>0u>0.
  • Common logarithm: log⁡\loglog uses base 101010. Natural logarithm: ln⁡\lnln uses base eee.
  • Recover the exponent with log⁡b(bt)=t\log_b(b^t)=tlogb​(bt)=t for every real ttt, and recover the argument with blog⁡bu=ub^{\log_b u}=ublogb​u=u for u>0u>0u>0.
  • Exchange coordinates, domain and range, and horizontal and vertical asymptotes when reversing an exponential.
  • Read direction from the base, and check a transformed logarithm’s whole argument before describing its graph.

A bit of history (optional)

John Napier published his work on logarithms in 1614 after years spent seeking ways to shorten difficult arithmetic. Astronomers, navigators, and surveyors benefited from the new tables. Napier’s original logarithms differed from the base-101010 form familiar today. Henry Briggs and his team undertook the labor of calculating revised tables, and Briggs published base-101010 logarithms in 1617.

National Museums Scotland describes Napier’s calculating inventions and the early logarithm tables.

Exponential and Logarithmic Equations

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

At what input does 2x2^x2x reach 777? The values 22=42^2=422=4 and 23=82^3=823=8 locate the answer between 222 and 333, but neither gives it exactly. A logarithm names the missing input. Solving equations means finding where outputs agree; solving inequalities means finding which side of a crossing gives the required order. In both, you must keep track of the inputs the original expressions accept.

Learning goals

  • Solve common-base equations by matching exponents, including bases between zero and one
  • Use logarithms to recover unknown exponents and report exact and approximate answers
  • Exponentiate logarithmic equations and check every candidate against the original arguments
  • Substitute a positive exponential quantity to solve equations with repeated powers
  • Determine inequality intervals using domains and the direction of each exponential or logarithm

Match a common base

Equal outputs identify equal exponents when the base is fixed and valid. For instance, 4x=84^x=84x=8 becomes 22x=232^{2x}=2^322x=23, so 2x=32x=32x=3 and x=3/2x=3/2x=3/2. The exponential does not repeat an output, so the exponents cannot differ.

For any positive base bbb different from 111, and real exponents u,vu,vu,v,

bu=bv⟺u=v.b^u=b^v\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u=v.bu=bv⟺u=v.

This uses the one-to-one property from Logarithmic Functions. It works for decreasing bases too: (1/2)2=(1/2)v(1/2)^2=(1/2)^v(1/2)2=(1/2)v forces v=2v=2v=2. Increasing is needed to preserve order, but being one-to-one is enough to preserve equality. Base 111 fails because every exponent gives the same output.

Worked example 1 Rewrite the bases before matching

Problem. Solve (1/9)x−1=272x+1(1/9)^{x-1}=27^{2x+1}(1/9)x−1=272x+1 over the real numbers. Verify the result in the original equation.

Solution. Both bases are powers of 333: 1/9=3−21/9=3^{-2}1/9=3−2 and 27=3327=3^327=33. The power-of-a-power rule gives

3−2x+2=36x+3.3^{-2x+2}=3^{6x+3}.3−2x+2=36x+3.

The exponents must agree:

−2x+2=6x+3,-2x+2=6x+3,−2x+2=6x+3,

so −8x=1-8x=1−8x=1 and x=−1/8x=-1/8x=−1/8.

For the check, the original left side is (3−2)−9/8=39/4(3^{-2})^{-9/8}=3^{9/4}(3−2)−9/8=39/4. The original right side is (33)3/4=39/4(3^3)^{3/4}=3^{9/4}(33)3/4=39/4. They agree exactly.

Thus 2x=62x=62x=6 and x=3x=3x=3. Both original exponents are then 7.

Use a logarithm for the missing exponent

The crossing in the opening question has an exact name: x=log⁡27x=\log_2 7x=log2​7. Since 2x2^x2x is always positive, both sides of 2x=72^x=72x=7 have a natural logarithm. Taking it and using the power rule gives

xln⁡2=ln⁡7.x\ln2=\ln7.xln2=ln7.

Dividing by the nonzero number ln⁡2\ln2ln2 gives x=ln⁡7/ln⁡2≈2.807x=\ln7/\ln2\approx2.807x=ln7/ln2≈2.807. The exact logarithm names the input; the decimal estimates it.

The same reasoning works for bu=cb^u=cbu=c when b>0b>0b>0, b≠1b\ne1b=1, and c>0c>0c>0:

u=ln⁡cln⁡b.u=\frac{\ln c}{\ln b}.u=lnblnc​.

Use the same logarithm base on the top and bottom. If an isolated positive-base power equals zero or a negative number, there is no real solution; taking its logarithm is not an available step. First isolate the whole exponential term, since ln⁡(A+B)\ln(A+B)ln(A+B) cannot be split into ln⁡A+ln⁡B\ln A+\ln BlnA+lnB.

Worked example 2 Separate the exact answer from its estimate

Problem. Solve 3⋅52t−1−4=203\cdot5^{2t-1}-4=203⋅52t−1−4=20 for real ttt. Give an exact answer and an approximation to three decimal places, and check its size before using a calculator.

Solution. Add 444, then divide by 333:

52t−1=8.5^{2t-1}=8.52t−1=8.

Because 5<8<255<8<255<8<25, the exponent 2t−12t-12t−1 is between 111 and 222. Therefore 1<t<3/21<t<3/21<t<3/2.

Both sides are positive. Taking natural logarithms gives

(2t−1)ln⁡5=ln⁡8.(2t-1)\ln5=\ln8.(2t−1)ln5=ln8.

Solve the linear equation for ttt:

t=1+ln⁡8/ln⁡52≈1.146.t=\frac{1+\ln8/\ln5}{2}\approx1.146.t=21+ln8/ln5​≈1.146.

Keep the logarithmic expression for the exact answer. At that unrounded input, 52t−1=5log⁡58=85^{2t-1}=5^{\log_5 8}=852t−1=5log5​8=8, so the original left side is 3(8)−4=203(8)-4=203(8)−4=20. The approximation lies in the predicted interval.

Answer choices

3x=ln⁡43x=\ln43x=ln4

Thus x=ln⁡4/3≈0.462x=\ln4/3\approx0.462x=ln4/3≈0.462. The logarithm is exact; the displayed decimal is rounded.

Undo a logarithm, retain its original domain

Exponentiating reverses a logarithm. For example, log⁡2(5−x)=3\log_2(5-x)=3log2​(5−x)=3 says 5−x=23=85-x=2^3=85−x=23=8, giving x=−3x=-3x=−3. This negative input is allowed because its argument is 8>08>08>0. Positivity applies to the argument, not to the variable.

For b>0b>0b>0 and b≠1b\ne1b=1, the statement log⁡bU=k\log_b U=klogb​U=k is equivalent to U=bkU=b^kU=bk. The right side is positive, so any solution of this last equation automatically makes that one logarithm legal. With several original logarithms, however, combining them can hide forbidden inputs.

For example, at x=−4x=-4x=−4 the two numbers x−1=−5x-1=-5x−1=−5 and x+3=−1x+3=-1x+3=−1 are both negative, while their product is 5>05>05>0. The logarithm of their product exists; the two separate logarithms do not. The product rule is valid only while both original arguments are positive.

Worked example 3 Check every original argument

Problem. Solve log⁡3(x−1)+log⁡3(x+3)=log⁡3(2x+9)\log_3(x-1)+\log_3(x+3)=\log_3(2x+9)log3​(x−1)+log3​(x+3)=log3​(2x+9) over the real numbers. List all algebraic candidates and explain any rejection.

Solution. The original arguments require x>1x>1x>1, x>−3x>-3x>−3, and x>−9/2x>-9/2x>−9/2. Together they require x>1x>1x>1.

On that domain, combine the left side and then equate the positive arguments:

(x−1)(x+3)=2x+9.(x-1)(x+3)=2x+9.(x−1)(x+3)=2x+9.

Expanding gives x2+2x−3=2x+9x^2+2x-3=2x+9x2+2x−3=2x+9, so x2=12x^2=12x2=12. The candidates are x=23x=2\sqrt3x=23​ and x=−23x=-2\sqrt3x=−23​.

At x=23x=2\sqrt3x=23​, all three original arguments are positive. The product on the left is 9+439+4\sqrt39+43​, equal to the right argument, so it is a solution.

At x=−23x=-2\sqrt3x=−23​, the first two arguments are negative, since 23>32\sqrt3>323​>3. Those logarithms are undefined. This is an extraneous candidate, a value satisfying the derived equation but not the original one. Only x=23x=2\sqrt3x=23​ remains.

Answer choices

Thus x+1=3x+1=3x+1=3 or x+1=−3x+1=-3x+1=−3, giving x=2x=2x=2 or x=−4x=-4x=−4. Each original argument is 9, so both values work.

Replace a repeated exponential by one quantity

A repeated exponential can hide an ordinary polynomial equation. Since e2x=(ex)2e^{2x}=(e^x)^2e2x=(ex)2, replacing exe^xex by uuu turns e2x−5ex+4=0e^{2x}-5e^x+4=0e2x−5ex+4=0 into u2−5u+4=0u^2-5u+4=0u2−5u+4=0. This is a substitution: one new name stands for an expression that appears repeatedly.

Worked example 4 Return from the substituted quantity

Problem. Find all real solutions of e2x−5ex+4=0e^{2x}-5e^x+4=0e2x−5ex+4=0. Explain why no real solution is lost by the substitution.

Solution. Put u=exu=e^xu=ex. The exponential takes every positive value exactly once, so the permitted substitution values are precisely u>0u>0u>0.

The equation becomes

u2−5u+4=(u−1)(u−4)=0.u^2-5u+4=(u-1)(u-4)=0.u2−5u+4=(u−1)(u−4)=0.

Both roots are positive. The root u=1u=1u=1 gives x=0x=0x=0; the root u=4u=4u=4 gives x=ln⁡4x=\ln4x=ln4.

At x=0x=0x=0, the original left side is 1−5+4=01-5+4=01−5+4=0. At x=ln⁡4x=\ln4x=ln4, it is 16−20+4=016-20+4=016−20+4=0. Every real xxx supplies a positive uuu, and each positive root supplies one real xxx, so these are all the solutions.

For any positive base b≠1b\ne1b=1, the substitution u=bxu=b^xu=bx has u>0u>0u>0. A polynomial root u=0u=0u=0 or u<0u<0u<0 gives no real xxx. If the problem also restricts xxx, translate that restriction into a smaller allowed interval for uuu before selecting roots.

Follow order when solving inequalities

For equations, increasing and decreasing bases both identify a unique exponent. Inequalities also need the direction. Since 21<232^1<2^321<23, base 222 preserves order. Since (1/2)1>(1/2)3(1/2)^1>(1/2)^3(1/2)1>(1/2)3, base 1/21/21/2 reverses it.

In general, for real u,vu,vu,v, a base b>1b>1b>1 gives bu<bvb^u<b^vbu<bv exactly when u<vu<vu<v. A base 0<b<10<b<10<b<1 gives bu<bvb^u<b^vbu<bv exactly when u>vu>vu>v. The same direction rules hold for logarithms of positive arguments. Replace strict comparisons by non-strict comparisons when equality is allowed.

For a target that is not a familiar power, logarithms give the boundary. Solving (0.8)t<0.3(0.8)^t<0.3(0.8)t<0.3 gives

tln⁡(0.8)<ln⁡(0.3).t\ln(0.8)<\ln(0.3).tln(0.8)<ln(0.3).

Because ln⁡(0.8)<0\ln(0.8)<0ln(0.8)<0, dividing reverses the inequality:

t>ln⁡(0.3)ln⁡(0.8).t>\frac{\ln(0.3)}{\ln(0.8)}.t>ln(0.8)ln(0.3)​.

The exact boundary is approximately 5.3965.3965.396. If ttt counts whole steps, the first accepted step is 666. Check adjacent steps: (0.8)5=0.32768>0.3(0.8)^5=0.32768>0.3(0.8)5=0.32768>0.3, while (0.8)6=0.262144<0.3(0.8)^6=0.262144<0.3(0.8)6=0.262144<0.3.

Logarithmic inequalities require the original domain as well as the comparison. For log⁡1/2(x+1)≤−2\log_{1/2}(x+1)\le-2log1/2​(x+1)≤−2, first require x+1>0x+1>0x+1>0. Because the logarithm decreases and (1/2)−2=4(1/2)^{-2}=4(1/2)−2=4, the output condition means x+1≥4x+1\ge4x+1≥4. Intersecting this with x>−1x>-1x>−1 gives x≥3x\ge3x≥3. At the boundary the logarithm is exactly −2-2−2.

First require 5−2x>05-2x>05−2x>0, so x<5/2x<5/2x<5/2. The decreasing logarithm gives 5−2x<35-2x<35−2x<3, since (1/3)−1=3(1/3)^{-1}=3(1/3)−1=3.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Matching different bases. Rewrite as one shared base before equating exponents, or take logarithms instead.
  • Taking a logarithm too early. Isolate the exponential term and check that its required value is positive.
  • Checking only the combined argument. A positive product does not guarantee that each original logarithm exists.
  • Keeping impossible substitution roots. A quantity representing bxb^xbx must be positive, and must satisfy any input restriction.
  • Keeping the same inequality direction. A decreasing exponential or logarithm reverses order; dividing by a negative logarithm does too.

Key takeaways

  • Match exponents on a shared positive base different from 111, whether that base increases or decreases.
  • For bu=cb^u=cbu=c with c>0c>0c>0, recover u=ln⁡c/ln⁡bu=\ln c/\ln bu=lnc/lnb and keep that exact form until rounding.
  • Write every original logarithm’s positive-argument restriction before combining, then test the candidates.
  • Replace repeated powers with a positive quantity, solve, and return to the original variable.
  • Use function direction to solve inequalities, then intersect with the original and contextual domains.

A bit of history (optional)

Henry Briggs was already making tables for navigation when he read John Napier’s book on logarithms, published in 1614. The new method promised to shorten the heavy calculations used in astronomy. In 1615, Briggs traveled from London to Edinburgh to discuss it with Napier, a journey that took days by horse and coach.

They discussed a more convenient system using base ten. Briggs then undertook the long arithmetic needed to produce tables. His 1624 book listed logarithms to fourteen decimal places for the integers from 1 to 20,000 and from 90,000 to 100,000. Briggs suggested that a team calculate the missing entries and offered specially prepared paper. An expanded edition appeared in 1628 with Adriaan Vlacq’s work filling that gap.

The University of St Andrews’ MacTutor biography of Briggs recounts the visits and the table-making project.

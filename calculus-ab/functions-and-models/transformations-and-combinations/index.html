Transformations and Combinations of Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Suppose a sensor produces a reading from a temperature. You could add a correction to the reading, change the temperature supplied to the sensor, or feed its reading into a second calculation. These produce different functions. Keeping track of what happens before and after the original function tells you both the new values and which inputs still work.

Learning goals

  • Describe shifts, reflections, and scaling by tracking changes to graph coordinates
  • Construct transformed formulas and determine their domains from the original function
  • Combine functions arithmetically while retaining all original restrictions and denominator exclusions
  • Evaluate compositions in the stated order and find their exact domains
  • Decompose nested expressions and match output units to the next function's input

Change an output or change an input

Adding to a function’s output moves its graph vertically. Changing its input can move the graph horizontally.

Suppose f(1)=4f(1)=4f(1)=4. The function p(x)=f(x)+3p(x)=f(x)+3p(x)=f(x)+3 has p(1)=7p(1)=7p(1)=7: the point (1,4)(1,4)(1,4) becomes (1,7)(1,7)(1,7). But q(x)=f(x−3)q(x)=f(x-3)q(x)=f(x−3) reaches the old output 444 at x=4x=4x=4, because 4−3=14-3=14−3=1. Its corresponding point is (4,4)(4,4)(4,4).

A transformation changes a function by modifying its inputs or outputs. In a vertical shift, f(x)+kf(x)+kf(x)+k, every output increases by kkk. In a horizontal shift, f(x−h)f(x-h)f(x−h), the new input must be hhh larger to supply the same old input to fff.

Thus f(x)+kf(x)+kf(x)+k moves the graph up kkk units when kkk is positive and down ∣k∣|k|∣k∣ units when kkk is negative. The graph of f(x−h)f(x-h)f(x−h) moves right hhh units when hhh is positive and left ∣h∣|h|∣h∣ units when hhh is negative. To check the horizontal direction, solve for the new input rather than guessing from the sign.

Scale and reflect coordinates

Multiplication outside a function scales heights; multiplication inside scales horizontal coordinates in the opposite way.

If (6,5)(6,5)(6,5) is on the graph of fff, then (6,10)(6,10)(6,10) is on y=2f(x)y=2f(x)y=2f(x). Feed one output into the next function

Composition uses the output of one function as the input to another. Suppose s(t)=3ts(t)=3ts(t)=3t converts hours of machine operation into meters of ribbon, and c(m)=m2c(m)=m^2c(m)=m2 gives a numerical cost in dollars for mmm meters under a simplified pricing model. At t=2t=2t=2, first find s(2)=6s(2)=6s(2)=6, then c(6)=36c(6)=36c(6)=36. The cost is c(s(2))c(s(2))c(s(2)), not c(2)s(2)c(2)s(2)c(2)s(2).

We write

(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)).(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)).(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)).

Read this as ”fff composed with ggg.” The inner function ggg acts first. Substitution means replacing every input slot of fff with the entire expression g(x)g(x)g(x), including parentheses where needed.

In a model, the output units of ggg must match the input units expected by fff. The composite accepts the input units of ggg and produces the output units of fff.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Horizontal direction. In f(x−h)f(x-h)f(x−h), solve x−h=ux-h=ux−h=u to locate the point that used to have input uuu.
  • Horizontal factor. In f(bx)f(bx)f(bx), divide old horizontal coordinates by bbb; multiplying them by bbb reverses the scale.
  • Cancellation. Keep every original domain exclusion when a formula simplifies.
  • Composition order. In f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)), evaluate ggg first and check that its output is allowed for fff.
  • Units. A numerical substitution can exist even when its output units do not fit the next model’s input.

Key takeaways

  • Changes outside fff act on outputs; changes inside fff act on the inputs it receives.
  • For nonzero a,ba,ba,b, the point (u,v)(u,v)(u,v) becomes (h+u/b,av+k)(h+u/b,av+k)(h+u/b,av+k) under af(b(x−h))+kaf(b(x-h))+kaf(b(x−h))+k.
  • Arithmetic combinations use a shared input in both domains, with denominator zeros excluded for division.
  • Composition follows the inner function and then the outer function, retaining restrictions from both stages.
  • A decomposition identifies the stages of a nested expression; in models, check the units between stages.

A bit of history (optional)

In his 1917 book On Growth and Form, the biologist D’Arcy Wentworth Thompson tried an unusual way of comparing animals. Instead of describing each difference in words, he drew an animal on a coordinate grid and changed the grid itself.

One striking example began with a porcupinefish. Thompson curved its vertical grid lines into circles and bent the horizontal lines into another family of curves. Transferring the fish’s outline point by point onto that grid produced a shape resembling a sunfish, with a much deeper body and a shortened tail. The head changed far less than the rear of the body.

Thompson acknowledged that this particular grid deformation was not mathematically perfect. He presented it as a comparison of forms, not a record of one fish physically turning into another. The original discussion and drawings are in his book (chapter XVII, figures 381 and 382).

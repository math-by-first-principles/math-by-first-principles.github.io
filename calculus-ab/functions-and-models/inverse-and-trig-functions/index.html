Inverse Functions and Trigonometry

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Squaring 444 gives 161616. Trying to reverse that instruction brings back both 444 and −4-4−4. To get one answer, you need more information about which inputs were allowed. The same problem appears when a sine value comes from many angles. An inverse function needs a rule that makes the return trip unambiguous.

Learning goals

  • Determine whether a function is one-to-one from its values or graph
  • Find restricted inverses and identify their domains, ranges, and graph points
  • Use radians and six trig functions with their domains, identities, and periods
  • Evaluate inverse trigonometric functions within their standard principal angle ranges
  • Simplify inverse compositions while checking input domains and output restrictions

When can a function be reversed?

A function can have an inverse function when each output remembers exactly one input. For example, the table below loses no information when its arrows are reversed.

Input xxx−2-2−2000333
Output f(x)f(x)f(x)777222−5-5−5

The output 222 identifies the input 000, and the other outputs each identify one input too. A one-to-one function sends different inputs to different outputs. This table describes a one-to-one function on the three listed inputs. It tells us nothing about unlisted inputs.

By contrast, p(x)=x2p(x)=x^2p(x)=x2 on all real numbers is not one-to-one. The horizontal line y=4y=4y=4 meets its graph at (−2,4)(-2,4)(−2,4) and (2,4)(2,4)(2,4). Reversing those pairs would send one input, 444, to two outputs, −2-2−2 and 222. Keeping only x≥0x\geq0x≥0 removes the left intersection. Each height then occurs at most once.

The graphical version is the horizontal line test: a function is one-to-one exactly when every horizontal line meets its graph at most once. Two intersections would mean two inputs with the same output. The vertical line test asks a different question, whether the graph is a function in the first place. A function that is strictly increasing throughout its domain, or strictly decreasing throughout its domain, is one-to-one because its outputs cannot repeat.

Inverse notation, domains, and restrictions

An inverse switches the roles of input and output. If f(3)=11f(3)=11f(3)=11 and fff is one-to-one, then f−1(11)=3f^{-1}(11)=3f−1(11)=3. The notation f−1f^{-1}f−1 means the inverse function, the function that returns each attained output to its original input. It does not mean the reciprocal 1/f1/f1/f. For f(x)=2x+5f(x)=2x+5f(x)=2x+5, for instance, f−1(11)=3f^{-1}(11)=3f−1(11)=3, while 1/f(11)=1/271/f(11)=1/271/f(11)=1/27.

If fff has domain DDD and range RRR, its inverse has domain RRR and range DDD. The inverse accepts precisely the outputs fff actually reaches. Its two reversal identities are

f−1(f(x))=xfor x∈D,f^{-1}(f(x))=x \quad \text{for }x\in D,f−1(f(x))=xfor x∈D, f(f−1(y))=yfor y∈R.f(f^{-1}(y))=y \quad \text{for }y\in R.f(f−1(y))=yfor y∈R.

These conditions belong to the identities. An input outside RRR cannot go into f−1f^{-1}f−1. On a graph, a point (a,b)(a,b)(a,b) on fff becomes (b,a)(b,a)(b,a) on f−1f^{-1}f−1. Exchanging horizontal and vertical coordinates reflects the graph across the line y=xy=xy=x.

A domain restriction keeps only selected inputs of a function. For p(x)=x2p(x)=x^2p(x)=x2, keeping x≥0x\geq 0x≥0 removes the negative partner of every positive input. Now each output has one source and the inverse is p−1(x)=xp^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x}p−1(x)=x​ for x≥0x\geq 0x≥0. Keeping x≤0x\leq 0x≤0 instead gives a different inverse, p−1(x)=−xp^{-1}(x)=-\sqrt{x}p−1(x)=−x​ for x≥0x\geq0x≥0. Both restrictions work, but they define different functions.

Radians and the unit circle

Radians measure a turn by comparing an arc with the circle’s radius. An arc of length 999 on a circle of radius 666 subtends an angle of 9/6=3/29/6=3/29/6=3/2 radians. Scaling both lengths leaves the ratio unchanged, which makes it a measure of the angle. For a nonnegative turn, arc length sss and radius r>0r>0r>0 give radian measure θ=s/r\theta=s/rθ=s/r. For directed turns, counterclockwise is positive and clockwise is negative. The ordinary arc length traveled is then s=r∣θ∣s=r|\theta|s=r∣θ∣, so a clockwise turn still covers a positive distance.

A positive full turn has arc length 2πr2\pi r2πr, so it is 2π2\pi2π radians. Thus 180∘=π180^\circ=\pi180∘=π radians. Multiply degrees by π/180\pi/180π/180 to convert to radians. Angles in this course are in radians unless a degree symbol or other unit is stated.

On the unit circle, the circle of radius 111 centered at the origin, the point reached by an angle θ\thetaθ is (cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)(cosθ,sinθ). Cosine is the horizontal coordinate and sine is the vertical coordinate. Both exist for every real angle and have range [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1].

The familiar special triangles give the first-quadrant values below. A 45∘45^\circ45∘ right triangle has equal legs, giving coordinates 2/2\sqrt2/22​/2. For an angle whose terminal side is not on an axis, its reference angle is the acute angle between that side and the horizontal axis. Use it to find the coordinate magnitudes, then choose their signs from the quadrant.

The remaining four trig functions use ratios of these coordinates. For example, cos⁡(π/3)=1/2\cos(\pi/3)=1/2cos(π/3)=1/2, so its reciprocal is sec⁡(π/3)=2\sec(\pi/3)=2sec(π/3)=2. The secant takes the reciprocal of cosine; the cosecant takes the reciprocal of sine. The tangent divides sine by cosine, while the cotangent divides cosine by sine. Every denominator must be nonzero.

Adding 2π2\pi2π makes a full turn and returns to the same point. Angles with the same terminal point are coterminal angles; their measures differ by an integer multiple of 2π2\pi2π. Principal inverse trigonometric values

To reverse a repeating trigonometric function, choose one interval that produces each output once. For example, both π/6\pi/6π/6 and 5π/65\pi/65π/6 have sine 1/21/21/2 in the figure. The function arcsin⁡\arcsinarcsin returns π/6\pi/6π/6, because we restrict sine to [−π/2,π/2][-\pi/2,\pi/2][−π/2,π/2] before reversing it. Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Inverse versus reciprocal: f−1f^{-1}f−1 reverses input and output; 1/f1/f1/f takes a reciprocal.
  • Missing restrictions: solving for an inverse formula does not choose a square-root sign or supply its domain.
  • Trig denominators: use each function’s own domain. Cotangent can be defined where tangent is not.
  • Principal ranges: an angle with the right trig value is not enough; it must belong to the inverse function’s output range.
  • Automatic cancellation: check the inner function’s domain and the outer inverse’s principal range before simplifying a composition.

Key takeaways

  • One-to-one functions have no repeated outputs from different inputs and pass the horizontal line test.
  • Inverse functions exchange domain and range, and their graphs reflect across y=xy=xy=x.
  • Radians and trig values: use (cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)(cosθ,sinθ) and the six definitions, retaining nonzero-denominator conditions and fundamental periods.
  • Principal ranges are [−π/2,π/2][-\pi/2,\pi/2][−π/2,π/2] for arcsine, [0,π][0,\pi][0,π] for arccosine, and (−π/2,π/2)(-\pi/2,\pi/2)(−π/2,π/2) for arctangent.
  • Evaluate compositions from the inside outward and keep every domain and range restriction.

A bit of history (optional)

For decades, the radian sat in an awkward category in the international measurement system. When the General Conference on Weights and Measures established the SI in 1960, it listed base units, derived units, and a third category called supplementary units. That small third category held just the radian and the steradian, the unit for solid angle.

The classification left a question unresolved. Were these two units independent building blocks, or were they formed from other measurements? In 1980, the International Committee for Weights and Measures interpreted them as dimensionless derived units. In 1995, the General Conference approved that interpretation and abolished the separate category. The radian kept its name and symbol, while the extra box in the classification disappeared.

The BIPM preserves the original 1960 resolution and the 1995 decision describing the debate.

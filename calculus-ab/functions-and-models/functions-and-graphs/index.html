Functions and Their Graphs

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A reservoir’s water level can be described by readings, a graph, or a formula. Each description helps answer different questions. A table gives particular measurements; a graph makes rises and falls visible; a formula can supply values between readings if the model is justified. Before using any of them, decide what the inputs mean and which values the description actually covers.

Learning goals

  • Identify functions and connect their verbal, numerical, graphical, and algebraic representations
  • Determine domains and ranges while respecting formula restrictions and model boundaries
  • Evaluate piecewise functions and interpret included or excluded graph endpoints
  • Read intercepts, symmetry, and increasing or decreasing intervals from complete graphs
  • Calculate average rates with units and distinguish signed position change from total distance

One input determines one output

A function makes the output unambiguous once you choose an allowed input. Suppose a sensor records a temperature of 181818 degrees at noon and 212121 degrees at 1 p.m. Different times give different temperatures. It could also record 181818 degrees again at 2 p.m. Repeated outputs cause no problem: each time still has one recorded temperature.

A function assigns exactly one output to every input in its domain, the set of allowed inputs. Its range is the set of outputs it actually attains. The input is also called the independent variable; the output is the dependent variable.

Let T(h)T(h)T(h) be the recorded temperature at hhh hours after midnight. Then T(13)=21T(13)=21T(13)=21 says the temperature at 1 p.m. is 212121 degrees. Read T(13)T(13)T(13) as “T of thirteen.” The parentheses identify an input; they do not mean multiplication by 131313.

The pairs (2,7)(2,7)(2,7) and (5,7)(5,7)(5,7) can belong to one function. The pairs (2,7)(2,7)(2,7) and (2,9)(2,9)(2,9) cannot: they would assign two different outputs to the same input. An equation need not define one variable as a function of another. For example, y2=xy^2=xy2=x allows both y=2y=2y=2 and y=−2y=-2y=−2 when x=4x=4x=4. Without an additional choice of branch, it does not define yyy as a function of xxx.

Four ways to represent a function

Allowed inputs and attained outputs

A formula’s operations and a situation’s meaning can both restrict its inputs. For q(x)=x+5/(x−2)q(x)=\sqrt{x+5}/(x-2)q(x)=x+5​/(x−2), the input −6-6−6 fails because it would require a real square root of −1-1−1. The input 222 fails because the denominator would be zero. The input −5-5−5 works: its numerator is zero and its denominator is −7-7−7.

Throughout this chapter, inputs and outputs are real unless stated otherwise. When no domain is supplied for a formula, use all real inputs for which its operations are defined. An even root needs a nonnegative radicand, the expression under the root. A denominator must be nonzero. Apply all restrictions together. A stated domain or a model’s context can narrow that set further.

For qqq, these restrictions give x≥−5x\ge-5x≥−5 and x≠2x\ne2x=2, so its domain is

[−5,2)∪(2,∞).[-5,2)\cup(2,\infty).[−5,2)∪(2,∞).

A square bracket includes an endpoint and a parenthesis excludes it. The union symbol ∪\cup∪ joins allowed pieces. Infinity describes an unbounded direction, not an included number, so it always takes a parenthesis.

To find a range, ask which outputs occur as the input varies over the stated domain. For s(x)=x2s(x)=x^2s(x)=x2 with −1≤x≤3-1\le x\le3−1≤x≤3, the smallest output is 000, attained at x=0x=0x=0. The largest is 999, attained at x=3x=3x=3. Every output between them occurs because each yyy from 000 to 999 is produced by the allowed input x=yx=\sqrt yx=y​. The range is [0,9][0,9][0,9], not the interval made from the two endpoint outputs 111 and 999.

Read the whole graph and its endpoints

A graph shows both input restrictions and output behavior, provided you know whether it shows the complete graph or only a viewing window. The following is the complete graph of ggg; its two pieces are straight segments.

Piecewise rules choose a formula by input

One function can use different rules on different parts of its domain. A study room costs 555 dollars for any booking up to one hour, then 222 dollars per additional hour, with fractional additional hours charged proportionally. A booking lasts more than zero and at most four hours. Its cost is

B(t)={5,0<t≤1,5+2(t−1),1<t≤4.B(t)= \begin{cases} 5,&0<t\le1,\\ 5+2(t-1),&1<t\le4. \end{cases}B(t)={5,5+2(t−1),​0<t≤1,1<t≤4.​

For t=0.5t=0.5t=0.5, use the first rule, giving B(0.5)=5B(0.5)=5B(0.5)=5.

For t=2.5t=2.5t=2.5, use the second rule, giving B(2.5)=5+2(1.5)=8B(2.5)=5+2(1.5)=8B(2.5)=5+2(1.5)=8.

At t=1t=1t=1, the first condition includes equality, so B(1)=5B(1)=5B(1)=5. The conditions tell you which rule to use before you calculate.

Overlapping rules can still give one output. At input 333, the rules 2x−32x-32x−3 and x+x−3x+x-3x+x−3 both give 333. Suppose the first rule applies when x≤5x\le5x≤5 and the second when x≥2x\ge2x≥2. Their conditions overlap on [2,5][2,5][2,5], and the rules agree throughout it because x+x−3=2x−3x+x-3=2x-3x+x−3=2x−3.

Now replace the second rule by 4x−74x-74x−7, keeping those conditions. At input 222, both rules still give 111.

At input 333, however, the first gives 333 and the replacement gives 555. This assigns two different outputs to one input. Agreement at just one shared input is not enough.

A piecewise function specifies rules for different input sets. Together the conditions must assign exactly one output to each input in the stated domain. Overlapping conditions are valid only if their rules agree on every shared input. Gaps are allowed only when those inputs are outside the domain. The pieces need not meet to define a function.

Change across an interval

Average change compares a net output change with the input span over which it happened. Suppose a reservoir contains 120120120 cubic meters at time 111 hour and 848484 cubic meters at time 555 hours. Its volume changes by 84−120=−3684-120=-3684−120=−36 cubic meters over 5−1=45-1=45−1=4 hours. Dividing gives an average rate of −9-9−9 cubic meters per hour. The negative sign describes a net loss. It does not say the reservoir lost water at that rate throughout the interval.

For any distinct inputs aaa and bbb at which fff is defined, the average rate of change from aaa to bbb is

f(b)−f(a)b−a.\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.b−af(b)−f(a)​.

Subtract outputs in the same order as inputs. The denominator must be nonzero, and the units are output units per input unit. Endpoint data suffice for the computation even if no values between the endpoints are known.

On a graph, the line through (a,f(a))(a,f(a))(a,f(a)) and (b,f(b))(b,f(b))(b,f(b)) is a secant line. Its slope is rise divided by run, the same quotient. For f(x)=x2/2f(x)=x^2/2f(x)=x2/2 from 111 to 333, the rise is 4.5−0.5=44.5-0.5=44.5−0.5=4 and the run is 3−1=23-1=23−1=2.

The average rate is 4/2=24/2=24/2=2. The secant measures the change between its two endpoints; the curve between those points need not follow the secant.

Signed position and total distance

Position records where an object is relative to a chosen origin; it can be positive, zero, or negative. Its change records the net movement in the chosen positive direction. Total distance traveled instead adds the lengths of all parts of the trip. Retracing a segment adds distance even when it cancels some position change.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Repeated outputs. Several inputs may share one output. It is conflicting outputs for one input that break the function rule.
  • Domain after cancellation. Check the original formula before simplifying; a canceled denominator still excludes its zero.
  • Open endpoints. An open point is absent, but its height can still be attained elsewhere on the graph.
  • Piece boundaries. Choose the rule from the input condition, including its equality sign, before substituting.
  • Average change. Use net output change with units; a position quotient does not give total distance or the rate at every instant.

Key takeaways

  • Functions assign one output to each allowed input; words, tables, formulas, and graphs reveal different information.
  • Domain and range track allowed inputs and attained outputs. Formula restrictions and a model’s meaning both matter.
  • Piecewise rules select formulas by input conditions; filled and open points record endpoint membership.
  • Average rate of change is (f(b)−f(a))/(b−a)\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)/(b-a)(f(b)−f(a))/(b−a) for distinct allowed endpoints, the slope of their secant.
  • Read intercepts, symmetry, and rising or falling intervals from the whole graph; position change differs from total distance.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1748, Leonhard Euler published a book with an ambitious title, Introduction to the Analysis of the Infinite. Before readers reached its later calculations, he asked them to think about variables and functions. That choice of starting point helped make functions central to the organization of mathematical analysis.

Euler explained his plan in the preface. He divided the work into two books, giving the first to analysis and the second to geometry. His reason for treating functions at length was that analysis concerned variable quantities and functions of them. The book’s grand title did not send readers straight into the infinite; its first chapter was “On Functions in General.”

The Euler Archive at the University of the Pacific catalogs the book as E101 and records its publication in Lausanne. The publisher’s page for the English translation reproduces the passage from Euler’s preface and preserves that opening in its table of contents.

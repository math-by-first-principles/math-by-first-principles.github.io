Exponential and Logarithmic Functions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A quantity that doubles goes from 666 to 121212 to 242424. An exponential answers how much you have after a given number of doublings. A logarithm answers the reverse question: how many doublings produce a given factor? The questions use the same relationship, with a different quantity unknown.

Learning goals

  • Translate between exponential and logarithmic statements with valid bases, arguments, and graph features
  • Apply logarithm laws while preserving the domain of the original expression
  • Use natural logarithms and change of base to solve exponential equations
  • Interpret exponential rates and solve targets, including half-life, within a stated model domain

Exponentials record multiplication

Equal input steps give equal multiplication factors in an exponential. Compare f(x)=3xf(x)=3^xf(x)=3x with a linear function at the same consecutive whole-number inputs:

xxx−2-2−2−1-1−1000111222
3x3^x3x1/91/91/91/31/31/3111333999
2x+52x+52x+5111333555777999

In the exponential row, moving right one column multiplies by 333; moving left divides by 333. Its successive additions are not equal. The linear row instead adds 222 each step, while its successive ratios differ. Equal input steps distinguish these rules by ratios versus differences. Fractional exponents fill in between the exponential values. For example, 31/2=33^{1/2}=\sqrt331/2=3​ because it is the positive number whose square is 333.

An exponential function has the form f(x)=bxf(x)=b^xf(x)=bx, where the base bbb is positive and different from 111. Its domain is all real numbers and its range is all positive numbers. The graph passes through (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and never reaches height zero.

If b>1b>1b>1, larger inputs give larger outputs. If 0<b<10<b<10<b<1, larger inputs give smaller outputs: (1/3)x=3−x(1/3)^x=3^{-x}(1/3)x=3−x turns growth into decay. These claims concern positive bases. A negative base does not define a real value for every real exponent, and base 111 would give only the constant value 111.

The exponent laws you already use still apply. For a positive base bbb and real exponents u,vu,vu,v,

bu+v=bubv,bu−v=bubv,(bu)v=buv.\begin{aligned} b^{u+v}&=b^u b^v,\\ b^{u-v}&=\frac{b^u}{b^v},\\ (b^u)^v&=b^{uv}. \end{aligned}bu+vbu−v(bu)v​=bubv,=bvbu​,=buv.​

A logarithm names the exponent

You can read an exponential statement in the other direction. Since 72=497^2=4972=49, the exponent needed to make 494949 from base 777 is 222. We write log⁡749=2\log_7 49=2log7​49=2.

A logarithm is that required exponent. For a base b>0b>0b>0 with b≠1b\ne1b=1, and a positive argument MMM,

log⁡bM=ymeansby=M.\log_b M=y\quad\text{means}\quad b^y=M.logb​M=ymeansby=M.

The argument is the input to the logarithm. It must be positive because every output of byb^yby is positive. Also, the required exponent is unique: a positive-base exponential with base different from 111 keeps increasing or keeps decreasing.

This reversal swaps the coordinates. The point (1,3)(1,3)(1,3) on y=3xy=3^xy=3x corresponds to (3,1)(3,1)(3,1) on y=log⁡3xy=\log_3 xy=log3​x. Swapping coordinates reflects the graphs across y=xy=xy=x.

In this base-333 picture, the exponential approaches the horizontal axis y=0y=0y=0 as xxx becomes more negative, without reaching it. Thus y=0y=0y=0 is its horizontal asymptote. The reflected logarithm has vertical asymptote x=0x=0x=0: its outputs decrease without bound as its positive inputs approach zero. The diagonal y=xy=xy=x is the reflection line. For any allowed base the two asymptotes are still y=0y=0y=0 and x=0x=0x=0; when 0<b<10<b<10<b<1, the exponential approaches zero as xxx increases and the logarithm grows without bound as xxx approaches zero from the right.

For every allowed base, y=log⁡bxy=\log_b xy=logb​x has domain (0,∞)(0,\infty)(0,∞) and range all real numbers. It passes through (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) because b0=1b^0=1b0=1. For b>1b>1b>1, it increases; for 0<b<10<b<10<b<1, it decreases. The reversal also gives

log⁡b(bx)=xfor every real x,\log_b(b^x)=x\quad\text{for every real }x,logb​(bx)=xfor every real x,

and

blog⁡bM=Mfor M>0.b^{\log_b M}=M\quad\text{for }M>0.blogb​M=Mfor M>0.

In both statements the base is positive and different from 111. The positive-argument condition in the second one is essential.

Solution. Calling the answer yyy means (1/4)y=8(1/4)^y=8(1/4)y=8.

Write both sides as powers of 222:

2−2y=23.2^{-2y}=2^3.2−2y=23.

The exponents must agree, so −2y=3-2y=3−2y=3 and y=−3/2y=-3/2y=−3/2.

Logarithm laws come from exponent laws

Multiplication of positive numbers becomes addition of their logarithms. For example, 25=5225=5^225=52 and 125=53125=5^3125=53, so their product is 52+35^{2+3}52+3. Its base-555 logarithm is therefore 2+32+32+3.

Why logarithms turn products into sums#

Take a fresh pair: 4=224=2^24=22 and 32=2532=2^532=25. Their product is 128=22+5128=2^{2+5}128=22+5. Thus log⁡2(4⋅32)=7\log_2(4\cdot32)=7log2​(4⋅32)=7, the same as log⁡24+log⁡232=2+5\log_2 4+\log_2 32=2+5log2​4+log2​32=2+5.

The numbers did not determine the reasoning; adding exponents did. For any positive M,NM,NM,N and base b>0b>0b>0 different from 111, set p=log⁡bMp=\log_b Mp=logb​M and q=log⁡bNq=\log_b Nq=logb​N. This says M=bpM=b^pM=bp and N=bqN=b^qN=bq. Multiplying gives

MN=bpbq=bp+q.MN=b^p b^q=b^{p+q}.MN=bpbq=bp+q.

The exponent that produces MNMNMN is p+qp+qp+q. Therefore log⁡b(MN)=log⁡bM+log⁡bN\log_b(MN)=\log_b M+\log_b Nlogb​(MN)=logb​M+logb​N.

∎

Division subtracts exponents, and raising a power multiplies exponents. For instance, 53/52=53−25^3/5^2=5^{3-2}53/52=53−2 and (52)4=52⋅4(5^2)^4=5^{2\cdot4}(52)4=52⋅4. Reading those exponent statements as logarithms gives the other two laws.

For the power law in general, let M>0M>0M>0, let b>0b>0b>0 with b≠1b\ne1b=1, and let rrr be real. Setting p=log⁡bMp=\log_b Mp=logb​M gives M=bpM=b^pM=bp, so Mr=bprM^r=b^{pr}Mr=bpr. The logarithm of MrM^rMr is therefore pr=rlog⁡bMpr=r\log_b Mpr=rlogb​M.

For every base b>0b>0b>0 with b≠1b\ne1b=1, positive arguments M,NM,NM,N, and real number rrr:

Productlog⁡b(MN)=log⁡bM+log⁡bN\log_b(MN)=\log_b M+\log_b Nlogb​(MN)=logb​M+logb​N

The natural base and calculator values

The constant eee is a particular positive number, approximately 2.718282.718282.71828. A natural logarithm, written ln⁡x\ln xlnx, is a logarithm with base eee: ln⁡x=log⁡ex\ln x=\log_e xlnx=loge​x. Thus ln⁡(e4)=4\ln(e^4)=4ln(e4)=4 and eln⁡13=13e^{\ln 13}=13eln13=13. The second statement requires the positive argument 131313; in general, eln⁡x=xe^{\ln x}=xelnx=x only for x>0x>0x>0.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Arguments and answers. A real logarithm needs a positive argument, but its answer can be negative or zero.
  • Sum versus product. Expand logarithms of products and quotients, not logarithms of sums.
  • Domains. A rewrite must retain the original allowed inputs. Check candidates in the original logarithms after solving.
  • Model parameters. In A0ektA_0e^{kt}A0​ekt, the per-unit factor is eke^kek. Do not report 100k%100k\%100k% as the exact per-unit percentage change.
  • Rounding. Keep exact logarithmic values during calculation, use ≈\approx≈ for rounded decimals, and check model domains and discrete observation times.

A bit of history (optional)

John Napier’s 1614 book arrived in a world where long calculations were done by hand. Astronomers, navigators, and surveyors needed products and quotients of large numbers; an arithmetic slip could spoil the work. Napier offered tables that helped replace those operations with addition and subtraction. The Smithsonian holds a digitized copy of the original book, published in Edinburgh.

The printed tables were the visible result of a much longer effort. National Museums Scotland describes Napier spending more than twenty years on ways to simplify calculation. His logarithms could be consulted repeatedly, so the work of making a table served many later calculations. The same inventor also developed calculating rods, now called Napier’s bones, to organize ordinary multiplication. Tables and rods were different tools aimed at the same practical difficulty: making a long calculation easier to carry out reliably.

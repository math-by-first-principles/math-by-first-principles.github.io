Intuition

Once you can name and evaluate functions, you can combine them. One way is to add, subtract, multiply or divide their outputs at the same input. Another is to run them in a chain: the output of one becomes the input of the next. The order of that chain can change the result.

Learning goals

  • Add, subtract, multiply and divide two functions, excluding any input where the divisor is zero
  • Compose with (f∘g)(x)=f(g(x))(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)), inner first
  • Substitute the whole inner rule to get a composed formula
  • Show that f∘gf \circ gf∘g usually differs from g∘fg \circ fg∘f
  • Require an input allowed into both functions in turn

Building new functions by arithmetic

Suppose f(x)=3xf(x)=3xf(x)=3x and g(x)=x+2g(x)=x+2g(x)=x+2 give two runners’ positions in meters from the same starting mark, xxx seconds after they start. The second runner has a two-meter head start. At time xxx, the sum of their positions is f(x)+g(x)f(x)+g(x)f(x)+g(x). The difference f(x)−g(x)f(x)-g(x)f(x)−g(x) tells how far the first runner is ahead; it is negative while the first runner is behind.

You evaluate both functions at the same input, then combine their outputs. In general:

(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x),(f−g)(x)=f(x)−g(x),(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x), \qquad (f - g)(x) = f(x) - g(x),(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x),(f−g)(x)=f(x)−g(x), (fg)(x)=f(x)⋅g(x),(fg)(x)=f(x)g(x).(fg)(x) = f(x) \cdot g(x), \qquad \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)(x) = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}.(fg)(x)=f(x)⋅g(x),(gf​)(x)=g(x)f(x)​.

Each combination is a new function. For example, f+gf+gf+g means “evaluate both functions at the input and add their outputs.”

The sum, difference and product use only inputs accepted by both functions. The quotient needs that same shared domain and one further condition: g(x)≠0g(x)\ne0g(x)=0. Even if both outputs exist, their quotient does not exist when its denominator is zero.

Worked example 1 Adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing two functions

Let f(x)=3xf(x) = 3xf(x)=3x and g(x)=x+2g(x) = x + 2g(x)=x+2, now treated simply as algebra rules that accept any real number xxx, not only the runner story’s elapsed time. Build all four combinations, and find where the quotient is defined.

(f+g)(x)=3x+(x+2)=4x+2,(f + g)(x) = 3x + (x + 2) = 4x + 2,(f+g)(x)=3x+(x+2)=4x+2,(f−g)(x)=3x−(x+2)=2x−2,(f - g)(x) = 3x - (x + 2) = 2x - 2,(f−g)(x)=3x−(x+2)=2x−2,(fg)(x)=3x⋅(x+2)=3x2+6x.(fg)(x) = 3x \cdot (x + 2) = 3x^2 + 6x.(fg)(x)=3x⋅(x+2)=3x2+6x.

Composing: feeding one function into another

Picture two machines in an assembly line. The first receives the input, and its output goes directly into the second. This differs from evaluating both functions at the original input.

Make it concrete with g(x)=x+4g(x) = x + 4g(x)=x+4 and f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2. Start with an input, say 333. Run ggg first: g(3)=3+4=7g(3) = 3 + 4 = 7g(3)=3+4=7. Now take that result, 777, and run it through fff: f(7)=72=49f(7) = 7^2 = 49f(7)=72=49. The end product of the two-step process is 494949. Written on one line, this is f(g(3))f(g(3))f(g(3)): the inner function ggg acts on 333, and fff acts on whatever ggg hands it.

This two-step operation is composition, and it has its own symbol. The composition of fff and ggg is written f∘gf \circ gf∘g (read ”fff composed with ggg,” or ”fff after ggg”), and it is defined by

(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)).(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)).(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)).

The little ring ∘\circ∘ is not multiplication and not the letter o; it is the composition sign. The right-hand side, f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)), is the form you actually calculate with.

Notice how different this is from the product fgfgfg from the last section. The product runs fff and ggg side by side on the same input and multiplies the two outputs. Composition runs them in a chain: ggg‘s output becomes fff‘s input. For f(x)=x+1f(x) = x + 1f(x)=x+1 and g(x)=2xg(x) = 2xg(x)=2x at x=3x = 3x=3, the product is (fg)(3)=f(3)⋅g(3)=4⋅6=24(fg)(3) = f(3) \cdot g(3) = 4 \cdot 6 = 24(fg)(3)=f(3)⋅g(3)=4⋅6=24, but the composition is (f∘g)(3)=f(g(3))=f(6)=7(f \circ g)(3) = f(g(3)) = f(6) = 7(f∘g)(3)=f(g(3))=f(6)=7. Same two functions, same input, two very different answers.

In f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)), work from the inside out, as with nested arithmetic parentheses. The inner function is ggg, the one applied directly to xxx. The outer function is fff, which receives g(x)g(x)g(x). Thus ggg runs first even though it is written second in f∘gf\circ gf∘g.

Worked example 2 Composing at a number in both orders

Let f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2 and g(x)=x+4g(x) = x + 4g(x)=x+4. You already found f(g(3))=49f(g(3)) = 49f(g(3))=49 above by running ggg first and then fff. Now reverse the order and compute g(f(3))g(f(3))g(f(3)): run fff on 333 first, then run ggg on the result.

f(3)=32=9,g(9)=9+4=13.f(3) = 3^2 = 9, \qquad g(9) = 9 + 4 = 13.f(3)=32=9,g(9)=9+4=13.

So g(f(3))=13g(f(3))=13g(f(3))=13, whereas f(g(3))=49f(g(3))=49f(g(3))=49. Reversing the order changed the answer.

Finding a formula for a composition

A formula for f∘gf\circ gf∘g describes the whole two-step process. Build it by substituting the entire expression g(x)g(x)g(x) wherever the rule for fff expects an input. Keep parentheses around that expression.

Worked example 3 Finding a formula for f∘gf \circ gf∘g and g∘fg \circ fg∘f

Let f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2 and g(x)=x+4g(x) = x + 4g(x)=x+4. Find formulas for (f∘g)(x)(f \circ g)(x)(f∘g)(x) and (g∘f)(x)(g \circ f)(x)(g∘f)(x).

For f∘gf \circ gf∘g, substitute the whole rule g(x)=x+4g(x) = x + 4g(x)=x+4 into fff in place of its input, then expand the square. Recall (a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2, so with a=xa = xa=x and b=4b = 4b=4:

(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x))=f(x+4)=(x+4)2=x2+2(4x)+42=x2+8x+16.\begin{aligned} (f \circ g)(x) &= f(g(x)) = f(x + 4) = (x + 4)^2 \\ &= x^2 + 2(4x) + 4^2 = x^2 + 8x + 16. \end{aligned}(f∘g)(x)​=f(g(x))=f(x+4)=(x+4)2=x2+2(4x)+42=x2+8x+16.​

For g∘fg \circ fg∘f, substitute the whole rule f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2 into ggg in place of its input:

(g∘f)(x)=g(f(x))=g(x2)=x2+4.(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(x^2) = x^2 + 4.(g∘f)(x)=g(f(x))=g(x2)=x2+4.

These two formulas are not the same function, and that matches the previous worked example: plugging x=3x = 3x=3 into x2+8x+16x^2 + 8x + 16x2+8x+16 and into x2+4x^2 + 4x2+4 reproduces the very numbers, 494949 and 131313, found there by running the machines step by step.

Order matters: a composition is not commutative

The examples show that composition is not commutative: you cannot generally swap its order. Squaring and then adding four gives x2+4x^2+4x2+4. Adding four first and then squaring gives (x+4)2=x2+8x+16(x+4)^2=x^2+8x+16(x+4)2=x2+8x+16. In the second order, the added four is part of what gets squared.

Some pairs do give the same function in both orders, but that must be checked. Equality of functions requires the same domain and the same output at every allowed input. A disagreement such as 49≠1349\ne1349=13 at input 333 proves these two compositions differ; agreement at one input alone would not prove equality.

The domain of a composition

A composition accepts an input only if both steps can be carried out.

Worked example 4 The domain of a composition

Let f(x)=1xf(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}f(x)=x1​ and g(x)=x−4g(x) = x - 4g(x)=x−4. Find (f∘g)(x)(f \circ g)(x)(f∘g)(x) and its domain.

Substitute ggg into fff to get the formula:

(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x))=f(x−4)=1x−4.(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x - 4) = \frac{1}{x - 4}.(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x))=f(x−4)=x−41​.

At x=4x=4x=4, the inner step works: g(4)=0g(4)=0g(4)=0. The outer step fails because f(0)=10f(0)=\dfrac10f(0)=01​ is undefined. At x=6x=6x=6, both steps work: g(6)=2g(6)=2g(6)=2, then f(2)=12f(2)=\dfrac12f(2)=21​. Since ggg accepts every real input and produces zero only at 444, the domain of f∘gf\circ gf∘g is all real numbers except 444.

The same two checks determine the domain of any composition.

Which inputs a composition accepts#

Take g(x)=1x−2g(x)=\dfrac1{x-2}g(x)=x−21​ and f(u)=uf(u)=\sqrt{u}f(u)=u​, using real numbers. At input 222, the first step fails because g(2)g(2)g(2) would divide by zero. At input 111, the first step gives g(1)=−1g(1)=-1g(1)=−1, but the second step fails because −1\sqrt{-1}−1​ is not real. Input 333 passes both steps: g(3)=1g(3)=1g(3)=1 and f(1)=1f(1)=1f(1)=1.

For any composition f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)), first xxx must be in the domain of ggg. Otherwise g(x)g(x)g(x) does not exist, so there is no output to pass to fff. Next, the produced value g(x)g(x)g(x) must be in the domain of fff. Otherwise the second step has no value.

These conditions are also sufficient: when g(x)g(x)g(x) exists and fff accepts it, f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)) exists. Thus the domain consists exactly of the inputs passing both checks.

Composition models a two-step process

When one quantity determines a second, which then determines a third, composition records the chain. Examples include discounts followed by fees, converted readings, and area determined by a changing length.

Worked example 5 A discount followed by shipping

An online store takes 202020 percent off every item, then adds a flat shipping fee of 555 dollars to the discounted price. Model the final price as a composition, and find it for an item that normally costs 404040 dollars.

Name the two steps. Taking 202020 percent off leaves 808080 percent of the price, so the discount function is d(p)=0.8pd(p) = 0.8pd(p)=0.8p. Adding shipping to a price is s(x)=x+5s(x) = x + 5s(x)=x+5. The store discounts first and then adds shipping to the discounted price, so shipping is the outer function and the final price is s(d(p))s(d(p))s(d(p)):

s(d(p))=s(0.8p)=0.8p+5.s(d(p)) = s(0.8p) = 0.8p + 5.s(d(p))=s(0.8p)=0.8p+5.

For an item with p=40p = 40p=40:

s(d(40))=0.8(40)+5=32+5=37.s(d(40)) = 0.8(40) + 5 = 32 + 5 = 37.s(d(40))=0.8(40)+5=32+5=37.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Composing in the wrong order. In f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)) the inner function ggg runs first, even though fff is written first in f∘gf \circ gf∘g. Work from the inside out, just like nested parentheses.
  • Assuming f∘gf \circ gf∘g equals g∘fg \circ fg∘f. The two orders often give different results, so respect the order the problem gives you.
  • Substituting only part of the inner rule. To build f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)) you drop the entire rule for g(x)g(x)g(x) into fff, not just the variable. For f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2 and g(x)=x+4g(x) = x + 4g(x)=x+4, that is (x+4)2(x + 4)^2(x+4)2, not x2+4x^2 + 4x2+4.
  • Forgetting to expand a squared binomial. (x+4)2(x + 4)^2(x+4)2 is x2+8x+16x^2 + 8x + 16x2+8x+16, not x2+16x^2 + 16x2+16. The middle term 8x8x8x is part of the square and drops out only if you forget it.
  • Ignoring the quotient’s forbidden inputs. fg\dfrac{f}{g}gf​ is undefined wherever g(x)=0g(x) = 0g(x)=0. Find those inputs and exclude them, even when fff itself is fine there.
  • Checking only the inner function’s domain. An input can be legal for ggg and still be barred from f∘gf \circ gf∘g because g(x)g(x)g(x) is a value fff rejects. Always test both steps.

Key takeaways

  • Two functions with the same input combine by arithmetic: (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x), (f−g)(x)=f(x)−g(x)(f - g)(x) = f(x) - g(x)(f−g)(x)=f(x)−g(x), (fg)(x)=f(x)⋅g(x)(fg)(x) = f(x) \cdot g(x)(fg)(x)=f(x)⋅g(x), and (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x)\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}(gf​)(x)=g(x)f(x)​.
  • The quotient fg\dfrac{f}{g}gf​ needs xxx accepted by both fff and ggg, the same as the other three combinations, plus one more requirement: g(x)≠0g(x) \neq 0g(x)=0, because dividing by zero is undefined.
  • Composition feeds one output into another: (f∘g)(x)=f(g(x))(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)). The inner function ggg runs first, then the outer function fff runs on its output.
  • To find a formula for f∘gf \circ gf∘g, substitute the whole rule for g(x)g(x)g(x) into fff and simplify.
  • Composition is not commutative: do not swap the order unless you check. In general f∘gf \circ gf∘g and g∘fg \circ fg∘f can be different functions. Some pairs give the same function in both orders, but check that from the rules, not from one input.
  • An input is in the domain of f∘gf \circ gf∘g exactly when it is allowed into ggg and g(x)g(x)g(x) is allowed into fff.

A bit of history (optional)

Before designing his calculating engines, Charles Babbage asked what algebra could do with an unknown function rather than an unknown number. His paper An Essay towards the Calculus of Functions appeared in 1815, when he was in his early twenties.

Ordinary equations ask for a number that makes a statement true. Babbage studied equations in which the unknown was a whole rule. Composition made it possible to describe how such a rule behaved when applied after another rule, or after itself.

Babbage’s later engines are better known, but the earlier question connects directly to this lesson. A chain of calculations must keep track of which operation acts first. Feeding ggg‘s result into fff describes a different order from feeding fff‘s result into ggg, even when some particular rules happen to give the same result in both orders.

Source: Babbage’s 1815 paper in the Royal Society archive and its published abstract.

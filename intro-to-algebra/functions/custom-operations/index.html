Defining New Operations Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra I. You can skip it.

Intuition

Over the last three lessons a function became a rule with its own name, something you could evaluate, chain, and reverse. Contest problems and textbooks like to spring a brand-new symbol on you, something like 3⋆53 \star 53⋆5 or a⋄ba \diamond ba⋄b, defined by a rule such as a⋆b=2a+b−1a \star b = 2a + b - 1a⋆b=2a+b−1. The symbol looks invented because it is, but nothing about it is new arithmetic: it names a function of two inputs. Read the definition, substitute the inputs, and simplify with the order of operations you already trust. A rule is a rule, whatever symbol names it.

Learning goals

  • Evaluate a new symbol by substituting both inputs, even whole expressions, into its rule
  • Test whether the operation is commutative or associative before assuming either
  • Work nested operations from the innermost pair outward
  • Look for an identity element by solving the defining condition on both sides
  • Turn an equation with the symbol into an ordinary linear or quadratic equation

A made-up symbol is only a rule

A binary operation takes two inputs and produces one output, exactly the way +++ takes 333 and 444 and hands back 777. That still fits the definition of a function from the first lesson: the pair of numbers together, not each one separately, is the single input the rule receives, and every allowed pair gets back exactly one output. The operations you grew up with, +++, −-−, ×\times×, and ÷\div÷, are the familiar four. But nothing stops someone from inventing another one and naming it with a fresh symbol. When a problem writes

a⋆b=2a+b−1,a \star b = 2a + b - 1,a⋆b=2a+b−1,

it is doing just that. The star is a made-up name, and the right-hand side is the rule that says what to do with the two inputs aaa and bbb. Think of aaa and bbb as two empty slots. To compute a particular value like 3⋆53 \star 53⋆5, drop the first input into every aaa and the second input into every bbb, then simplify:

3⋆5=2(3)+5−1=6+5−1=10.3 \star 5 = 2(3) + 5 - 1 = 6 + 5 - 1 = 10.3⋆5=2(3)+5−1=6+5−1=10.

That is the whole method, and it never changes: substitute the inputs into the defining rule, then evaluate with the ordinary order of operations. The order of operations matters as much here as anywhere. In 2a+b−12a + b - 12a+b−1 the multiplication 2a2a2a happens before the additions, so 2(3)2(3)2(3) becomes 666 before anything is added. Skip that and you would get a different, wrong answer. The star is new; the arithmetic inside it is not.

Worked example 1 Evaluating a custom operation, including negatives

Let a⋆b=2a+b−1a \star b = 2a + b - 1a⋆b=2a+b−1. Compute 4⋆74 \star 74⋆7, then (−2)⋆5(-2) \star 5(−2)⋆5, then 6⋆(−3)6 \star (-3)6⋆(−3).

For 4⋆74 \star 74⋆7, substitute a=4a = 4a=4 and b=7b = 7b=7, and do the multiplication first:

4⋆7=2(4)+7−1=8+7−1=14.4 \star 7 = 2(4) + 7 - 1 = 8 + 7 - 1 = 14.4⋆7=2(4)+7−1=8+7−1=14.

For (−2)⋆5(-2) \star 5(−2)⋆5, the first input is negative, so 2a2a2a becomes 2(−2)=−42(-2) = -42(−2)=−4. Keep track of the sign:

(−2)⋆5=2(−2)+5−1=−4+5−1=0.(-2) \star 5 = 2(-2) + 5 - 1 = -4 + 5 - 1 = 0.(−2)⋆5=2(−2)+5−1=−4+5−1=0.

For 6⋆(−3)6 \star (-3)6⋆(−3), the second input is negative, and it lands in the +b+b+b slot, so you add a negative:

6⋆(−3)=2(6)+(−3)−1=12−3−1=8.6 \star (-3) = 2(6) + (-3) - 1 = 12 - 3 - 1 = 8.6⋆(−3)=2(6)+(−3)−1=12−3−1=8.

Each answer came from the same two moves: put the inputs in their slots, then simplify. The negatives needed care only because signs always need care, not because the star does anything unusual.

A new operation is defined by a△b=3a−ba \triangle b = 3a - ba△b=3a−b. What is 4△54 \triangle 54△5?

Answer choices

Substitute a=4a = 4a=4 and b=5b = 5b=5 into the rule, multiplying before subtracting.

4△5=3(4)−5=12−5=74 \triangle 5 = 3(4) - 5 = 12 - 5 = 74△5=3(4)−5=12−5=7

The triangle is a made-up name for the rule 3a−b3a - b3a−b, so evaluating it is ordinary arithmetic once the inputs are in place.

When an input is an expression

Nothing says the inputs have to be numbers. A slot can hold a whole expression, and the rule still works the same way: whatever sits in the slot gets substituted in one piece. This is the exact substitution you used to build composite functions last lesson, where the entire rule for g(x)g(x)g(x) dropped into fff. Watch the order of operations especially closely when an input is squared or wrapped in parentheses.

Worked example 2 Substituting an expression into a slot

Let a⋄b=a2−ba \diamond b = a^2 - ba⋄b=a2−b. Compute 5⋄25 \diamond 25⋄2 and (−4)⋄3(-4) \diamond 3(−4)⋄3, then simplify (k+1)⋄4(k + 1) \diamond 4(k+1)⋄4.

For 5⋄25 \diamond 25⋄2, square the first input before subtracting the second:

5⋄2=52−2=25−2=23.5 \diamond 2 = 5^2 - 2 = 25 - 2 = 23.5⋄2=52−2=25−2=23.

For (−4)⋄3(-4) \diamond 3(−4)⋄3, the first input is negative, and squaring a negative gives a positive:

(−4)⋄3=(−4)2−3=16−3=13.(-4) \diamond 3 = (-4)^2 - 3 = 16 - 3 = 13.(−4)⋄3=(−4)2−3=16−3=13.

Now let the first input be the expression k+1k + 1k+1. It goes into the a2a^2a2 slot as a single block, so the whole thing gets squared:

(k+1)⋄4=(k+1)2−4=(k2+2k+1)−4=k2+2k−3.(k + 1) \diamond 4 = (k + 1)^2 - 4 = (k^2 + 2k + 1) - 4 = k^2 + 2k - 3.(k+1)⋄4=(k+1)2−4=(k2+2k+1)−4=k2+2k−3.

The square applies to all of k+1k + 1k+1, not just the kkk, which is why the middle term 2k2k2k appears. Treat the slot as a container: the entire input drops in, then you simplify.

Check your understanding

With a⋄b=a2−ba \diamond b = a^2 - ba⋄b=a2−b, what is (m−3)⋄7(m - 3) \diamond 7(m−3)⋄7 after substituting, before simplifying?

Answer choices

The whole expression m−3m - 3m−3 is the first input, so it drops into the a2a^2a2 slot as one block and gets squared as a unit.

(m−3)⋄7=(m−3)2−7(m - 3) \diamond 7 = (m - 3)^2 - 7(m−3)⋄7=(m−3)2−7

Squaring only the mmm and leaving the −3-3−3 outside the square is the mistake this checks for.

The order of the two inputs can matter

Ordinary addition does not care about order, since 3+53 + 53+5 and 5+35 + 35+3 are both 888. Do not assume every operation is that polite; check it instead. Test the star on the two orders of 333 and 555:

3⋆5=2(3)+5−1=10,5⋆3=2(5)+3−1=12.3 \star 5 = 2(3) + 5 - 1 = 10, \qquad 5 \star 3 = 2(5) + 3 - 1 = 12.3⋆5=2(3)+5−1=10,5⋆3=2(5)+3−1=12.

Same two numbers, but 101010 and 121212 are different, so 3⋆5≠5⋆33 \star 5 \ne 5 \star 33⋆5=5⋆3. The reason is built into the rule. The definition 2a+b−12a + b - 12a+b−1 doubles the first input but not the second, so the two slots are treated differently. Swap the inputs and the one that gets doubled changes, which changes the answer. An operation where the order of the inputs can change the result is called not commutative.

A single pair like this one already settles the question: since 10≠1210 \ne 1210=12, the star is not commutative, full stop. But the reverse test takes more care. If a pair happens to agree, that shows only that the two orders match for that one pair, not for every pair. To show an operation is commutative for every input takes a general argument from the rule itself, not one lucky match. Here is one where that argument works.

Worked example 3 One operation that is not commutative, and one that is

Compare the two orders for two different rules.

The star already showed the pattern in the last section: it is not commutative, and one counterexample was enough to settle that.

Now take a different rule, a⊕b=a+b+aba \oplus b = a + b + aba⊕b=a+b+ab, and test it on 222 and 777:

2⊕7=2+7+(2)(7)=2+7+14=23,2 \oplus 7 = 2 + 7 + (2)(7) = 2 + 7 + 14 = 23,2⊕7=2+7+(2)(7)=2+7+14=23,7⊕2=7+2+(7)(2)=7+2+14=23.7 \oplus 2 = 7 + 2 + (7)(2) = 7 + 2 + 14 = 23.7⊕2=7+2+(7)(2)=7+2+14=23.

This time the two orders agree. As before, that is only a hint, not a proof: it shows this one pair matches, not that every pair does. The next result gives the general argument for why ⊕\oplus⊕ really is commutative for every choice of inputs.

The operation a⊕b=a+b+aba \oplus b = a + b + aba⊕b=a+b+ab is commutative#

To show ⊕\oplus⊕ is commutative, we must show a⊕b=b⊕aa \oplus b = b \oplus aa⊕b=b⊕a for every pair of numbers aaa and bbb, not just for one lucky example. Start from the definition applied in each order:

a⊕b=a+b+ab,b⊕a=b+a+ba.a \oplus b = a + b + ab, \qquad b \oplus a = b + a + ba.a⊕b=a+b+ab,b⊕a=b+a+ba.

Now compare the two right-hand sides using facts you already know about ordinary numbers. Addition of numbers is commutative, so b+ab + ab+a is the same as a+ba + ba+b. Multiplication of numbers is commutative too, so bababa is the same as ababab. Replacing each piece of b⊕ab \oplus ab⊕a with its equal turns it into

b⊕a=a+b+ab,b \oplus a = a + b + ab,b⊕a=a+b+ab,

which is exactly the expression for a⊕ba \oplus ba⊕b. Since the two are identical for every aaa and bbb, the operation ⊕\oplus⊕ is commutative. Notice what did the work: ⊕\oplus⊕ is commutative because the +++ and ×\times× hiding inside its rule are commutative themselves. A rule built the same way in both inputs stays the same when they trade places.

∎

Check your understanding

For the operation a⋄b=a2−ba \diamond b = a^2 - ba⋄b=a2−b, which statement is true?

Answer choices

Test the two orders on the inputs 333 and 111.

3⋄1=32−1=8,1⋄3=12−3=−23 \diamond 1 = 3^2 - 1 = 8, \qquad 1 \diamond 3 = 1^2 - 3 = -23⋄1=32−1=8,1⋄3=12−3=−2

Since 8≠−28 \ne -28=−2, the operation gives different answers in the two orders, so 3⋄1≠1⋄33 \diamond 1 \ne 1 \diamond 33⋄1=1⋄3 and ⋄\diamond⋄ is not commutative.

Nested operations: work from the inside out

You can combine three inputs by applying the operation twice, but you need parentheses to say which pair goes first, the same way (1⋆2)⋆3(1 \star 2) \star 3(1⋆2)⋆3 and 1⋆(2⋆3)1 \star (2 \star 3)1⋆(2⋆3) are two different plans. Evaluate the innermost parentheses first, turn that result into a single number, and use it as an input for the next step, exactly like nested parentheses in ordinary arithmetic.

Watch what the grouping does to the answer.

Worked example 4 Two groupings, two different answers

Let a⋆b=2a+b−1a \star b = 2a + b - 1a⋆b=2a+b−1. Compute (1⋆2)⋆3(1 \star 2) \star 3(1⋆2)⋆3 and 1⋆(2⋆3)1 \star (2 \star 3)1⋆(2⋆3).

For (1⋆2)⋆3(1 \star 2) \star 3(1⋆2)⋆3, the parentheses on the left go first. Evaluate the inner star, then use its result as the first input of the outer star:

1⋆2=2(1)+2−1=3,3⋆3=2(3)+3−1=8.1 \star 2 = 2(1) + 2 - 1 = 3, \qquad 3 \star 3 = 2(3) + 3 - 1 = 8.1⋆2=2(1)+2−1=3,3⋆3=2(3)+3−1=8.

So (1⋆2)⋆3=8(1 \star 2) \star 3 = 8(1⋆2)⋆3=8. Now regroup to the right. This time the inner star on 222 and 333 goes first, and its result becomes the second input of the outer star:

2⋆3=2(2)+3−1=6,1⋆6=2(1)+6−1=7.2 \star 3 = 2(2) + 3 - 1 = 6, \qquad 1 \star 6 = 2(1) + 6 - 1 = 7.2⋆3=2(2)+3−1=6,1⋆6=2(1)+6−1=7.

So 1⋆(2⋆3)=71 \star (2 \star 3) = 71⋆(2⋆3)=7. The two groupings give 888 and 777, which are not equal.

The two answers disagreeing tells you something important. An operation is called associative when the grouping never matters, the way (2+3)+4(2 + 3) + 4(2+3)+4 and 2+(3+4)2 + (3 + 4)2+(3+4) are both 999. The star fails that test, since (1⋆2)⋆3=8(1 \star 2) \star 3 = 8(1⋆2)⋆3=8 but 1⋆(2⋆3)=71 \star (2 \star 3) = 71⋆(2⋆3)=7, so the star is not associative. Just like commutativity, associativity is a property to check rather than assume, and when it fails the parentheses are not optional. Without them, "1⋆2⋆31 \star 2 \star 31⋆2⋆3" would be ambiguous, because the two readings genuinely produce different values.

Check your understanding

With a⋄b=a2−ba \diamond b = a^2 - ba⋄b=a2−b, compare (1⋄2)⋄3(1 \diamond 2) \diamond 3(1⋄2)⋄3 with 1⋄(2⋄3)1 \diamond (2 \diamond 3)1⋄(2⋄3). Is ⋄\diamond⋄ associative?

Answer choices

Evaluate each grouping from the inside out.

(1⋄2)⋄3:1⋄2=12−2=−1,(−1)⋄3=(−1)2−3=−2(1 \diamond 2) \diamond 3: \quad 1 \diamond 2 = 1^2 - 2 = -1, \quad (-1) \diamond 3 = (-1)^2 - 3 = -2(1⋄2)⋄3:1⋄2=12−2=−1,(−1)⋄3=(−1)2−3=−2

1⋄(2⋄3):2⋄3=22−3=1,1⋄1=12−1=01 \diamond (2 \diamond 3): \quad 2 \diamond 3 = 2^2 - 3 = 1, \quad 1 \diamond 1 = 1^2 - 1 = 01⋄(2⋄3):2⋄3=22−3=1,1⋄1=12−1=0

Since −2≠0-2 \ne 0−2=0, the two groupings disagree, so ⋄\diamond⋄ is not associative either.

Looking for an identity element

Ordinary addition has a special number, 000, that changes nothing: a+0=aa + 0 = aa+0=a for every aaa. Ordinary multiplication has 111, since a×1=aa \times 1 = aa×1=a. A value that leaves every input untouched from both sides is called an identity element for the operation. Untouched from both sides means the value gives back aaa whether it is combined on the left or the right of aaa. Requiring both sides matters here, because these operations need not be commutative, so leaving an input unchanged from one side alone is not enough. It is natural to ask whether an invented operation has such a value, and the question is answered by solving an equation.

Worked example 5 Finding an identity element

Does a⊕b=a+b+aba \oplus b = a + b + aba⊕b=a+b+ab have an identity element? If so, find it.

An identity is a fixed value eee that leaves every input unchanged from both sides, meaning a⊕e=aa \oplus e = aa⊕e=a and e⊕a=ae \oplus a = ae⊕a=a no matter what aaa is. Start with the first condition, writing it out with the rule and solving for eee:

a⊕e=a+e+ae=a.a \oplus e = a + e + ae = a.a⊕e=a+e+ae=a.

Subtract aaa from both sides to isolate the terms with eee:

e+ae=0⟹e(1+a)=0.e + ae = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e(1 + a) = 0.e+ae=0⟹e(1+a)=0.

For this to hold for every aaa, the factor eee itself must be 000, since 1+a1 + a1+a is not always zero. So the only candidate is e=0e = 0e=0. Now check that it works from both sides:

a⊕0=a+0+a⋅0=a,0⊕a=0+a+0⋅a=a.a \oplus 0 = a + 0 + a\cdot 0 = a, \qquad 0 \oplus a = 0 + a + 0\cdot a = a.a⊕0=a+0+a⋅0=a,0⊕a=0+a+0⋅a=a.

Both orders return aaa, which they must here, since ⊕\oplus⊕ is commutative. The identity element of ⊕\oplus⊕ is 000. Not every operation is so lucky. For the star, the condition a⋆e=2a+e−1=aa \star e = 2a + e - 1 = aa⋆e=2a+e−1=a already forces e=1−ae = 1 - ae=1−a, a value that depends on aaa, so no single number leaves every input unchanged even from that one side. With nothing able to satisfy the condition, the star has no identity element. Whether an identity exists is one more property you discover by testing, not by assuming.

Check your understanding

With a⊗b=a+2b−aba \otimes b = a + 2b - aba⊗b=a+2b−ab, does ⊗\otimes⊗ have an identity element?

Answer choices

Solve the left condition first.

a⊗e=a+2e−ae=a⟹e(2−a)=0a \otimes e = a + 2e - ae = a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e(2 - a) = 0a⊗e=a+2e−ae=a⟹e(2−a)=0

For this to hold for every aaa, the only candidate is e=0e = 0e=0, and it does satisfy the left side for every aaa: a⊗0=a+0−0=aa \otimes 0 = a + 0 - 0 = aa⊗0=a+0−0=a. But an identity must work from both sides, so the right condition still has to be checked separately.

0⊗a=0+2a−0=2a0 \otimes a = 0 + 2a - 0 = 2a0⊗a=0+2a−0=2a

Since 2a≠a2a \ne a2a=a except when a=0a = 0a=0, the candidate fails on the right, so no value is a two-sided identity: ⊗\otimes⊗ has no identity element. Passing one side is not enough; both conditions must hold.

Solving an equation that hides a custom operation

Because a defined operation is only a rule, an equation that contains one is only an ordinary equation in disguise. Substitute the definition to strip the invented symbol away, and you are left with the linear or simple quadratic equations you already solve. The unknown can sit in either slot.

Worked example 6 Solving for an unknown input

Let a⋆b=2a+b−1a \star b = 2a + b - 1a⋆b=2a+b−1. Solve x⋆4=11x \star 4 = 11x⋆4=11, then 3⋆x=103 \star x = 103⋆x=10. Finally, with a⋄b=a2−ba \diamond b = a^2 - ba⋄b=a2−b, solve x⋄3=22x \diamond 3 = 22x⋄3=22.

For x⋆4=11x \star 4 = 11x⋆4=11, substitute a=xa = xa=x and b=4b = 4b=4 into the rule to turn the starred equation into a plain one:

2x+4−1=11⟹2x+3=11⟹2x=8⟹x=4.2x + 4 - 1 = 11 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x + 3 = 11 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x = 8 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 4.2x+4−1=11⟹2x+3=11⟹2x=8⟹x=4.

For 3⋆x=103 \star x = 103⋆x=10, the unknown is the second input, so substitute a=3a = 3a=3 and b=xb = xb=x:

2(3)+x−1=10⟹x+5=10⟹x=5.2(3) + x - 1 = 10 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x + 5 = 10 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 5.2(3)+x−1=10⟹x+5=10⟹x=5.

For x⋄3=22x \diamond 3 = 22x⋄3=22, the rule squares the first input, so substituting gives a simple quadratic:

x2−3=22⟹x2=25⟹x=5  or  x=−5.x^2 - 3 = 22 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 = 25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 5 \ \text{ or } \ x = -5.x2−3=22⟹x2=25⟹x=5  or  x=−5.

Squaring produces two solutions, both valid, since 525^252 and (−5)2(-5)^2(−5)2 each equal 252525. In every case, substituting the definition turned the starred or diamonded equation into one you already know how to solve.

2x+5−1=16⟹2x+4=16⟹2x=12⟹x=62x + 5 - 1 = 16 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x + 4 = 16 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x = 12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 62x+5−1=16⟹2x+4=16⟹2x=12⟹x=6

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating the symbol as a mystery. A star, diamond, or circle-plus is not new arithmetic. It is a rule written with the usual operations, so read the definition and substitute the inputs into it.
  • Ignoring the order of operations inside the rule. In a⋆b=2a+b−1a \star b = 2a + b - 1a⋆b=2a+b−1 you must compute 2a2a2a before adding. Substituting the numbers does not suspend the order of operations.
  • Assuming the operation is commutative. Do not assume it: a⋆ba \star ba⋆b and b⋆ab \star ab⋆a can differ. Check the two orders instead of assuming they match.
  • Assuming the operation is associative. Do not assume this either: (a⋆b)⋆c(a \star b) \star c(a⋆b)⋆c can differ from a⋆(b⋆c)a \star (b \star c)a⋆(b⋆c). Evaluate the innermost parentheses first, and do not drop them.
  • Substituting only part of an expression input. When a slot holds an expression like k+1k + 1k+1, the whole expression goes in. For a⋄b=a2−ba \diamond b = a^2 - ba⋄b=a2−b that means (k+1)2(k + 1)^2(k+1)2, not k2+1k^2 + 1k2+1.
  • Expecting an identity element to exist. Some operations have one, like 000 for ⊕\oplus⊕, and some have none, like the star. Test both a⋆e=aa \star e = aa⋆e=a and e⋆a=ae \star a = ae⋆a=a; if either fails for some value of aaa, there is no identity.

Key takeaways

  • A custom operation is a binary operation defined by a rule for its two inputs, usually written as a formula with familiar operations. The symbol is the only new thing.
  • Evaluate it by substituting the inputs into the defining rule and simplifying with the ordinary order of operations. A slot may hold a whole expression, which drops in as a single block.
  • A custom operation is not commutative unless you show otherwise: a⋆ba \star ba⋆b and b⋆ab \star ab⋆a can differ, because the rule treats its two slots differently. Commutativity is a property to check, not assume.
  • A custom operation is not associative unless you show otherwise: (a⋆b)⋆c(a \star b) \star c(a⋆b)⋆c and a⋆(b⋆c)a \star (b \star c)a⋆(b⋆c) can differ. Work nested operations from the inside out, evaluating the innermost parentheses first.
  • An identity element is a value eee with a⋆e=aa \star e = aa⋆e=a and e⋆a=ae \star a = ae⋆a=a for every aaa (both sides, since these operations need not be commutative). Find it, or rule it out, by solving that condition; some operations have one and some do not.
  • Solve an equation containing a custom operation by substituting the definition, which turns it into an ordinary linear or simple quadratic equation.

A bit of history (optional)

Every operation symbol on this page was invented by somebody, and the famous ones started out looking just as strange as a star or a diamond.

The plus and minus marks first reached print in 1489, in a German arithmetic book by Johannes Widmann. They gave no instruction there: they labelled crates that held more or less than their stated weight, a surplus and a shortfall. Only later did other writers promote them into operations you carry out.

The equals sign is younger still. Robert Recorde, a Welsh physician who wrote the first algebra book in English, introduced it in 1557. He chose two parallel lines, he explained, because no two things could be more equal, a reason argued for, not a rule handed down from anywhere.

A reader in 1489 had to be told what a new sign did, the same way a contest problem today has to tell you that a⋆b=2a+b−1a \star b = 2a + b - 1a⋆b=2a+b−1. The definition is the entire content of the symbol; the shape is only a label. So when a fresh symbol turns up, read the rule written beside it, drop the two inputs into their slots, and simplify with the order of operations you already trust.

    3. Two rows are not the whole table . Reasoning, 8 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Reasoning 8 pts

    A table can look like direct variation if you only glance at two of its rows. This question tests whether two rows are ever enough to trust the whole table.

    1. A
      Part A.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Both give y/x=1.5y/x = 1.5y/x=1.5, so the two rows alone are consistent with direct variation with k=1.5k = 1.5k=1.5.

    Part B

    No. At x=6x = 6x=6, y=1.5xy = 1.5xy=1.5x predicts y=9y = 9y=9, but the table gives y=8y = 8y=8. The third row breaks the pattern, so the claim is false: two rows agreeing does not force the whole table to be direct variation.

    Part C

    Test y/xy/xy/x across every row, not just one pair. Any two rows where xxx doubles and yyy also doubles give matching ratios automatically, provided x≠0x \neq 0x=0, so while other rows stay untested that agreement carries no information about them.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Divide yyy by xxx in each of the first two rows.

    32=1.5,64=1.5.\frac{3}{2} = 1.5, \qquad \frac{6}{4} = 1.5.23​=1.5,46​=1.5.

    Both ratios agree at 1.51.51.5, so these two rows by themselves look exactly like direct variation with k=1.5k = 1.5k=1.5.

    Part C

    The correct test checks y/xy/xy/x (or xyxyxy, for inverse variation) across ALL of the rows, not a single chosen pair.

    The reason a matching pair says nothing about the rows it did not touch is algebraic, not just bad luck: if xxx doubles from x0x_0x0​ to 2x02x_02x0​ and yyy also doubles from y0y_0y0​ to 2y02y_02y0​, then

    2y02x0=y0x0\frac{2y_0}{2x_0} = \frac{y_0}{x_0}2x0​2y0​​=x0​y0​​

    automatically, for any starting pair with x0≠0x_0 \neq 0x0​=0 (and a row with x0=0x_0 = 0x0​=0 has no ratio to test in the first place). Two rows related by doubling will always look consistent with direct variation, whatever the untested rows do, because that agreement is a fact about doubling itself, not about the table.

    Note the exact limit of this. If those two rows are the only rows the table has, then checking both of them IS checking every row, and the matching ratios do settle it. The gap opens only when rows are left untested, which is why the test is stated as every row rather than as some fixed number of rows.

    Another way: Test every row's ratio at once, rather than building an equation first

    Instead of finding kkk from the first two rows and then checking the third against it, compute y/xy/xy/x for all three rows in one pass: 3/2=1.53/2 = 1.53/2=1.5, 6/4=1.56/4 = 1.56/4=1.5, and 8/6=4/38/6 = 4/38/6=4/3.     Worked solution

    Part A

    Substitute the known triple into the equation and isolate kkk.

    y0=kx0z0  ⟹  k=y0z0x0.y_0 = \frac{k x_0}{z_0} \implies k = \frac{y_0 z_0}{x_0}.y0​=z0​kx0​​⟹k=x0​y0​z0​​.

    Combined variation, like direct and inverse variation, has exactly one thing left open once the form of the equation is assumed: the constant kkk. A single equation in a single unknown pins down that unknown completely, so one complete triple of matching values is exactly enough, no more and no less, to determine kkk and therefore the whole relationship for every other pair of xxx and zzz.

    Part B

    Substitute into the formula from part A.

    k=y0z0x0=8×54=10.k = \frac{y_0 z_0}{x_0} = \frac{8 \times 5}{4} = 10.k=x0​y0​z0​​=48×5​=10.

    Use that same constant at the new values.

    y=10×63=20.y = \frac{10 \times 6}{3} = 20.y=310×6​=20.

    Part C

    Use y=10x/zy = 10x/zy=10x/z with k=10k = 10k=10 carried from part B.

    Doubling both: x=12x = 12x=12, z=6z = 6z=6.

    y=10×126=20.y = \frac{10 \times 12}{6} = 20.y=610×12​=20.

    Unchanged from the original 20, because the ratio x/z=6/3=2x/z = 6/3 = 2x/z=6/3=2 is the same as 12/6=212/6 = 212/6=2.

    Doubling only xxx: x=12x = 12x=12, zzz stays at 333.

    y=10×123=40.y = \frac{10 \times 12}{3} = 40.y=310×12​=40.

    This time the ratio x/zx/zx/z itself doubled, from 222 to 444, and yyy doubled right along with it. The difference between the two outcomes comes entirely from whether the ratio x/zx/zx/z changed or stayed put.

    Part D

    Suppose xxx and zzz are both scaled by the same factor p≠0p \neq 0p=0, so x→pxx \to pxx→px and z→pzz \to pzz→pz. Substitute into the combined-variation equation.

    ynew=k(px)pz=pp⋅kxz=kxz=y.y_{\text{new}} = \frac{k(px)}{pz} = \frac{p}{p} \cdot \frac{kx}{z} = \frac{kx}{z} = y.ynew​=pzk(px)​=pp​⋅zkx​=zkx​=y.

    The factor ppp appears in both the numerator and the denominator, so it cancels regardless of what ppp is, and yyy is left exactly as it was.

    Adding the same number nnn to both instead, x→x+nx \to x + nx→x+n and z→z+nz \to z + nz→z+n, does not produce this cancellation:

    ynew=k(x+n)z+n,y_{\text{new}} = \frac{k(x + n)}{z + n},ynew​=z+nk(x+n)​,

    and there is no common factor to divide out of the top and bottom, so this new value generally differs from yyy. Multiplying by the same factor preserves the ratio x/zx/zx/z that the formula actually depends on; adding the same number changes that ratio, which is why only the multiplicative version leaves yyy untouched.

    In one line

    k=y0z0/x0k = y_0 z_0 / x_0k=y0​z0​/x0​ for combined variation, exactly as one triple fixed kkk for direct and inverse variation; for (4,5,8)(4, 5, 8)(4,5,8) this gives k=10k = 10k=10, and at x=6,z=3x = 6, z = 3x=6,z=3, y=20y = 20y=20.

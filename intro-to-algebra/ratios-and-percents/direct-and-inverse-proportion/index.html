Direct and Inverse Proportion

Intuition

The ratio and percent lessons kept circling one idea without ever naming it. Buy twice as many oranges and you pay twice as much; feed twice as many people and a recipe needs twice the flour. Some pairs of quantities are locked together so that their ratio never changes. For those pairs a single fixed number, the amount of one for each unit of the other, controls the whole relationship. Other pairs pull the opposite way: put twice as many workers on a job and it takes half as long. In that kind of pair it is the product that stays fixed. This lesson names both patterns, direct and inverse variation, and pins down the one constant that governs each. For those two patterns, the lesson hands you a single reliable method: use one known pair of values to find the constant, then let the constant answer every other question. The same idea returns later for relationships that depend on more than one quantity, where it takes a complete set of matching values, not just a pair, to pin down the constant.

Learning goals

  • Recognize direct variation as y=kxy = kxy=kx with a constant ratio
  • Identify inverse variation as y=kxy = \dfrac{k}{x}y=xk​ with a constant product
  • Find kkk from one known pair for direct or inverse variation, then reuse it
  • Read a table by testing whether y/xy/xy/x or xyxyxy repeats
  • Extend to joint y=kxzy = kxzy=kxz and combined variation

Direct variation

Two quantities are in direct variation when one is always a fixed multiple of the other. We say yyy varies directly with xxx, or that yyy is directly proportional to xxx, when there is a constant kkk with

y=kx.y = kx.y=kx.

The number kkk is the constant of proportionality, the single value that turns each xxx into its matching yyy. Dividing both sides by xxx (for x≠0x \neq 0x=0) shows what stays fixed:

k=yx.k = \frac{y}{x}.k=xy​.

The ratio y/xy/xy/x is the same for every matching pair, and that shared ratio is exactly kkk. Because yyy is always the same multiple of xxx, doubling xxx doubles yyy, tripling xxx triples yyy, and halving xxx halves yyy. In every example in this lesson the quantities are positive counts or amounts, apples, dollars, weight, so more of one always means more of the other: the two rise and fall together, locked in step.

Here is the cost of apples sold at a fixed price. Watch the ratio column hold steady:

Every row has y/x=1.5y/x = 1.5y/x=1.5, so the cost varies directly with the number of apples, and the constant of proportionality is k=1.5k = 1.5k=1.5 dollars per apple. Plot those pairs on the coordinate plane and they line up: the points (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3), (4,6)(4, 6)(4,6), (6,9)(6, 9)(6,9), and (10,15)(10, 15)(10,15) all sit on one straight line. That line runs through the origin, because x=0x = 0x=0 forces y=k⋅0=0y = k \cdot 0 = 0y=k⋅0=0. A smaller example makes the shape plain.

The line through the origin is the visual signature of direct variation. Whatever the value of kkk, a direct relationship always graphs as a straight line through the origin, and kkk sets how steep that line is.

Find the constant, then solve

The reason variation is so useful is that a single matching pair (or, for joint and combined variation met later in this lesson, one complete matching set) unlocks the whole relationship. You do not need a table; you need one complete measurement. The four-step method never changes, only what you substitute does:

  1. Write the variation equation with kkk still unknown (y=kxy = kxy=kx for direct variation).
  2. Substitute the known values, one matching pair for direct or inverse variation, one complete matching set for joint or combined variation, and solve for kkk.
  3. Rewrite the equation with kkk filled in.
  4. Substitute the new values and compute the answer.

Finding kkk first turns the problem into simple arithmetic: once kkk is known, the equation produces every other answer directly.

Worked example 1 A spring stretch (direct variation)

The distance a spring stretches varies directly with the weight hung from it. An 888 kg weight stretches the spring 121212 cm. How far will a 101010 kg weight stretch it?

Let yyy be the stretch and xxx the weight. Direct variation means y=kxy = kxy=kx. Substitute the known pair, y=12y = 12y=12 when x=8x = 8x=8, and solve for the constant:

12=k×8,k=128=1.5.12 = k \times 8, \qquad k = \frac{12}{8} = 1.5.12=k×8,k=812​=1.5.

The constant of proportionality is 1.51.51.5 cm per kg. Put it back into the equation:

y=1.5x.y = 1.5x.y=1.5x.

Now answer the question with x=10x = 10x=10:

y=1.5×10=15 cm.y = 1.5 \times 10 = 15 \text{ cm}.y=1.5×10=15 cm.

So a 101010 kg weight stretches the spring 151515 cm. Check the ratio: 128=1510=1.5\frac{12}{8} = \frac{15}{10} = 1.5812​=1015​=1.5, so both pairs share the same constant, exactly as direct variation demands.

Inverse variation

Not every pair of quantities rises together. Sometimes making one larger makes the other smaller in exact proportion. Add more workers and a job finishes sooner; drive faster and a fixed trip takes less time. We say yyy varies inversely with xxx, or that yyy is inversely proportional to xxx, when there is a constant kkk with

y=kx,equivalentlyxy=k(x≠0).y = \frac{k}{x}, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad xy = k \qquad (x \neq 0).y=xk​,equivalentlyxy=k(x=0).

Now it is the product xyxyxy that stays fixed, not the ratio. Because y=k/xy = k/xy=k/x, doubling xxx halves yyy, tripling xxx cuts yyy to a third, and halving xxx doubles yyy. The quantities move in opposite directions, but not by adding and subtracting: they move so that their product never budges.

Here is a fixed trip covered at different speeds, so the distance stays the same. Watch the product column:

xxx (mph)yyy (hours)xyxyxy

Every row has xy=120xy = 120xy=120, so the travel time varies inversely with the speed, and the constant is k=120k = 120k=120 (the fixed 120120120-mile distance). The find-the-constant method works just as before, only now the equation is y=k/xy = k/xy=k/x.

The difference between the two kinds of variation is which thing you are holding still, and a rectangle built one square at a time can show both, as long as you are careful about which two quantities are playing the roles of xxx and yyy in each demonstration. A rectangle’s area is the product of its two side lengths, and its perimeter is not.

Start with inverse variation. Here xxx is the width and yyy is the height. Keep the area reading 242424: build 333 by 888, then 444 by 666, then 666 by 444, then 888 by 333. Doubling the width from 333 to 666 halves the height from 888 to 444, precisely what y=24/xy = 24/xy=24/x demands, and the area never moves off 242424. Watch the perimeter while you do it. It reads 222222, 202020, 202020, 222222, so the sum of the sides is emphatically not what is being held constant here. Only the product, width times height, is, which is the whole content of xy=kxy = kxy=k.

Now direct variation, from the same figure, but pair the width with a different quantity this time: the area itself, not the height. Set the height to 333 and leave it alone, then step the width up from 111: the area runs 333, 666, 999, 121212, and so on. Divide each area by its width and you get 333 every time, so with xxx the width and yyy the area, y=3xy = 3xy=3x with a constant of proportionality k=3k = 3k=3, which is just the height you fixed. Width and height are not the variation pair this time, width and area are, and holding the height still is exactly what keeps that ratio constant. The same rectangle can demonstrate either pattern, but only one pairing of quantities at a time; deciding what is playing the role of xxx and what is playing the role of yyy is part of reading any variation problem.

Worked example 2 Workers and time (inverse variation)

Assume every worker paves at the same steady rate and none gets in another’s way. Under that assumption the time to pave a road varies inversely with the number of workers assigned. With 444 workers the job takes 999 days. How long will it take with 666 workers?

Let yyy be the days and xxx the number of workers. Inverse variation means xy=kxy = kxy=k, so find the constant by multiplying the known pair:

k=4×9=36.k = 4 \times 9 = 36.k=4×9=36.

The fixed amount of work is 363636 worker-days. Write the equation for the time:

y=36x.y = \frac{36}{x}.y=x36​.

Now set x=6x = 6x=6:

y=366=6 days.y = \frac{36}{6} = 6 \text{ days}.y=636​=6 days.

So 666 workers finish in 666 days. Notice the time fell from 999 days to 666, not by subtracting the two extra workers but because the product of workers and days must stay 363636. The tempting wrong move is to subtract; the correct relationship divides. Headcount alone predicts the time only because every worker paves at the same rate; with workers of different speeds it is their combined rate, not their count, that the time varies against.

Check your understanding

k=45×8=360 milesk = 45 \times 8 = 360 \text{ miles}k=45×8=360 miles

Then y=360xy = \frac{360}{x}y=x360​, so at x=60x = 60x=60, y=36060=6y = \frac{360}{60} = 6y=60360​=6 hours. More speed means less time, so the answer must fall below 888.

Why a single constant is enough

For direct and inverse variation, one data pair fixes the whole relationship#

Take direct variation first. To say yyy varies directly with xxx is to say y=kxy = kxy=kx for some fixed number kkk, and that single number is the only thing the relationship leaves open: the equation, the table, and the graph are all settled the moment kkk is known. So how much information does it take to find kkk? Exactly one pair of matching values. Suppose yyy equals y0y_0y0​ when xxx equals x0x_0x0​, with x0≠0x_0 \neq 0x0​=0. Substituting gives y0=kx0y_0 = k x_0y0​=kx0​, and dividing by x0x_0x0​ gives k=y0/x0k = y_0 / x_0k=y0​/x0​. One pair, one division, and kkk is known; from there y=kxy = kxy=kx produces yyy for any xxx you like.

Inverse variation runs the same way with one change. Here y=k/xy = k/xy=k/x, so a known pair (x0,y0)(x_0, y_0)(x0​,y0​) gives y0=k/x0y_0 = k / x_0y0​=k/x0​, and multiplying by x0x_0x0​ gives k=x0y0k = x_0 y_0k=x0​y0​. Again a single pair pins down kkk, and again the whole relationship follows. This is why every direct or inverse variation problem needs just one complete pair of values to begin: that pair is enough to find the constant, and the constant answers everything else. Joint and combined variation, met later in this lesson, follow the identical logic with more quantities: instead of one pair, it takes one complete set of matching values, one value for every quantity in the equation, to pin down kkk.

Reading a table: ratio or product?

When a problem hands you a table and does not say which kind of variation it is, let the numbers decide. Test the ratio y/xy/xy/x down the rows first: if it repeats, the relationship is direct and that repeated value is kkk. If the ratio drifts, test the product xyxyxy: if it repeats, the relationship is inverse and that repeated value is kkk. If neither holds steady, the relationship is something else (a quantity that grows with the square of another varies by a power, and those wait for later chapters).

Worked example 3 Which kind is it? Reading a table

A table pairs these values: (x,y)=(3,8), (4,6), (6,4), (8,3)(x, y) = (3, 8),\ (4, 6),\ (6, 4),\ (8, 3)(x,y)=(3,8), (4,6), (6,4), (8,3). Decide whether the variation is direct or inverse, find the constant, and predict yyy when x=12x = 12x=12.

First test the ratio y/xy/xy/x for direct variation. The first two ratios are 83\frac{8}{3}38​ and 64=1.5\frac{6}{4} = 1.546​=1.5, which already disagree, so the ratio is not constant and the variation is not direct.

Now test the product xyxyxy for inverse variation:

3×8=24,4×6=24,6×4=24,8×3=24.3 \times 8 = 24, \quad 4 \times 6 = 24, \quad 6 \times 4 = 24, \quad 8 \times 3 = 24.3×8=24,4×6=24,6×4=24,8×3=24.

The product is 242424 in every column, so the variation is inverse with k=24k = 24k=24, and the equation is y=24xy = \frac{24}{x}y=x24​. Predict the value at x=12x = 12x=12:

y=2412=2.y = \frac{24}{12} = 2.y=1224​=2.

So when x=12x = 12x=12, y=2y = 2y=2. A steady product, not a steady ratio, is what marks the table as inverse.

Joint and combined variation

Real quantities often depend on several others at once, and the constant-of-proportionality idea stretches to cover them. When yyy varies directly with two quantities together, we say yyy varies jointly with xxx and zzz:

y=kxz.y = kxz.y=kxz.

When yyy varies directly with one quantity and inversely with another, the relationship is called combined variation:

y=kxz.y = \frac{kx}{z}.y=zkx​.

A quantity in the numerator drives yyy up as it grows; a quantity in the denominator drives yyy down. In both cases the method is unchanged: substitute one complete set of matching values to find kkk, then use the finished equation. The only new care is placing each quantity on the correct side of the fraction.

Worked example 4 A banner's cost (joint variation)

The cost of a printed banner varies jointly with its width and its height. A banner 333 ft wide and 444 ft tall costs 181818 dollars. What does a banner 555 ft wide and 666 ft tall cost?

Let ccc be the cost, www the width, and hhh the height. Joint variation means c=kwhc = kwhc=kwh. Substitute the known banner to find the constant:

18=k×3×4=12k,k=1812=1.5.18 = k \times 3 \times 4 = 12k, \qquad k = \frac{18}{12} = 1.5.18=k×3×4=12k,k=1218​=1.5.

The constant is 1.51.51.5 dollars per square foot. Write the finished equation and apply it to the new banner:

c=1.5wh=1.5×5×6=45 dollars.c = 1.5wh = 1.5 \times 5 \times 6 = 45 \text{ dollars}.c=1.5wh=1.5×5×6=45 dollars.

So the larger banner costs 454545 dollars. Joint variation is just direct variation with two partners at once, and one complete measurement, cost together with both side lengths, fixes the constant.

Worked example 5 A beam's load (combined variation)

The safe load a wooden beam can carry varies directly with its width and inversely with its length. A beam 444 inches wide and 101010 feet long safely carries 800800800 pounds. How much can a beam 666 inches wide and 888 feet long carry?

Let LLL be the safe load, www the width, and ℓ\ellℓ the length. Combined variation means

L=kwℓ.L = \frac{kw}{\ell}.L=ℓkw​.

Substitute the first beam, L=800L = 800L=800 when w=4w = 4w=4 and ℓ=10\ell = 10ℓ=10:

800=k×410=2k5,k=800×52=2,000.800 = \frac{k \times 4}{10} = \frac{2k}{5}, \qquad k = 800 \times \frac{5}{2} = 2{,}000.800=10k×4​=52k​,k=800×25​=2,000.

Write the finished equation and apply it to the second beam, w=6w = 6w=6 and ℓ=8\ell = 8ℓ=8:

L=2,000×68=12,0008=1,500 pounds.L = \frac{2{,}000 \times 6}{8} = \frac{12{,}000}{8} = 1{,}500 \text{ pounds}.L=82,000×6​=812,000​=1,500 pounds.

So the second beam safely carries 1,5001{,}5001,500 pounds. This beam is both wider and shorter. Width sits in the numerator, so more width raises the load. Length sits in the denominator, so less length also raises it, and both changes push the safe load up from 800800800 to 1,5001{,}5001,500 pounds.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing direct with inverse. Direct variation keeps the ratio y/xy/xy/x constant and uses y=kxy = kxy=kx; inverse variation keeps the product xyxyxy constant and uses y=k/xy = k/xy=k/x. One quantity growing while the other shrinks is a clue for inverse variation, not proof of it. Before solving, test the ratio and the product and decide which one actually stays fixed; forcing an inverse relationship into the direct formula makes the answer grow when it should shrink.
  • Adding or subtracting instead of scaling. With 444 workers a job takes 999 days, so 888 workers do not take 9−4=59 - 4 = 59−4=5 days. They take half the time, 4.54.54.5 days, because doubling the workers halves the time. The direct, inverse, joint, and combined patterns in this lesson all multiply and divide; none of them add or subtract.
  • Skipping the constant. Do not leap from the given values straight to the answer without a method behind it. Scaling directly between two known values works when the numbers line up evenly for direct or inverse variation, but finding kkk first works every time, for all four patterns in this lesson: write the equation, solve for kkk, then use it.
  • Misplacing a variable in combined variation. A quantity you vary directly with belongs in the numerator; one you vary inversely with belongs in the denominator. Swapping them turns y=kx/zy = kx/zy=kx/z into the wrong relationship.

Key takeaways

  • Direct variation: y=kxy = kxy=kx, so the ratio y/x=ky/x = ky/x=k stays constant; doubling xxx doubles yyy, and the graph is a straight line through the origin.
  • Inverse variation: y=kxy = \dfrac{k}{x}y=xk​, so the product xy=kxy = kxy=k stays constant; doubling xxx halves yyy.
  • kkk is the constant of proportionality, and a relationship has exactly one, so one matching set of values determines it, as long as every directly varying quantity in that set is nonzero (for direct and inverse variation, any (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) pair with x≠0x \neq 0x=0 will do; for joint and combined variation, every directly varying variable in the set must be nonzero).
  • The method: write the variation equation, then find kkk, using one known pair for direct or inverse variation, or one complete matching set for joint or combined variation; rewrite the equation with kkk filled in, then substitute the new values.
  • Recognize from a table: if y/xy/xy/x repeats, the variation is direct; if xyxyxy repeats, it is inverse.
  • Joint variation is y=kxzy = kxzy=kxz (direct with two quantities); combined variation is y=kxzy = \dfrac{kx}{z}y=zkx​ (direct with one, inverse with another).

A bit of history (optional)

Greek mathematics could prove that two quantities kept step, and it did so without ever writing down a number for how.

Eudoxus, a Greek mathematician of about 350 BCE, built the theory that did the proving. It was powerful and it was general. It handled lengths, areas, weights and times, and it held up even for the pairs that no fraction could describe. What it never produced is the thing this lesson opens with. There is no constant anywhere in it. A proportion was a relation among four quantities, not a single number you could measure once and keep.

Naming that number changed what a proportion is for. Once you can write y=kxy = kxy=kx, a single measured pair fixes the constant. That one constant answers every question you have not thought to ask yet.

Robert Boyle showed what that is worth. In 1662 he trapped a column of air, holding the amount of gas and the temperature fixed. However hard he squeezed it, the pressure times the volume held very nearly steady. One measurement, one constant, and every later squeeze predicted.

That is this lesson’s method, at work on the physical world: find the constant, and then let the constant do the work.

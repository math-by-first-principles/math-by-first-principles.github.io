Rate and Work Problems

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

The last lesson used equally fast workers: twice as many could finish a fixed job in half the time. Workers with different speeds need a different comparison. A painter who finishes a room in 444 hours paints 14\frac1441​ of it each hour; one who needs 666 hours paints 16\frac1661​ each hour.

Those hourly shares add when both work steadily on different parts of the same room without slowing each other down. Their completion times do not add. Thinking in rates lets you combine workers, account for a drain, and calculate travel times and average speeds.

Learning goals

  • Turn a completion time into the rate 1T\tfrac{1}{T}T1​
  • Add or subtract work rates to find a team's time or a missing worker's time
  • Subtract a drain's rate from a filling rate, and tell whether the tank can fill at all
  • Apply d=rtd = rtd=rt, solving for whichever is unknown
  • Compute average speed from totals, not from a mean

Part 1: Work rate problems

From time to rate

Suppose that a job is done at a steady pace, and that it takes TTT units of time from start to finish. Then in one unit of time exactly the fraction 1T\frac{1}{T}T1​ of that job gets done. That fraction per unit time is the work rate. A pump that empties a pool in 555 hours removes one fifth of the pool each hour, so its rate is 15\frac{1}{5}51​ pool per hour. In general,

rate=1T(job per unit time).\text{rate} = \frac{1}{T} \quad \text{(job per unit time)}.rate=T1​(job per unit time).

For one whole job, rate times time is 111, so rate and completion time are reciprocals:

rate=1time,time=1rate.\text{rate} = \frac{1}{\text{time}}, \qquad \text{time} = \frac{1}{\text{rate}}.rate=time1​,time=rate1​.

This is inverse variation: a job done twice as fast takes half as long. Use the same time unit for all rates. Work with rates while combining contributions, then convert the final rate back to a time.

Why work rates add

Count what each worker finishes in the same hour.

Worked example 1 Two painters, one room

One painter can paint a room alone in 444 hours; a second painter can paint the same room alone in 666 hours. Working together, how long do they take?

Turn each time into a rate. The first paints 14\frac{1}{4}41​ of the room per hour, the second paints 16\frac{1}{6}61​ per hour. Add the rates over the common denominator 121212 to get the combined rate:

14+16=312+212=512 of the room per hour.\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{12} + \frac{2}{12} = \frac{5}{12} \text{ of the room per hour}.41​+61​=123​+122​=125​ of the room per hour.

Together they paint 512\frac{5}{12}125​ of the room each hour. The time for the whole room is the reciprocal of that rate:

T=1 5/12 =125=2.4 hours.T = \frac{1}{\,5/12\,} = \frac{12}{5} = 2.4 \text{ hours}.T=5/121​=512​=2.4 hours.

So they finish in 125\frac{12}{5}512​ hours, which is 222 hours and 242424 minutes (since 0.4×60=240.4 \times 60 = 240.4×60=24). Notice the answer is smaller than either painter’s solo time of 444 or 666 hours, exactly as it must be: two people working together beat either one alone.

Nothing about that reasoning depended on the specific numbers 444 and 666. The same addition works for any two workers with steady rates on the same job, as long as they neither duplicate each other’s work nor slow each other down, which is worth stating in general.

Why work rates add#

Suppose two workers take 666 and 121212 hours alone. In one hour at steady rates, they complete 16\frac1661​ and 112\frac1{12}121​ of the same job. If they work on different parts without slowing each other down, those completed shares add:

16+112=212+112=14.\frac16 + \frac1{12} = \frac2{12} + \frac1{12} = \frac14.61​+121​=122​+121​=41​.

Together they finish a quarter of the job per hour, so the whole job takes 1/(1/4)=41/(1/4) = 41/(1/4)=4 hours. Nothing in this reasoning depends on the particular times 666 and 121212.

Suppose two workers take positive times T1T_1T1​ and T2T_2T2​ alone. At steady rates, their contributions per unit of time are 1/T11/T_11/T1​ and 1/T21/T_21/T2​ of the job. If neither repeats the other’s work or slows the other down, the total work per unit time is their sum. Calling the team’s completion time TTT gives

1T=1T1+1T2.\frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2}.T1​=T1​1​+T2​1​.

Take the reciprocal of the combined rate to find the time for one whole job. The same reasoning adds three or more workers’ rates. An action that undoes the work, such as a drain opposing a pipe, contributes a negative rate. A reciprocal gives a completion time only if the net rate is positive.

∎

Working together

As long as each worker holds a steady rate on the same job, and they neither slow each other down nor redo each other’s work, you find how long a team takes by adding the individual rates to get the combined rate, then taking the reciprocal of that sum, exactly as with the two painters above.

Check your understanding

One hose fills a pool in 333 hours; another fills the same pool in 666 hours. Running together, how long do they take to fill it?

Answer choices

Add the rates, not the times. The first hose fills 13\frac{1}{3}31​ per hour and the second 16\frac{1}{6}61​ per hour.

13+16=26+16=12 per hour\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{2} \text{ per hour}31​+61​=62​+61​=21​ per hour

The combined rate is 12\frac{1}{2}21​ pool per hour, so the time is the reciprocal, 222 hours. Averaging the times to 4.54.54.5 or adding them to 999 both ignore that it is the rates that combine.

Finding an unknown time

If you know the team’s rate and all but one worker’s rate, subtract the known contributions. The leftover rate belongs to the missing worker. Take its reciprocal to find that worker’s solo time.

Worked example 2 How long would the second worker take?

Two people raking leaves finish a yard together in 666 hours. Working alone, the first would take 101010 hours. How long would the second take working alone?

Let the second worker’s solo time be T2T_2T2​ hours, so the second rate is 1T2\frac{1}{T_2}T2​1​. The combined rate is 16\frac{1}{6}61​ per hour and the first rate is 110\frac{1}{10}101​ per hour, and the rates add:

110+1T2=16.\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{6}.101​+T2​1​=61​.

Solve for the unknown rate by subtracting the known one, using the common denominator 303030:

1T2=16−110=530−330=230=115.\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{10} = \frac{5}{30} - \frac{3}{30} = \frac{2}{30} = \frac{1}{15}.T2​1​=61​−101​=305​−303​=302​=151​.

The second worker’s rate is 115\frac{1}{15}151​ of the yard per hour, so the solo time is the reciprocal:

T2=15 hours.T_2 = 15 \text{ hours}.T2​=15 hours.

Check it: 110+115=330+230=530=16\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{15} = \frac{3}{30} + \frac{2}{30} = \frac{5}{30} = \frac{1}{6}101​+151​=303​+302​=305​=61​, the combined rate, so the pair really does finish in 666 hours.

Check your understanding

Two people can mow a lawn together in 444 hours. Working alone, one of them would take 555 hours. How long would the other take alone?

Answer choices

Subtract the known rate from the team rate. The team's rate is 14\frac{1}{4}41​ per hour and the known worker's rate is 15\frac{1}{5}51​ per hour:

14−15=520−420=120 per hour\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{5}{20} - \frac{4}{20} = \frac{1}{20} \text{ per hour}41​−51​=205​−204​=201​ per hour

The other worker's rate is 120\frac{1}{20}201​ of the lawn per hour, so the solo time is the reciprocal, 202020 hours. Subtracting or averaging the times themselves, 111 hour or 4.54.54.5 hours, ignores that only rates combine additively.

Filling against a drain

Treat filling as positive and draining as negative. The net rate is the filling rate minus the draining rate. These problems assume steady rates while water is available; a real drain may slow as the level falls.

For an empty tank to fill, the net rate must be positive. If it is zero, the inflow merely replaces the outflow and the level does not rise. If it is negative, water drains faster than it enters, so a partly filled tank loses water until it is empty. It cannot fall below empty, and it cannot fill under these assumptions.

Worked example 3 A pipe and an open drain

A pipe fills an empty tank in 444 hours. At a steady draining rate, an open drain would empty a full tank in 666 hours. If the pipe runs while the drain stays open, how long does the tank take to fill?

The pipe works at +14+\frac{1}{4}+41​ tank per hour. The drain removes water, so it works at −16-\frac{1}{6}−61​ tank per hour. Add the signed rates over the common denominator 121212 to get the net rate:

14−16=312−212=112 tank per hour.\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{12} - \frac{2}{12} = \frac{1}{12} \text{ tank per hour}.41​−61​=123​−122​=121​ tank per hour.

The net rate is positive, so the tank does fill, gaining 112\frac{1}{12}121​ of a tank each hour. The time is the reciprocal of the net rate:

T=1 1/12 =12 hours.T = \frac{1}{\,1/12\,} = 12 \text{ hours}.T=1/121​=12 hours.

So the tank fills in 121212 hours. The open drain stretches a 444-hour fill out to 121212 hours, which fits the picture: some of every hour’s inflow is lost back out the bottom.

Check your understanding

A faucet fills a basin in 333 hours. With the plug out, a drain would empty the full basin in 666 hours. With the faucet on and the drain open, how long does the basin take to fill?

Answer choices

The faucet adds 13\frac{1}{3}31​ per hour and the drain subtracts 16\frac{1}{6}61​ per hour, so combine them with a minus sign.

13−16=26−16=16 per hour\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{6} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{6} \text{ per hour}31​−61​=62​−61​=61​ per hour

The net rate is 16\frac{1}{6}61​ basin per hour, so the fill time is the reciprocal, 666 hours. Adding the rates instead of subtracting would wrongly give 222 hours.

Sometimes the drain wins. Check the net rate’s sign before you take a reciprocal of anything.

Check your understanding

A hose fills a tank in 888 hours. A leak at the bottom would empty a full tank in 555 hours. With the hose running and the leak open, what happens?

Answer choices

The hose contributes 18\frac{1}{8}81​ per hour and the leak subtracts 15\frac{1}{5}51​ per hour:

18−15=540−840=−340 tank per hour\frac{1}{8} - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{5}{40} - \frac{8}{40} = -\frac{3}{40} \text{ tank per hour}81​−51​=405​−408​=−403​ tank per hour

The net rate is negative: the leak drains faster than the hose fills, so the tank cannot fill. Any water already present drains away until the tank is empty. Its reciprocal is negative, so it cannot be a positive time to fill the tank.

When some work is already done

A rate tells you how much work is done in each unit of time. Multiply it by the elapsed time to find the work completed. Subtract that from one whole job. If work remains and the next stage has a positive rate, divide the remaining fraction by that rate:

remaining time=remaining workrate.\text{remaining time} = \frac{\text{remaining work}}{\text{rate}}.remaining time=rateremaining work​.

A zero or negative net rate cannot finish the remaining work. The reciprocal rule is the special case where the remaining work is 111.

Worked example 4 A helper arrives after work has started

Use the painters who take 444 and 666 hours alone. The faster painter works for 111 hour before the other joins. How long does the whole job take, assuming the rates stay steady and the work does not overlap?

The first painter completes 1×14=141 \times \frac14 = \frac141×41​=41​ of the room, leaving 34\frac3443​. Together they work at 14+16=512\frac14 + \frac16 = \frac5{12}41​+61​=125​ room per hour. The remaining work takes

3/45/12=34×125=95=1.8 hours.\frac{3/4}{5/12} = \frac34 \times \frac{12}{5} = \frac95 = 1.8 \text{ hours}.5/123/4​=43​×512​=59​=1.8 hours.

Add the first hour to get 1+1.8=2.81 + 1.8 = 2.81+1.8=2.8 hours from start to finish. Check the completed work: 14+512⋅95=14+34=1\frac14 + \frac5{12}\cdot\frac95 = \frac14 + \frac34 = 141​+125​⋅59​=41​+43​=1.

A partly filled tank uses the same idea. If it is 14\frac1441​ full and gains 112\frac1{12}121​ tank per hour, only 34\frac3443​ remains, so it takes (34)/(112)=9(\frac34)/(\frac1{12}) = 9(43​)/(121​)=9 hours to fill.

Part 2: Distance and speed problems

The distance formula

Travel problems use distance per unit time instead of jobs per unit time. At a steady speed, distance is rate multiplied by time:

d=rt.d = rt.d=rt.

Here rrr is speed, ttt is time, and ddd is distance. Solve for the missing quantity, keeping units consistent and dividing only by nonzero values:

r=dt,t=dr,d=rt.r = \frac{d}{t}, \qquad t = \frac{d}{r}, \qquad d = rt.r=td​,t=rd​,d=rt.

Worked example 5 A steady drive, two ways

A delivery van covers 165165165 miles in 333 hours at a steady speed. First find that speed, then find how far the van travels in 777 hours at the same speed.

The speed is distance divided by time:

r=dt=1653=55 miles per hour.r = \frac{d}{t} = \frac{165}{3} = 55 \text{ miles per hour}.r=td​=3165​=55 miles per hour.

Now hold that speed and run the relationship the other way, with t=7t = 7t=7:

d=rt=55×7=385 miles.d = rt = 55 \times 7 = 385 \text{ miles}.d=rt=55×7=385 miles.

So the van travels at 555555 mph and covers 385385385 miles in 777 hours. The single relationship d=rtd = rtd=rt answered both questions; you only changed which letter you solved for.

Check your understanding

A cyclist covers 181818 miles in 222 hours at a steady speed. Find the speed, the time to cover 636363 miles, and the distance covered in 555 hours at that speed.

Answer choices

First, divide the distance by the time to find the speed:

r=18 mi2 h=9 mi/h.r=\frac{18\text{ mi}}{2\text{ h}}=9\text{ mi/h}.r=2 h18 mi​=9 mi/h.

For the 636363-mile trip, divide the distance by that speed:

t=63 mi9 mi/h=7 h.t=\frac{63\text{ mi}}{9\text{ mi/h}}=7\text{ h}.t=9 mi/h63 mi​=7 h.

For 555 hours of travel, multiply the speed by the time:

d=9 mi/h×5 h=45 mi.d=9\text{ mi/h}\times5\text{ h}=45\text{ mi}.d=9 mi/h×5 h=45 mi.

The units agree: miles divided by miles per hour gives hours, and miles per hour times hours gives miles.

The second choice uses the new travel time as a speed; the third multiplies the new distance by the old time; the fourth starts by multiplying 181818 by 222 instead of dividing.

Average speed done right

For a trip with several legs, add the distances and add the times. Its average speed is

average speed=total distancetotal time.\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}.average speed=total timetotal distance​.

A plain mean of the speeds can give the wrong answer because you may spend more time at one speed. The round trip below shows why.

Worked example 6 There and back at different speeds

You drive to a town 606060 miles away at 303030 mph, then return home along the same road at 606060 mph. What is your average speed for the whole round trip?

Resist averaging 303030 and 606060 to get 454545. Go back to the definition, and build the total distance and the total time from the two legs.

The total distance is the round trip:

d=60+60=120 miles.d = 60 + 60 = 120 \text{ miles}.d=60+60=120 miles.

Now the time for each leg, using t=d/rt = d/rt=d/r. The trip out at 303030 mph takes 6030=2\frac{60}{30} = 23060​=2 hours; the trip back at 606060 mph takes 6060=1\frac{60}{60} = 16060​=1 hour. So the total time is

t=2+1=3 hours.t = 2 + 1 = 3 \text{ hours}.t=2+1=3 hours.

Divide total distance by total time:

average speed=1203=40 miles per hour.\text{average speed} = \frac{120}{3} = 40 \text{ miles per hour}.average speed=3120​=40 miles per hour.

The average is 404040 mph, below the naive 454545 mph. That is because you spent 222 of the 333 hours crawling at the slower 303030 mph and only 111 hour at 606060 mph. More time at the low speed pulls the average down.

Why average speed is total distance over total time#

Consider a different trip: 909090 miles out at 454545 mph and 909090 miles back at 303030 mph. The legs take 222 and 333 hours, so the trip covers 180180180 miles in 555 hours. A steady 363636 mph for 555 hours would cover those same 180180180 miles. That is what average speed measures: the single steady speed that would cover the total distance in the total time. For any trip of positive total time, the equation d=rtd=rtd=rt therefore gives r=d/tr=d/tr=d/t.

On equal-distance legs at two different positive speeds, the slower leg takes longer because time is distance divided by speed. Each extra hour spent at the lower speed pulls the average closer to it. The ordinary mean gives both speeds equal weight, so it is too high in this case.

For this trip, 222 hours at 454545 mph and 333 hours at 303030 mph give (45⋅2+30⋅3)/(2+3)=36(45\cdot2+30\cdot3)/(2+3)=36(45⋅2+30⋅3)/(2+3)=36 mph, below the ordinary mean of 37.537.537.5 mph. In general, speeds r1,r2r_1,r_2r1​,r2​ held for times t1,t2t_1,t_2t1​,t2​ give (r1t1+r2t2)/(t1+t2)(r_1t_1+r_2t_2)/(t_1+t_2)(r1​t1​+r2​t2​)/(t1​+t2​). Each speed is weighted by its share of the total time. When the times are equal, this becomes the ordinary mean. Equal speeds also give that mean, but equal distances at different speeds do not.

∎

Check your understanding

A cyclist rides 121212 miles out at 121212 mph and returns the same 121212 miles at 444 mph. What is the average speed for the whole ride?

Do not average 121212 and 444 to get 888. Use total distance over total time. The distance is 12+12=2412 + 12 = 2412+12=24 miles. The times are 1212=1\frac{12}{12} = 11212​=1 hour out and 124=3\frac{12}{4} = 3412​=3 hours back, so the total time is 444 hours.

average speed=244=6 mph\text{average speed} = \frac{24}{4} = 6 \text{ mph}average speed=424​=6 mph

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Averaging or adding the times. Two workers’ solo times never average or add to give the team’s time. Add their rates instead, then take the reciprocal; for workers holding steady rates on the same job without duplicating work or slowing each other down, positive rates add to a rate exceeding either worker’s alone, so the team finishes faster than either could solo.
  • Forgetting to flip the rate back to a time. Adding rates gives you a combined rate, not a time. The question asks for a time, so the last step is a reciprocal, and that reciprocal is a completion time only when the combined or net rate is positive; a zero or negative net rate means the job never finishes at all.
  • Adding a drain instead of subtracting it. A drain undoes work, so its rate enters the sum with a minus sign, never a plus sign.
  • Averaging the two speeds. Average speed is total distance over total time, not simply the mean of the speeds. For equal-distance legs at different positive speeds, the true average is below the mean. Equal-time legs do use the mean, because both speeds get the same time weight.
  • Mixing up the parts of d=rtd = rtd=rt. Keep the units straight: a speed is miles per hour, a time is hours, and their product is miles. Divide to get a time, multiply to get a distance.

Key takeaways

  • A job finished steadily in positive time TTT is done at the rate 1T\frac{1}{T}T1​ per unit time, and rate and time are reciprocals: time=1rate\text{time} = \frac{1}{\text{rate}}time=rate1​.
  • Rates add when workers hold steady rates on the same job without duplicating work or slowing each other down: 1T1+1T2=1T\frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{T}T1​1​+T2​1​=T1​. Add the rates, then take the reciprocal to get the together time TTT.
  • To find a missing worker’s time, subtract the known rates from the combined rate, then flip the leftover rate. For a partly completed job, divide the remaining work by the rate for that stage.
  • A drain is a negative rate: the net rate is the signed sum, and the fill time is its reciprocal when the sum is positive.
  • Distance, rate, time: d=rtd = rtd=rt, so r=dtr = \frac{d}{t}r=td​ and t=drt = \frac{d}{r}t=rd​; solve for whichever one is unknown.
  • Average speed is total distancetotal time\dfrac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}total timetotal distance​, not the mean of the speeds. Over equal distances at two different speeds it falls below the mean, because more time is spent at the slower speed.

A bit of history (optional)

A reservoir has five channels bringing water into it. Working alone, they fill it in 1/31/31/3, 111, 5/25/25/2, 333, and 555 days. How long will all five take together?

At steady rates, their combined contribution is

3+1+25+13+15=74153+1+\frac25+\frac13+\frac15=\frac{74}{15}3+1+52​+31​+51​=1574​

reservoirs per day. Taking the reciprocal gives 15/7415/7415/74 of a day to fill the reservoir.

This is not a modern plumbing puzzle. It appears in The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, a Chinese collection assembled around the beginning of the Common Era from older material. Its problems connect arithmetic with practical needs such as sharing goods, measuring land, and organizing work. The reservoir question asks the reader to combine unequal contributions, just as the painters’ problem does here. University notes adapt the five-channel problem.

Cistern problems also appear in Greek mathematical writing, including Heron’s work and the Greek Anthology. Their settings differ, but the problem survives: several streams are working at once, and their individual filling times cannot simply be added. The daily need to move and store water gave generations of writers a reason to ask the same mathematical question. Cajori’s history traces these cistern problems.

    1. Two pumps, one pool . Foundational, 9 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 9 pts

    A garden pool is empty. Pump A can fill it alone in 888 hours; pump B can fill it alone in 121212 hours. The gardener runs both pumps at once, starting from empty.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find pump A's rate and pump B's rate, each in pools per hour, then add them to find the combined rate.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Using the combined rate, find how long the two pumps take to fill the pool together, to the nearest tenth of an hour.

      Carry your own answer forward Continue from the combined rate you found in part A, whatever fraction that came out to be.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Pump A, at 888 hours, is the faster of the two. Check whether the team's fill time from part B is at least HALF of that 888 hours. Then argue that this floor can never be broken by any pair of pumps: whichever of the two is the faster, the team's time is always at least half of THAT pump's solo time. Finish by saying what would have to be true of the two pumps for the team to reach the floor exactly.

      Carry your own answer forward Compare against the fill time you found in part B, whatever value it came out to be.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Turn each solo time into a RATE before you combine anything. A rate is one job over the time it takes, and when two workers act at once it is the rates, never the times, that add.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Once you have a combined rate, getting back to a TIME is the same flip you used for each pump alone: take the reciprocal.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      The second pump is the slower one, so ask what the combined rate would be at its very largest, if that partner were as strong as pump A but no stronger. A ceiling on the rate becomes a floor on the reciprocal time.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Pump A's rate is 18\frac{1}{8}81​ pool per hour, pump B's rate is 112\frac{1}{12}121​ pool per hour, and the combined rate is 524\frac{5}{24}245​ pool per hour.

    Part B

    The pumps together fill the pool in 245=4.8\frac{24}{5} = 4.8524​=4.8 hours, that is, 444 hours and 484848 minutes.

    Part C

    The team's time sits above half of pump A's 888 hours, and for any pair the same floor holds against the faster pump's time. The slower pump's rate is at most the faster one's, capping the combined rate at twice the faster rate; a rate at most doubled gives a time at least halved. Only equal solo times reach the floor exactly.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Each pump's rate is the reciprocal of its solo time: pump A does 18\frac{1}{8}81​ of the pool each hour, and pump B does 112\frac{1}{12}121​ each hour. Add the rates over the common denominator 242424:

    18+112=324+224=524 pool per hour.\frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{12} = \frac{3}{24} + \frac{2}{24} = \frac{5}{24} \text{ pool per hour}.81​+121​=243​+242​=245​ pool per hour.

    Part B

    The time together is the reciprocal of the combined rate:

    T=1 5/24 =245=4.8 hours.T = \frac{1}{\,5/24\,} = \frac{24}{5} = 4.8 \text{ hours}.T=5/241​=524​=4.8 hours.

    Since 0.8×60=480.8 \times 60 = 480.8×60=48, that is 444 hours and 484848 minutes.

    Part C

    Call the two rates rAr_ArA​ and rBr_BrB​, both positive. Pump A has the SMALLER solo time, so it has the LARGER rate: rB≤rAr_B \le r_ArB​≤rA​. Replacing rBr_BrB​ by the largest thing it could be gives a ceiling on the combined rate:

    rA+rB≤rA+rA=2rA.r_A + r_B \le r_A + r_A = 2 r_A.rA​+rB​≤rA​+rA​=2rA​.

    Time is the reciprocal of a positive rate, and a reciprocal gets BIGGER as the number it inverts gets SMALLER. A combined rate capped at 2rA2 r_A2rA​ therefore has a reciprocal that cannot fall below the reciprocal of 2rA2 r_A2rA​:

    T=1rA+rB≥12rA=TA2.T = \frac{1}{r_A + r_B} \ge \frac{1}{2 r_A} = \frac{T_A}{2}.T=rA​+rB​1​≥2rA​1​=2TA​​.

    With TA=8T_A = 8TA​=8 hours, that floor is 444 hours: no partner that is not itself faster than pump A can bring the fill time under 444 hours, and part B's time does sit above the floor. (A partner faster than pump A would simply be the faster pump, and the same bound would then be measured against ITS solo time.) The whole argument turns on the single inequality rB≤rAr_B \le r_ArB​≤rA​, which is strict unless the two pumps are equally fast. So the floor is reached exactly when the two pumps have the SAME solo time, each doing half the pool; any genuinely slower partner leaves the team's time strictly above half.

    In one line

    Pump A's rate is 18\frac{1}{8}81​ and pump B's rate is 112\frac{1}{12}121​ pool per hour, combining to 524\frac{5}{24}245​ pool per hour; the team therefore fills the pool in 245=4.8\frac{24}{5} = 4.8524​=4.8 hours. That time cannot fall below half of pump A's 888 hours: the slower pump's rate is at most the faster pump's, so the combined rate is at most 2×182 \times \frac{1}{8}2×81​, and a rate that is at most doubled gives a time that is at least halved. The floor of 444 hours is reached only by two pumps with the same solo time.

    Another way: A direct shortcut for exactly two workers

    For exactly two contributors, the two steps of adding rates and then flipping the sum can be collapsed into a single formula, obtained by carrying the same algebra through in letters:

    T=T1T2T1+T2.T = \frac{T_1 T_2}{T_1 + T_2}.T=T1​+T2​T1​T2​​.

    Here that gives T=8×128+12=9620=4.8T = \dfrac{8 \times 12}{8 + 12} = \dfrac{96}{20} = 4.8T=8+128×12​=2096​=4.8 hours, the same answer reached the long way.

    When it is worth it A fast check for exactly two workers. It does not generalize the way adding rates does once a third worker, or a drain, joins in.

    2. The addition law, extended . Reasoning, 12 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Reasoning 12 pts

    The proof that two workers' rates add rests on one fact: in a single unit of time, whatever the first worker finishes is work the second one never has to redo, so the two fractions simply add. Nothing in that reasoning is really about the number two.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Let three workers, working alone, take T1T_1T1​, T2T_2T2​, and T3T_3T3​ units of time to finish the same job. Reasoning exactly as in the two-worker case, state what fraction of the job each of the three completes in one unit of time, argue why the three fractions can simply be added, and conclude that the time TTT for all three working together satisfies 1T=1T1+1T2+1T3\frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} + \frac{1}{T_3}T1​=T1​1​+T2​1​+T3​1​.

      Complete the derivation Each line should follow from the one above it. Say what lets you take each step. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Suppose now that, instead of a third worker, the job is a tank with two pipes filling it, with individual times T1T_1T1​ and T2T_2T2​, and one drain that, left alone, would empty a full tank in T3T_3T3​ units of time. All three run at once. Using the same one-unit-of-time reasoning as part A, explain why the drain's contribution must enter the combined rate as −1T3-\frac{1}{T_3}−T3​1​ rather than +1T3+\frac{1}{T_3}+T3​1​, and write the resulting net-rate equation.

      Complete the derivation Each line should follow from the one above it. Say what lets you take each step. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Using the addition law from parts A and B, prove that adding any further POSITIVE-rate worker to an existing team can never increase the time the team takes, no matter what that new worker's own solo time is. State clearly where in your argument you use the fact that the new worker's rate is strictly positive.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Everything here comes from one idea: in a single unit of time, add up what each contributor does, or undoes, to the whole job. That idea never mentions how many contributors there are.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Ask what the drain accomplishes in one unit of time: does it move the tank toward full, or away from it? Whichever direction that is settles the sign its term carries.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Compare the OLD combined rate to the NEW one after a positive rate joins it. A bigger positive number always has a smaller reciprocal, so once you know the rate only grew, you already know something about the time.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    In one unit of time the three complete 1T1\frac{1}{T_1}T1​1​, 1T2\frac{1}{T_2}T2​1​, and 1T3\frac{1}{T_3}T3​1​ of the job; none interferes with another's progress, so the fractions add to the team's rate, whose reciprocal is TTT.

    Part B

    In one unit of time the drain removes 1T3\frac{1}{T_3}T3​1​ of a full tank instead of adding it, so its contribution is negative work. The net rate is 1T1+1T2−1T3\frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_3}T1​1​+T2​1​−T3​1​, and the fill time is its reciprocal, provided that net rate is positive.

    Part C

    Adding a new worker's positive rate to the existing combined rate always produces a strictly larger rate. Because time is the reciprocal of a positive rate, and a reciprocal shrinks as the number it inverts grows, a strictly larger rate always gives a strictly smaller time.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    In one unit of time, working alone, the first worker completes 1T1\frac{1}{T_1}T1​1​ of the job, the second 1T2\frac{1}{T_2}T2​1​, and the third 1T3\frac{1}{T_3}T3​1​, exactly as in the two-worker case. Whatever any one of them finishes is simply work that the other two no longer need to do; none of the three progress figures depends on what the others are doing, so the three fractions of the job completed in that unit of time can be added with no adjustment:

    1T1+1T2+1T3.\frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} + \frac{1}{T_3}.T1​1​+T2​1​+T3​1​.

    But the fraction of the job a team finishes in one unit of time is, by definition, the team's combined rate. So this sum IS the combined rate, and its reciprocal is the time TTT the three take together:

    1T=1T1+1T2+1T3.\frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} + \frac{1}{T_3}.T1​=T1​1​+T2​1​+T3​1​.

    Nothing in this argument singled out having exactly two or exactly three helpers; the same one-unit-of-time reasoning would add a fourth, fifth, or any further worker's rate the same way.

    Part B

    Apply the same one-unit-of-time reasoning as part A, but look carefully at what each contributor DOES to the tank in that unit of time. Each pipe adds 1T1\frac{1}{T_1}T1​1​ and 1T2\frac{1}{T_2}T2​1​ of a full tank, exactly as an ordinary worker would. The drain, however, is undoing work rather than doing it: left alone for one unit of time, it removes 1T3\frac{1}{T_3}T3​1​ of a full tank, so its contribution to how much fuller the tank gets in that unit of time is −1T3-\frac{1}{T_3}−T3​1​, not +1T3+\frac{1}{T_3}+T3​1​. Adding the three contributions, exactly as in part A, gives the net rate:

    net rate=1T1+1T2−1T3.\text{net rate} = \frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_3}.net rate=T1​1​+T2​1​−T3​1​.

    The fill time is the reciprocal of this net rate, on the understanding that the net rate is positive; if the drain were strong enough to make it negative, the tank would never fill at all.

    Part C

    Let the existing team have combined rate R>0R > 0R>0 and time 1R\frac{1}{R}R1​. A new worker with solo time TnT_nTn​ contributes a rate 1Tn\frac{1}{T_n}Tn​1​, and the addition law gives the new combined rate as

    R′=R+1Tn.R' = R + \frac{1}{T_n}.R′=R+Tn​1​.

    Because TnT_nTn​ is a finite solo time, 1Tn\frac{1}{T_n}Tn​1​ is strictly positive, so R′=R+1Tn>RR' = R + \frac{1}{T_n} > RR′=R+Tn​1​>R: this is exactly where the positivity of the new worker's rate is used, since adding zero or a negative amount would not force the inequality. Time is the reciprocal of a positive rate, and the reciprocal function reverses order on positive numbers (a bigger input gives a smaller output), so

    1R′<1R.\frac{1}{R'} < \frac{1}{R}.R′1​<R1​.

    The left side is the new team's time and the right side is the old team's time, so joining a new, genuinely positive-rate worker to any existing team can only shrink the time, never grow it, whatever that worker's own solo time happens to be.

    In one line

    For three workers, 1T=1T1+1T2+1T3\frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} + \frac{1}{T_3}T1​=T1​1​+T2​1​+T3​1​, by the same one-unit-of-time reasoning as the two-worker case; replacing one worker by a drain flips its term's sign, giving 1T1+1T2−1T3\frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_3}T1​1​+T2​1​−T3​1​. In general, adding any further worker with a genuinely positive rate strictly increases the combined rate and therefore strictly decreases the time, because the reciprocal of a positive number shrinks as that number grows.

    Hints

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

    Answer and solution

    The answer

    Part A

    They meet after 333 hours.

    Part B

    They meet 363636 miles from the first cyclist's starting town (and so 272727 miles from the second's).

    Part C

    The meeting equation adds the two speeds, since the gap closes from both ends at once; the catch-up equation instead uses their DIFFERENCE, since only that leftover speed closes the gap. Catch-up: 63=(12−9)t63 = (12 - 9)t63=(12−9)t, so t=21t = 21t=21 hours.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Let ttt be the time until they meet. Each cyclist covers a distance d=rtd = rtd=rt, and together the two distances must add to the full 636363 miles that separated them:

    12t+9t=63.12t + 9t = 63.12t+9t=63.

    Combine the like terms and solve:

    21t=63⟹t=3 hours.21t = 63 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 3 \text{ hours}.21t=63⟹t=3 hours.

    Part B

    Apply d=rtd = rtd=rt to the first cyclist alone, using the meeting time from part A:

    d=12×3=36 miles.d = 12 \times 3 = 36 \text{ miles}.d=12×3=36 miles.

    As a check, the second cyclist covers 9×3=279 \times 3 = 279×3=27 miles, and 36+27=6336 + 27 = 6336+27=63, the full distance between the towns.

    Part C

    In the meeting scenario, the gap closes from BOTH ends: every hour, the gap shrinks by the sum of the two speeds, 12+9=2112 + 9 = 2112+9=21 mph, which is exactly why part A's equation was 12t+9t=6312t + 9t = 6312t+9t=63, or equivalently (12+9)t=63(12+9)t = 63(12+9)t=63.

    In the chase scenario, both riders move the SAME way, so the front rider's own motion does not help close the gap between them at all; only the extra speed the second rider has over the first shrinks the gap, at a rate of 12−9=312 - 9 = 312−9=3 mph. That gives the equation

    63=(12−9)t=3t.63 = (12 - 9)t = 3t.63=(12−9)t=3t.

    Solving,

    t=633=21 hours.t = \frac{63}{3} = 21 \text{ hours}.t=363​=21 hours.

    The two equations share the same shape, distance equals a rate times a time, but the meeting scenario's rate is a SUM of speeds and the chase scenario's rate is a DIFFERENCE of speeds, because moving toward each other and moving the same way close the gap in entirely different ways.

    In one line

    The cyclists meet after 333 hours, 363636 miles from the first cyclist's town and 272727 miles from the second's. Moving in the same direction instead, the gap closes only at the DIFFERENCE of the two speeds, 12−9=312 - 9 = 312−9=3 mph, giving a catch-up equation 63=3t63 = 3t63=3t and a catch-up time of 212121 hours.

    4. The line that breaks the chain . Application, 10 points. Question 4 of 5.

    Application 10 pts

    A pipe fills an empty tank in 555 hours. A drain, left open the whole time, would empty a full tank in 202020 hours. Both run at once.     Part A

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Test the lines in order rather than judging by the final answer.

    Line 1 correctly reads each rate off the given solo times, so it stands. The trouble is in Line 2: it treats the drain exactly like a second pipe, adding its rate to the pipe's rate. But a drain undoes work rather than doing it, so in one hour it should be SUBTRACTED from what the pipe contributes, not added to it. The corrected line reads

    15−120=420−120=320 tank per hour.\frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{20} = \frac{4}{20} - \frac{1}{20} = \frac{3}{20} \text{ tank per hour}.51​−201​=204​−201​=203​ tank per hour.

    Lines 3, 4, and 5 make no NEW mistake: each one correctly restates, inverts, or reports the rate handed to it, so they are simply built on Line 2's wrong number rather than introducing an error of their own.

    Part B

    The time is the reciprocal of the corrected net rate:

    T=1 3/20 =203 hours.T = \frac{1}{\,3/20\,} = \frac{20}{3} \text{ hours}.T=3/201​=320​ hours.

    Since 203=6+23\frac{20}{3} = 6 + \frac{2}{3}320​=6+32​ and 23×60=40\frac{2}{3} \times 60 = 4032​×60=40, this is 666 hours and 404040 minutes.

    Part C

    The flawed Line 2 used 15+120=14\frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{20} = \frac{1}{4}51​+201​=41​, while the corrected rate is the smaller 15−120=320\frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{20} = \frac{3}{20}51​−201​=203​:

    15+120=14(flawed)15−120=320(correct).\frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{20} = \frac{1}{4} \quad \text{(flawed)} \qquad\qquad \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{20} = \frac{3}{20} \quad \text{(correct)}.51​+201​=41​(flawed)51​−201​=203​(correct).

    Adding the drain's rate instead of subtracting it can only make the resulting number BIGGER than it should be, since it added a positive amount that should have been removed. Time is the reciprocal of the rate, and a reciprocal shrinks as the number it inverts grows, so a rate that came out too big produces a time that comes out too SMALL. That is exactly the direction of the error here: the flawed 444 hours is smaller than the true time, because the sign mistake inflated the rate rather than deflating it.

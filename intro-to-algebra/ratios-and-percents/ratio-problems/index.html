Ratio Problems

Intuition

In the Ratios lesson you split a total by counting parts. A ratio 2:32 : 32:3 has 555 parts, so you divided the total by 555 and handed out 222 shares and 333 shares. That works, but it stalls the moment a problem gives you something other than a plain total. That other fact might be a difference between two amounts, a second ratio to merge, or a change that resets the ratio partway through. Algebra hands you one tool that covers all of these. Name the size of a single part sss, and write every quantity as a multiple of sss. Each fact in the problem then turns into an equation you already know how to solve. This is the common-multiplier method, and it makes ratio problems ordinary algebra.

Learning goals

  • Write a ratio's parts as asasas, bsbsbs, cscscs with one multiplier
  • Pin sss down with a total, a difference, or one known amount
  • Combine two ratios by matching the shared term, then pin down sss from a count
  • Adjust before-and-after amounts, then cross-multiply the new ratio
  • Check that the finished amounts reduce to the original ratio

The common multiplier

A ratio pins down how quantities compare, not how big they are. If two quantities stand in the ratio 2:32 : 32:3, they might be 222 and 333, or 202020 and 303030, or 200020002000 and 300030003000. What every one of those pairs shares is a single hidden number, the size of one part. Call it sss. Then the first quantity is 2s2s2s and the second is 3s3s3s. That is because the ratio says the first quantity holds 222 parts and the second holds 333 parts, with each part the same size sss.

Write it the same way for any ratio. A two-term ratio a:ba : ba:b describes quantities asasas and bsbsbs. A three-term ratio a:b:ca : b : ca:b:c describes quantities asasas, bsbsbs, and cscscs, all sharing the one multiplier sss. The multiplier is identical for every term, and that is the whole point: the parts must be equal in size for the ratio to mean what it says.

Why one multiplier captures a whole ratio#

Suppose the actual amounts are xxx and yyy, and they stand in the ratio a:ba : ba:b. From the Ratios lesson, two ratios are equal exactly when one comes from the other by multiplying both parts by the same nonzero number. Since x:yx : yx:y equals a:ba : ba:b, the pair x:yx : yx:y is an equivalent ratio to a:ba : ba:b, so there is a single number sss with

x=asandy=bs.x = as \qquad \text{and} \qquad y = bs.x=asandy=bs.

The same sss scales both parts, never two different multipliers, because scaling the parts unequally would change the comparison and break the ratio.

Now separate what is fixed from what is free. The ratio nails down the proportion a:ba : ba:b, but it says nothing about sss: every nonzero choice of sss rescales both amounts together and leaves the ratio a:ba : ba:b unchanged. So a ratio, however many terms it carries, hides exactly one unknown, the multiplier sss. A fact that fixes the actual scale, such as a total or a difference, turns straight into one equation in that single unknown and solves it. A second ratio works differently: it links in another quantity and lets you write a longer ratio, but by itself it still leaves every part unscaled, so you still need one scale-setting fact before you can read off actual amounts. Once you know sss, multiply it back through asasas, bsbsbs, cscscs and you have every amount. The whole method is this: turn the ratio into expressions in sss, use a scale-setting fact to solve for sss, then read off the quantities.

∎

From a ratio and a total

The most common extra fact is a total. If the quantities asasas and bsbsbs add to a known total NNN, then

as+bs=N,(a+b)s=N,s=Na+b.as + bs = N, \qquad (a + b)s = N, \qquad s = \frac{N}{a + b}.as+bs=N,(a+b)s=N,s=a+bN​.

The sum a+ba + ba+b is just the number of parts, so this is the part-counting rule from before, now written as an equation. The multiplier sss is then the value of one of those parts. The real payoff shows up with three terms, where sharing out by hand gets awkward but the algebra does not change at all.

Worked example 1 Three colors from a total

A necklace is strung with red, white, and blue beads in the ratio 3:5:73 : 5 : 73:5:7, and it uses 909090 beads in all. How many beads of each color does it use?

Write the three counts with one multiplier sss: there are 3s3s3s red, 5s5s5s white, and 7s7s7s blue beads. The extra fact is the total of 909090 beads, so add the three expressions and set the sum equal to 909090:

3s+5s+7s=90.3s + 5s + 7s = 90.3s+5s+7s=90.

Combine the like terms and solve:

15s=90,s=6.15s = 90, \qquad s = 6.15s=90,s=6.

Each part is 666 beads. Multiply back through each term:

3s=18,5s=30,7s=42.3s = 18, \qquad 5s = 30, \qquad 7s = 42.3s=18,5s=30,7s=42.

So the necklace has 181818 red, 303030 white, and 424242 blue beads. Check by adding them: 18+30+42=9018 + 30 + 42 = 9018+30+42=90, which matches the total, and the three counts still reduce to the ratio 3:5:73 : 5 : 73:5:7.

Check your understanding

Two numbers are in the ratio 5:35 : 35:3 and add to 646464. What is the larger number?

Answer choices

The two parts total 5+3=85 + 3 = 85+3=8, so eight equal parts make 646464. Solve for one part, then the larger number is five parts.

8s=64  ⟹  s=8,5s=408s = 64 \implies s = 8, \quad 5s = 408s=64⟹s=8,5s=40

The smaller number is 3s=243s = 243s=24, and 40+24=6440 + 24 = 6440+24=64 checks.

From a ratio and a difference

Sometimes a problem gives a difference instead of a total. The setup is identical; only the equation changes. If the quantities are asasas and bsbsbs with a>ba > ba>b, their difference is

as−bs=(a−b)s.as - bs = (a - b)s.as−bs=(a−b)s.

Set that equal to the known difference and solve for sss exactly as before.

Worked example 2 A difference instead of a total

An older sister and her younger brother collect baseball cards in the ratio 7:47 : 47:4. The sister has 151515 more cards than her brother. How many cards does each have?

Let one part be sss. The sister holds 7s7s7s cards and the brother holds 4s4s4s. The extra fact is that the sister has 151515 more, so their difference is 151515:

7s−4s=15.7s - 4s = 15.7s−4s=15.

Combine and solve:

3s=15,s=5.3s = 15, \qquad s = 5.3s=15,s=5.

Multiply back: the sister has 7s=357s = 357s=35 cards and the brother has 4s=204s = 204s=20. Check the difference: 35−20=1535 - 20 = 1535−20=15, as stated, and 35:2035 : 2035:20 reduces to 7:47 : 47:4.

Check your understanding

Two numbers are in the ratio 7:47 : 47:4 and differ by 212121. What is the smaller number?

Answer choices

The two ratio numbers differ by 7−4=37 - 4 = 37−4=3 parts, so three equal parts make 212121. Solve for one part, then the smaller number is four parts.

3s=21  ⟹  s=7,4s=283s = 21 \implies s = 7, \quad 4s = 283s=21⟹s=7,4s=28

The larger number is 7s=497s = 497s=49, and 49−28=2149 - 28 = 2149−28=21 checks.

Combining two ratios

A problem sometimes links three quantities with two separate ratios that share a middle term, and asks you to merge them into a single three-term ratio. The two ratios agree on the shared quantity only if its number is the same in both. When it is not, scale each ratio so the shared term matches, using the same equivalent-ratio move as always, then read off all three terms at once.

Worked example 3 Combining two ratios

A workshop holds chairs, tables, and benches. The ratio of chairs to tables is 2:32 : 32:3, and the ratio of tables to benches is 5:45 : 45:4. Find the ratio of chairs to tables to benches, and then the actual counts if there are 606060 tables.

The shared quantity is tables. It appears as 333 in the first ratio and as 555 in the second, so the two ratios do not yet agree on it. Make them agree by scaling each so the tables term becomes the least common multiple of 333 and 555, which is 151515.

Scale the first ratio by 555 so its tables term is 151515:

chairs:tables=2:3=10:15.\text{chairs} : \text{tables} = 2 : 3 = 10 : 15.chairs:tables=2:3=10:15.

Scale the second ratio by 333 so its tables term is also 151515:

tables:benches=5:4=15:12.\text{tables} : \text{benches} = 5 : 4 = 15 : 12.tables:benches=5:4=15:12.

Both now agree that tables is 151515, so line them up into one ratio:

chairs:tables:benches=10:15:12.\text{chairs} : \text{tables} : \text{benches} = 10 : 15 : 12.chairs:tables:benches=10:15:12.

For the counts, bring back the multiplier. There are 606060 tables, and tables is the 151515 term, so

15s=60,s=4.15s = 60, \qquad s = 4.15s=60,s=4.

Then chairs =10s=40= 10s = 40=10s=40 and benches =12s=48= 12s = 48=12s=48. So the workshop has 404040 chairs, 606060 tables, and 484848 benches.

Check your understanding

If A:B=3:4A : B = 3 : 4A:B=3:4 and B:C=6:5B : C = 6 : 5B:C=6:5, what is A:B:CA : B : CA:B:C?

Answer choices

The shared term BBB is 444 in the first ratio and 666 in the second, so scale each ratio so BBB matches their least common multiple, 121212.

A:B=3:4=9:12,B:C=6:5=12:10A : B = 3 : 4 = 9 : 12, \qquad B : C = 6 : 5 = 12 : 10A:B=3:4=9:12,B:C=6:5=12:10

Both now agree that BBB is 121212, so A:B:C=9:12:10A : B : C = 9 : 12 : 10A:B:C=9:12:10. Every other option leaves BBB mismatched between the two ratios, either by stapling the original numbers together with no scaling at all, or by scaling one ratio without matching the other's BBB.

Scaling a recipe or mixture

Recipes and mixtures are ratios you scale up or down. When a problem fixes the amount of one ingredient, that single amount determines sss, and every other ingredient follows. You do not need a total; one known part is enough.

Worked example 4 Scaling a recipe from one ingredient

A punch recipe mixes juice, soda, and sherbet in the ratio 4:3:14 : 3 : 14:3:1. You have exactly 666 cups of soda and want to use all of it. How much juice and sherbet do you need, and how much punch will you make?

Write each ingredient with the multiplier sss: juice is 4s4s4s, soda is 3s3s3s, and sherbet is 1s1s1s cups. The fixed ingredient is soda, and it is the 333 term, so

3s=6,s=2.3s = 6, \qquad s = 2.3s=6,s=2.

Each part is 222 cups. Multiply back:

juice=4s=8,sherbet=1s=2.\text{juice} = 4s = 8, \qquad \text{sherbet} = 1s = 2.juice=4s=8,sherbet=1s=2.

So you need 888 cups of juice and 222 cups of sherbet. The total punch is 8+6+2=168 + 6 + 2 = 168+6+2=16 cups, which also equals (4+3+1)s=8s=16(4 + 3 + 1)s = 8s = 16(4+3+1)s=8s=16, a handy check.

Before and after: when the ratio changes

The richest ratio problems change one or both quantities partway through and then tell you the new ratio. Adding or removing an amount does not fit the equivalent-ratio picture, because the two quantities no longer scale together. The multiplier still rescues you: write the original quantities as asasas and bsbsbs. Then adjust those quantities by the stated change, and set the changed pair equal to the new ratio. That equation, cleared of its fraction, is a linear equation in sss.

Worked example 5 A before-and-after age ratio

The ratio of Anna’s age to Ben’s age is 4:34 : 34:3. In 666 years, the ratio of their ages will be 6:56 : 56:5. How old is each now?

Write their current ages with the multiplier: Anna is 4s4s4s and Ben is 3s3s3s years old. In 666 years each is 666 years older, so Anna will be 4s+64s + 64s+6 and Ben will be 3s+63s + 63s+6. Their new ratio is 6:56 : 56:5, which as a proportion says

4s+63s+6=65.\frac{4s + 6}{3s + 6} = \frac{6}{5}.3s+64s+6​=56​.

Clear the fraction the way Linear Equations in Disguise justified it, by cross-multiplying, which is really just multiplying both sides by both denominators:

5(4s+6)=6(3s+6).5(4s + 6) = 6(3s + 6).5(4s+6)=6(3s+6).

Expand both sides and solve:

20s+30=18s+36,2s=6,s=3.20s + 30 = 18s + 36, \qquad 2s = 6, \qquad s = 3.20s+30=18s+36,2s=6,s=3.

So Anna is 4s=124s = 124s=12 and Ben is 3s=93s = 93s=9. Check against the words: right now 12:912 : 912:9 is 4:34 : 34:3, and in 666 years they are 181818 and 151515, whose ratio 18:1518 : 1518:15 reduces to 6:56 : 56:5. Both conditions hold, so Anna is 121212 and Ben is 999.

Check your understanding

A box holds red and green pens in the ratio 3:23 : 23:2. After 101010 more red pens are added, the ratio becomes 2:12 : 12:1. How many green pens are in the box?

Answer choices

Start with 3s3s3s red and 2s2s2s green. Adding 101010 red makes the new red count 3s+103s + 103s+10, and the ratio to the unchanged green is now 2:12 : 12:1, so red is twice green.

3s+10=2(2s)  ⟹  s=10,2s=203s + 10 = 2(2s) \implies s = 10, \quad 2s = 203s+10=2(2s)⟹s=10,2s=20

So there are 202020 green pens (and 303030 red, which becomes 404040 after the addition, giving 40:20=2:140 : 20 = 2 : 140:20=2:1).

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating the ratio numbers as the amounts. A ratio 3:5:73 : 5 : 73:5:7 does not mean there are 333, 555, and 777 of each thing. It means 3s3s3s, 5s5s5s, and 7s7s7s. Solve for sss first, then report the amounts.
  • Giving each term its own multiplier. Every term of a ratio shares the one multiplier sss. Writing one quantity as 3s3s3s and another as 5m5m5m lets the parts be different sizes, which breaks the comparison the ratio states.
  • Merging ratios without matching the shared term. To combine A:BA : BA:B and B:CB : CB:C you must first scale until BBB is the same number in both, then line them up. You cannot staple 2:32 : 32:3 and 5:45 : 45:4 into 2:3:42 : 3 : 42:3:4.
  • Adding to the ratio number instead of the quantity. In a before-and-after problem the change (+10+10+10, −8-8−8) is added to the actual amount asasas, not to the ratio number aaa. The new ratio then applies to the changed amounts.
  • Confusing part-to-part with part-to-whole. A ratio 2:32 : 32:3 makes the first quantity 25\frac{2}{5}52​ of the total, not 23\frac{2}{3}32​, because the whole is the sum of the parts.

Key takeaways

  • Every quantity in a ratio a:b:ca : b : ca:b:c is the same multiple of one part: write them asasas, bsbsbs, cscscs, sharing a single multiplier sss.
  • A ratio hides exactly one unknown, sss. A scale-setting fact (a total, a difference, or one known amount) is one equation, and it pins sss down. A second ratio does not by itself; it only extends the comparison to a new quantity.
  • Total: (a+b+c)s=N(a + b + c)s = N(a+b+c)s=N. Difference of two terms: (a−b)s=D(a - b)s = D(a−b)s=D. Solve for sss, then multiply back through every term.
  • Combine two ratios by scaling until the shared term matches (their least common multiple keeps the numbers small), then read off all three terms and use a scale-setting fact to find sss.
  • Before and after: adjust asasas and bsbsbs by the change, set the result equal to the new ratio, cross-multiply, and solve the linear equation in sss.
  • Always check: the final amounts should add or differ as stated and reduce to the original ratio.

A bit of history (optional)

A scribe with no letter for the unknown still had to answer the question. The trick he used sounds like cheating, and it lands on the exact answer.

It is called false position. It runs through the Rhind papyrus, an Egyptian scroll from about 1650 BCE. You guess an answer, choosing whatever number keeps the arithmetic pleasant. Then you push that guess through the problem. The result comes out wrong. So compare the result you wanted with the result you got. Because these problems scale the way a ratio does, scaling your guess by the ratio between the two results lands exactly on the right answer.

That scaling step is the common multiplier of this lesson, reached by trial instead of named in advance.

Proportions had a fixed recipe of their own, the Rule of Three. Given three numbers of a proportion, you multiplied two of them and divided by the third, always in the same order. Indian mathematicians had written the rule down centuries earlier, and Fibonacci carried it into Europe in his Liber Abaci of 1202. It became a staple of commercial arithmetic, so useful and so widely taught that it was also known as the Golden Rule, and a merchant could apply it as a memorized procedure to price a cargo.

Writing every part as a multiple of sss names one part before you know its size. The old methods reached the same numbers, by trial or by a memorized rule, without ever naming that shared multiplier outright.

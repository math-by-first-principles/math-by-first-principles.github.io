Exponential Functions

Intuition

Imagine a single water lily on a pond that doubles the area it covers every day. After one day there is twice as much cover, after two days four times, after three days eight times. For a long while the pond looks almost empty, and then, in the last day or two, it fills. Nothing about steady, even growth prepares you for that final jump. The reason is that the cover is not growing by a fixed amount each day, it is being multiplied by a fixed amount each day. When the quantity that changes, the day count, rides up in the exponent, you have an exponential function. This lesson is about what that one move does to how a function behaves.

Learning goals

  • Put the variable in the exponent, unlike a power function
  • Require b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \ne 1b=1 for the base
  • Read bx+y=bxbyb^{x+y} = b^x b^ybx+y=bxby as adding input multiplies output
  • Spot exponential data by a positive constant ratio, never 111, at equal steps
  • Describe the graph through (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) with asymptote y=0y = 0y=0
  • Tell growth from decay by whether bbb exceeds one

The variable belongs in the exponent

You have spent whole chapters on expressions like x2x^2x2, x3x^3x3, and general polynomials. In every one of them the variable sits in the base and a fixed number sits in the exponent. An exponential function turns that arrangement around.

A function is exponential when the variable appears in the exponent while the base is a fixed number. Its basic form is

f(x)=bx,f(x) = b^x,f(x)=bx,

where the constant bbb is called the base. A slightly fuller form, f(x)=a⋅bxf(x) = a \cdot b^xf(x)=a⋅bx, includes a constant multiplier aaa that sets the starting value; the plain form bxb^xbx is just the case a=1a = 1a=1. Throughout the graphing below we take a>0a > 0a>0, so every output stays positive.

The whole idea lives in one comparison. Look at 2x2^x2x next to x2x^2x2:

2xversusx2.2^x \qquad \text{versus} \qquad x^2.2xversusx2.

They are built from the same two symbols, a 222 and an xxx, and beginners read them as if they were the same thing. They are not even close. In x2x^2x2 the exponent is frozen at 222 and the base xxx moves; that is a power function. In 2x2^x2x the base is frozen at 222 and the exponent xxx moves; that is an exponential function. Watch how differently they grow:

xxx000111222333444555
x2x^2x2000111444999161616252525
2x2^x2x111222444888161616323232

The two happen to agree at x=2x = 2x=2 and again at x=4x = 4x=4, but their engines are different, and past x=4x = 4x=4 the exponential pulls away and never looks back. By x=10x = 10x=10 the power function reaches only 102=10010^2 = 100102=100, while the exponential is already at 210=10242^{10} = 1024210=1024, more than ten times larger.

Worked example 1 Exponential, power, or neither?

Sort each rule by asking one question: where is the variable, the base or the exponent?

f(x)=2xf(x) = 2^xf(x)=2x has its variable in the exponent over the fixed base 222, so it is exponential.

g(x)=x2g(x) = x^2g(x)=x2 has its variable in the base under the fixed exponent 222, so it is a power function, not exponential.

k(x)=1xk(x) = 1^xk(x)=1x looks exponential, but the base is 111, and 111 multiplied by itself any number of times is still 111:

1x=1for every x.1^x = 1 \quad \text{for every } x.1x=1for every x.

That is a constant, a flat horizontal line, so it is not a genuine exponential function. This is exactly why the base 111 is ruled out, a point the next section settles.

Which bases make sense

Not every number is allowed as a base. Two restrictions come straight from what bxb^xbx has to mean if it is going to be a function defined for every input xxx.

The base must be positive. Suppose you tried a negative base such as b=−4b = -4b=−4. As long as the exponent is a whole number you get an answer, but the sign flips back and forth: (−4)1=−4(-4)^1 = -4(−4)1=−4, (−4)2=16(-4)^2 = 16(−4)2=16, (−4)3=−64(-4)^3 = -64(−4)3=−64. Worse, a fractional exponent asks for a root of a negative number, and

(−4)1/2=−4(-4)^{1/2} = \sqrt{-4}(−4)1/2=−4​

is not a real number at all. So a negative base leaves the function undefined at many inputs and lurching in sign at the rest, which is no way to build a smooth curve. The base b=0b = 0b=0 fails too, since 0x0^x0x is 000 for positive xxx and undefined for x≤0x \le 0x≤0. Ruling these out, we require b>0b > 0b>0.

The base must not be 111. As the worked example showed, 1x=11^x = 11x=1 for every xxx, so the “function” is just the constant 111. It carries none of the growth or decay that makes an exponential interesting, so we set it aside.

Putting the two conditions together, an exponential function f(x)=bxf(x) = b^xf(x)=bx uses a base with

b>0andb≠1.b > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad b \ne 1.b>0andb=1.

Answer choices

A negative base breaks down at fractional exponents: (−2)1/2=−2(-2)^{1/2} = \sqrt{-2}(−2)1/2=−2​ is not a real number, so the function would be undefined at many inputs. That is why the base must be positive.

There is no upper limit on bbb, and 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ is a perfectly good base between 000 and 111 (it gives decay); only b=1b = 1b=1 itself is excluded, not bases below it.

From whole-number powers to a smooth curve

The definition bxb^xbx has to make sense for every real input, not just whole numbers, before we can draw it as an unbroken curve. Everything needed is already in hand from earlier chapters on exponents:

  • b0=1b^0 = 1b0=1 for any allowed base.
  • A negative exponent gives a reciprocal, b−n=1bnb^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{b^n}b−n=bn1​, which is always positive, never a negative number.
  • A unit-fraction exponent gives a root, b1/n=bnb^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{b}b1/n=nb​, and a general fraction combines the two, bm/n=(bn)mb^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{b}\right)^mbm/n=(nb​)m.

Those rules pin down bxb^xbx at every rational input. The remaining inputs, the irrational ones like x=2x = \sqrt{2}x=2​, get filled in so the curve has no gaps or jumps between the rational points. The result is that bxb^xbx is defined for every real number xxx and traces one smooth, unbroken curve that is positive everywhere.

The rational inputs already pin down values of 2x2^x2x crowding as close as you like to 222^{\sqrt 2}22​ from both sides. Read in Go deeper how that crowding settles 222^{\sqrt 2}22​ on a single value, with no gap left in the curve.

The rule that makes a function exponential

One identity governs the base power bxb^xbx, and it is the reason every exponential function, including the fuller form a⋅bxa \cdot b^xa⋅bx, behaves the way it does. For the base power, adding inputs turns into multiplying outputs.

Why bx+y=bx⋅byb^{x+y} = b^x \cdot b^ybx+y=bx⋅by#

Start with whole-number exponents, where you can count factors directly. Write bxb^xbx as xxx copies of bbb, and byb^yby as yyy copies of bbb. The product bx⋅byb^x \cdot b^ybx⋅by then lays the two runs of factors end to end, giving x+yx + yx+y copies of bbb in all:

bx⋅by=b⋯b⏟x⋅b⋯b⏟y=b⋯b⏟x+y=bx+y.b^x \cdot b^y = \underbrace{b \cdots b}_{x} \cdot \underbrace{b \cdots b}_{y} = \underbrace{b \cdots b}_{x + y} = b^{x + y}.bx⋅by=xb⋯b​​⋅yb⋯b​​=x+yb⋯b​​=bx+y.

This whole-number case is not new material on its own: it is the product rule for exponents, the same rule you already used and extended to negative and fractional exponents in earlier chapters. It continues to hold for every real number xxx and yyy, not just whole numbers, exactly the way the earlier chapters extended it to fractions:

bx+y=bx⋅by.b^{x + y} = b^x \cdot b^y.bx+y=bx⋅by.

Set y=1y = 1y=1 to see what this means step by step. Then bx+1=bx⋅bb^{x+1} = b^x \cdot bbx+1=bx⋅b, which says that increasing the input by 111 multiplies the output by the base bbb. Equal steps added in the exponent produce equal factors multiplied in the value, and that is precisely the exponential behavior.

∎

That one-step consequence, multiply by bbb per unit step, carries over to the fuller form a⋅bxa \cdot b^xa⋅bx too, even though the addition identity above is stated for the base power alone. Since a⋅bx+1=b⋅(a⋅bx)a \cdot b^{x+1} = b \cdot \left(a \cdot b^x\right)a⋅bx+1=b⋅(a⋅bx), each step of 111 in the input still multiplies the value of the function by bbb, whatever aaa is.

This turns hard-looking calculations into easy ones. Since 210=10242^{10} = 1024210=1024, you can find nearby powers without multiplying out a long string of 222s. Adding 333 in the exponent multiplies by 23=82^3 = 823=8, and subtracting 333 divides by 888:

213=210⋅23=1024⋅8=8192,27=210⋅2−3=10248=128.2^{13} = 2^{10} \cdot 2^{3} = 1024 \cdot 8 = 8192, \qquad 2^{7} = 2^{10} \cdot 2^{-3} = \frac{1024}{8} = 128.213=210⋅23=1024⋅8=8192,27=210⋅2−3=81024​=128.

Equal steps, constant ratio

The step-by-step reading of the rule, “each unit added to xxx multiplies the output by bbb,” gives a practical test for spotting exponential behavior in a table. Line up equally spaced inputs and look at what happens between neighbors. A linear function adds the same amount each step, a constant difference. An exponential function multiplies by the same amount each step, a constant ratio.

Compare a linear rule g(x)=3+5xg(x) = 3 + 5xg(x)=3+5x with an exponential rule f(x)=3⋅2xf(x) = 3 \cdot 2^xf(x)=3⋅2x over the same inputs:

xxx000111222333444
g(x)=3+5xg(x) = 3 + 5xg(x)=3+5x333888131313181818232323
f(x)=3⋅2xf(x) = 3 \cdot 2^xf(x)=3⋅2x333666121212242424484848

For ggg, the jumps are 5,5,5,55, 5, 5, 55,5,5,5, a constant difference, so successive values climb by the same amount. For fff, the jumps are 3,6,12,243, 6, 12, 243,6,12,24, which are not constant at all; instead the ratios 63,126,2412,4824\tfrac{6}{3}, \tfrac{12}{6}, \tfrac{24}{12}, \tfrac{48}{24}36​,612​,1224​,2448​ are all 222. That is the test: whenever equally spaced inputs produce a constant ratio between the listed outputs, and that ratio is positive and not equal to 111, the data fits an exponential rule, and when those inputs step by 111 that ratio is the base. Those two conditions on the ratio are not extra fine print, they are the base restrictions b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \ne 1b=1 showing up in the data: a ratio of 111 means every output repeats, which is the flat, non-growing case already ruled out, and a ratio can never come out negative, since multiplying by the same positive base again and again never flips the sign. A finite table can only show that the data fits the pattern; it does not by itself pin down what an unknown function does at every other input.

Worked example 3 Recognize exponential data and find the rule

A function’s outputs at x=0,1,2,3x = 0, 1, 2, 3x=0,1,2,3 are 5,15,45,1355, 15, 45, 1355,15,45,135. Decide whether it could be exponential, and if so, find its rule.

First test the differences, the linear signature:

15−5=10,45−15=30,135−45=90.15 - 5 = 10, \quad 45 - 15 = 30, \quad 135 - 45 = 90.15−5=10,45−15=30,135−45=90.

Growth, decay, and the shape of the graph

Whether an exponential rises or falls is decided entirely by the base.

When b>1b > 1b>1, each step to the right multiplies by a number bigger than 111, so the outputs get larger. The function increases, and the picture is exponential growth.

When 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1, each step to the right multiplies by a number smaller than 111, so the outputs shrink toward zero. The function decreases, and the picture is exponential decay. A decay curve is a mirror image of a growth curve, because raising one-half to the xxx is the same as raising two to the negative xxx: (12)x=2−x\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^x = 2^{-x}(21​)x=2−x.

Both curves, growth and decay, share the same list of features, and reading them off the picture is the payoff of this lesson:

  • Passes through (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1). Every bxb^xbx hits 111 at x=0x = 0x=0, since b0=1b^0 = 1b0=1. For the fuller form a⋅bxa \cdot b^xa⋅bx the yyy-intercept is aaa, because a⋅b0=aa \cdot b^0 = aa⋅b0=a.
  • Always positive. A positive base raised to any real power is positive, so the graph lives entirely above the horizontal axis. The range is y>0y > 0y>0.
  • Horizontal asymptote y=0y = 0y=0. On the flat side, growth to the left and decay to the right, the curve creeps toward the axis and gets arbitrarily close without ever touching it. The output is never 000 and never negative.
  • Domain is all real numbers. You may raise the base to any power, so every xxx is allowed.
  • One-to-one. The curve is strictly increasing (growth) or strictly decreasing (decay), so it never repeats an output. A one-to-one function has an inverse, and the inverse of an exponential function is the logarithm, the subject that closes this chapter. We only name it here; the tools for computing with it come later.

One base deserves a mention by name. The number e≈2.718e \approx 2.718e≈2.718 is a famous choice of base whose special role shows up in the next lesson, on compound interest. For now, treat it as just another base greater than 111: its graph is a growth curve like the one above.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing bxb^xbx with xbx^bxb. These are different species. 2x2^x2x is exponential (variable in the exponent); x2x^2x2 is a power function (variable in the base). At x=3x = 3x=3 they give 23=82^3 = 823=8 and 32=93^2 = 932=9, and their graphs, a rising curve versus a parabola, look nothing alike.
  • Treating growth as steep linear. A line adds a fixed amount per step; an exponential multiplies by a fixed ratio per step. That is why exponential values start slow and then explode past any line. Check a table for a constant ratio, not a constant difference.
  • Thinking b0=0b^0 = 0b0=0. The value at x=0x = 0x=0 is 111, not 000, because b0=1b^0 = 1b0=1 for every allowed base. The graph crosses the vertical axis at (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1), or at (0,a)(0, a)(0,a) for a⋅bxa \cdot b^xa⋅bx.
  • Thinking the graph can reach or cross zero. For a>0a > 0a>0, the case graphed throughout this lesson, an exponential output is never 000 and never negative. The curve has a horizontal asymptote at y=0y = 0y=0 that it approaches but never crosses, so the range is y>0y > 0y>0.
  • Mishandling decay and negative exponents. With 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1 the graph falls but stays positive, and a negative exponent gives a positive reciprocal, not a negative number: 2−3=182^{-3} = \tfrac{1}{8}2−3=81​, not −8-8−8.

Key takeaways

  • An exponential function has the variable in the exponent over a fixed base: f(x)=bxf(x) = b^xf(x)=bx, or more generally f(x)=a⋅bxf(x) = a \cdot b^xf(x)=a⋅bx. This is the opposite of a power function like x2x^2x2, where the variable is the base.
  • The base must satisfy b>0b > 0b>0 and b≠1b \ne 1b=1: a negative base breaks at fractional exponents, and b=1b = 1b=1 collapses to the constant 111.
  • The defining rule is bx+y=bx⋅byb^{x+y} = b^x \cdot b^ybx+y=bx⋅by, so adding to the input multiplies the output. Each unit step multiplies by the base bbb.
  • Equally spaced inputs give a constant, positive ratio other than 111 between outputs (the base, when the steps are 111), which is how you recognize exponential data. Linear data has a constant difference instead.
  • The graph passes through (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) (or (0,a)(0, a)(0,a)), stays positive, has the horizontal asymptote y=0y = 0y=0, has domain all real numbers and range y>0y > 0y>0, and is one-to-one.
  • b>1b > 1b>1 gives growth (increasing); 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1 gives decay (decreasing). Being one-to-one, an exponential has an inverse, the logarithm, which is coming up.

How the irrational inputs get filled in

The rules above pin down bxb^xbx at every rational input, b0=1b^0 = 1b0=1, b−n=1bnb^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{b^n}b−n=bn1​, and bm/n=(bn)mb^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{b}\right)^mbm/n=(nb​)m. An irrational input like x=2x = \sqrt{2}x=2​ is not covered by any of those rules directly, but it is boxed in tightly by rational numbers on both sides: 1.4<2<1.51.4 < \sqrt{2} < 1.51.4<2​<1.5, then 1.41<2<1.421.41 < \sqrt{2} < 1.421.41<2​<1.42, then 1.414<2<1.4151.414 < \sqrt{2} < 1.4151.414<2​<1.415, and so on, closer every time.

Apply 2x2^x2x to each pair of bounds. Since 2x2^x2x only gets bigger as xxx gets bigger, the outputs are squeezed the same way:

21.4<22<21.5,21.41<22<21.42,21.414<22<21.415,2^{1.4} < 2^{\sqrt 2} < 2^{1.5}, \qquad 2^{1.41} < 2^{\sqrt 2} < 2^{1.42}, \qquad 2^{1.414} < 2^{\sqrt 2} < 2^{1.415},21.4<22​<21.5,21.41<22​<21.42,21.414<22​<21.415,

and so on. Each of those bounds is a power of 222 with a rational exponent, so every one of them is already a known number. As the rational bounds close in on 2\sqrt{2}2​, the gap between 2lower bound2^{\text{lower bound}}2lower bound and 2upper bound2^{\text{upper bound}}2upper bound shrinks toward nothing, trapping 222^{\sqrt{2}}22​ at exactly one value with no room left for it to be anything else. The same squeezing argument works for every irrational input and every allowed base (for a base below 111, where the function decreases instead of increasing, the bounds simply squeeze from the opposite sides), which is why bxb^xbx is defined for every real xxx, and why the curve has no gaps or jumps anywhere along it.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1965 a trade magazine asked an engineer to predict the coming ten years. Gordon Moore, who helped build the American chip industry, had almost nothing to work from. He had a handful of yearly counts of how many parts one silicon chip could carry.

Read one way the counts looked modest. Read another way they were startling. Each year the number had roughly doubled.

Doubling is not a step, it is a factor, and Moore chose to trust the factor. He wrote that the doubling would carry on. A straight line drawn through his early points promised a few hundred parts by 1975. The doubling promised sixty-five thousand, and the doubling turned out to be the closer guess. He later stretched the period to about two years, but the shape of the claim never moved.

What Moore did is the test in this lesson, run on real data. Line up equally spaced inputs and compare the outputs. Equal differences mean the data fits a linear pattern. A positive constant ratio other than 111 means it fits an exponential one, and that ratio is the base. His counts sat close to a ratio of two, so they were roughly a⋅2xa \cdot 2^xa⋅2x in disguise, for whatever count aaa he started from. That is why the straight lines drawn through them fell so absurdly short.

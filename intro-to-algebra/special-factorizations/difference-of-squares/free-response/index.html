    1. Two conditions are not enough . Foundational, 11 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    The three-part test for a difference of squares has three separate conditions, and an expression can satisfy any two of them while still failing the pattern. This question asks you to run the full test, not just part of it, and then to factor the expression that passes.

    Part A

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Run all three conditions on each expression: exactly two terms, both perfect squares, and a minus sign between them.

    For 16x2−916x^2-916x2−9: two terms, 16x2=(4x)216x^2=(4x)^216x2=(4x)2 and 9=329=3^29=32 are both perfect squares, and they are subtracted. All three conditions hold, so it factors:

    For x2+121x^2+121x2+121: both terms are perfect squares and there are two of them, but they are ADDED, so the sign condition fails. This is a sum of squares and does not factor over the real numbers.

    For x2−60x^2-60x2−60: there are two terms and the sign is a minus, but 606060 is not a perfect square, since no whole number squares to 606060. The perfect-square condition fails.

    Part B

    Take the square root of each whole term, coefficient and variable together.

    Part C

    The three-part test has three separate conditions, and part A shows that satisfying any two is not enough on its own.

    3. Why the middle terms vanish, and why they don't for a sum . Reasoning, 13 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Reasoning 13 pts

    The identity a2−b2=(a+b)(a−b)a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)a2−b2=(a+b)(a−b) is not something to take on faith. This question asks you to derive it by expanding, and then to use that same expansion to explain why a sum of squares does not get the same treatment.

    The answer

    Part A

    (a+b)(a−b)=a2−ab+ba−b2=a2−b2(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-ab+ba-b^2=a^2-b^2(a+b)(a−b)=a2−ab+ba−b2=a2−b2, since −ab-ab−ab and bababa are exact opposites and cancel.

    Part B

    It equals a2−b2a^2-b^2a2−b2, never a2+b2a^2+b^2a2+b2: the two would agree only if −b2=b2-b^2=b^2−b2=b2, which forces b=0b=0b=0. So for any nonzero real bbb, the product (a+b)(a−b)(a+b)(a-b)(a+b)(a−b) can never equal a2+b2a^2+b^2a2+b2.

    Part C

    Part A shows a2−b2a^2-b^2a2−b2 always factors as (a+b)(a−b)(a+b)(a-b)(a+b)(a−b) for real numbers; part B shows that same product can never build a2+b2a^2+b^2a2+b2 for nonzero bbb.     Worked solution

    Part A

    Multiply every term of the first factor by every term of the second factor.

    (a+b)(a−b)=a⋅a−a⋅b+b⋅a−b⋅b.(a+b)(a-b)=a\cdot a - a\cdot b + b\cdot a - b\cdot b.(a+b)(a−b)=a⋅a−a⋅b+b⋅a−b⋅b.

    This holds for every real aaa and bbb, since nothing in the expansion assumed any particular values.

    By part A, (a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2(a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2 for every real aaa and bbb. Ask when this could also equal a2+b2a^2+b^2a2+b2.

    Part C

    Part A establishes a fact for every real a,ba,ba,b: a2−b2a^2-b^2a2−b2 always equals (a+b)(a−b)(a+b)(a-b)(a+b)(a−b), so a difference of squares always factors this way.

    The habit has a fixed order: remove any common factor first, and only then look for, and re-check for, a difference of squares.

    The first claim squared two factors of the SAME sign, which always produces a three-term expression, not a difference of squares.     Worked solution

    Part A

    Expand the proposed right side and compare it term by term with the left side.

    (8x−5)2=(8x)2−2(8x)(5)+52=64x2−80x+25.(8x-5)^2=(8x)^2-2(8x)(5)+5^2=64x^2-80x+25.(8x−5)2=(8x)2−2(8x)(5)+52=64x2−80x+25.

    This has a middle term, −80x-80x−80x, that 64x2−2564x^2-2564x2−25 does not have, so the claim is false. A difference of squares needs two factors with OPPOSITE signs, not the same sign squared.

    64x2−25=(8x)2−52=(8x+5)(8x−5).64x^2-25=(8x)^2-5^2=(8x+5)(8x-5).64x2−25=(8x)2−52=(8x+5)(8x−5).

    Part B

    Expand the proposed right side.

    (4x+5)(4x−5)=(4x)2−52=16x2−25.(4x+5)(4x-5)=(4x)^2-5^2=16x^2-25.(4x+5)(4x−5)=(4x)2−52=16x2−25.

    That is not 4x2−254x^2-254x2−25: the coefficient came out wrong. The mistake is in the square root taken for the first term. Since (2x)2=4x2(2x)^2=4x^2(2x)2=4x2, the correct root of 4x24x^24x2 is 2x2x2x, not 4x4x4x.

    4x2−25=(2x)2−52=(2x+5)(2x−5).4x^2-25=(2x)^2-5^2=(2x+5)(2x-5).4x2−25=(2x)2−52=(2x+5)(2x−5).

    Part C

    The two claims fail for genuinely different reasons, and naming the reason in general terms is what makes it useful on a NEW problem, not just this one.

    The first claim's error is a SIGN error: squaring two factors of the same sign, (A−B)2(A-B)^2(A−B)2, always leaves a middle term, −2AB-2AB−2AB, that a difference of squares never has. This mistake will show up on any problem where both factors are given the same sign.

    The second claim's error is a ROOT error: the square root of a coefficient-bearing term was read off incorrectly. This mistake will show up on any problem where the root of a coefficient is guessed rather than computed.

    Expanding catches both because it is unconditional: a genuine difference of squares always has the form A2−B2A^2-B^2A2−B2 with no middle term at all,

    (A+B)(A−B)=A2−B2,(A+B)(A-B)=A^2-B^2,(A+B)(A−B)=A2−B2,

    so any surviving middle term, or any constant that does not match, proves the claim wrong, regardless of which of the two mistakes produced it.

    In one line

    (8x−5)2=64x2−80x+25≠64x2−25(8x-5)^2=64x^2-80x+25\neq64x^2-25(8x−5)2=64x2−80x+25=64x2−25, so the correct factorization is (8x+5)(8x−5)(8x+5)(8x-5)(8x+5)(8x−5); (4x+5)(4x−5)=16x2−25≠4x2−25(4x+5)(4x-5)=16x^2-25\neq4x^2-25(4x+5)(4x−5)=16x2−25=4x2−25, so the correct factorization is (2x+5)(2x−5)(2x+5)(2x-5)(2x+5)(2x−5); the first error was squaring two same-signed factors, the second was taking the wrong square root of a coefficient, and expanding catches both because a genuine difference of squares always loses its middle term.

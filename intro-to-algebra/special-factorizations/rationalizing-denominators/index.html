Rationalizing Denominators

Intuition

You already know how to simplify a square root and how a difference of squares factors as (a+b)(a−b)(a + b)(a - b)(a+b)(a−b). This lesson puts both to work on a small but universal problem: a fraction with a square root on the bottom, like 12\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}2​1​ or 11+2\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{2}}1+2​1​. By long-standing convention we rewrite such a fraction so that no radical is left in the denominator. The value never changes; only the way it is written does. The single tool is multiplication by a cleverly chosen form of 111. For two-term denominators, the form of 111 you choose is built from the conjugate, the very same (a+b)(a−b)(a + b)(a - b)(a+b)(a−b) pattern you just learned.

Learning goals

  • Multiply by a disguised form of one to clear a root
  • Turn ab\frac{a}{\sqrt{b}}b​a​ into abb\frac{a\sqrt{b}}{b}bab​​
  • Use the conjugate on a two-term denominator
  • Recognize the difference of squares the conjugate creates
  • Simplify the result, reducing factors and any leftover radical

Multiplying by a disguised form of 1

Every rationalizing step is one idea in disguise, which is to multiply the fraction by 111. Any nonzero quantity divided by itself equals 111, so 22=1\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = 12​2​​=1 and 2−32−3=1\frac{2 - \sqrt{3}}{2 - \sqrt{3}} = 12−3​2−3​​=1. Multiplying a number by 111 leaves its value untouched, which is exactly why the move is allowed. What changes is the form of the fraction, not the number it stands for.

The reason a well-chosen form of 111 can clear a root comes down to one fact about square roots: a square root times itself gives back the number under it.

Why b⋅b=b\sqrt{b}\cdot\sqrt{b} = bb​⋅b​=b#

By definition, for b≥0b \ge 0b≥0, b\sqrt{b}b​ is the nonnegative number that gives bbb back when you square it. Squaring a number and multiplying it by itself are the same operation, so that definition says exactly this:

b⋅b=b.\sqrt{b}\cdot\sqrt{b} = b.b​⋅b​=b.

This is why a lone b\sqrt{b}b​ in a denominator disappears the moment you multiply by another b\sqrt{b}b​: the product is the plain number bbb, with no radical left.

∎

So the whole method is to pick the form of 111 that turns the denominator into something with no radical. For a single root the choice is easy, and for a two-term denominator it is the conjugate.

Clearing a single square root

When the denominator is a single square root, the form of 111 to use is that same root over itself. To rationalize ab\frac{a}{\sqrt{b}}b​a​ for any b>0b > 0b>0 (so the denominator isn’t zero to begin with), multiply the numerator and the denominator by b\sqrt{b}b​:

ab=ab⋅bb=abb.\frac{a}{\sqrt{b}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{b}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}} = \frac{a\sqrt{b}}{b}.b​a​=b​a​⋅b​b​​=bab​​.

The denominator b⋅b\sqrt{b}\cdot\sqrt{b}b​⋅b​ became bbb, with the root gone, and the root moved up to the numerator where the convention allows it. Notice that you must multiply the top by b\sqrt{b}b​ as well; multiplying only the bottom would change the value.

Worked example 1 Rationalize 12\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}2​1​ and 35\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}5​3​

For 12\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}2​1​, multiply top and bottom by 2\sqrt{2}2​. The bottom becomes 2⋅2=2\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 22​⋅2​=2:

12=12⋅22=22.\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.2​1​=2​1​⋅2​2​​=22​​.

As a check, 12≈0.707\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7072​1​≈0.707 and 22≈0.707\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.70722​​≈0.707, the same number in a tidier form.

For 35\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}5​3​, multiply top and bottom by 5\sqrt{5}5​. The bottom becomes 555:

35=35⋅55=355.\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{3\sqrt{5}}{5}.5​3​=5​3​⋅5​5​​=535​​.

The 333 stays out front and the new root 5\sqrt{5}5​ rides along in the numerator.

Worked example 2 Rationalize and then simplify

Often the fraction reduces after you rationalize, or the radical simplifies first. Take 82\frac{8}{\sqrt{2}}2​8​. Multiply by 22\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}}2​2​​:

82=822=42.\frac{8}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{2} = 4\sqrt{2}.2​8​=282​​=42​.

The 888 and the 222 share a factor of 222, so the fraction reduces to 424\sqrt{2}42​.

Now take 612\frac{6}{\sqrt{12}}12​6​. Here it pays to simplify the radical first, since 12=4⋅3=23\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4\cdot 3} = 2\sqrt{3}12​=4⋅3​=23​:

612=623=33=333=3.\frac{6}{\sqrt{12}} = \frac{6}{2\sqrt{3}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{3} = \sqrt{3}.12​6​=23​6​=3​3​=333​​=3​.

Dividing the 666 and the 222 by their common factor of 222 turns 623\frac{6}{2\sqrt{3}}23​6​ into 33\frac{3}{\sqrt{3}}3​3​, which then rationalizes to 333\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{3}333​​ and reduces to 3\sqrt{3}3​. Simplifying 12\sqrt{12}12​ up front kept the numbers small.

Answer choices

Multiply the top and bottom by 3\sqrt{3}3​. The bottom becomes 3⋅3=3\sqrt{3}\cdot\sqrt{3} = 33​⋅3​=3.

63=63⋅33=633\frac{6}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{3}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{6\sqrt{3}}{3}3​6​=3​6​⋅3​3​​=363​​

The 666 and the 333 share a factor of 333, so the fraction reduces further to 232\sqrt{3}23​. Stopping at 633\frac{6\sqrt{3}}{3}363​​ (an unreduced fraction) skips that last simplification step.

Clearing a binomial denominator with its conjugate

A denominator with two terms, like 1+21 + \sqrt{2}1+2​, cannot be fixed by multiplying by a single root: multiplying 1+21 + \sqrt{2}1+2​ by 2\sqrt{2}2​ gives 2+2\sqrt{2} + 22​+2, which still has a root. The move that works uses the difference-of-squares pattern you proved earlier in this chapter.

The conjugate of a two-term expression is the same expression with the sign between its terms flipped. The conjugate of a+ba + \sqrt{b}a+b​ is a−ba - \sqrt{b}a−b​, and the conjugate of a−ba - \sqrt{b}a−b​ is a+ba + \sqrt{b}a+b​. Multiplying an expression by its conjugate produces a difference of squares, and the square of a square root is rational, so every radical disappears.

For example, the conjugate of 3+53 + \sqrt{5}3+5​ is 3−53 - \sqrt{5}3−5​. Multiply the two together and watch the radical vanish:

(3+5)(3−5)=9−35+35−5=9−5=4.(3 + \sqrt{5})(3 - \sqrt{5}) = 9 - 3\sqrt{5} + 3\sqrt{5} - 5 = 9 - 5 = 4.(3+5​)(3−5​)=9−35​+35​−5=9−5=4.

The two middle terms, −35-3\sqrt{5}−35​ and +35+3\sqrt{5}+35​, are exact opposites, so they cancel, leaving the whole number 444. That cancellation happens for the same reason every time, which is what the next proof shows in general.

Why (a+b)(a−b)=a2−b(a + \sqrt{b})(a - \sqrt{b}) = a^2 - b(a+b​)(a−b​)=a2−b#

Multiply the expression by its conjugate exactly as you multiply any difference of squares, treating b\sqrt{b}b​ as the second term:

(a+b)(a−b)=a2−ab+ab−(b)2.(a + \sqrt{b})(a - \sqrt{b}) = a^2 - a\sqrt{b} + a\sqrt{b} - (\sqrt{b})^2.(a+b​)(a−b​)=a2−ab​+ab​−(b​)2.

The two middle products, −ab-a\sqrt{b}−ab​ and +ab+a\sqrt{b}+ab​, are exact opposites and cancel, which is the same mechanism that made the difference of squares work. The remaining (b)2(\sqrt{b})^2(b​)2 is just bbb, because a square root squared returns the number under it:

(a+b)(a−b)=a2−b.(a + \sqrt{b})(a - \sqrt{b}) = a^2 - b.(a+b​)(a−b​)=a2−b.

The result a2−ba^2 - ba2−b is a plain rational expression with no radical in sight. That is why the conjugate is the right form of 111 to use: it converts a two-term radical denominator into a rational one in a single stroke.

∎

To rationalize 1a+b\frac{1}{a + \sqrt{b}}a+b​1​, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate a−ba - \sqrt{b}a−b​:

1a+b=1a+b⋅a−ba−b=a−ba2−b.\frac{1}{a + \sqrt{b}} = \frac{1}{a + \sqrt{b}}\cdot\frac{a - \sqrt{b}}{a - \sqrt{b}} = \frac{a - \sqrt{b}}{a^2 - b}.a+b​1​=a+b​1​⋅a−b​a−b​​=a2−ba−b​​.

The denominator is now rational, and as always, the conjugate over itself is just 111, so the value is unchanged. As in every example in this lesson, take aaa to be a positive whole number and bbb to be a whole number that is not a perfect square. That keeps a−ba - \sqrt{b}a−b​ from ever being zero: if bbb were a perfect square, b\sqrt{b}b​ would already be a whole number, and there would be no radical left to clear in the first place.

Worked example 3 Rationalize 11+2\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{2}}1+2​1​

The denominator is 1+21 + \sqrt{2}1+2​, so its conjugate is 1−21 - \sqrt{2}1−2​. Multiply top and bottom by it:

11+2=11+2⋅1−21−2=1−212−(2)2.\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{1 - \sqrt{2}} = \frac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{1^2 - (\sqrt{2})^2}.1+2​1​=1+2​1​⋅1−2​1−2​​=12−(2​)21−2​​.

The denominator is 12−(2)2=1−2=−11^2 - (\sqrt{2})^2 = 1 - 2 = -112−(2​)2=1−2=−1:

1−21−2=1−2−1=2−1.\frac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{1 - 2} = \frac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{-1} = \sqrt{2} - 1.1−21−2​​=−11−2​​=2​−1.

Dividing by −1-1−1 flips both signs, so 1−21 - \sqrt{2}1−2​ becomes 2−1\sqrt{2} - 12​−1. A quick check: 11+2≈0.414\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{2}} \approx 0.4141+2​1​≈0.414 and 2−1≈0.414\sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.4142​−1≈0.414.

Rationalize 12+3\frac{1}{2 + \sqrt{3}}2+3​1​.

Answer choices

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate 2−32 - \sqrt{3}2−3​. The denominator is a difference of squares: (2+3)(2−3)=4−3=1(2 + \sqrt{3})(2 - \sqrt{3}) = 4 - 3 = 1(2+3​)(2−3​)=4−3=1. The numerator becomes 1⋅(2−3)=2−31\cdot(2 - \sqrt{3}) = 2 - \sqrt{3}1⋅(2−3​)=2−3​.

12+3=2−34−3=2−31=2−3\frac{1}{2 + \sqrt{3}} = \frac{2 - \sqrt{3}}{4 - 3} = \frac{2 - \sqrt{3}}{1} = 2 - \sqrt{3}2+3​1​=4−32−3​​=12−3​​=2−3​

Multiplying by 2+32 + \sqrt{3}2+3​ again keeps the same sign and leaves a root behind. Computing 4+3=74 + 3 = 74+3=7 flips the sign of a2−ba^2 - ba2−b. And multiplying only the denominator by the conjugate, while leaving the numerator's 111 untouched, changes the value of the fraction.

Worked example 4 Two more conjugate denominators

Rationalize 12−3\frac{1}{2 - \sqrt{3}}2−3​1​. The conjugate is 2+32 + \sqrt{3}2+3​, and (2−3)(2+3)=22−(3)2=4−3=1(2 - \sqrt{3})(2 + \sqrt{3}) = 2^2 - (\sqrt{3})^2 = 4 - 3 = 1(2−3​)(2+3​)=22−(3​)2=4−3=1:

12−3=2+34−3=2+31=2+3.\frac{1}{2 - \sqrt{3}} = \frac{2 + \sqrt{3}}{4 - 3} = \frac{2 + \sqrt{3}}{1} = 2 + \sqrt{3}.2−3​1​=4−32+3​​=12+3​​=2+3​.

When the difference of squares comes out to 111, the denominator disappears entirely.

Now rationalize 45+1\frac{4}{\sqrt{5} + 1}5​+14​, where the numerator is not 111. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate 5−1\sqrt{5} - 15​−1. The denominator is (5)2−12=5−1=4(\sqrt{5})^2 - 1^2 = 5 - 1 = 4(5​)2−12=5−1=4, and the numerator is 4(5−1)4(\sqrt{5} - 1)4(5​−1):

45+1=4(5−1)5−1=4(5−1)4=5−1.\frac{4}{\sqrt{5} + 1} = \frac{4(\sqrt{5} - 1)}{5 - 1} = \frac{4(\sqrt{5} - 1)}{4} = \sqrt{5} - 1.5​+14​=5−14(5​−1)​=44(5​−1)​=5​−1.

The 444 out front cancels the 444 in the denominator, leaving 5−1\sqrt{5} - 15​−1. Always look for a common factor to cancel at the end.

When both terms are square roots

The conjugate trick works just as well when both terms are square roots. The product (a+b)(a−b)(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b})(a​+b​)(a​−b​) is again a difference of squares:

(a+b)(a−b)=(a)2−(b)2=a−b.(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) = (\sqrt{a})^2 - (\sqrt{b})^2 = a - b.(a​+b​)(a​−b​)=(a​)2−(b​)2=a−b.

Both squares collapse to whole numbers, so the denominator becomes the plain difference a−ba - ba−b, with no radical remaining. Here aaa and bbb are nonnegative whole numbers with a≠ba \neq ba=b, so the two roots are genuinely different. If a=ba = ba=b, the two roots would already be equal, making the original denominator a−a=0\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{a} = 0a​−a​=0 before you ever reached for a conjugate.

Worked example 5 Rationalize 15−3\frac{1}{\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{3}}5​−3​1​ and 67−5\frac{6}{\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{5}}7​−5​6​

For 15−3\frac{1}{\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{3}}5​−3​1​, the conjugate is 5+3\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{3}5​+3​, and (5−3)(5+3)=5−3=2(\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{3})(\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{3}) = 5 - 3 = 2(5​−3​)(5​+3​)=5−3=2:

15−3=5+35−3=5+32.\frac{1}{\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{3}}{5 - 3} = \frac{\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{3}}{2}.5​−3​1​=5−35​+3​​=25​+3​​.

The denominator 5−3=25 - 3 = 25−3=2 has no radical, and the numerator keeps both roots.

For 67−5\frac{6}{\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{5}}7​−5​6​, multiply by the conjugate 7+5\sqrt{7} + \sqrt{5}7​+5​. The denominator is 7−5=27 - 5 = 27−5=2, and the numerator is 6(7+5)6(\sqrt{7} + \sqrt{5})6(7​+5​):

67−5=6(7+5)7−5=6(7+5)2=3(7+5).\frac{6}{\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{5}} = \frac{6(\sqrt{7} + \sqrt{5})}{7 - 5} = \frac{6(\sqrt{7} + \sqrt{5})}{2} = 3(\sqrt{7} + \sqrt{5}).7​−5​6​=7−56(7​+5​)​=26(7​+5​)​=3(7​+5​).

The 666 over 222 reduces to 333, giving 37+353\sqrt{7} + 3\sqrt{5}37​+35​.

Answer choices

16−5=6+56−5=6+5\frac{1}{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{5}} = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{5}}{6 - 5} = \sqrt{6} + \sqrt{5}6​−5​1​=6−56​+5​​=6​+5​

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Getting the sign wrong. To clear a+ba + \sqrt{b}a+b​, multiply by the conjugate a−ba - \sqrt{b}a−b​, not by a+ba + \sqrt{b}a+b​ again; the same sign gives (a+b)2=a2+2ab+b(a + \sqrt{b})^2 = a^2 + 2a\sqrt{b} + b(a+b​)2=a2+2ab​+b, which still contains a root. And the conjugate’s product is always a difference, a2−ba^2 - ba2−b or a−ba - ba−b, never a sum: for example (2−3)(2+3)=4−3=1(2 - \sqrt{3})(2 + \sqrt{3}) = 4 - 3 = 1(2−3​)(2+3​)=4−3=1, not 4+3=74 + 3 = 74+3=7.
  • Forgetting to multiply the numerator. A form of 111 like bb\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}}b​b​​ only equals 111 when you multiply both the top and the bottom. Changing just the denominator changes the value.
  • Squaring a root incorrectly. A square root squared undoes the root, so (5)2=5(\sqrt{5})^2 = 5(5​)2=5, not 252525.
  • Not simplifying at the end. After rationalizing, cancel common factors and simplify the radical: 82=822=42\frac{8}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{2} = 4\sqrt{2}2​8​=282​​=42​, not left as 822\frac{8\sqrt{2}}{2}282​​.

Key takeaways

  • The convention is to leave no square root in a denominator. You rewrite the fraction by multiplying by a form of 111, which changes the form but not the value.
  • For a single root, ab=abb\frac{a}{\sqrt{b}} = \frac{a\sqrt{b}}{b}b​a​=bab​​, because b⋅b=b\sqrt{b}\cdot\sqrt{b} = bb​⋅b​=b.
  • For a two-term denominator, multiply both the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate, the same terms with the sign flipped: (a+b)(a−b)=a2−b(a + \sqrt{b})(a - \sqrt{b}) = a^2 - b(a+b​)(a−b​)=a2−b, a difference of squares with no radical.
  • Two-root denominators work the same way: (a+b)(a−b)=a−b(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) = a - b(a​+b​)(a​−b​)=a−b.
  • Simplify the result: reduce common factors and simplify any remaining radical.

A bit of history (optional)

A rule nobody can defend usually had a solid reason once. Keeping square roots out from under a fraction bar is one of those.

Divide 111 by the square root of two with pencil and paper. You will feel the problem at once: you are dividing by an unending decimal, 1.41421…1.41421\ldots1.41421…, and long division by it never quite settles. Move the root upstairs, and the same value asks you to divide by 222 instead, using whatever decimal for 2\sqrt{2}2​ a table already printed. Halving that decimal is a division anyone can finish in a line; dividing by it, as the original problem asked, is not. Generations of students rationalized denominators for that reason: their textbooks printed tables of square roots in the back, and only the rewritten form put that decimal within easy reach of ordinary division.

Then the reason evaporated. In 197219721972 a shirt-pocket machine called the HP-35 arrived with a square root key on its face. Within a few years the slide rule was a museum piece. Both versions of the fraction now cost one button.

The convention outlived its motive because it quietly does a second job. It gives every answer one standard shape. Two students can then compare results instead of arguing about them. That is the real payoff of the conjugate here. Turning 1+21 + \sqrt{2}1+2​ into the plain number −1-1−1 makes the fraction no faster to evaluate. It does make the answer recognizable.

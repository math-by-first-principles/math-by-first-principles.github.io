Factoring by Grouping

Intuition

You have factored trinomials into two binomials, and this chapter added the special patterns: a perfect-square trinomial, a difference of squares, a sum or difference of cubes. Plenty of polynomials match none of those, especially ones with four terms like x3+2x2+3x+6x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 6x3+2x2+3x+6. There is still a way in. Split the four terms into two pairs, pull the greatest common factor out of each pair, and watch for the same binomial to appear in both. When it does, that shared binomial is a common factor of the whole expression, and it comes out front exactly as a common number would. This move, called factoring by grouping, is one useful method for four-term polynomials, and it also gives a systematic way to factor the harder trinomials from the last chapter.

Learning goals

  • Split four terms into pairs and factor each
  • Pull out the shared binomial that both pairs leave
  • Factor a negative out of a pair whose leftover binomial is the opposite of the other pair's
  • Reorder the terms when the first pairing stalls
  • Run the AC method to group a harder trinomial

Grouping a four-term polynomial

Factoring has always been the distributive property run backward. To factor 6x+96x + 96x+9 you spot the common factor 333 and write 3(2x+3)3(2x + 3)3(2x+3). Now look at x2(x+2)+3(x+2)x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2)x2(x+2)+3(x+2): both terms carry the same binomial, x+2x + 2x+2, the way 6x6x6x and 999 both carried 333. Treat x+2x + 2x+2 as the common factor and pull it out the same way:

x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(x2+3).x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x^2 + 3).x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(x2+3).

The rule behind both moves is ka+kb=k(a+b)ka + kb = k(a + b)ka+kb=k(a+b), and it never cared whether kkk was a number or a whole binomial. That single fact, that a binomial can be a common factor just like a number, is the engine of the whole lesson.

For a four-term polynomial the plan follows directly: split it into two pairs, and factor the greatest common factor out of each pair. Then check whether the two pairs leave behind the same binomial. If they do, factor that binomial out.

Worked example 1 Factor x3+2x2+3x+6x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 6x3+2x2+3x+6

Split the polynomial into the first two terms and the last two terms, then factor each pair on its own. The first pair shares x2x^2x2, and the second pair shares 333:

x3+2x2+3x+6=(x3+2x2)+(3x+6)=x2(x+2)+3(x+2).x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 6 = (x^3 + 2x^2) + (3x + 6) = x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2).x3+2x2+3x+6=(x3+2x2)+(3x+6)=x2(x+2)+3(x+2).

Both pieces now carry the same binomial x+2x + 2x+2, the pattern from the opening. Factor it out the same way:

x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(x2+3).x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x^2 + 3).x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(x2+3).

Check by expanding: (x+2)(x2+3)=x3+3x+2x2+6=x3+2x2+3x+6(x + 2)(x^2 + 3) = x^3 + 3x + 2x^2 + 6 = x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 6(x+2)(x2+3)=x3+3x+2x2+6=x3+2x2+3x+6, the original polynomial. The factor x2+3x^2 + 3x2+3 does not factor further with integer coefficients, so (x+2)(x2+3)(x + 2)(x^2 + 3)(x+2)(x2+3) is the complete factorization.

This is not the only pairing that works. Grouping the first and third terms with the second and fourth also leaves a matching binomial, x2+3x^2 + 3x2+3, and factors to the same answer. Any pairing that leaves matching binomials leads to the same result.

Why a shared binomial factors out#

This is the same rule from the opening, ka+kb=k(a+b)ka + kb = k(a + b)ka+kb=k(a+b), applied with k=x+2k = x + 2k=x+2 as Worked Example 1 just did. Factoring by grouping is nothing more than rewriting a polynomial so that a shared binomial like this becomes visible; that rule does the rest.

Watch the sign in the second group

The single most common grouping error is a sign slip in the second pair. When that pair leads with a negative term, factoring out the plain positive GCF leaves a binomial with its signs flipped, and the two pieces no longer match. The fix is to factor out a negative on purpose.

Worked example 2 Factor x3−2x2−3x+6x^3 - 2x^2 - 3x + 6x3−2x2−3x+6

Group the first two and last two terms. The first pair gives x2(x−2)x^2(x - 2)x2(x−2) with no trouble:

x3−2x2−3x+6=(x3−2x2)+(−3x+6).x^3 - 2x^2 - 3x + 6 = (x^3 - 2x^2) + (-3x + 6).x3−2x2−3x+6=(x3−2x2)+(−3x+6).

Now look hard at the second pair, −3x+6-3x + 6−3x+6. Its terms share 333, but factoring out +3+3+3 gives 3(−x+2)=3(2−x)3(-x + 2) = 3(2 - x)3(−x+2)=3(2−x), whose binomial 2−x2 - x2−x does not match the x−2x - 2x−2 from the first pair. Factor out −3-3−3 instead, so the binomial comes out as x−2x - 2x−2:

−3x+6=−3(x−2).-3x + 6 = -3(x - 2).−3x+6=−3(x−2).

With both pairs carrying x−2x - 2x−2, the shared binomial factors out:

x2(x−2)−3(x−2)=(x−2)(x2−3).x^2(x - 2) - 3(x - 2) = (x - 2)(x^2 - 3).x2(x−2)−3(x−2)=(x−2)(x2−3).

Check: (x−2)(x2−3)=x3−3x−2x2+6=x3−2x2−3x+6(x - 2)(x^2 - 3) = x^3 - 3x - 2x^2 + 6 = x^3 - 2x^2 - 3x + 6(x−2)(x2−3)=x3−3x−2x2+6=x3−2x2−3x+6. The factor x2−3x^2 - 3x2−3 has no factorization with integer coefficients, because a difference of squares needs a perfect square and 333 is not one. So this is as far as grouping takes it. The rule to remember: if a pair’s leftover binomial is the opposite of the other pair’s, like 2−x2 - x2−x is the opposite of x−2x - 2x−2, factor a negative out of that pair so the two binomials match.

In the second pair, factoring out +2+2+2 would give 2(4−x)2(4 - x)2(4−x), and 4−x4 - x4−x is the opposite of x−4x - 4x−4, not a match, so factor out −2-2−2 to match.

x2(x−4)−2(x−4)=(x−4)(x2−2)x^2(x - 4) - 2(x - 4) = (x - 4)(x^2 - 2)x2(x−4)−2(x−4)=(x−4)(x2−2)

Expanding confirms (x−4)(x2−2)=x3−4x2−2x+8(x - 4)(x^2 - 2) = x^3 - 4x^2 - 2x + 8(x−4)(x2−2)=x3−4x2−2x+8. Treating 4−x4 - x4−x as if it matched x−4x - 4x−4 produces the tempting wrong option (x−4)(x2+2)(x - 4)(x^2 + 2)(x−4)(x2+2); that mix-up is the sign trap.

Reordering, and when grouping fails

The first pairing you write down is not sacred. If the two pairs do not leave the same binomial, reorder the terms and try again, or pair them differently. Putting the polynomial in descending order by degree is almost always the first thing to try.

Worked example 3 Reorder before grouping

Take x3+6+2x2+3xx^3 + 6 + 2x^2 + 3xx3+6+2x2+3x, with its terms out of the usual order. Pairing them as they stand gives

(x3+6)+(2x2+3x)=(x3+6)+x(2x+3),(x^3 + 6) + (2x^2 + 3x) = (x^3 + 6) + x(2x + 3),(x3+6)+(2x2+3x)=(x3+6)+x(2x+3),

and this stalls: x3+6x^3 + 6x3+6 has no common factor beyond 111, so no shared binomial can appear. Rewrite the polynomial in descending order first:

x3+6+2x2+3x=x3+2x2+3x+6.x^3 + 6 + 2x^2 + 3x = x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 6.x3+6+2x2+3x=x3+2x2+3x+6.

Now the neighboring pairs cooperate, exactly as in Worked Example 1:

(x3+2x2)+(3x+6)=x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(x2+3).(x^3 + 2x^2) + (3x + 6) = x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x^2 + 3).(x3+2x2)+(3x+6)=x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(x2+3).

A grouping that stalls is often just a signal to reorder the terms, not a dead end.

Factor 3x3+28+7x2+12x3x^3 + 28 + 7x^2 + 12x3x3+28+7x2+12x completely by grouping.

Answer choices

Pairing the terms as written stalls: (3x3+28)+(7x2+12x)(3x^3 + 28) + (7x^2 + 12x)(3x3+28)+(7x2+12x) leaves 3x3+283x^3 + 283x3+28 with no common factor beyond 111. Reorder in descending degree first: 3x3+7x2+12x+283x^3 + 7x^2 + 12x + 283x3+7x2+12x+28. Now the pairs cooperate: (3x3+7x2)+(12x+28)=x2(3x+7)+4(3x+7)=(3x+7)(x2+4)(3x^3 + 7x^2) + (12x + 28) = x^2(3x + 7) + 4(3x + 7) = (3x + 7)(x^2 + 4)(3x3+7x2)+(12x+28)=x2(3x+7)+4(3x+7)=(3x+7)(x2+4). Expanding checks: (3x+7)(x2+4)=3x3+12x+7x2+28(3x + 7)(x^2 + 4) = 3x^3 + 12x + 7x^2 + 28(3x+7)(x2+4)=3x3+12x+7x2+28, the original polynomial reordered.

The other two products expand to different polynomials, and the polynomial does in fact group once reordered, so it is not an example of one that fails.

Some four-term polynomials genuinely do not group, and it is worth being able to recognize one. Consider x3+x2+2x+6x^3 + x^2 + 2x + 6x3+x2+2x+6. There are only three ways to split four terms into two pairs, and none of them leaves a shared binomial:

PairingFirst pair factors asSecond pair factors as
1st & 2nd, 3rd & 4thx2(x+1)x^2(x + 1)x2(x+1)2(x+3)2(x + 3)2(x+3)
1st & 3rd, 2nd & 4thx(x2+2)x(x^2 + 2)x(x2+2)no common factor beyond 111
1st & 4th, 2nd & 3rdno common factor beyond 111x(x+2)x(x + 2)x(x+2)

No pairing leaves matching binomials, so this polynomial does not factor by grouping, and that is a fact about the polynomial, not a mistake in your work. Grouping factors a four-term polynomial precisely when some pairing leaves a shared binomial.

The AC method: grouping the harder trinomials

In the last chapter you factored a non-monic trinomial such as 2x2+7x+32x^2 + 7x + 32x2+7x+3 by reverse FOIL. That method asks you to choose factor pairs, build a candidate, expand to check the middle term, and adjust until it fits. You also met the clue that steers the search, that the middle coefficient splits into two numbers whose product is a⋅ca \cdot ca⋅c and whose sum is bbb. Grouping turns that clue into a targeted search: hunt for the two numbers that satisfy both conditions at once, split the middle term into those two pieces, and the trinomial becomes a four-term polynomial you already know how to group.

Worked example 4 Factor 2x2+7x+32x^2 + 7x + 32x2+7x+3 by grouping

Here a=2a = 2a=2, b=7b = 7b=7, and c=3c = 3c=3, so the product to aim for is a⋅c=2⋅3=6a \cdot c = 2 \cdot 3 = 6a⋅c=2⋅3=6. Find two numbers with product 666 and sum 777, namely 666 and 111. Split the middle term 7x7x7x into 6x+1x6x + 1x6x+1x:

2x2+7x+3=2x2+6x+x+3.2x^2 + 7x + 3 = 2x^2 + 6x + x + 3.2x2+7x+3=2x2+6x+x+3.

Now group the four terms and factor each pair:

(2x2+6x)+(x+3)=2x(x+3)+1(x+3)=(x+3)(2x+1).(2x^2 + 6x) + (x + 3) = 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) = (x + 3)(2x + 1).(2x2+6x)+(x+3)=2x(x+3)+1(x+3)=(x+3)(2x+1).

Keep the 111 in front of the second x+3x + 3x+3: it is the coefficient that becomes the 2x+12x + 12x+1. Check by expanding: (x+3)(2x+1)=2x2+x+6x+3=2x2+7x+3(x + 3)(2x + 1) = 2x^2 + x + 6x + 3 = 2x^2 + 7x + 3(x+3)(2x+1)=2x2+x+6x+3=2x2+7x+3.

Why the split with product acacac and sum bbb lets you group#

Worked Example 4 split 7x7x7x into 6x+x6x + x6x+x because 666 and 111 multiply to a⋅c=6a \cdot c = 6a⋅c=6 and add to b=7b = 7b=7. That match is not luck. 2x2+7x+32x^2 + 7x + 32x2+7x+3 factors as (x+3)(2x+1)(x + 3)(2x + 1)(x+3)(2x+1), and multiplying those two binomials back out shows exactly where 6x6x6x and xxx come from:

(x+3)(2x+1)=2x2+6x+x+3.(x + 3)(2x + 1) = 2x^2 + 6x + x + 3.(x+3)(2x+1)=2x2+6x+x+3.

6x6x6x is the 333 from the first binomial times the 2x2x2x from the second, and xxx is the xxx from the first binomial times the 111 from the second. Adding those two pieces is exactly what collecting terms after multiplying out does, so of course they add to the middle term, 7x7x7x.

Multiplying the two pieces’ coefficients, 6⋅1=66 \cdot 1 = 66⋅1=6, also equals a⋅ca \cdot ca⋅c. The trinomial’s a=2a = 2a=2 is itself the product of the binomials’ two xxx-coefficients, 111 and 222, and c=3c = 3c=3 is the product of their two constants, 333 and 111. So a⋅c=(1⋅2)(3⋅1)a \cdot c = (1 \cdot 2)(3 \cdot 1)a⋅c=(1⋅2)(3⋅1), the same four numbers, 111, 222, 333, 111, as 6⋅1=(3⋅2)(1⋅1)6 \cdot 1 = (3 \cdot 2)(1 \cdot 1)6⋅1=(3⋅2)(1⋅1), just grouped differently, and multiplication does not care how numbers are grouped, so the two products match.

This is not special to 2x2+7x+32x^2 + 7x + 32x2+7x+3: whenever a trinomial factors into two binomials at all, expanding them the same way always builds the middle term from two pieces made this same way, so they always add to bbb and multiply to a⋅ca \cdot ca⋅c. That is exactly why the search for a pair with product a⋅ca \cdot ca⋅c and sum bbb works, and why splitting the middle term with that pair and grouping always rebuilds the factored form.

Two numbers with product −18-18−18 and sum 777 must have opposite signs, and 999 and −2-2−2 fit, since 9+(−2)=79 + (-2) = 79+(−2)=7 and 9⋅(−2)=−189 \cdot (-2) = -189⋅(−2)=−18. Split 7x7x7x into 9x−2x9x - 2x9x−2x:

Group, and mind the sign in the second pair. Its leading term −2x-2x−2x is negative, so factor out −1-1−1:

(6x2+9x)+(−2x−3)=3x(2x+3)−1(2x+3)=(2x+3)(3x−1).(6x^2 + 9x) + (-2x - 3) = 3x(2x + 3) - 1(2x + 3) = (2x + 3)(3x - 1).(6x2+9x)+(−2x−3)=3x(2x+3)−1(2x+3)=(2x+3)(3x−1).

Pull out a common factor first

Before splitting a middle term or pairing anything up, check whether every term shares a common factor. If so, pull it out in front. It shrinks the numbers you have to work with and keeps the final answer completely factored.

Worked example 6 Factor 4x3+8x2+6x+124x^3 + 8x^2 + 6x + 124x3+8x2+6x+12 completely

Every coefficient is even, so factor out 222 before doing anything else:

4x3+8x2+6x+12=2(2x3+4x2+3x+6).4x^3 + 8x^2 + 6x + 12 = 2(2x^3 + 4x^2 + 3x + 6).4x3+8x2+6x+12=2(2x3+4x2+3x+6).

Now group inside the parentheses. The first pair shares 2x22x^22x2 and the second shares 333:

2x3+4x2+3x+6=(2x3+4x2)+(3x+6)=2x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(2x2+3).\begin{aligned} 2x^3 + 4x^2 + 3x + 6 &= (2x^3 + 4x^2) + (3x + 6) \\ &= 2x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2) = (x + 2)(2x^2 + 3). \end{aligned}2x3+4x2+3x+6​=(2x3+4x2)+(3x+6)=2x2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(2x2+3).​

Carry the 222 back out front for the complete factorization:

4x3+8x2+6x+12=2(x+2)(2x2+3).4x^3 + 8x^2 + 6x + 12 = 2(x + 2)(2x^2 + 3).4x3+8x2+6x+12=2(x+2)(2x2+3).

Skipping the common factor and grouping the original directly gives 4x2(x+2)+6(x+2)=(x+2)(4x2+6)4x^2(x + 2) + 6(x + 2) = (x + 2)(4x^2 + 6)4x2(x+2)+6(x+2)=(x+2)(4x2+6). The result (x+2)(4x2+6)(x + 2)(4x^2 + 6)(x+2)(4x2+6) is a correct factorization but not a complete one, since 4x2+64x^2 + 64x2+6 still hides a factor of 222. Pulling the common factor out first, or at the very end, makes sure nothing is left behind.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • The sign trap. If a pair’s leftover binomial is the opposite of the other pair’s, like 2−x2 - x2−x is the opposite of x−2x - 2x−2, factor a negative out of that pair so the two binomials match.
  • Dropping the 111. When a pair factors down to a bare binomial, as with x+3=1(x+3)x + 3 = 1(x + 3)x+3=1(x+3), keep the 111; it becomes the coefficient in the final answer, the 2x+12x + 12x+1 in (x+3)(2x+1)(x + 3)(2x + 1)(x+3)(2x+1).
  • Stopping at the grouped line. x2(x+2)+3(x+2)x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2)x2(x+2)+3(x+2) is not factored yet. Pull out the shared (x+2)(x + 2)(x+2) to finish: (x+2)(x2+3)(x + 2)(x^2 + 3)(x+2)(x2+3).
  • Splitting the middle term with the wrong pair. The two numbers must multiply to a⋅ca \cdot ca⋅c AND add to bbb. For 3x2+10x+83x^2 + 10x + 83x2+10x+8, both 5+55 + 55+5 and 6+46 + 46+4 add to 101010, but only 6⋅4=246 \cdot 4 = 246⋅4=24 also equals a⋅ca \cdot ca⋅c.
  • Forgetting the overall common factor. Grouping 4x3+8x2+6x+124x^3 + 8x^2 + 6x + 124x3+8x2+6x+12 without pulling out the 222 first can leave (x+2)(4x2+6)(x + 2)(4x^2 + 6)(x+2)(4x2+6), which still hides a factor of 222.
  • Giving up after one pairing. A single mismatched pairing does not mean the polynomial cannot group; reorder the terms or try a different pairing before concluding it does not.

Key takeaways

  • Factoring by grouping factors a four-term polynomial: split it into two pairs, factor the greatest common factor out of each, and if both pairs leave the same binomial, factor that binomial out. It works because a shared binomial is a common factor, a(x+2)+b(x+2)=(x+2)(a+b)a(x + 2) + b(x + 2) = (x + 2)(a + b)a(x+2)+b(x+2)=(x+2)(a+b), the distributive property with a binomial playing the role of the common factor.
  • If a pair’s leftover binomial is the opposite of the other pair’s, factor out a negative so they match. If the pairs never match, reorder or re-pair the terms; some four-term polynomials do not group at all.
  • The AC method factors ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c by grouping: find two numbers with product a⋅ca \cdot ca⋅c and sum bbb, split bxbxbx into those two pieces, then group.
  • Pull out any overall common factor before grouping, and confirm every factorization by expanding it back out.

You did something in this lesson that took mathematicians a long time to state plainly: pulling a whole binomial out of a sum, the same way you would pull out a number. The proof earlier in this lesson, that ka+kb=k(a+b)ka + kb = k(a + b)ka+kb=k(a+b) holds for any expressions kkk, aaa, and bbb, not just numbers, is exactly what allows that move. For most of algebra’s history, though, that move rested more on habit than on a stated principle. A symbol was shorthand for a quantity, and a quantity meant a number, so a rule proved about numbers seemed to say nothing about a bracket. Mathematicians used the shortcut anyway, well before the general principle behind it was written down.

George Peacock, an English scholar, gave that principle an influential statement in a book of 183018301830, as part of a broader move toward treating algebra as its own system of symbols rather than arithmetic in disguise. His idea: a rule of arithmetic, once settled for numbers, carries over to symbols, whatever those symbols go on to stand for, as long as the new system’s operations still obey that same rule, not merely that the operations are defined for it. Algebra on this view is not just arithmetic with letters filling in for missing numbers. It is a body of rules that plain arithmetic happens to obey.

Factoring x+2x + 2x+2 out of x2(x+2)+3(x+2)x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2)x2(x+2)+3(x+2) treats a binomial exactly as though it were a number, the way the proof above allows. Peacock’s principle is part of how mathematicians came to state clearly why that kind of move is safe, not merely convenient. Later mathematics leaned on the same idea hard: it handed the same rules to objects that are not numbers at all.

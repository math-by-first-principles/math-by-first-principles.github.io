Solving for a Variable

Intuition

A rectangle has area 151515 and length 555. What is its width? Divide the area by the length: 15÷5=315 \div 5 = 315÷5=3. That works whenever you know the area and the length, because width=area÷length\text{width} = \text{area} \div \text{length}width=area÷length every time.

Now write that pattern as a formula instead of redoing the division each time. The area formula A=lwA = lwA=lw gives the area once you already have the length and width. Rearranged once, it gives the width directly instead: w=Alw = \dfrac{A}{l}w=lA​. Check it against the rectangle above: w=Al=155=3w = \dfrac{A}{l} = \dfrac{15}{5} = 3w=lA​=515​=3, the same width you already found by dividing. Rearranging a formula this way is the whole of this lesson. You do it with the same balancing moves you already use on equations full of numbers: whatever you do to one side, you do to the other, until the letter you want stands alone. The one new wrinkle, and the interesting part, is what to do when the letter you are chasing shows up in two places at once.

Learning goals

  • Isolate a chosen letter, treating the others as fixed numbers
  • Rearrange A=lwA = lwA=lw into w=Alw = \dfrac{A}{l}w=lA​
  • Factor the target out when it appears in several terms
  • State the nonzero condition that dividing by a letter assumes
  • Check a rearranged formula with simple numbers from the original

What it means to solve for a variable

A literal equation is an equation that contains more than one letter, and once you start looking, literal equations are everywhere. The equations A=lwA = lwA=lw, d=rtd = rtd=rt, P=2l+2wP = 2l + 2wP=2l+2w, and C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr are all literal equations, and so is nearly every formula in geometry and science.

To solve for a particular variable in such an equation is to rewrite the equation so that one variable stands alone on a side. Every other letter and number is gathered on the other side. Solving A=lwA = lwA=lw for www means reaching w=Alw = \dfrac{A}{l}w=lA​; solving the same equation for lll means reaching l=Awl = \dfrac{A}{w}l=wA​. The relationship never changes what it says. You are only rewriting it so a different letter becomes the subject.

The key that unlocks all of this is a small shift in how you read the other letters. When you solve A=lwA = lwA=lw for www, treat lll as one fixed quantity, a stand-in for some length whose value you do not need to know while you work. That is exactly the way the first lesson of this subject taught you to see a letter as a placeholder for a number. Then A=lwA = lwA=lw has the same shape as 12=4w12 = 4w12=4w, and you already know to divide by the coefficient. Solving for a variable is nothing more than the equation solving of the last few lessons, carried out with letters sitting patiently where the numbers used to be.

The same moves, now with letters

Because the other letters behave like fixed numbers, the tools are the ones you already trust: the properties of equality. You may add, subtract, multiply, or divide both sides by the same quantity (nonzero for multiplying and dividing), and each move produces an equivalent equation. To solve for a target variable, undo the operations wrapped around it in reverse order, using inverse operations, until it stands alone. The only difference from before is that the numbers you add or divide by now wear letters.

Why isolating a letter uses the very same moves#

The properties of equality do not care whether the multiplier you divide by is a numeral or a letter: they let you divide both sides by it all the same, provided it is not zero.

So divide both sides of A=lwA = lwA=lw by lll. On the right, the lll in the numerator cancels the lll you divided by, leaving www alone:

Al=lwl=w.\frac{A}{l} = \frac{lw}{l} = w.lA​=llw​=w.

Read the result the usual way round and w=Alw = \dfrac{A}{l}w=lA​, provided l≠0l \neq 0l=0. The single move, dividing both sides by the coefficient of the target, is the same one that finished every equation in the last few lessons. Treating lll as a fixed quantity is what makes a letter behave like the 444 in 4w=124w = 124w=12.

∎

Worked example 1 Solve d=rtd = rtd=rt for the rate rrr

The distance formula d=rtd = rtd=rt multiplies the rate rrr by the time ttt. To solve for rrr, undo that multiplication by dividing both sides by ttt, which a real trip guarantees is not zero:

dt=rtt=r,sor=dt.\frac{d}{t} = \frac{rt}{t} = r, \qquad\text{so}\qquad r = \frac{d}{t}.td​=trt​=r,sor=td​.

The rearranged formula reads rate as distance divided by time, which is exactly the average rate over the whole trip. Solving instead for ttt is the mirror image: dividing by rrr gives t=drt = \dfrac{d}{r}t=rd​, provided r≠0r \neq 0r=0, that is, the trip actually covers some distance.

Rearranging a formula in more than one step

Most formulas wrap the target in more than one operation, so you peel them off in reverse order, just as you did solving two-step equations. Strip away anything added to the target first, then divide by whatever multiplies it. The whole routine from the equation-solving lesson carries over unchanged; the constants are simply letters now.

Worked example 2 Solve P=2l+2wP = 2l + 2wP=2l+2w for the width www

The perimeter of a rectangle is P=2l+2wP = 2l + 2wP=2l+2w. The target www sits inside the term 2w2w2w, with 2l2l2l added on, so undo in reverse. Subtract the added 2l2l2l from both sides first:

P−2l=2w.P - 2l = 2w.P−2l=2w.

Now divide both sides by the coefficient 222. The division must reach the whole left side, every term of it:

w=P−2l2.w = \frac{P - 2l}{2}.w=2P−2l​.

Watch that last step. Dividing only the PPP and forgetting the 2l2l2l gives the wrong P2−2l\dfrac{P}{2} - 2l2P​−2l; the correct result is P−2l2\dfrac{P - 2l}{2}2P−2l​, which equals P2−l\dfrac{P}{2} - l2P​−l once each term is divided.

Worked example 3 Solve C=59(F−32)C = \dfrac{5}{9}(F - 32)C=95​(F−32) for FFF

The formula C=59(F−32)C = \dfrac{5}{9}(F - 32)C=95​(F−32) turns a Fahrenheit temperature into Celsius. To aim it the other way and read Fahrenheit from Celsius, solve for FFF. The target is under two layers: it is inside the parentheses, and the whole parenthesis is multiplied by 59\frac{5}{9}95​. Undo the outer layer first by multiplying both sides by the reciprocal 95\frac{9}{5}59​:

95C=F−32.\frac{9}{5}C = F - 32.59​C=F−32.

Then add 323232 to both sides to free FFF:

F=95C+32.F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32.F=59​C+32.

That is the companion formula every weather report uses. Rearranging once, rather than re-solving from scratch each time, is the whole payoff: one derivation hands you a formula aimed straight at Fahrenheit.

Worked example 4 Solve y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b for xxx

The equation y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b describes a line. Solving for yyy takes no work at all, since yyy already stands alone. Solving for xxx takes the familiar two steps. Subtract bbb from both sides:

y−b=mx.y - b = mx.y−b=mx.

Then divide by the coefficient mmm, assuming the line is not horizontal so that m≠0m \neq 0m=0:

x=y−bm.x = \frac{y - b}{m}.x=my−b​.

Keep the whole numerator y−by - by−b together over mmm. Throughout, yyy is treated as a fixed quantity, no different from the constants mmm and bbb, and that is what lets the ordinary moves work.

Check your understanding

Solve ax+b=cax + b = cax+b=c for xxx, where a≠0a \neq 0a=0 and aaa, bbb, ccc are fixed constants.

Answer choices

Undo the operations in reverse: subtract the added bbb from both sides, then divide by the coefficient aaa (assuming a≠0a \neq 0a=0).

ax=c−b,x=c−baax = c - b, \qquad x = \frac{c - b}{a}ax=c−b,x=ac−b​

ca−b\dfrac{c}{a} - bac​−b comes from dividing the axaxax term by aaa but forgetting to divide the bbb that moved to the other side; a division must reach every term, not just the letter you are chasing. Dropping the sign on bbb gives c+ba\dfrac{c + b}{a}ac+b​. Treat aaa, bbb, and ccc as fixed numbers and the steps match a numerical two-step equation.

When the target appears in more than one term

Every formula so far held the target in a single term. The one genuinely new situation is a target that appears in two or more terms at once. Try a numeric case first. In 3x+2x=203x + 2x = 203x+2x=20, both terms already hold xxx, so combine them the way you combine like terms anywhere: 3x+2x=5x3x + 2x = 5x3x+2x=5x, which turns the equation into 5x=205x = 205x=20, so x=4x = 4x=4. Writing 3x+2x3x + 2x3x+2x as (3+2)x(3 + 2)x(3+2)x is exactly the distributive law read backward.

The same idea rescues a symbolic equation like ax+bx=cax + bx = cax+bx=c, where the coefficients are letters instead of numbers. Dividing both sides by aaa (assuming a≠0a \neq 0a=0) does remove the coefficient from the first term, but the second term becomes bax\dfrac{b}{a}xab​x: xxx is still sitting in two places, just with different coefficients now. No single division finishes the job until the two copies of xxx are combined into one, the same combining step that turned 3x+2x3x + 2x3x+2x into 5x5x5x above. The fix is the factoring skill from the expanding lesson, the distributive law read in reverse.

Why a target in two terms must be factored out#

Suppose the variable you are solving for, xxx, appears in two separate terms, as in ax+bx=cax + bx = cax+bx=c. The distributive law says x(a+b)=ax+bxx(a + b) = ax + bxx(a+b)=ax+bx. Read from right to left, that same equation says

ax+bx=x(a+b),ax + bx = x(a + b),ax+bx=x(a+b),

which gathers the two copies of xxx into a single factor. This is the crucial move: once xxx appears only once, multiplied by the quantity a+ba + ba+b, one division finishes the job. Divide both sides by a+ba + ba+b, which is legal as long as a+b≠0a + b \neq 0a+b=0:

x=ca+b.x = \frac{c}{a + b}.x=a+bc​.

Factoring is not a trick saved for tidy expressions; it is the one step that turns two appearances of the target into one. And once the target appears a single time, the ordinary inverse operations isolate it exactly as before.

∎

The same routine handles a target that starts on both sides of the equation. Bring every term containing the target to one side first, then factor it out.

Worked example 5 Solve ax=bx+cax = bx + cax=bx+c for xxx

Here the target xxx appears on both sides, in axaxax and in bxbxbx. First bring both xxx terms to the same side by subtracting bxbxbx from both sides:

ax−bx=c.ax - bx = c.ax−bx=c.

Now the two copies of xxx sit together, so factor xxx out by reading the distributive law backward:

x(a−b)=c.x(a - b) = c.x(a−b)=c.

With xxx in a single place, divide both sides by a−ba - ba−b, which requires a≠ba \neq ba=b so the divisor is not zero:

x=ca−b.x = \frac{c}{a - b}.x=a−bc​.

Collecting the target on one side and factoring it out is the routine whenever the variable you want appears more than once.

Worked example 6 Solve A=P+PrtA = P + PrtA=P+Prt for the principal PPP

The amount in a simple-interest account after a time ttt is A=P+PrtA = P + PrtA=P+Prt, the principal PPP plus the interest PrtPrtPrt it earns. To recover the principal from the final amount, solve for PPP. The target appears in both terms on the right, so factor it out. Both PPP and PrtPrtPrt share the factor PPP:

A=P(1+rt).A = P(1 + rt).A=P(1+rt).

Now PPP multiplies the single quantity 1+rt1 + rt1+rt, so divide both sides by it, provided 1+rt≠01 + rt \neq 01+rt=0:

P=A1+rt.P = \frac{A}{1 + rt}.P=1+rtA​.

For an account earning interest, rrr and ttt are positive, so 1+rt1 + rt1+rt is always at least 111 and the division is safe. The factor pulled out was 1+rt1 + rt1+rt, not rtrtrt. The lone PPP is P⋅1P \cdot 1P⋅1, so a 111 stays behind inside the parentheses; dropping it and writing P=ArtP = \dfrac{A}{rt}P=rtA​ is the most common slip here.

Check your understanding

Solve mx+nx=pmx + nx = pmx+nx=p for xxx, where m+n≠0m + n \neq 0m+n=0.

Answer choices

Both terms on the left contain xxx, so factor it out, then divide by the quantity left behind (assuming m+n≠0m + n \neq 0m+n=0).

x(m+n)=p,x=pm+nx(m + n) = p, \qquad x = \frac{p}{m + n}x(m+n)=p,x=m+np​

Dividing by mmm alone (assuming m≠0m \neq 0m=0) would rescale both terms rather than remove xxx from just one: mx+nx=pmx + nx = pmx+nx=p becomes x+nmx=pmx + \dfrac{n}{m}x = \dfrac{p}{m}x+mn​x=mp​, and xxx is still in two places. Factoring gathers both copies of xxx into one before the single division, and the factor is the sum m+nm + nm+n, not the product mnmnmn.

Dividing by a letter, and checking the result

Every division in this lesson, by lll, by ttt, by a+ba + ba+b, quietly assumed the divisor was not zero. With a written number, you can see whether it is zero before you divide. With a letter, or an expression built from letters, you cannot see its value, so you name the condition instead: the same nonzero-denominator rule from the algebraic-fractions lesson. An expression you divide by, or place under a fraction bar, must not be zero, and any value that would make it zero is excluded. For the geometry and motion formulas above, the condition takes care of itself: a side length is positive, and for a trip that actually happens, its distance and duration are both positive, which forces its rate to be positive too. In a purely symbolic rearrangement you should name the condition out loud: w=Alw = \dfrac{A}{l}w=lA​ holds provided l≠0l \neq 0l=0, and x=ca−bx = \dfrac{c}{a - b}x=a−bc​ holds provided a≠ba \neq ba=b. When the excluded value does happen, in this case a=ba = ba=b, the rearranged formula simply does not apply, and the original equation needs its own look, separate from the rearranging done here.

Check your understanding

Solve C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr for rrr. Which condition, if any, belongs with your answer?

Answer choices

Isolating rrr means dividing both sides by 2π2\pi2π.

r=C2πr = \frac{C}{2\pi}r=2πC​

A condition is needed only when the divisor could be zero. Here the divisor is 2π2\pi2π, a fixed number, about 6.286.286.28, that is never zero, unlike dividing by a letter such as lll or a−ba - ba−b, whose value you cannot see. rrr is the answer you are solving for, not something you divided by, so r≠0r \neq 0r=0 is not the relevant condition here. Flipping the fraction to 2πC\dfrac{2\pi}{C}C2π​ solves for the wrong quantity.

Now try one where the divisor really is a letter. The volume of a box is V=lwhV = lwhV=lwh, the length times the width times the height.

Check your understanding

Solve V=lwhV = lwhV=lwh for hhh. What condition must accompany your rearranged formula?

Answer choices

Isolating hhh means dividing both sides by the product lwlwlw.

h=Vlwh = \frac{V}{lw}h=lwV​

Unlike 2π2\pi2π above, lwlwlw is built from letters, so its value is unknown and could be zero, and a condition is required: lw≠0lw \neq 0lw=0. VVV is the quantity in the numerator, not the divisor, so V≠0V \neq 0V=0 is not the condition the division creates. Flipping the fraction to lwV\dfrac{lw}{V}Vlw​ solves for the wrong quantity. And skipping the condition entirely is wrong precisely because the divisor here is letters, not a fixed number.

A rearranged formula is easy to check, and the check is worth the few seconds. Pick simple numbers that fit the original, then confirm the rearranged version agrees. Take w=Alw = \dfrac{A}{l}w=lA​. A rectangle with l=5l = 5l=5 and w=3w = 3w=3 has area A=15A = 15A=15, and the rearranged formula returns Al=155=3\dfrac{A}{l} = \dfrac{15}{5} = 3lA​=515​=3, matching the width you started with. When a test case comes out wrong, a sign or a stray term in the rearrangement is off. This test catches a slip exactly as substituting a numeric answer back into the original equation does.

Now try it yourself. The first checkpoint rearranged ax+b=cax + b = cax+b=c into x=c−bax = \dfrac{c - b}{a}x=ac−b​. Pick a=2a = 2a=2, b=1b = 1b=1, c=7c = 7c=7: compute xxx from the rearranged formula, then substitute that value into the original 2x+1=72x + 1 = 72x+1=7 and confirm both sides match.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Dividing only one term by the coefficient. After reaching 2w=P−2l2w = P - 2l2w=P−2l, dividing by 222 must hit the entire other side: w=P−2l2w = \dfrac{P - 2l}{2}w=2P−2l​, which equals P2−l\dfrac{P}{2} - l2P​−l, never P2−2l\dfrac{P}{2} - 2l2P​−2l. Every term crosses the division, just as every term does when you distribute.
  • Losing a sign when subtracting a term. Solving y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b for xxx means subtracting bbb from both sides, which gives y−by - by−b, not y+by + by+b. Apply the same operation, with the same sign, to both sides rather than sliding a term across unchanged.
  • Forgetting to factor when the target is in two terms. If xxx appears in ax+bxax + bxax+bx, dividing by aaa (assuming a≠0a \neq 0a=0) rescales both terms but does not isolate xxx: it leaves x+baxx + \dfrac{b}{a}xx+ab​x, with xxx still in two places. Collect the xxx terms, factor xxx out to x(a+b)x(a + b)x(a+b), and only then divide.
  • Pulling out the wrong factor. Factoring P+PrtP + PrtP+Prt gives P(1+rt)P(1 + rt)P(1+rt), not P(rt)P(rt)P(rt): the lone PPP is P⋅1P \cdot 1P⋅1, so a 111 stays inside. Check any factoring by expanding it back.
  • Dividing by a letter without noting it must be nonzero. The answer x=ca−bx = \dfrac{c}{a - b}x=a−bc​ is valid only when a≠ba \neq ba=b. State the condition whenever you divide by an expression that contains a letter.
  • Solving for the wrong variable. “Solve for www” means www ends up alone on one side with no other www anywhere in the equation. Isolating lll instead, or leaving the target on both sides, does not answer the question that was asked.

Key takeaways

  • A literal equation has more than one letter, and most formulas are literal equations. To solve for a chosen variable is to isolate it on one side, treating every other letter as a fixed but unknown number.
  • The moves are the properties of equality from the last lessons, unchanged. Undo the operations wrapped around the target in reverse order, with inverse operations, so A=lwA = lwA=lw becomes w=Alw = \dfrac{A}{l}w=lA​.
  • When the target appears in more than one term, collect those terms on one side and factor the target out, using the distributive law in reverse. Then divide by the quantity left behind: ax+bx=cax + bx = cax+bx=c becomes x(a+b)=cx(a + b) = cx(a+b)=c, so x=ca+bx = \dfrac{c}{a + b}x=a+bc​.
  • Dividing by a letter assumes it is nonzero, the same nonzero condition as a denominator; name the excluded values in a symbolic answer.
  • Check a rearranged formula by substituting simple numbers that fit the original.
  • Rearranging a formula lets you compute a different quantity directly, and solving a two-variable equation for yyy is the setup for graphing a line in the next chapters.

A bit of history (optional)

A single line like A=lwA = lwA=lw speaks for every rectangle there has ever been. That reach is younger than it looks. For most of the history of mathematics a general rule was stated in words, or shown once with particular numbers, and readers copied the pattern. The known amounts in a problem had no symbols of their own. Only the unknown got a letter.

Rene Descartes fixed the habit we still keep, in a book he published in 1637. Letters from the front of the alphabet, aaa, bbb and ccc, would stand for quantities being treated as known. Letters from the far end, xxx, yyy and zzz, would stand for the ones being hunted. Nothing deep sits behind the choice, and it could have gone the other way. What it bought was a formula you could read at a glance, its fixed parts and its target already sorted.

That sorting is the move this lesson asks of you, and you make it yourself every time. Solving A=lwA = lwA=lw for www treats lll as a fixed quantity, a length whose value you do not need yet. Solve the same formula for lll instead and the two letters trade places. The line on the page never changes. Your decision about which letter is the target is the thing that changes.

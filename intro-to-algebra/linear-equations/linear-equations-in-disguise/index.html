Linear Equations in Disguise

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

The equation x2+x3=5\dfrac{x}{2} + \dfrac{x}{3} = 52x​+3x​=5 looks different from 2x+3=112x + 3 = 112x+3=11, but one multiplication removes its fractions. Parentheses and decimals can also hide a familiar linear equation. Some equations with variable denominators simplify this way too, provided you keep track of the values their denominators rule out. You will also meet equations whose variable cancels completely. The statement left behind tells you whether any values work.

Learning goals

  • Clear fractions by multiplying every term by the LCD
  • Multiply through by a power of ten to clear decimals
  • Cross-multiply a proportion, which just scales both sides by bdbdbd
  • Name the excluded values before solving with a variable denominator
  • Read a canceled variable as no solution or infinitely many
  • Ask which equation a verdict belongs to after clearing

Clearing the fractions

The multiplication property of equality from the previous lesson lets you multiply both sides by a nonzero number without changing the solutions. A common denominator can remove all the fractions at once. Take x2+x3=5\dfrac{x}{2} + \dfrac{x}{3} = 52x​+3x​=5. For positive whole-number denominators, the least common denominator (LCD) is the smallest positive whole number divisible by each denominator. For 222 and 333, it is 666.

Multiply each side by 666 and distribute to every term:

6⋅x2+6⋅x3=6⋅5.6 \cdot \frac{x}{2} + 6 \cdot \frac{x}{3} = 6 \cdot 5.6⋅2x​+6⋅3x​=6⋅5.

Since 6÷2=36 \div 2 = 36÷2=3 and 6÷3=26 \div 3 = 26÷3=2, this becomes 3x+2x=303x + 2x = 303x+2x=30. Combining gives 5x=305x = 305x=30, so x=6x = 6x=6. The constant 555 must also be multiplied: the property of equality acts on the whole side, not just its fractional terms.

Why multiplying by the LCD keeps the same solutions#

For x4+x6=5\dfrac{x}{4} + \dfrac{x}{6} = 54x​+6x​=5, the LCD is 121212. Multiplying every term by 121212 gives 3x+2x=603x + 2x = 603x+2x=60. Dividing every term by 121212 returns the original equation, so this multiplication neither loses nor gains a solution.

The same reasoning holds for any nonzero numerical LCD. If an equation has sides LLL and RRR, multiplying by its LCD mmm gives mL=mRmL = mRmL=mR. Dividing by mmm reverses the move. The two equations therefore have exactly the same solutions.

The LCD clears the fractions because it is a multiple of every denominator. Dividing mmm by each denominator leaves an integer multiplier for that numerator. Distribution sends mmm to every term, so all denominators cancel in the same step.

∎

When a numerator is itself a sum, such as x+1x + 1x+1, keep it wrapped in parentheses as the LCD lands on it. That way the multiplication reaches both terms of the numerator, and any following subtraction reaches both as well.

Worked example 1 Solve x+12−x−35=2\dfrac{x+1}{2} - \dfrac{x-3}{5} = 22x+1​−5x−3​=2

The denominators are 222 and 555, so the LCD is 101010. Multiply every term by 101010, keeping each numerator in parentheses:

10⋅x+12−10⋅x−35=10⋅2,5(x+1)−2(x−3)=20.\begin{gathered} 10 \cdot \frac{x+1}{2} - 10 \cdot \frac{x-3}{5} = 10 \cdot 2, \\ 5(x+1) - 2(x-3) = 20. \end{gathered}10⋅2x+1​−10⋅5x−3​=10⋅2,5(x+1)−2(x−3)=20.​

Now distribute, and watch the minus sign in front of the second product reach both of its terms:

5x+5−2x+6=20,3x+11=20.\begin{gathered} 5x + 5 - 2x + 6 = 20, \\ 3x + 11 = 20. \end{gathered}5x+5−2x+6=20,3x+11=20.​

Subtract 111111, then divide by 333:

3x=9,x=3.3x = 9, \qquad x = 3.3x=9,x=3.

Check in the original equation: 3+12−3−35=42−0=2\dfrac{3+1}{2} - \dfrac{3-3}{5} = \dfrac{4}{2} - 0 = 223+1​−53−3​=24​−0=2, which matches. The −2-2−2 multiplies both xxx and −3-3−3, which is why the second constant becomes +6+6+6.

Clearing parentheses and decimals

For nested parentheses, work from the innermost group outward, distributing and combining like terms one layer at a time. This uses the distribution and inside-out grouping from Arithmetic with Expressions in the Language of Algebra chapter.

Worked example 2 Solve 2(3x−(x−4))=282\big(3x - (x - 4)\big) = 282(3x−(x−4))=28

Start with the inner group. Subtracting (x−4)(x - 4)(x−4) flips both of its terms, so 3x−(x−4)=3x−x+4=2x+43x - (x - 4) = 3x - x + 4 = 2x + 43x−(x−4)=3x−x+4=2x+4. The equation becomes

2(2x+4)=28.2(2x + 4) = 28.2(2x+4)=28.

Distribute the outer 222, then solve the two-step equation that appears:

4x+8=28,4x=20,x=5.4x + 8 = 28, \qquad 4x = 20, \qquad x = 5.4x+8=28,4x=20,x=5.

Check in the original: 2(3(5)−(5−4))=2(15−1)=2⋅14=282\big(3(5) - (5 - 4)\big) = 2\big(15 - 1\big) = 2 \cdot 14 = 282(3(5)−(5−4))=2(15−1)=2⋅14=28. Peeling the inner parentheses first is what kept the signs straight.

Terminating decimals can be cleared by multiplying by a power of ten. Multiplying by 101010 shifts each digit one place to the left: 10⋅0.1x=x10 \cdot 0.1x = x10⋅0.1x=x and 10⋅0.5=510 \cdot 0.5 = 510⋅0.5=5. Use 101010 when the numbers have at most one decimal place, 100100100 for at most two, and so on. Choose enough factors of ten to make all coefficients and constants integers. Negative numbers work too: 10⋅(−0.2)=−210 \cdot (-0.2) = -210⋅(−0.2)=−2. The multiplier is nonzero, so division reverses the step and preserves the solutions.

Worked example 3 Solve 0.5x−0.2=0.1x+10.5x - 0.2 = 0.1x + 10.5x−0.2=0.1x+1

The decimals have one place, so multiply both sides by 101010 to clear them:

10(0.5x−0.2)=10(0.1x+1),5x−2=x+10.\begin{gathered} 10(0.5x - 0.2) = 10(0.1x + 1), \\ 5x - 2 = x + 10. \end{gathered}10(0.5x−0.2)=10(0.1x+1),5x−2=x+10.​

Collect the variable on the left by subtracting xxx, then finish:

4x−2=10,4x=12,x=3.4x - 2 = 10, \qquad 4x = 12, \qquad x = 3.4x−2=10,4x=12,x=3.

Check in the original: the left is 0.5(3)−0.2=1.5−0.2=1.30.5(3) - 0.2 = 1.5 - 0.2 = 1.30.5(3)−0.2=1.5−0.2=1.3 and the right is 0.1(3)+1=0.3+1=1.30.1(3) + 1 = 0.3 + 1 = 1.30.1(3)+1=0.3+1=1.3, so x=3x = 3x=3.

The decimals have one place, so multiply every term by 101010 to clear them.

10(0.3x+1)=10(0.1x+5)3x+10=x+50.\begin{aligned}10(0.3x + 1) &= 10(0.1x + 5) \\ 3x + 10 &= x + 50.\end{aligned}10(0.3x+1)3x+10​=10(0.1x+5)=x+50.​

Subtract xxx, then subtract 101010: 2x=402x = 402x=40, so x=20x = 20x=20. Checking, 0.3(20)+1=6+1=70.3(20) + 1 = 6 + 1 = 70.3(20)+1=6+1=7 and 0.1(20)+5=2+5=70.1(20) + 5 = 2 + 5 = 70.1(20)+5=2+5=7. Multiplying only the coefficients and leaving the constants unscaled gives the wrong x=2x = 2x=2.

Proportions and cross-multiplication

A proportion states that two ratios are equal. The cross-multiplication shortcut from Pre-Algebra multiplies each numerator by the opposite denominator. For example, 35=610\dfrac{3}{5} = \dfrac{6}{10}53​=106​ gives 3⋅10=5⋅63 \cdot 10 = 5 \cdot 63⋅10=5⋅6, or 30=3030 = 3030=30. Multiplying both sides by 5⋅10=505 \cdot 10 = 505⋅10=50 cancels the denominators and leaves those products. In general, ab=cd\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}ba​=dc​ gives ad=bcad = bcad=bc, provided both denominators bbb and ddd are nonzero.

Why cross-multiplication is legal#

Start with 23=46\dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{4}{6}32​=64​. Multiply both sides by 3⋅6=183 \cdot 6 = 183⋅6=18. The left becomes 6⋅26 \cdot 26⋅2 and the right becomes 3⋅43 \cdot 43⋅4, giving 12=1212 = 1212=12. Each numerator has been multiplied by the opposite denominator. Dividing both sides by 181818 recovers the original proportion.

A proportion ab=cd\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}ba​=dc​ is an equation like any other, with b≠0b \neq 0b=0 and d≠0d \neq 0d=0 so that both fractions are defined. To clear both denominators in one stroke, multiply both sides by the product bdbdbd. That product is nonzero, since neither factor is zero, so the multiplication property of equality applies and the step can be undone.

On the left, the bbb in the denominator cancels the bbb in bdbdbd, leaving

bd⋅ab=bdb⋅a=d⋅a=ad.bd \cdot \frac{a}{b} = \frac{bd}{b} \cdot a = d \cdot a = ad.bd⋅ba​=bbd​⋅a=d⋅a=ad.

On the right, the ddd cancels in the same way, giving bd⋅cd=bcbd \cdot \dfrac{c}{d} = bcbd⋅dc​=bc. So the proportion becomes

ad=bc.ad = bc.ad=bc.

The numerical example used 181818; the general argument uses bdbdbd. Nothing else changes. Because bdbdbd is nonzero, division by bdbdbd reverses the move, so the proportion and ad=bcad = bcad=bc are equivalent.

∎

Worked example 4 Solve x6=x+49\dfrac{x}{6} = \dfrac{x+4}{9}6x​=9x+4​

Cross-multiply, sending each numerator across to the opposite denominator:

9⋅x=6⋅(x+4),9x=6x+24.9 \cdot x = 6 \cdot (x + 4), \qquad 9x = 6x + 24.9⋅x=6⋅(x+4),9x=6x+24.

The result is an ordinary linear equation with the variable on both sides. Subtract 6x6x6x, then divide by 333:

3x=24,x=8.3x = 24, \qquad x = 8.3x=24,x=8.

Check the proportion at x=8x = 8x=8: the left ratio is 86=43\dfrac{8}{6} = \dfrac{4}{3}68​=34​ and the right ratio is 8+49=129=43\dfrac{8 + 4}{9} = \dfrac{12}{9} = \dfrac{4}{3}98+4​=912​=34​. The two ratios agree, so x=8x = 8x=8.

7⋅x=4⋅(x+3)7x=4x+12.\begin{aligned}7 \cdot x &= 4 \cdot (x + 3) \\ 7x &= 4x + 12.\end{aligned}7⋅x7x​=4⋅(x+3)=4x+12.​

Subtract 4x4x4x, then divide by 333: 3x=123x = 123x=12, so x=4x = 4x=4. Checking, 44=1\frac{4}{4} = 144​=1 and 4+37=77=1.\frac{4+3}{7} = \frac{7}{7} = 1.74+3​=77​=1.

When the variable sits in the denominator

In 6x=2\dfrac{6}{x} = 2x6​=2 and x+1x−2=3\dfrac{x+1}{x-2} = 3x−2x+1​=3, multiplying by the denominator leaves a linear equation. Not every equation with variable denominators does, but these examples do.

First list the excluded values, values that make an original denominator zero. At every allowed value, the denominator is nonzero, so multiplying by it can be undone. After solving the cleared equation, reject any excluded candidate and check the others in the original.

For 6x=2\dfrac{6}{x} = 2x6​=2 the only excluded value is x=0x = 0x=0. Multiplying both sides by xxx gives 6=2x6 = 2x6=2x, so x=3.x = 3.x=3. This value is allowed, and substituting into the original gives 63=2\dfrac{6}{3} = 236​=2, confirming that it is a solution.

If a side has a term outside the fraction, multiply that term too. For 4x+1=3\dfrac{4}{x} + 1 = 3x4​+1=3, exclude x=0x = 0x=0. Multiplying every term by xxx gives

4+x=3x,4=2x,x=2.\begin{aligned} 4 + x &= 3x, \\ 4 &= 2x, \\ x &= 2. \end{aligned}4+x4x​=3x,=2x,=2.​

The value 222 is allowed, and 42+1=3\dfrac{4}{2} + 1 = 324​+1=3 checks in the original equation.

Worked example 5 Solve x+1x−2=3\dfrac{x+1}{x-2} = 3x−2x+1​=3

The denominator x−2x - 2x−2 is zero when x=2x = 2x=2, so record the restriction x≠2x \neq 2x=2 before doing any algebra. This is already a proportion, x+1x−2=31\dfrac{x+1}{x-2} = \dfrac{3}{1}x−2x+1​=13​, so cross-multiply:

x+1=3(x−2),x+1=3x−6.x + 1 = 3(x - 2), \qquad x + 1 = 3x - 6.x+1=3(x−2),x+1=3x−6.

Collect the variable and solve:

7=2x,x=72.7 = 2x, \qquad x = \frac{7}{2}.7=2x,x=27​.

The excluded value was x=2x = 2x=2, and 72=3.5\dfrac{7}{2} = 3.527​=3.5 is not 222, so the answer survives the check. Substituting confirms it: 72+172−2=9232=93=3\dfrac{\frac{7}{2} + 1}{\frac{7}{2} - 2} = \dfrac{\frac{9}{2}}{\frac{3}{2}} = \dfrac{9}{3} = 327​−227​+1​=23​29​​=39​=3.

A cleared equation can produce a candidate the original forbids. In xx−3=3x−3\dfrac{x}{x-3} = \dfrac{3}{x-3}x−3x​=x−33​, exclude x=3x = 3x=3 first. Multiplying by x−3x - 3x−3 leaves x=3x = 3x=3, but that value makes both original denominators zero. Reject it: there is no solution.

Check your understanding

Before solving xx+1=−1x+1\dfrac{x}{x + 1} = \dfrac{-1}{x + 1}x+1x​=x+1−1​, name the excluded value. Which conclusion is correct after clearing the denominator?

Answer choices

The original denominator x+1x + 1x+1 is zero at x=−1x = -1x=−1, so exclude −1-1−1 before multiplying by x+1x + 1x+1.

x=−1x = -1x=−1

The cleared equation has one solution, but it is the excluded value. Substituting it into the original would require division by zero. Reject it: the original equation has no solution. The cleared equation's verdict does not remove the original restriction.

One solution, no solution, or infinitely many

Even with no variable denominator, an equation can have no solution. It can also have infinitely many. After you collect like terms, the variable may cancel, leaving a statement about numbers.

Watch it happen. In 2x+1=2x+32x + 1 = 2x + 32x+1=2x+3, subtracting 2x2x2x from both sides removes the variable and leaves

1=3,1 = 3,1=3,

which is false. In 2(x+1)=2x+22(x + 1) = 2x + 22(x+1)=2x+2, the left side is 2x+22x + 22x+2, and subtracting 2x2x2x leaves

2=2,2 = 2,2=2,

which is true. The first equation demands something impossible, so no value of xxx can satisfy it. The second says something that holds no matter what, so every value of xxx satisfies it. The next proof explains exactly why the leftover statement is the whole story.

Why a vanished variable settles the number of solutions#

Compare three reduced equations. The equation 3x=93x = 93x=9 gives x=3x = 3x=3. The equation 0x=20x = 20x=2 can never hold because its left side is always zero. The equation 0x=00x = 00x=0 holds for every real number.

Once every term is a number or a number times xxx, collecting variable terms on one side and constants on the other gives the general form

ax=b.ax = b.ax=b.

Here aaa is the combined variable coefficient and bbb is the remaining constant, both fixed numbers. The three numerical cases illustrate the possibilities for any aaa and bbb. If a≠0a \neq 0a=0, division by aaa gives exactly one solution, x=bax = \dfrac{b}{a}x=ab​. The variable cancels when a=0a = 0a=0.

If a=0a = 0a=0, the left side is zero for every xxx. When b≠0b \neq 0b=0, the equation asks zero to equal a nonzero number, so no value works. Such an equation is a contradiction. When b=0b = 0b=0, the equation is 0=00 = 00=0, true for every real number. This is an identity: an equation true for every value for which both sides are defined. You first met identities in From Arithmetic to Algebra.

Each verdict applies to the original equation if all earlier steps were reversible for every real xxx. Clearing fractions with a nonzero number meets that condition: dividing by the same number reverses the move.

When a denominator contains xxx, clearing it is reversible only at the original allowed values. Keep those restrictions. A single candidate may be excluded, as x=3x = 3x=3 was in the earlier equation with x−3x - 3x−3 in both denominators. An identity after clearing means every allowed value works, not that excluded values have become solutions. For example, x−3x−3=1\dfrac{x-3}{x-3} = 1x−3x−3​=1 clears to x−3=x−3x - 3 = x - 3x−3=x−3, but x=3x = 3x=3 is still excluded. It has infinitely many solutions: all real numbers except 333.

∎

Sometimes the cancellation appears only after you distribute and combine like terms.

Worked example 6 Solve 6x−2(x−1)=4x+56x - 2(x - 1) = 4x + 56x−2(x−1)=4x+5

Distribute the −2-2−2 on the left and combine like terms:

6x−2x+2=4x+5,4x+2=4x+5.\begin{gathered} 6x - 2x + 2 = 4x + 5, \\ 4x + 2 = 4x + 5. \end{gathered}6x−2x+2=4x+5,4x+2=4x+5.​

Both sides carry 4x4x4x, so subtract 4x4x4x from both. The variable cancels and leaves

2=5.2 = 5.2=5.

This statement is false, and no value of xxx can make 222 equal 555, so the equation has no solution. Note the trap: the answer is not x=0x = 0x=0 or x=2x = 2x=2. A canceled variable with a false remainder means there is no solution at all.

Worked example 7 Solve 5x−(x−4)=4(x+1)5x - (x - 4) = 4(x + 1)5x−(x−4)=4(x+1)

Clear the parentheses on each side, letting the minus sign reach both terms of x−4x - 4x−4:

5x−x+4=4x+4,4x+4=4x+4.\begin{gathered} 5x - x + 4 = 4x + 4, \\ 4x + 4 = 4x + 4. \end{gathered}5x−x+4=4x+4,4x+4=4x+4.​

The two sides are identical. Subtract 4x4x4x from both and the variable disappears, leaving

4=4,4 = 4,4=4,

which is true for every value of xxx. The equation is an identity, so every real number is a solution. Report “all real numbers,” rather than a single value.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying only some terms by the LCD. The multiplier lands on every term of both sides, not just the fractions. Clearing x2+x3=5\frac{x}{2} + \frac{x}{3} = 52x​+3x​=5 with the LCD 666 gives 3x+2x=303x + 2x = 303x+2x=30; forgetting to multiply the 555 leaves the wrong 3x+2x=53x + 2x = 53x+2x=5.
  • Dropping a sign when a numerator is a sum. A fraction bar groups its numerator, so −x−35-\frac{x-3}{5}−5x−3​ multiplied by the LCD 101010 from Example 1 becomes −2(x−3)=−2x+6-2(x - 3) = -2x + 6−2(x−3)=−2x+6, not −2x−6-2x - 6−2x−6. Keep each numerator in parentheses as the LCD arrives.
  • Cross-multiplying something that is not a single ratio equal to a single ratio. Cross-multiplication applies to ab=cd\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}ba​=dc​. If a side is a sum of fractions or has extra terms, multiply every term by a common denominator instead. With variable denominators, keep the original excluded values.
  • Forgetting a candidate can be an excluded value. When the variable sits in a denominator, list the excluded values first and check your answer against them. If the value you find is one that was excluded, reject it; the equation has no solution there.
  • Calling a contradiction "x=0x = 0x=0". When the variable cancels and leaves a false statement such as 2=52 = 52=5, the answer is “no solution,” never x=0x = 0x=0. Likewise a true statement such as 0=00 = 00=0 means every allowed value works, not “no solution.” Keep any original excluded values.

Key takeaways

  • Clearing fractions: multiply every term on both sides by the LCD. For numerical denominators, this is a reversible multiplication by a nonzero number. Keep sum numerators in parentheses.
  • Clearing decimals: multiply every term by a power of ten that makes all coefficients and constants integers.
  • Cross-multiplication: ab=cd\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}ba​=dc​ becomes ad=bcad = bcad=bc, provided bbb and ddd are nonzero. This is multiplication of both sides by bdbdbd.
  • Excluded values: list values that make an original denominator zero before clearing. Reject excluded candidates and check the remaining answers in the original equation.
  • Solution counts: ax=bax = bax=b has one solution when a≠0a \neq 0a=0. When a=0a = 0a=0, a false statement means none and a true statement means all real numbers. If you cleared a variable denominator, keep its exclusions: an identity then means all allowed values.

A bit of history (optional)

In the 1930s, a group of mathematicians writing under the shared name Nicolas Bourbaki wanted consistent notation for their books. Rather than wait until their full account of set theory was ready, they published a summary of results. It appeared in 1939 and included a sign for the empty set, a collection with no members: ∅\varnothing∅.

André Weil later recalled suggesting the sign. He borrowed the letter Ø from the Norwegian alphabet, which he said he alone in the group knew. Years later, his daughter Nicolette learned the symbol at school. In his memoir, Weil recalled telling her that he was responsible for its adoption, and said that this earned him her respect.

The empty set and the number zero play different roles: a collection containing zero has one member, whereas the empty set has none. That is why “no solutions” and “the solution is zero” need different notation.

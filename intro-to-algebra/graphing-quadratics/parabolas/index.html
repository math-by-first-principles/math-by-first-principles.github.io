Parabolas

Intuition

You already know how to graph a straight line: the equation y=ax+by = ax + by=ax+b traces a line, and two points pin it down. A quadratic raises the stakes by one power. Its highest term is a square, and the moment an x2x^2x2 appears, the graph stops being straight and starts to bend. Plot the solutions of y=x2y = x^2y=x2 and they curve into a smooth bowl. That bowl-shaped curve is a parabola. Ignoring air resistance, it is close to the path a thrown ball traces through the air, it is the cross-section of a satellite dish, and it is the shape of every quadratic you will ever graph. This lesson takes the parabola apart piece by piece, so that from the three numbers aaa, bbb, and ccc alone you can say which way it opens. Those numbers also tell you where its lowest or highest point sits, and where it crosses the axes, without plotting a single point once you see why.

Learning goals

  • Read aaa for which way it opens and how wide
  • Locate the axis of symmetry at x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​
  • Find the vertex, the lowest or highest point
  • Complete the square to reach vertex form y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x - h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k
  • Take the yyy-intercept as (0,c)(0, c)(0,c)
  • Count the xxx-intercepts with the discriminant b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac

The graph of a quadratic is a curve

A quadratic is an equation whose highest power of xxx is a square, written in the standard form

y=ax2+bx+c,a≠0.y = ax^2 + bx + c, \qquad a \ne 0.y=ax2+bx+c,a=0.

The restriction a≠0a \ne 0a=0 is what makes it quadratic: if aaa were 000 the x2x^2x2 term would disappear and you would be left with the line y=bx+cy = bx + cy=bx+c. With a genuine x2x^2x2 term the graph is no longer straight. To find out what it is, do exactly what you did for a line: build a table of solutions, plot the points, and connect them.

Start with the plainest quadratic there is, y=x2y = x^2y=x2, where a=1a = 1a=1, b=0b = 0b=0, and c=0c = 0c=0. Pick a spread of xxx-values and square each one.

Plot the five points. They do not line up; they bend. Joining them with a smooth curve instead of straight segments gives a rounded U. That U dips to a lowest point at the origin and sweeps upward on both sides. That curve is a parabola, and every quadratic graphs as one.

Look again at the table, because it hides the parabola’s most important secret. The rows pair up: x=−2x = -2x=−2 and x=2x = 2x=2 both give y=4y = 4y=4, and x=−1x = -1x=−1 and x=1x = 1x=1 both give y=1y = 1y=1. Squaring throws away the sign, so two inputs that are opposite in sign land at exactly the same height. That is why the left half of the curve is a mirror image of the right half, and it is the first fact we will make precise below.

The same table-and-plot routine graphs any quadratic. As long as aaa stays the same, changing bbb or ccc only slides the bowl around the plane; its shape never changes. (The next section looks at what changing aaa itself does.)

Which way it opens, and how wide

The single number aaa, the coefficient of x2x^2x2, controls both the direction the parabola opens and how wide it is. You can read the reason straight off y=x2y = x^2y=x2.

Every output of y=x2y = x^2y=x2 is a square, so yyy is never negative, and y=0y = 0y=0 happens only at x=0x = 0x=0. As xxx moves away from 000 in either direction, x2x^2x2 grows, so the curve climbs on both sides. That is why it forms a valley with its lowest point at the origin. Now bring back the coefficient aaa. Multiplying by a positive aaa only rescales those heights, so a valley stays a valley and the parabola opens upward. Multiplying by a negative aaa flips the sign of every height, turning the valley upside down into a hill, so the parabola opens downward.

The size of aaa sets the steepness. A large ∣a∣\lvert a \rvert∣a∣ multiplies each height by more, so the arms shoot up faster and the parabola looks narrow. A small ∣a∣\lvert a \rvert∣a∣ scales each height down, so the arms rise gently and the parabola looks wide.

The axis of symmetry

That mirror pattern in the table is worth pinning down, because it is the key to everything else. A parabola is symmetric about a vertical line, its axis of symmetry, and for y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c that line is

x=−b2a.x = -\frac{b}{2a}.x=−2ab​.

For y=x2y = x^2y=x2, where b=0b = 0b=0, the axis is x=0x = 0x=0, the y-axis itself, which matches the fold you saw in the table. The reason the axis sits at −b2a-\tfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​ comes straight from completing the square, the tool you learned two lessons ago.

In plain words, completing the square rewrites yyy so that xxx appears only once, wrapped inside a single squared term. A square cannot tell a positive distance from a negative one, so two inputs the same distance from the axis, one on each side, always land at the same height. The proof below makes that precise.

Why a parabola is symmetric about x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​#

Complete the square on y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c. Factor aaa out of the first two terms, add and subtract the constant that finishes the square inside, and collect what is left:

y=a(x2+bax)+c=a(x+b2a)2+(c−b24a).y = a\left(x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x\right) + c = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + \left(c - \frac{b^2}{4a}\right).y=a(x2+ab​x)+c=a(x+2ab​)2+(c−4ab2​).

Look at where xxx appears in that final form: only once, inside the square (x+b2a)2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2(x+2ab​)2. Everything else is a constant. So the height yyy depends on xxx entirely through that one squared term.

Now compare two inputs placed the same distance ddd on either side of the line x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, namely x=−b2a+dx = -\tfrac{b}{2a} + dx=−2ab​+d and x=−b2a−dx = -\tfrac{b}{2a} - dx=−2ab​−d. For the first, x+b2a=dx + \tfrac{b}{2a} = dx+2ab​=d; for the second, x+b2a=−dx + \tfrac{b}{2a} = -dx+2ab​=−d. Squaring both gives the same value d2d^2d2:

(−b2a+d+b2a)2=d2=(−b2a−d+b2a)2.\left(-\tfrac{b}{2a} + d + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = d^2 = \left(-\tfrac{b}{2a} - d + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2.(−2ab​+d+2ab​)2=d2=(−2ab​−d+2ab​)2.

Since the squared term is identical, the whole expression for yyy is identical, so the two mirror-image inputs produce the same height. That is exactly what it means for the graph to be symmetric about the vertical line x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​. Fold the plane along that line and the left half of the parabola lands on the right half. The line is the axis of symmetry.

∎

The vertex

The axis of symmetry passes through one special point on the parabola, the place where the curve stops falling and starts rising (or stops rising and starts falling). That turning point is the vertex, and it sits right where the axis meets the curve, at

x=−b2a.x = -\frac{b}{2a}.x=−2ab​.

To get the matching yyy, substitute that xxx back into the equation. There is nothing new to memorize for the y-coordinate: find the x-coordinate from −b2a-\tfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​, then plug it in.

Because the axis is the fold line, the vertex is the one point that has no mirror twin, so it is the turning point of the whole curve. When the parabola opens upward (a>0a > 0a>0) the vertex is the lowest point on the graph, and when it opens downward (a<0a < 0a<0) the vertex is the highest point. You can see why from the completed-square form y=a(x+b2a)2+(c−b24a)y = a\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + \left(c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}\right)y=a(x+2ab​)2+(c−4ab2​), where the square is never negative. So for a>0a > 0a>0 the term a(…)2a(\ldots)^2a(…)2 only adds to the constant, making yyy smallest when the square is 000, which is exactly at the vertex. For a<0a < 0a<0 the same term only subtracts, so yyy is largest there. (Reading a real-world lowest cost or highest point off a parabola is the job of a later lesson; here it is simply a fact about the shape.)

Vertex form

The completed-square form from the proof above is so useful for graphing that it has its own name. Vertex form is

y=a(x−h)2+k,y = a(x - h)^2 + k,y=a(x−h)2+k,

and its whole point is that you can read the vertex straight off it: the vertex is (h,k)(h, k)(h,k). Set x=hx = hx=h and the square is 000, so y=ky = ky=k, which is the turning value. The leading coefficient aaa is also the same aaa as in standard form, so it still tells you the direction and width. Matching this against the completed square shows h=−b2ah = -\tfrac{b}{2a}h=−2ab​ and k=c−b24ak = c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}k=c−4ab2​, the same axis and vertex height as before.

Watch the sign inside the parentheses. Vertex form is x−hx - hx−h, so y=(x−3)2+2y = (x - 3)^2 + 2y=(x−3)2+2 has h=3h = 3h=3 (vertex x-coordinate 333), while y=(x+3)2+2y = (x + 3)^2 + 2y=(x+3)2+2 is really (x−(−3))2+2(x - (-3))^2 + 2(x−(−3))2+2, so h=−3h = -3h=−3. The number inside the parentheses is the opposite of the vertex’s x-coordinate.

Converting standard form to vertex form is just completing the square; converting back is just expanding. Both directions are worth practicing, because each form shows you something the other hides. Standard form hands you ccc as the y-intercept, and vertex form hands you the vertex.

Worked example 3 Write y=x2−6x+5y = x^2 - 6x + 5y=x2−6x+5 in vertex form, then convert it back

The y-intercept

The y-intercept is where the graph crosses the y-axis, and every point on the y-axis has x=0x = 0x=0. Set x=0x = 0x=0 in y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c and the first two terms vanish:

y=a(0)2+b(0)+c=c.y = a(0)^2 + b(0) + c = c.y=a(0)2+b(0)+c=c.

So the y-intercept is the point (0,c)(0, c)(0,c), read off instantly as the constant term of standard form. For y=x2−2x−3y = x^2 - 2x - 3y=x2−2x−3 the y-intercept is (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3); for y=−x2+4y = -x^2 + 4y=−x2+4 it is (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4). Every graph of a quadratic function has exactly one y-intercept, because a vertical line meets the curve just once.

The x-intercepts and the discriminant

The x-intercepts are where the graph crosses the x-axis, and every point on the x-axis has y=0y = 0y=0. So to find them, set y=0y = 0y=0 and solve the quadratic equation

ax2+bx+c=0,ax^2 + bx + c = 0,ax2+bx+c=0,

by factoring when it splits nicely, or by the quadratic formula when it does not. Each real solution is one x-intercept. Unlike the y-intercept, a parabola can have two, one, or zero x-intercepts. Which of the three you get is decided entirely by the discriminant b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac that you met with the quadratic formula.

Why the discriminant counts the x-intercepts#

The x-intercepts are the real solutions of ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0. By the quadratic formula those solutions are

x=−b±b2−4ac2a,x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a},x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​,

and the quantity under the root, the discriminant D=b2−4acD = b^2 - 4acD=b2−4ac, controls how many real values come out. When D>0D > 0D>0 the root D\sqrt{D}D​ is a nonzero real number, so the ±\pm± produces two different real xxx-values: the parabola crosses the x-axis twice. When D=0D = 0D=0 the root is 000, the ±\pm± collapses, and there is exactly one real xxx-value: the parabola just touches the axis at its vertex. When D<0D < 0D<0 the root is not real, so no real xxx makes y=0y = 0y=0 and the parabola misses the x-axis entirely.

The picture agrees with the algebra. Completing the square put the vertex height at k=c−b24a=−b2−4ac4a=−D4ak = c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a} = -\tfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a} = -\tfrac{D}{4a}k=c−4ab2​=−4ab2−4ac​=−4aD​. Take an upward parabola, a>0a > 0a>0, whose vertex is its lowest point. That lowest point lies below the x-axis when k<0k < 0k<0, on it when k=0k = 0k=0, and above it when k>0k > 0k>0, which is D>0D > 0D>0, D=0D = 0D=0, and D<0D < 0D<0 in turn. A bowl whose bottom is below the axis must cross it twice, and one sitting exactly on the axis touches once. A bowl held above the axis never reaches it. So the sign of the discriminant and the number of x-intercepts tell the same story, once from the formula and once from the shape.

Putting it all together

Each feature is quick on its own; together they let you sketch a parabola without a long table. The recipe is always the same: read the direction from the sign of aaa, then find the vertex from −b2a-\tfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​. Mark the y-intercept at (0,c)(0, c)(0,c), and find the x-intercepts by setting y=0y = 0y=0.

Common mistakes

  • Connecting parabola points with straight segments. A parabola bends everywhere. Plot enough points, including the vertex, and draw one smooth curve, not a dot-to-dot polygon.
  • Reading the opening from bbb or ccc instead of aaa. Only the leading coefficient aaa decides the direction: a>0a > 0a>0 opens up, a<0a < 0a<0 opens down. The signs of bbb and ccc do not.
  • Forgetting the minus sign in −b2a-\tfrac{b}{2a}−2ab​. For y=x2−4x+1y = x^2 - 4x + 1y=x2−4x+1 the axis is x=−−42=2x = -\tfrac{-4}{2} = 2x=−2−4​=2, not −2-2−2. The formula flips the sign of bbb.
  • Stopping at the vertex x-coordinate. The vertex is a point. After finding x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, you still have to substitute it back to get the y-coordinate.
  • Mixing up the sign in vertex form. In y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x - h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k the vertex is (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), so y=(x+4)2y = (x + 4)^2y=(x+4)2 has h=−4h = -4h=−4, not 444. The number inside is the opposite of the vertex’s x-coordinate.
  • Confusing the intercepts. The y-intercept comes from setting x=0x = 0x=0 (it is (0,c)(0, c)(0,c)); the x-intercepts come from setting y=0y = 0y=0 (solve the quadratic). Zeroing the wrong variable finds the wrong intercept.
  • Assuming every parabola crosses the x-axis. When the discriminant b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac is negative there are no x-intercepts, because the whole curve stays on one side of the axis.

Key takeaways

  • The graph of a quadratic y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c (with a≠0a \ne 0a=0) is a parabola, a smooth U-shaped curve. Plot a table of points and join them with a curve to see it.
  • The leading coefficient aaa sets the shape: a>0a > 0a>0 opens upward, a<0a < 0a<0 opens downward, a large ∣a∣\lvert a \rvert∣a∣ is narrow, and a small ∣a∣\lvert a \rvert∣a∣ is wide.
  • The axis of symmetry is the vertical line x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​. The parabola is a mirror image across it because completing the square makes yyy depend on xxx only through (x+b2a)2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2(x+2ab​)2.
  • The vertex is the turning point at x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​; substitute back for its height. It is the lowest point when a>0a > 0a>0 and the highest when a<0a < 0a<0.
  • Vertex form y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x - h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k shows the vertex (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) directly; complete the square to reach it and expand to return to standard form.
  • The y-intercept is (0,c)(0, c)(0,c). The x-intercepts are the real solutions of ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0, and the discriminant b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac counts them: positive gives two, zero gives one, negative gives none.

A bit of history (optional)

Hang a chain between two hooks and it settles into a shape you have been drawing all lesson. It sags in the middle and sweeps upward on both sides. It looks exactly like a parabola, and for a long time almost everyone assumed that it was one.

In 1646 a seventeen-year-old Dutch student named Christiaan Huygens proved that it is not. The chain traces a different curve. That curve hugs a parabola near the bottom, then drifts away from it further out. The two shapes agree at the vertex and disagree on the arms. It is exactly the kind of difference an eye is powerless to settle.

So the eye is the wrong instrument here, and the equation is the right one. The parabolas this lesson has been reading all along are precisely the graphs of quadratic functions, y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c with a≠0a \ne 0a=0. The hanging chain does not fit that description, however convincing it looks.

Then a second surprise. The main cable of a suspension bridge really is a parabola, or near enough to build on. What separates it from the chain is the load. A bare chain carries only its own weight, spread evenly along its own length. A bridge cable carries the roadway hanging beneath it, spread evenly across the gap. That different burden bends it into a different curve.

Two cables, hung from the same two hooks, take different shapes according to what pulls on them. Sag and width alone will not tell you which curve you are looking at, since a parabola and the chain’s true curve can look almost identical. Finding the actual equation, and checking whether it has the form y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c, will, which is why this lesson spent its time learning to read aaa, bbb, and ccc.

    1. What a and c tell you before you plot anything . Foundational, 8 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 8 pts

    Two of the three numbers in y=ax2+bx+cy=ax^2+bx+cy=ax2+bx+c decide two features of the graph immediately, with no plotting and no solving. This question asks you to read those features off a single quadratic, then say what the third number is doing instead.

    2. Reading a second point off the axis of symmetry . Application, 9 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Application 9 pts

    The axis of symmetry is a fold line: once you know it, every point on one side hands you a matching point on the other side for free, with no new substitution. This question puts that shortcut to work on a quadratic's own y-intercept.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find the axis of symmetry of y=2x2−12x+7y=2x^2-12x+7y=2x2−12x+7.

    2. B
      Part B.

    The answer

    Part A

    x=3x=3x=3.

    Part B

    (6,7)(6,7)(6,7).

    Part C

    Completed-square form shows yyy depends on xxx only through the squared term (x+b2a)2\left(x+\tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2(x+2ab​)2. Two points ddd units on either side of the axis give that square the same value, d2d^2d2, so yyy comes out identical at both.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Use x=−b2ax=-\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​ with a=2a=2a=2, b=−12b=-12b=−12.

    x=−−122(2)=124=3.x = -\frac{-12}{2(2)} = \frac{12}{4} = 3.x=−2(2)−12​=412​=3.

    So the axis of symmetry is the line x=3x=3x=3.

    Part B

    The y-intercept sits at x=0x=0x=0, a distance of 333 units to the left of the axis x=3x=3x=3. Its mirror point sits the same distance, 333 units, on the other side of the axis.

    3+3=6.3 + 3 = 6.3+3=6.

    Since a mirror pair always shares the same height, the point is (6,7)(6,7)(6,7), with no new substitution needed.

    Part C

    4. Two intercepts is not a law . Reasoning, 10 points. Question 4 of 5.

    Reasoning 10 pts

    A claim: "a parabola's graph always crosses the x-axis at exactly two points." That claim sounds safe because so many textbook examples do cross twice. This question breaks it, with the discriminant standing as the tool that always knows the count in advance.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Refute the claim in the stem: give one specific quadratic y=ax2+bx+cy=ax^2+bx+cy=ax2+bx+c for which it fails, and confirm the failure by computing its discriminant.

      Construct a counterexample Give one specific case, and show it breaks the claim. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      For y=x2−10x+25y=x^2-10x+25y=x2−10x+25, state how many x-intercepts the graph has, and say exactly where the vertex sits relative to the x-axis in that case.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      State all three ways the number of x-intercepts relates to the sign of the discriminant, and explain why 'a parabola always has exactly two x-intercepts' cannot be salvaged even by restricting the claim to upward-opening parabolas only.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 4

      A claim about EVERY parabola only needs one honest counterexample to fall. The discriminant tells you the intercept count before you try to plot or factor anything.

    2. Hint 2 of 4 · Part A

      Pick any quadratic where the constant term is unusually large relative to bbb; a big enough ccc tends to push the whole curve away from the x-axis.

    3. Hint 3 of 4 · Part B

      A discriminant of exactly zero is the boundary case between two intercepts and none. Work out what that boundary looks like geometrically, not just algebraically.

    4. Hint 4 of 4 · Part C

      Ask whether the sign of aaa by itself has any power to force DDD positive. Look back at your own part A quadratic for the answer.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    y=2x2−4x+7y=2x^2-4x+7y=2x2−4x+7 is a counterexample: D=(−4)2−4(2)(7)=16−56=−40<0D=(-4)^2-4(2)(7)=16-56=-40<0D=(−4)2−4(2)(7)=16−56=−40<0, so it has no real x-intercepts at all, not two.

    Part B

    One x-intercept. The discriminant is 000, so the vertex sits ON the x-axis.

    Part C

    D>0D>0D>0 gives two intercepts, D=0D=0D=0 gives one, D<0D<0D<0 gives none, regardless of aaa's sign; restricting to upward parabolas cannot rescue 'always two', since aaa alone never forces D>0D>0D>0.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    One counterexample is enough to break a claim about EVERY parabola, so pick any quadratic and check it honestly.

    Take y=2x2−4x+7y=2x^2-4x+7y=2x2−4x+7, so a=2a=2a=2, b=−4b=-4b=−4, c=7c=7c=7.

    Part B

Quadratic Optimization

Intuition

A great many ordinary questions ask for the best: the largest garden you can fence, or the price that brings in the most money. Earlier in this chapter you learned that the graph of y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c is a parabola with a single turning point, the vertex, the lowest point of the curve when it opens up and the highest point when it opens down. That is exactly what an optimization question is after. When every input is allowed, the best value of a quadratic quantity is not something you hunt for by testing numbers: it sits at the vertex, and you already know how to find the vertex. The one habit to build from the start is to keep two different things apart: the input that produces the best result, and the best result itself. They answer two different questions, and mixing them up is the single most common way these problems go wrong.

Learning goals

  • Locate the vertex input x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​ where the best value occurs
  • Read the sign of aaa to tell a maximum from a minimum
  • Separate where the best occurs from how good it is
  • Model a problem as a quadratic, then answer what was asked
  • Apply it to fixed perimeter, revenue and projectile height

Why the vertex is the best value

Take any quadratic quantity written as y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c. From the parabola lesson you can find its vertex in two moves: the input is

x=−b2a,x = -\frac{b}{2a},x=−2ab​,

and the value there is whatever you get by substituting that xxx back into the equation. If the quadratic is already in vertex form y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x - h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k, it is even quicker, because the vertex is (h,k)(h, k)(h,k). The value kkk at the vertex is then sitting right in front of you.

Which of the two, a maximum or a minimum, you have found is decided entirely by the sign of aaa. A positive aaa opens the parabola upward into a valley, so the vertex is the lowest point and yyy has a minimum there. A negative aaa opens it downward into a hill, so the vertex is the highest point and yyy has a maximum. There is no separate rule to memorize for word problems. Instead, read the sign of the leading coefficient, and you know whether the vertex is a floor or a ceiling.

Why the vertex holds the largest or smallest value#

Write the quadratic in vertex form by completing the square, y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x - h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k, where the vertex is (h,k)(h, k)(h,k). Everything hangs on the squared piece (x−h)2(x - h)^2(x−h)2. A square is never negative, so (x−h)2≥0(x - h)^2 \ge 0(x−h)2≥0, and it equals 000 at exactly one input, x=hx = hx=h.

Suppose a>0a > 0a>0. Multiplying a nonnegative number by a positive number keeps it nonnegative, so a(x−h)2≥0a(x - h)^2 \ge 0a(x−h)2≥0, and therefore

y=a(x−h)2+k≥k.y = a(x - h)^2 + k \ge k.y=a(x−h)2+k≥k.

The value yyy can never dip below kkk, and it actually reaches kkk only when the square is 000, that is at x=hx = hx=h. So kkk is the smallest value the quadratic ever takes: a minimum, occurring at the vertex.

Now suppose a<0a < 0a<0. Multiplying a nonnegative number by a negative number makes it nonpositive, so a(x−h)2≤0a(x - h)^2 \le 0a(x−h)2≤0, and therefore y≤ky \le ky≤k. This time yyy can never rise above kkk, and it reaches kkk only at x=hx = hx=h, so kkk is the largest value: a maximum, again at the vertex.

Either way, the extreme value kkk occurs at the single input x=h=−b2ax = h = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=h=−2ab​, and the sign of aaa decides which extreme it is. That is why optimizing a quadratic never means testing many inputs: the two-step vertex calculation lands on the answer directly.

∎

This picture is worth holding in your mind for the rest of the lesson. The quantity you want to make as large or as small as possible traces a parabola, and you are simply locating its peak or its valley.

Before any word problems, practice the pure mechanics on a bare quadratic, keeping the input and the value in separate boxes in your head.

Worked example 1 The maximum value of y=−2x2+12x−7y = -2x^2 + 12x - 7y=−2x2+12x−7

The leading coefficient is a=−2a = -2a=−2. Since a<0a < 0a<0 the parabola opens downward, so its vertex is the highest point and the quantity has a maximum. Find the input where that happens with x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, reading a=−2a = -2a=−2 and b=12b = 12b=12:

x=−b2a=−122(−2)=−12−4=3.x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{12}{2(-2)} = -\frac{12}{-4} = 3.x=−2ab​=−2(−2)12​=−−412​=3.

The maximum occurs at the input x=3x = 3x=3. To get the maximum value, substitute x=3x = 3x=3 back into the equation:

y=−2(3)2+12(3)−7=−18+36−7=11.y = -2(3)^2 + 12(3) - 7 = -18 + 36 - 7 = 11.y=−2(3)2+12(3)−7=−18+36−7=11.

So the maximum value of yyy is 111111, and it happens at x=3x = 3x=3. Notice how different those two numbers are. If a question asks for the maximum value, the answer is 111111; the number 333 is only where it occurs. Answering 333 to “what is the maximum” is the classic slip this lesson is built to prevent.

x=−−62(1)=3x = -\frac{-6}{2(1)} = 3x=−2(1)−6​=3

A method for optimization problems

Real problems arrive as sentences, not as y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c. Turning the words into a quadratic reuses the same modeling discipline you built for quadratic applications. There is one change at the end: instead of setting the quantity equal to a target and solving, you find its vertex.

  1. Name the variable. Pick a letter for the quantity you get to choose (a width, a price, a time), and write every other quantity in the problem in terms of it.
  2. Build the quadratic. Express the thing you want to make biggest or smallest (an area AAA, a revenue RRR, a height hhh, a product PPP) as a quadratic in your variable.
  3. Find the vertex. Compute the optimizing input with x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​, and read the sign of aaa to confirm you have a maximum (a<0a < 0a<0) or a minimum (a>0a > 0a>0).
  4. Answer the exact question, with units. Decide whether the problem wants the optimizing input, the optimal value, or both, then substitute back as needed and state the result in a sentence. Check that the answer is physically sensible: a length is positive, a count is a whole number, and if the vertex itself is not an allowed input, use the nearest input that is.

Step 4 is where the input-versus-value distinction earns its keep. “What price maximizes revenue” wants the input; “what is the greatest revenue” wants the value. Read the final sentence of the problem carefully, and give back precisely what it asks.

Greatest area for a fixed amount of fence

Area problems are the heart of this topic, because area is a product of two lengths. When a fixed perimeter ties those two lengths together, the product of the two becomes a quadratic. A fixed supply of fence is the usual setup.

Worked example 2 Largest rectangular pen from 40 metres of fence

A farmer has 404040 metres of fencing to enclose a rectangular pen. What dimensions enclose the greatest area, and what is that area?

Name the width xxx (in metres). The four sides are two widths and two lengths, and they use all 404040 metres, so 2x+2(length)=402x + 2(\text{length}) = 402x+2(length)=40, which gives a length of 20−x20 - x20−x. Area is length times width:

A=x(20−x)=20x−x2.A = x(20 - x) = 20x - x^2.A=x(20−x)=20x−x2.

This is a quadratic in xxx with a=−1a = -1a=−1 and b=20b = 20b=20. Since a<0a < 0a<0 the parabola opens downward, so its vertex is a maximum. The optimizing width is

x=−b2a=−202(−1)=10.x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{20}{2(-1)} = 10.x=−2ab​=−2(−1)20​=10.

The width is 101010 metres, and the length is 20−x=1020 - x = 1020−x=10 metres, so the best pen is a 101010 by 101010 square. Its area is the value at the vertex:

A=10(20−10)=10⋅10=100.A = 10(20 - 10) = 10 \cdot 10 = 100.A=10(20−10)=10⋅10=100.

The greatest area is 100100100 square metres. For a fixed perimeter, the maximum-area rectangle always turns out to be a square, which the algebra has just confirmed.

That last claim is worth catching in the act rather than taking on trust, because it rests on many different rectangles sharing a single perimeter. The figure below reports both quantities for whatever rectangle you build, so you can pin one of them and watch the other.

Hold the perimeter at 202020 by keeping the width and the height adding to 101010. Build 111 by 999, then 555 by 555, then 999 by 111. All three share the perimeter 202020, but their areas are 999, 252525, and 999: the two lopsided rectangles tie, and the square between them is the peak, exactly where x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​ says the vertex of A=x(10−x)A = x(10 - x)A=x(10−x) should be, no walking required. This is why an optimization problem has to say which quantity it is holding fixed before “the best rectangle” means anything at all.

Change the setup so the pen backs onto a wall, and only three sides need fencing. The wall gives one side for free, so the fence is split differently, but the method does not budge.

Worked example 3 Largest pen against a wall with 60 metres of fence

A rectangular garden is built against a long straight wall, so the wall forms one side and fencing is needed only for the other three. With 606060 metres of fence, what is the largest area?

Let xxx be the length of each side that runs out from the wall (there are two of these). The remaining fence forms the side parallel to the wall, so that side is 60−2x60 - 2x60−2x. The area is

A=x(60−2x)=60x−2x2.A = x(60 - 2x) = 60x - 2x^2.A=x(60−2x)=60x−2x2.

Here a=−2a = -2a=−2 and b=60b = 60b=60, and a<0a < 0a<0 signals a maximum. The optimizing input is

x=−b2a=−602(−2)=−60−4=15.x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{60}{2(-2)} = -\frac{60}{-4} = 15.x=−2ab​=−2(−2)60​=−−460​=15.

Each side out from the wall is 151515 metres, and the side parallel to the wall is 60−2(15)=3060 - 2(15) = 3060−2(15)=30 metres. The greatest area is

A=15(30)=450.A = 15(30) = 450.A=15(30)=450.

So the largest garden is 450450450 square metres, from a 151515 by 303030 rectangle. Notice the shape is no longer a square: freeing up one side with the wall changes the balance. In this setup the side parallel to the wall comes out twice as long as each of the other two.

The same product structure runs through pure number problems. Two numbers with a fixed sum have a product that is a quadratic. With the sum held fixed, that product is largest when the two numbers are equal, which is the number version of the square being the best rectangle.

Check your understanding

Two positive numbers add up to 161616. What is the greatest possible value of their product?

Answer choices

Name one number xxx; since the two add to 161616, the other is 16−x16 - x16−x. Their product is a quadratic.

P=x(16−x)=16x−x2P = x(16 - x) = 16x - x^2P=x(16−x)=16x−x2

With a=−1<0a = -1 < 0a=−1<0 the vertex is a maximum, at x=−162(−1)=8x = -\tfrac{16}{2(-1)} = 8x=−2(−1)16​=8. So both numbers are 888, and the greatest product is

P=8⋅8=64.P = 8 \cdot 8 = 64.P=8⋅8=64.

The answer is the product 646464, not the number 888 that achieves it. The two equal numbers are what a fixed sum wants for the biggest product.

Greatest revenue

Money problems create a quadratic whenever raising the price drives down the number sold. Revenue is price times quantity, and if the quantity falls off in a straight line as the price climbs, that product is again a quadratic with a peak.

Worked example 4 The ticket price that maximizes revenue

A theater sells 200200200 tickets per show at 101010 dollars each. A survey predicts that every 111 dollar increase in the ticket price will sell 101010 fewer tickets. What price brings in the greatest revenue?

Let xxx be the number of 111 dollar increases. Then the price is 10+x10 + x10+x dollars, and the number of tickets sold is 200−10x200 - 10x200−10x. Revenue is price times quantity:

R=(10+x)(200−10x).R = (10 + x)(200 - 10x).R=(10+x)(200−10x).

Expand so you can see the coefficients:

R=2000−100x+200x−10x2=−10x2+100x+2000.R = 2000 - 100x + 200x - 10x^2 = -10x^2 + 100x + 2000.R=2000−100x+200x−10x2=−10x2+100x+2000.

Here a=−10a = -10a=−10 and b=100b = 100b=100, and a<0a < 0a<0 means the vertex is a maximum. The optimizing number of increases is

x=−b2a=−1002(−10)=−100−20=5.x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{100}{2(-10)} = -\frac{100}{-20} = 5.x=−2ab​=−2(−10)100​=−−20100​=5.

Five increases means the best price is 10+5=1510 + 5 = 1510+5=15 dollars, at which the theater sells 200−10(5)=150200 - 10(5) = 150200−10(5)=150 tickets. The greatest revenue is the value at the vertex:

R=(15)(150)=2250.R = (15)(150) = 2250.R=(15)(150)=2250.

So a ticket price of 151515 dollars maximizes revenue, bringing in 225022502250 dollars. The question asked for the price, so 151515 dollars is the headline answer; the revenue of 225022502250 dollars is a separate figure. The raw vertex input x=5x = 5x=5 (the number of increases) is neither of those, just a step along the way.

A projectile’s highest point

The falling-object model from the applications chapter is a quadratic in time, so its highest point is another vertex problem: the leading coefficient is negative, the graph of height against time is a downward parabola, and its vertex is the top of the flight.

Worked example 5 Maximum height of a launched ball

A ball is launched upward from a 666 foot platform with an initial speed of 808080 feet per second. So its height in feet after ttt seconds is h=−16t2+80t+6h = -16t^2 + 80t + 6h=−16t2+80t+6. Find the maximum height and the time it occurs.

The coefficients are a=−16a = -16a=−16, b=80b = 80b=80, and c=6c = 6c=6. Because a<0a < 0a<0, the vertex is the highest point. The time at the peak is

t=−b2a=−802(−16)=−80−32=2.5.t = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{80}{2(-16)} = -\frac{80}{-32} = 2.5.t=−2ab​=−2(−16)80​=−−3280​=2.5.

The ball peaks at t=2.5t = 2.5t=2.5 seconds. That is the time, not the height. Substitute t=2.5t = 2.5t=2.5 back to get the maximum height:

h=−16(2.5)2+80(2.5)+6=−16(6.25)+200+6=−100+200+6=106.\begin{aligned} h &= -16(2.5)^2 + 80(2.5) + 6 \\ &= -16(6.25) + 200 + 6 = -100 + 200 + 6 = 106. \end{aligned}h​=−16(2.5)2+80(2.5)+6=−16(6.25)+200+6=−100+200+6=106.​

The maximum height is 106106106 feet, reached at t=2.5t = 2.5t=2.5 seconds. A question that asks “how high” wants 106106106 feet; a question that asks “when” wants 2.52.52.5 seconds. The model hands you both, one as the vertex input and one as the vertex value.

Every launch or throw near the surface of the Earth follows the same pattern, just with different starting numbers. In general,

h=−16t2+v0 t+h0(height in feet, time t in seconds),h = -16t^2 + v_0\,t + h_0 \qquad (\text{height in feet, time } t \text{ in seconds}),h=−16t2+v0​t+h0​(height in feet, time t in seconds),

with h0h_0h0​ the starting height and v0v_0v0​ the initial upward speed, exactly the 666 and the 808080 from the ball above. The input at the vertex is the time the object peaks; the value there is the maximum height. Keep those two apart exactly as before.

Least cost and other minimums

When the leading coefficient is positive the parabola opens upward, and the vertex becomes a lowest point rather than a highest one. Everything about the method is identical; only the sign of aaa has flipped, and with it the word “maximum” becomes “minimum”. Costs that fall and then rise are a natural home for these.

Worked example 6 The production level that minimizes cost

A workshop finds that its daily cost in dollars to make xxx units of a product is C=2x2−40x+500C = 2x^2 - 40x + 500C=2x2−40x+500. How many units should it make to minimize the cost, and what is that minimum cost?

The coefficient a=2a = 2a=2 is positive, so the parabola opens upward and the vertex is the lowest point, a minimum. The optimizing number of units is

x=−b2a=−−402(2)=404=10.x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-40}{2(2)} = \frac{40}{4} = 10.x=−2ab​=−2(2)−40​=440​=10.

Making 101010 units minimizes the cost. The minimum cost is the value there:

C=2(10)2−40(10)+500=200−400+500=300.C = 2(10)^2 - 40(10) + 500 = 200 - 400 + 500 = 300.C=2(10)2−40(10)+500=200−400+500=300.

So the workshop should make 101010 units, and the least possible daily cost is 300300300 dollars. The positive leading coefficient is the whole reason this is a minimum. The cost drops as production rises toward 101010 units, bottoms out there, and climbs again beyond it.

Everything in this lesson comes back to one image. Whatever the quantity, area, revenue, height, product, or cost, if it is a quadratic then its graph is a parabola. The answer you want is the peak or the valley of that parabola. Find the vertex, check the sign of aaa to know which one it is, and then read off precisely the input or the value the question asks for.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reporting the input when the question wants the value (or the reverse). The vertex has two coordinates. The input x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​ tells you where the best happens; the value found by substituting back tells you how good it is. Decide which the sentence asks for before you answer.
  • Stopping at x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​. That is only the first coordinate of the vertex. For a maximum height, a greatest area, or a least cost, you still have to substitute back to get the value.
  • Guessing maximum versus minimum. Do not assume every problem is a maximum. Read the sign of aaa: a<0a < 0a<0 opens down to a maximum, a>0a > 0a>0 opens up to a minimum, the same rule that made the workshop’s cost problem a minimum.
  • Fencing a wall problem like a four-sided one. When a wall supplies one side, the fixed fence covers only three sides, so the relationship is 2x+(opposite side)=fence2x + (\text{opposite side}) = \text{fence}2x+(opposite side)=fence, not the full perimeter. Writing all four sides double-counts the free wall.
  • Building the wrong product. Revenue is price times quantity, and area is one length times the other. Multiply the two quantities the problem actually ties together, and write the second in terms of the first before expanding.
  • Dropping units or the physical check. An optimization answer is a sentence with units, and it should make sense: a width is positive, a count is a whole number, a price is not negative. When the vertex itself is not an allowed value, use the nearest input that is instead of reporting the vertex anyway.

Key takeaways

  • To maximize or minimize a quadratic quantity, find the vertex of its parabola. The optimizing input is x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​; the optimal value is what you get by substituting that input back (or the kkk in vertex form y=a(x−h)2+ky = a(x - h)^2 + ky=a(x−h)2+k).
  • The sign of aaa decides which extreme it is: a<0a < 0a<0 opens downward, so the vertex is a maximum; a>0a > 0a>0 opens upward, so the vertex is a minimum.
  • Keep the input and the value apart. One answers where the best occurs, the other answers how good it is, and a question wants one specific piece.
  • Model in four steps: name the variable, write the quantity as a quadratic in it, then find the vertex. The fourth step is to answer exactly what is asked, with correct units and a sanity check.
  • Fixed perimeter: write one side as xxx and the other as (half the perimeter) minus xxx. That makes the area a downward parabola, largest when the rectangle is a square. Against a wall: with a fence of length FFF, area A=x(F−2x)A = x(F - 2x)A=x(F−2x). Revenue: R=(price)(quantity)R = (\text{price})(\text{quantity})R=(price)(quantity) with the quantity falling linearly as the price rises. Projectile: h=−16t2+v0t+h0h = -16t^2 + v_0 t + h_0h=−16t2+v0​t+h0​ peaks at its vertex.

A bit of history (optional)

Before there was a formula, how would you find the best of infinitely many possibilities? You cannot try them all. Different problems of that kind often needed different, ingenious arguments, each built from scratch.

Around 1636 a French lawyer named Pierre de Fermat wrote down a single method that could take the place of those separate arguments.

The idea begins with something you can see for yourself. At the summit of a hill the ground is nearly level, so two points a short step apart stand at nearly the same height. Fermat took that seriously. He wrote the quantity at an input, then wrote it again a tiny step away. He treated the two as equal, cleared away whatever cancelled, and let the step shrink to nothing. What survived was the position of the peak.

Run his method on y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c and watch. Comparing an input with its neighbour leaves 2ax+b=02ax + b = 02ax+b=0, once the step is divided out and then dropped. Solve for the input and you have x=−b2ax = -\tfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​.

That is the vertex, from a method that never mentions parabolas. Fermat’s reasoning reaches much further, which is why it is remembered. Turned on a quadratic, it hands back the recipe you used on every fence, ticket price and thrown ball above.

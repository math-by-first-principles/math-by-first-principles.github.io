The Equation of a Circle

Intuition

A straight line has an equation, and so does a parabola. A circle should have one too. What sets a circle apart is a single rule: every point on it sits the same distance from one middle point. A wheel, a clock face, and the ripple spreading from a stone dropped in a pond all trace the points that are one fixed distance from a center. To turn that rule into algebra you need just one tool: a way to measure the distance between two points on the grid. That tool is the Pythagorean theorem wearing a new coat. Once you can measure distance, the equation of a circle almost writes itself, and reading a center and a radius straight off an equation becomes routine.

Learning goals

  • Measure distance with d=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}d=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​
  • Define a circle as points a fixed distance from a center
  • Write standard form (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2
  • Read the center and radius carefully, minding the signs
  • Complete the square on each variable to recover standard form from general form
  • Recognize a point or no graph from the right side

Measuring distance in the plane

On a number line the distance between two numbers is the size of their difference: the points 222 and 999 are ∣9−2∣=7|9 - 2| = 7∣9−2∣=7 apart. In the plane a point needs two coordinates, so the two points no longer sit on one straight track, and a plain subtraction cannot reach across the gap. What saves you is that any two points are joined by a right triangle hiding in the grid.

Take (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) and (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​). Travel from the first to the second in two steps that meet at a right angle: slide straight across to the corner (x2,y1)(x_2, y_1)(x2​,y1​), then straight up to (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​). The across step spans the gap between the xxx-coordinates, and the up step spans the gap between the yyy-coordinates. Those two steps are the legs of a right triangle, and the direct path you actually want, the straight distance, is its hypotenuse. That is exactly the setup the Pythagorean theorem was built for.

Why the distance is (x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​#

Place the two points (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) and (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​) on the grid, and drop to the corner (x2,y1)(x_2, y_1)(x2​,y1​). That corner shares its height with the first point and its side-to-side position with the second. The horizontal leg runs from (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) to that corner, so its length is the gap between the xxx-coordinates, ∣x2−x1∣|x_2 - x_1|∣x2​−x1​∣. The vertical leg runs from the corner up to (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​), so its length is the gap between the yyy-coordinates, ∣y2−y1∣|y_2 - y_1|∣y2​−y1​∣. These legs meet at a right angle, and the distance ddd you want is the hypotenuse.

The Pythagorean theorem says the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the two legs:

d2=∣x2−x1∣2+∣y2−y1∣2.d^2 = |x_2 - x_1|^2 + |y_2 - y_1|^2.d2=∣x2​−x1​∣2+∣y2​−y1​∣2.

Squaring a number throws away its sign, so ∣x2−x1∣2|x_2 - x_1|^2∣x2​−x1​∣2 is simply (x2−x1)2(x_2 - x_1)^2(x2​−x1​)2, and the same holds for the vertical leg. The absolute-value bars are no longer doing anything:

d2=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2.d^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2.d2=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2.

A distance is never negative, so take the positive square root of both sides:

d=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2.d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}.d=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​.

Because each difference is squared, it makes no difference which point you list first. Swapping them flips the sign inside each set of parentheses, and squaring erases that flip, so the distance comes out the same either way.

∎

This result is the distance formula, and it is nothing more than the Pythagorean theorem applied to the legs you can read off the coordinates.

Worked example 1 Find the distance between (−1,2)(-1, 2)(−1,2) and (3,5)(3, 5)(3,5)

Label the points (x1,y1)=(−1,2)(x_1, y_1) = (-1, 2)(x1​,y1​)=(−1,2) and (x2,y2)=(3,5)(x_2, y_2) = (3, 5)(x2​,y2​)=(3,5), and substitute into the distance formula. The horizontal gap is 3−(−1)=43 - (-1) = 43−(−1)=4 and the vertical gap is 5−2=35 - 2 = 35−2=3:

d=(3−(−1))2+(5−2)2=42+32.d = \sqrt{(3 - (-1))^2 + (5 - 2)^2} = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2}.d=(3−(−1))2+(5−2)2​=42+32​.

Now square and add before taking the root, since 42+32=16+9=254^2 + 3^2 = 16 + 9 = 2542+32=16+9=25:

d=25=5.d = \sqrt{25} = 5.d=25​=5.

The two points are 555 units apart. It is the same 333-444-555 right triangle you would get by counting 444 across and 333 up between them.

What a circle is

Now that distance is under control, define the shape precisely. A circle is the set of all points in the plane that lie a fixed distance rrr from a fixed point. The fixed point is the center, usually written (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), and the fixed distance rrr (a positive number) is the radius. Every point on the circle is exactly rrr from the center, and every point that is exactly rrr from the center is on the circle. Nothing else qualifies.

That definition is stated entirely in terms of distance, which is why the distance formula is the key that unlocks the equation. The equation of a circle is just the sentence “this point is rrr away from the center” written in algebra.

Answer choices

A circle is every point at exactly the fixed distance rrr from the center, no closer and no farther. Points less than rrr from the center fill the disk inside the circle, not the circle itself. Fixing only the xxx-coordinate describes a vertical line, not a curve of constant distance, and a single point above the center is just one point on the circle, not the whole set.

The standard equation of a circle

Try a specific circle first: center (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1) and radius 555. A point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) is on this circle exactly when it is 555 units from (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1), so the distance formula gives (x−2)2+(y−1)2=5\sqrt{(x - 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2} = 5(x−2)2+(y−1)2​=5, and squaring both sides gives (x−2)2+(y−1)2=25(x - 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 25(x−2)2+(y−1)2=25. Every circle follows that same pattern. Replace the specific numbers 222, 111, and 555 with a general center and radius, and the pattern becomes a rule you can use for any circle.

Let (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) be a point anywhere on a circle with center (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) and radius rrr. Saying (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) is on the circle means its distance from the center equals rrr. Feed the center and the point into the distance formula and set the result equal to rrr, then clear the square root by squaring both sides.

Why a circle's equation is (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2#

A point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) lies on the circle exactly when its distance from the center (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) equals the radius rrr. Measure that distance with the formula just derived, treating (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) as one point and (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) as the other:

(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r.\sqrt{(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2} = r.(x−h)2+(y−k)2​=r.

This already describes the circle, but the square root is clumsy to work with. Remove it by squaring both sides. The left side is a distance and the right side is a positive radius, so both sides are nonnegative, and squaring a nonnegative number never flips the truth of an equation between nonnegative numbers. So squaring both sides here changes nothing about which points satisfy it:

(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2.(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.

Every step above reverses, so the statement runs in both directions. A point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) satisfies this equation if and only if it sits exactly rrr from (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), which is precisely what it means to be on the circle. That is why this one equation captures the entire curve and no other points.

∎

This is the standard form, also called the center-radius form, of a circle:

(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2.(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.

Its power is that the center and radius sit right on the surface. The numbers subtracted from xxx and yyy are the center coordinates, and the number on the right is the radius squared.

Circles centered at the origin

The simplest circles are centered at the origin, where h=0h = 0h=0 and k=0k = 0k=0. Substituting those into the standard form makes the subtractions vanish:

(x−0)2+(y−0)2=r2⟹x2+y2=r2.(x - 0)^2 + (y - 0)^2 = r^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 + y^2 = r^2.(x−0)2+(y−0)2=r2⟹x2+y2=r2.

So a circle centered at the origin with radius rrr has the tidy equation x2+y2=r2x^2 + y^2 = r^2x2+y2=r2. Here the right side is r2r^2r2, so the radius is the square root of it: x2+y2=9x^2 + y^2 = 9x2+y2=9 has radius 9=3\sqrt{9} = 39​=3, not 999. This circle meets each axis exactly rrr from the origin, at (r,0)(r, 0)(r,0), (−r,0)(-r, 0)(−r,0), (0,r)(0, r)(0,r), and (0,−r)(0, -r)(0,−r).

Reading the center and radius

The standard form works in both directions. Going forward, you build the equation from a center and a radius; going backward, you read a center and a radius out of a finished equation. Two small traps trip people up in the backward direction, and both come from taking the form literally.

The sign trap comes from the subtraction built into x−hx - hx−h and y−ky - ky−k. A circle written with (x+3)(x + 3)(x+3) does not have center coordinate 333. Rewrite x+3x + 3x+3 as x−(−3)x - (-3)x−(−3) to see that h=−3h = -3h=−3. The form always subtracts the center coordinate, so a plus sign inside the parentheses means that coordinate is negative.

The radius trap comes from the right side being r2r^2r2, not rrr. To get the radius you must take the square root of the number on the right. In (x−1)2+(y−2)2=16(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 16(x−1)2+(y−2)2=16 the radius is 16=4\sqrt{16} = 416​=4, and when the right side is not a perfect square, such as 202020, the radius stays as a simplified root like 20=25\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}20​=25​.

The general form

Multiply out a circle’s standard form and it stops looking like a circle. Expanding (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2 gives x2−2hx+h2+y2−2ky+k2=r2x^2 - 2hx + h^2 + y^2 - 2ky + k^2 = r^2x2−2hx+h2+y2−2ky+k2=r2, and moving everything to one side collects into

x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0,x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0,x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0,

where D=−2hD = -2hD=−2h, E=−2kE = -2kE=−2k, and F=h2+k2−r2F = h^2 + k^2 - r^2F=h2+k2−r2. This is the general form. This DDD is just the coefficient of xxx; it has nothing to do with a quadratic’s discriminant, which used the same letter for a different purpose. Written this way, the center and radius are buried and you cannot read them off directly, and it is not even obvious whether the equation describes a real circle at all: reversing the algebra, by completing the square, will answer both questions at once.

To dig them back out, reverse the expansion by completing the square on the xxx-terms and on the yyy-terms separately. That is the same move you learned in the completing-the-square lesson. Group the xxx-terms, group the yyy-terms, and turn each group into a perfect square. Once the equation is back in standard form, the center and radius reappear.

Worked example 5 Convert x2+y2−6x+8y−11=0x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 8y - 11 = 0x2+y2−6x+8y−11=0 to standard form

Group the xxx-terms and the yyy-terms, and move the lone constant to the right side:

(x2−6x)+(y2+8y)=11.(x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 8y) = 11.(x2−6x)+(y2+8y)=11.

Complete the square on each group separately. For x2−6xx^2 - 6xx2−6x, half of −6-6−6 is −3-3−3 and (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9. For y2+8yy^2 + 8yy2+8y, half of 888 is 444 and 42=164^2 = 1642=16. Add each of those constants to both sides so the equation stays balanced:

(x2−6x+9)+(y2+8y+16)=11+9+16.(x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 8y + 16) = 11 + 9 + 16.(x2−6x+9)+(y2+8y+16)=11+9+16.

Each group is now a perfect square, and the right side totals 363636:

(x−3)2+(y+4)2=36.(x - 3)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 36.(x−3)2+(y+4)2=36.

Read it off: the center is (3,−4)(3, -4)(3,−4), from x−3x - 3x−3 and y+4y + 4y+4, and the radius is 36=6\sqrt{36} = 636​=6. What looked like a scrambled general form was a clean circle all along.

When the circle collapses

Completing the square on a general form always lands you at (x−h)2+(y−k)2=R(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = R(x−h)2+(y−k)2=R for some number RRR on the right. Usually RRR is positive and you have an honest circle of radius R\sqrt{R}R​. But the right side is not guaranteed to be positive, and two special cases are worth recognizing.

If R=0R = 0R=0, the equation reads (x−h)2+(y−k)2=0(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = 0(x−h)2+(y−k)2=0. A sum of two squares can be zero only when each square is zero, which forces x=hx = hx=h and y=ky = ky=k. The “circle” has shrunk to the single point (h,k)(h, k)(h,k). For instance x2+y2−2x+4y+5=0x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 4y + 5 = 0x2+y2−2x+4y+5=0 becomes (x−1)2+(y+2)2=0(x - 1)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 0(x−1)2+(y+2)2=0, satisfied only by the point (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2).

If R<0R < 0R<0, the equation asks for a sum of two squares to be negative. Squares are never negative, so no real point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) can satisfy it, and the graph is empty: there is no curve at all. For instance x2+y2−2x+4y+8=0x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 4y + 8 = 0x2+y2−2x+4y+8=0 becomes (x−1)2+(y+2)2=−3(x - 1)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = -3(x−1)2+(y+2)2=−3, which no real point solves.

A true circle needs R>0R > 0R>0. Always check the sign of the right side after completing the square before you announce a radius.

Graphing a circle

Once you know the center and radius, a circle is quick to draw by hand. Plot the center, then step out the radius rrr in each of the four cardinal directions. That marks four points: right to (h+r,k)(h + r, k)(h+r,k), left to (h−r,k)(h - r, k)(h−r,k), up to (h,k+r)(h, k + r)(h,k+r), and down to (h,k−r)(h, k - r)(h,k−r). Those four anchors pin the circle in place, and a smooth round curve through them completes it.

Worked example 6 Graph (x−1)2+(y+1)2=9(x - 1)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 9(x−1)2+(y+1)2=9

First read the center and radius. The parentheses are x−1x - 1x−1 and y+1=y−(−1)y + 1 = y - (-1)y+1=y−(−1), so the center is (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1). The right side is 9=r29 = r^29=r2, so the radius is 9=3\sqrt{9} = 39​=3.

Plot the center at (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1), then step out 333 units in each direction to reach four points of the circle:

(1+3, −1)=(4,−1),(1−3, −1)=(−2,−1),(1, −1+3)=(1,2),(1, −1−3)=(1,−4).\begin{aligned} (1 + 3,\, -1) &= (4, -1), &\qquad (1 - 3,\, -1) &= (-2, -1), \\ (1,\, -1 + 3) &= (1, 2), &\qquad (1,\, -1 - 3) &= (1, -4). \end{aligned}(1+3,−1)(1,−1+3)​=(4,−1),=(1,2),​(1−3,−1)(1,−1−3)​=(−2,−1),=(1,−4).​

Sometimes you are handed the two endpoints of a diameter instead of the center. The center is the midpoint of that diameter, exactly halfway between the endpoints, so average their xxx-coordinates and average their yyy-coordinates to locate it. The radius is then the distance from the center to either endpoint. For instance, if a diameter has endpoints (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2) and (9,8)(9, 8)(9,8), the center is (1+92,2+82)=(5,5)\left(\dfrac{1 + 9}{2}, \dfrac{2 + 8}{2}\right) = (5, 5)(21+9​,22+8​)=(5,5), and the radius is the distance from (5,5)(5, 5)(5,5) to either endpoint, such as (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2).

Is a point inside, on, or outside a circle?

Every point in the plane sits in exactly one of three places relative to a circle: inside it, on it, or outside it. A point is inside the circle when it is closer to the center than the radius, on the circle when it is exactly the radius away, and outside the circle when it is farther than the radius. That is the fixed-distance definition of a circle again, just compared against a specific point instead of used to build the equation.

You do not need to compute an actual distance to decide. Squaring never changes the order of two nonnegative numbers, so comparing the point’s squared distance from the center with r2r^2r2 gives the same verdict as comparing the distance itself with rrr, without ever taking a square root:

  • Squared distance less than r2r^2r2: the point is inside.
  • Squared distance equal to r2r^2r2: the point is on the circle.
  • Squared distance greater than r2r^2r2: the point is outside.

Worked example 7 Is (6,8)(6, 8)(6,8) inside, on, or outside the circle x2+y2=100x^2 + y^2 = 100x2+y2=100?

The center is the origin and r2=100r^2 = 100r2=100. Find the squared distance from the origin to (6,8)(6, 8)(6,8) using the same expression that builds the left side of the circle’s equation:

62+82=36+64=100.6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100.62+82=36+64=100.

The squared distance equals r2r^2r2 exactly, so (6,8)(6, 8)(6,8) lies on the circle, with no square root needed anywhere in the comparison.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Flipping the sign of the center. The form is (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2, so it subtracts the center coordinate. A circle with (x+3)(x + 3)(x+3) has h=−3h = -3h=−3, not +3+3+3. Rewrite x+3x + 3x+3 as x−(−3)x - (-3)x−(−3) to keep the sign straight.
  • Using the right side as the radius. The right side is r2r^2r2. The radius is its square root, so (x−1)2+(y−2)2=16(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 16(x−1)2+(y−2)2=16 has radius 444, not 161616, and a right side of 202020 gives radius 20=25\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}20​=25​, not 202020.
  • Forgetting the square root in the distance formula. The formula ends in a square root: d=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}d=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​. Stopping at (x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2 gives d2d^2d2, not ddd.
  • Adding coordinates instead of squaring differences. Distance is not ∣x2−x1∣+∣y2−y1∣|x_2 - x_1| + |y_2 - y_1|∣x2​−x1​∣+∣y2​−y1​∣. You square each difference, add, then take the root, because the two gaps are the legs of a right triangle.
  • Completing the square on only one variable. The general form needs the square completed on the xxx-terms and on the yyy-terms, each with its own constant. Both constants must then be added to the right side.
  • Announcing a circle without checking the right side. After completing the square, if the right side is 000 the graph is a single point, and if it is negative there is no graph at all. Only a positive right side gives a real circle.
  • Ignoring a leading coefficient on x2x^2x2 and y2y^2y2. For an equation to be a circle, the x2x^2x2 and y2y^2y2 terms must have equal coefficients. If those coefficients are equal but not 111 (say both 222), first divide the whole equation by that common value so both become 111, then complete the square. If the coefficients are unequal, the equation is not a circle at all.

Key takeaways

  • The distance between (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) and (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​) is d=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}d=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​, which is the Pythagorean theorem on the horizontal and vertical gaps.
  • A circle is the set of points a fixed distance rrr (the radius) from a fixed center (h,k)(h, k)(h,k).
  • Writing “distance from (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) to (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) equals rrr” and squaring gives the standard form (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2; centered at the origin it becomes x2+y2=r2x^2 + y^2 = r^2x2+y2=r2.
  • Read it off carefully: the center is (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) where the form subtracts hhh and kkk (so x+3x + 3x+3 means h=−3h = -3h=−3). The radius is right side\sqrt{\text{right side}}right side​, not the right side itself.
  • The general form x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0x2+y2+Dx+Ey+F=0 becomes standard form by completing the square on the xxx-terms and the yyy-terms separately, then reading the center and radius.
  • After completing the square, a right side of 000 gives a single point and a negative right side gives no graph. A real circle needs a positive right side.
  • To graph a circle, plot the center and step out the radius right, left, up, and down to four anchor points, then draw a smooth curve through them.
  • To place a point relative to a circle, compare its squared distance from the center with r2r^2r2: less means inside, equal means on, greater means outside, with no square root needed.

A bit of history (optional)

On a spring morning in 1889, a delicate instrument in Germany traced a slow wobble onto its paper. Nothing had happened in the room. Nothing had happened in the town. About an hour earlier, the ground had shaken hard in Japan, half a world away.

That was the first time anyone matched a wobble on a machine to an earthquake so far off. The instrument had been built for another job, measuring the faint tilts of the ground beneath it. It could report that something enormous had happened somewhere, and not one thing more.

Direction is the hard part. Shaking spreads outward in two kinds of wave, one quicker than the other, and the gap between their arrivals widens with distance. So a single station can work out how far away the earthquake was, but never which way. Every point at that distance remains a candidate, and those points form a circle around the station.

One circle is not an answer. Two station circles often cross at a pair of candidate points, though two circles can also meet at just one point or not at all, and when a pair of candidates remains, a third station decides which of the two is the real one.

That is this lesson doing a day’s work. Each station contributes one equation, (x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2. Its own position on the map is the center (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), and the distance it measured is the radius rrr. The place the ground broke is the one point that satisfies all three equations at once.

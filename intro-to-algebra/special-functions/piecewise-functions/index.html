Piecewise Functions

Intuition

The rules that run everyday life rarely fit into a single formula. A parking garage might charge a flat 555 dollars for a stay of one hour or less, then 888 dollars for anything longer: one function, but a different formula depending on how long you stay. A function built that way, from several formulas each in charge of its own stretch of the domain, is a piecewise function. You already met three of them earlier in this chapter without the name: the absolute value, the floor, and the ceiling all switch rules at certain points. The last lesson’s hyperbola also broke into two separate branches, but for a different reason: one formula governed every allowed input, and the break was only a forbidden input falling out of the domain. A piecewise function splits on purpose, handing different inputs to different formulas by design. This lesson gives that pattern its name and settles the two skills that matter most, picking the right piece when you evaluate and drawing the graph with the correct open and closed dots.

Learning goals

  • Evaluate a piecewise function by choosing the piece whose condition holds
  • Graph each piece over its own interval with a closed or open dot at the boundary
  • Compare the two boundary heights to tell a jump from a join
  • Find the domain and range from each piece's restricted interval
  • Recognize absolute value, floor, and ceiling as piecewise functions from earlier lessons

What a piecewise function is

Take the parking garage from the introduction: 555 dollars for a stay of one hour or less, 888 dollars for anything longer. Call the charge C(h)C(h)C(h) for a stay of hhh hours. There are two rules, and each one owns its own share of the possible stays:

A piecewise function is defined by two or more formulas, called pieces, each paired with a condition that says which inputs it governs. The pieces are collected under one large brace, exactly as CCC is above. A larger piecewise function works the same way with more pieces. Here is one with three:

f(x)={x+3if x<−1,x2if −1≤x≤2,8−xif x>2.f(x) = \begin{cases} x + 3 & \text{if } x < -1, \\ x^2 & \text{if } -1 \le x \le 2, \\ 8 - x & \text{if } x > 2. \end{cases}f(x)=⎩⎨⎧​x+3x28−x​if x<−1,if −1≤x≤2,if x>2.​

Read the brace as a short list of instructions. Given an input xxx, run down the conditions on the right, find the single one that xxx satisfies, and apply the matching formula on the left. Nothing about the notation is mysterious; it is just a compact way to say “the value is whichever of these lines applies to your input.”

To evaluate a piecewise function at a number, always decide the condition first and compute second. Take x=3x = 3x=3. Scanning the conditions, 333 is not less than −1-1−1, and it does not lie between −1-1−1 and 222. But 333 is greater than 222, so the third piece owns it and f(3)=8−3=5f(3) = 8 - 3 = 5f(3)=8−3=5. Take x=0x = 0x=0 instead. It satisfies −1≤0≤2-1 \le 0 \le 2−1≤0≤2, the middle condition, so f(0)=02=0f(0) = 0^2 = 0f(0)=02=0. Choosing the formula before substituting is the entire skill. Plugging 000 into the first or third piece would produce a number the function never actually returns at 000.

The care is needed at a boundary, an input where one condition ends and the next begins. Look at x=−1x = -1x=−1. The first condition asks for x<−1x < -1x<−1, which −1-1−1 fails, and the second asks for −1≤x≤2-1 \le x \le 2−1≤x≤2, which −1-1−1 passes thanks to the “or equal to.” So the boundary x=−1x = -1x=−1 belongs to the middle piece alone, and f(−1)=(−1)2=1f(-1) = (-1)^2 = 1f(−1)=(−1)2=1. The inequality signs are not decoration. The strict <<< on one side and the ≤\le≤ on the other are exactly what hand the shared point to a single piece. Those two signs also keep the other piece from claiming that point.

That reasoning can be spelled out as a short formal argument about what makes a piecewise definition a genuine function. Read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Graphing a piecewise function

To graph a piecewise function, graph each piece on its own interval and nowhere else. For the running fff, you draw the line y=x+3y = x + 3y=x+3 only to the left of −1-1−1, and the parabola y=x2y = x^2y=x2 only from −1-1−1 to 222. The line y=8−xy = 8 - xy=8−x is drawn only to the right of 222. The one new question a piecewise graph raises is what to draw at the ends of each interval, and the inequality signs answer it.

At a boundary the piece that includes the endpoint (its condition uses ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥) genuinely reaches that point. Because that piece reaches the point, you mark its end with a closed dot, a filled circle. The piece that excludes the endpoint (its condition uses <<< or >>>) approaches the point but never arrives. Because that piece never arrives, you mark its end with an open dot, a hollow circle. A filled dot says “this exact point is on the graph.” A hollow dot says “this piece stops just short of here, so its own boundary input is decided by another condition instead.”

Watch how that plays out on a simple two-piece function:

g(x)={x+1if x≤1,4if x>1.g(x) = \begin{cases} x + 1 & \text{if } x \le 1, \\ 4 & \text{if } x > 1. \end{cases}g(x)={x+14​if x≤1,if x>1.​

The first piece is the line y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 drawn up to x=1x = 1x=1. Its condition includes the boundary (x≤1x \le 1x≤1), so it truly reaches the point (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2), drawn as a closed dot. The second piece is the constant y=4y = 4y=4 drawn for every input past 111. Its condition excludes the boundary (x>1x > 1x>1), so at x=1x = 1x=1 it stops just short with an open dot at (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4). Between those two dots the graph leaps straight from height 222 up to height 444 without passing through the values in between. That leap is a jump.

Whether a piecewise graph jumps or connects at a boundary comes down to one comparison, as long as both pieces actually settle on a definite height there: do the two pieces reach the same height? Plug the boundary into both formulas and see. For ggg, the left piece gives 1+1=21 + 1 = 21+1=2 while the right piece gives 444. The heights differ, so the graph cannot be drawn without lifting the pen, and a jump appears. When the two pieces land on the same height instead, the closed dot and the open dot fall on the very same point. That shared point fills the gap, and the graph connects into an unbroken curve. No limits or calculus are needed for this; you simply evaluate both pieces at the boundary and compare the two numbers, provided both pieces actually have a number to give there.

That last condition is not automatic. Every piece in this lesson is a line, a constant, or a polynomial, and each of those has a definite value at every input, so the plug-in test above always applies here. It would not work for every possible piece. The last lesson’s rational functions can fail to have a height at a boundary in two different ways: a hole, where the formula is simply undefined at that one input, or a vertical asymptote, where the height runs off without settling on any number as the input gets close. Either way there is no finite height to plug in, so the jump-or-connect comparison above does not apply, and deciding what happens there takes the separate tools the last lesson built for exactly that purpose.

Compare ggg with a function whose pieces agree at the boundary:

h(x)={2xif x<1,x+1if x≥1.h(x) = \begin{cases} 2x & \text{if } x < 1, \\ x + 1 & \text{if } x \ge 1. \end{cases}h(x)={2xx+1​if x<1,if x≥1.​

At x=1x = 1x=1 the first piece heads toward 2⋅1=22 \cdot 1 = 22⋅1=2 and the second piece sits at 1+1=21 + 1 = 21+1=2. Same height. The excluded point from the left piece and the included point from the right piece are both (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2). Because they are the same point, they merge into one filled dot and the graph has no gap. It still turns a sharp corner, because the slopes differ (222 on the left, 111 on the right), but a corner is not a jump. You can trace the whole graph without lifting the pen.

Domain and range

The domain of a piecewise function is the set of inputs it accepts, and it is the union of the intervals attached to the pieces. When the conditions partition the domain, as they should, those intervals fit together with no overlap and no gap. Because those intervals fit together that way, you can read the domain straight off the conditions. For ggg, the conditions x≤1x \le 1x≤1 and x>1x > 1x>1 together account for every real number, so the domain of ggg is all real numbers.

The range is the set of outputs, and you build it one piece at a time. Find the values each piece produces over its own interval, then take the union. A piece is only responsible for the part of its graph that actually gets drawn, so restrict each formula to its interval before reading off its outputs. For ggg, the line y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 on x≤1x \le 1x≤1 produces every height up to and including 222 (reached at the boundary x=1x = 1x=1). The constant piece contributes only the single height 444. The range of ggg is therefore y≤2y \le 2y≤2 together with y=4y = 4y=4, with everything strictly between 222 and 444 skipped over by the jump.

The special functions were piecewise all along

This lesson is a capstone for the chapter, because three functions you studied earlier in this chapter were piecewise the whole time. The clearest case is the absolute value. Its rule keeps a nonnegative input as is and flips the sign of a negative input, which is precisely a two-piece definition:

∣x∣={xif x≥0,−xif x<0.\lvert x \rvert = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } x \ge 0, \\ -x & \text{if } x < 0. \end{cases}∣x∣={x−x​if x≥0,if x<0.​

At the boundary x=0x = 0x=0 the top piece gives 000 and the bottom piece heads toward 000 as well, so the two pieces meet at the same height. That agreement is exactly why the V of the absolute value graph connects at its vertex instead of jumping. Same height at the boundary means no gap, just the sharp corner where the slopes switch from −1-1−1 to +1+1+1.

The floor and ceiling are piecewise too, only with infinitely many pieces. The floor holds the constant value nnn on each interval n≤x<n+1n \le x < n + 1n≤x<n+1. So writing one line per integer gives a piecewise definition with a separate constant piece for every whole-number step. Unlike the absolute value, neighboring floor pieces reach different heights at each integer boundary, one unit apart, so the floor jumps by one at every integer. That is why its graph is a staircase of separate steps rather than a connected curve. A piecewise function with constant pieces like this, whose graph is a row of flat segments, is called a step function. The floor and ceiling are the headline examples.

The ceiling has the same step shape, built from its own pieces. It holds the constant value nnn on the interval n−1<x≤nn - 1 < x \le nn−1<x≤n, the mirror image of the floor’s interval: instead of owning its left endpoint, each ceiling piece owns its right endpoint. That is why the ceiling’s steps land one row higher than the floor’s on most of each interval, and why the two functions agree only exactly at the integers.

Piecewise models in the real world

Piecewise functions are the natural language for any rule that changes at thresholds, which is most real pricing. Postage climbs in jumps as a package crosses weight limits. A parking garage bills one amount for the first hour and another after that. A phone plan charges a flat monthly fee up to some data cap, then a per-gigabyte rate beyond it. Income tax applies one rate to the first band of income and steeper rates to higher bands. In every case a single function describes the cost, and its formula depends on which band the input falls into.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Substituting before checking the condition. Always find which condition the input satisfies first, then use that piece. Plugging x=0x = 0x=0 into a piece meant for x>2x > 2x>2 produces a value the function never returns at 000.
  • Getting boundary ownership backward. At a boundary, the piece that owns the point is the one whose condition carries the “or equal to.” For x≤1x \le 1x≤1 against x>1x > 1x>1, the input x=1x = 1x=1 belongs to the first piece, not the second.
  • Overlapping or gapped conditions. Writing x≤1x \le 1x≤1 together with x≥1x \ge 1x≥1 claims x=1x = 1x=1 twice; if the two formulas disagree there, the rule offers two outputs, which is not a function. Writing x<1x < 1x<1 together with x>1x > 1x>1 leaves x=1x = 1x=1 with no piece at all, undefined there even though 111 was meant to be an input. The safest habit, and the one this lesson always uses, is conditions that partition the domain, one piece per input.
  • Open and closed dots reversed. The closed (filled) dot goes on the piece that includes the boundary (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥). The open (hollow) dot goes on the piece that excludes it (<<< or >>>). The filled dot always sits where the function genuinely equals that value.
  • Drawing a vertical segment across a jump. A jump is a gap, shown by an open dot and a closed dot at different heights, not a vertical line connecting them. The function never takes the in-between values.
  • Assuming a piecewise function always jumps. It jumps only when the two pieces reach different heights at the boundary. When they reach the same height, the graph connects; compare the two values, do not guess (as long as both pieces actually have a height there, see the note above about rational pieces).
  • Reading the range from the whole formulas. Restrict each piece to its own interval before collecting outputs. The line y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 has range all real numbers on its own, but on x≤1x \le 1x≤1 it only reaches y≤2y \le 2y≤2.

Key takeaways

  • A piecewise function is defined by several formulas (pieces), each with a condition on xxx, collected under a brace with {\begin{cases}\end{cases}{. A well-formed piecewise function’s conditions partition the domain, so exactly one piece owns each input.
  • To evaluate f(a)f(a)f(a), first decide which condition aaa satisfies, then apply that piece. At a boundary, the inequality signs (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥ versus <<< or >>>) decide which piece claims the shared point.
  • To graph, draw each piece over only its own interval. Put a closed dot where a piece includes the boundary and an open dot where it excludes it.
  • The graph jumps at a boundary when the two pieces reach different heights there, and connects when they reach the same height. Compare the two values; no limits are needed, just arithmetic, as long as both pieces actually reach a definite height there, true for every line, constant, and polynomial piece in this lesson. A rational piece can instead be undefined at the boundary (a hole) or run off without bound (an asymptote), and then this comparison does not apply.
  • The domain is the union of the pieces’ intervals. The range is the union of the outputs each piece produces once restricted to its interval, which can leave gaps.
  • The absolute value is piecewise (xxx for x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, −x-x−x for x<0x < 0x<0). The floor and ceiling are step functions, piecewise with a constant piece on every integer interval, jumping by one at each integer.

Why each input must belong to exactly one piece

Why each input must belong to exactly one piece#

A function is a rule that assigns to every input in its domain exactly one output. A piecewise definition can only be a genuine function when its conditions do two things. Those conditions must never assign one input to two conflicting pieces, and they must never leave an intended input with no piece at all.

Suppose some input aaa satisfied two conditions whose formulas disagree, giving one piece the value p(a)p(a)p(a) and another the value q(a)q(a)q(a) with p(a)≠q(a)p(a) \ne q(a)p(a)=q(a). Then the rule would offer two different outputs at the same input, and a rule that returns two values is not a function. So the conditions must never overlap in a way that hands a single input two different results. The safe design, and the one this lesson always uses, is to make the conditions mutually exclusive, so no input satisfies more than one; overlap only becomes an error when it hands the same input two disagreeing outputs.

Now suppose instead that some input we intend to feed in satisfied no condition at all. Then the rule would give it no output, and the function would simply be undefined there. So the conditions must also cover every input in the intended domain. Building a piecewise definition that avoids both problems, the way every example in this lesson does, means its conditions partition the domain: each input lands in one piece and only one.

This is the whole reason for the careful inequality signs at a boundary. Where two pieces meet at a value bbb, exactly one of them must claim bbb. Writing one condition as x≤bx \le bx≤b and the next as x>bx > bx>b hands bbb to the first piece and to no other. Writing them as x≤bx \le bx≤b and x≥bx \ge bx≥b would be an error, because then bbb satisfies both and the function risks two values. Writing them as x<bx < bx<b and x>bx > bx>b would be an error too, because then bbb satisfies neither and the function has a gap.

A bit of history (optional)

Pull a guitar string aside with one finger and hold it there. Look at the shape just before you let go. It is not a curve at all. It is two straight segments meeting at a sharp corner, one rule to the left of your finger and a different rule to the right.

In 1747 the French mathematician Jean d’Alembert wrote down an equation governing how such a string moves. That should have settled the matter. Instead it opened an argument that ran for fifty years, and the argument was about the shape your finger had just made.

Was a bent string even allowed as a starting position? For d’Alembert it was not. A function meant a single formula, and a plucked string is two formulas taped together at a crease. He wanted the starting shape produced by one rule, smoothly, with no corner in it anywhere. His opponents made the obvious objection. Anybody can pluck a string, so the mathematics had better cope.

The pluckers won, and the victory cost mathematics its old idea of what a function is. A rule was allowed to be assembled from pieces, each governing its own territory. The bent string is a piecewise function, drawn exactly as you have been drawing them. Its two pieces meet at the same height, so the graph connects rather than jumping. That is the test you applied at every boundary here.

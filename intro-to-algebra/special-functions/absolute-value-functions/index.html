Absolute Value Equations and Graphs

Intuition

You already know how to measure the gap between two numbers on the number line. Absolute value answers the simplest version of that question: how far is a number from zero? The symbol ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ reads as “the absolute value of xxx,” and it reports that distance, always as a plain nonnegative amount with the direction thrown away. A thermometer reading of −8-8−8 degrees and one of 888 degrees are both eight degrees from zero. Absolute value is what turns each of them into the same 888. That one idea, distance from zero, explains everything that follows: the sharp V of the graph, where the graph’s corner lands after a shift, and why an equation like ∣2x−3∣=7\lvert 2x - 3 \rvert = 7∣2x−3∣=7 can have two answers, one answer, or none at all.

Learning goals

  • Read ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ as distance, never negative
  • Graph the V, meeting at the vertex
  • Locate the vertex (h,k)(h, k)(h,k) in y=a∣x−h∣+ky = a\lvert x - h \rvert + ky=a∣x−h∣+k

  • Turn ∣X∣<c\lvert X \rvert < c∣X∣<c into a band and >c> c>c into two rays

What absolute value measures

On the number line, ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ is the distance from xxx to 000, counted as a positive length no matter which side of zero the number sits on. Since 555 is five units to the right of zero, ∣5∣=5\lvert 5 \rvert = 5∣5∣=5. Since −5-5−5 is five units to the left, ∣−5∣=5\lvert -5 \rvert = 5∣−5∣=5 as well. And 000 is no distance from itself, so ∣0∣=0\lvert 0 \rvert = 0∣0∣=0.

Distance is a geometric idea, but you need an algebraic rule to compute with. For a number that is already zero or positive, its distance from zero is just the number itself. For a negative number, its distance is the number with the sign stripped off, which you get by negating it. Two different rules for two different kinds of input, packaged as one function: that is a piecewise definition, a pattern the last lesson of this chapter names and studies on its own, and here it is

∣x∣={xif x≥0,−xif x<0.\lvert x \rvert = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } x \ge 0, \\ -x & \text{if } x < 0. \end{cases}∣x∣={x−x​if x≥0,if x<0.​

The second line trips people up, because −x-x−x looks negative. It is not. When xxx is negative, −x-x−x is the opposite of a negative number, which is positive. For example, with x=−5x = -5x=−5 the rule gives ∣−5∣=−(−5)=5\lvert -5 \rvert = -(-5) = 5∣−5∣=−(−5)=5, exactly the distance you expect.

Both branches of the rule always output zero or a positive number, so

∣x∣≥0for every real x,\lvert x \rvert \ge 0 \quad \text{for every real } x,∣x∣≥0for every real x,

with equality only at x=0x = 0x=0. This fact matters twice: it is why the graph never dips below the x-axis, and why some of the equations later in this lesson turn out to have no solution at all.

The graph of the absolute value function

Packaging the rule as a function gives the absolute value function

f(x)=∣x∣.f(x) = \lvert x \rvert.f(x)=∣x∣.

To see its shape, make a short table, choosing inputs on both sides of zero:

xxx−3-3−3−1-1−1000222444
y=∣x∣y = \lvert x \rverty=∣x∣333111000222444

Plot these and a pattern jumps out. To the right of zero every output equals the input, so those points lie on the line y=xy = xy=x. To the left of zero every output is the input negated, so those points lie on the line y=−xy = -xy=−x. The graph is therefore two straight rays, the right half of y=xy = xy=x and the left half of y=−xy = -xy=−x, joined where they meet at the origin. Two lines with slopes +1+1+1 and −1-1−1 meeting at a point make a sharp corner, and that is the V shape the absolute value function is known for.

The corner, called the vertex, sits at (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0). The vertex is the lowest point of the graph because every other output is a positive distance and so lies above it. The function accepts every real number, since every number has a distance from zero, so the domain is all real numbers. The outputs are exactly the nonnegative numbers. Each positive height is reached by two inputs, one on each branch, and the single lowest point is reached only at the vertex, so the range is y≥0y \ge 0y≥0.

Reading transformations off the equation

Every absolute value graph in this course is a shifted, stretched copy of the basic V. Look at one concrete shift before naming the general pattern. Replace xxx by x−2x - 2x−2 and add 111 outside the bars, turning y=∣x∣y = \lvert x \rverty=∣x∣ into y=∣x−2∣+1y = \lvert x - 2 \rvert + 1y=∣x−2∣+1:

The dashed V is the original, with vertex (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0). The solid V is the same shape, just relocated: the bars ∣x−2∣\lvert x - 2 \rvert∣x−2∣ are smallest, equal to zero, exactly when x=2x = 2x=2, and there the +1+1+1 makes the output 111. So the new vertex sits at (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1), two units right and one unit up from where it started. The branches keep their shape; only the corner moved.

That pattern generalizes to any shift and any stretch. The general form is

y=a∣x−h∣+k,a≠0.y = a\lvert x - h \rvert + k, \qquad a \ne 0.y=a∣x−h∣+k,a=0.

The coefficient aaa must be nonzero: at a=0a = 0a=0 the bars vanish from the output entirely and the V flattens into the horizontal line y=ky = ky=k, which is not a V at all. Read the three constants one at a time:

  • The x−hx - hx−h inside the bars shifts the graph right by hhh, carrying the vertex sideways, exactly as the x−2x - 2x−2 above carried it right by 222.
  • The +k+k+k outside shifts the graph up by kkk, carrying the vertex vertically, exactly as the +1+1+1 above carried it up by 111.
  • The aaa in front scales the branches. Its size ∣a∣\lvert a \rvert∣a∣ makes the V narrower when ∣a∣>1\lvert a \rvert > 1∣a∣>1 and wider when ∣a∣<1\lvert a \rvert < 1∣a∣<1, since the branch slopes become +a+a+a and −a-a−a. When aaa is negative it also flips the graph across the horizontal line through the vertex, so the V opens downward instead of upward.

Putting the shifts together, the vertex moves from (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) to (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), exactly as it just moved to (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1) above. The domain stays all real numbers. The range is y≥ky \ge ky≥k when a>0a > 0a>0 (the V opens up from its lowest point) or y≤ky \le ky≤k when a<0a < 0a<0 (it opens down from its highest point).

Match y=∣x−2∣+1y = \lvert x - 2 \rvert + 1y=∣x−2∣+1 to the form y=a∣x−h∣+ky = a\lvert x - h \rvert + ky=a∣x−h∣+k. Here a=1a = 1a=1, h=2h = 2h=2, and k=1k = 1k=1, so nothing is stretched or flipped, and the V is only shifted.

The inside x−2x - 2x−2 moves the graph right by 222 and the +1+1+1 moves it up by 111, so the vertex travels from (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) to

(h,k)=(2,1).(h, k) = (2, 1).(h,k)=(2,1).

From the vertex the branches keep the ordinary slopes +1+1+1 and −1-1−1. To pin down one more point, step right by 333: at x=5x = 5x=5, the height is ∣5−2∣+1=3+1=4\lvert 5 - 2 \rvert + 1 = 3 + 1 = 4∣5−2∣+1=3+1=4, giving the point (5,4)(5, 4)(5,4). The domain is all real numbers, and since a=1>0a = 1 > 0a=1>0 the graph opens upward from its lowest point, so the range is y≥1y \ge 1y≥1.

Worked example 2 Describe the graph of y=−2∣x∣y = -2\lvert x \rverty=−2∣x∣

Read y=−2∣x∣y = -2\lvert x \rverty=−2∣x∣ as y=a∣x∣y = a\lvert x \rverty=a∣x∣ with a=−2a = -2a=−2, one coefficient doing two jobs at once.

Its size, ∣a∣=2\lvert a \rvert = 2∣a∣=2, is a vertical stretch, so each branch is twice as steep as the basic V. Its sign, negative, reflects the graph across the x-axis, turning the V upside down so it opens downward. Tracking the anchor points of y=∣x∣y = \lvert x \rverty=∣x∣ through the rule makes both effects concrete:

(1,1)⟶(1,−2),(−1,1)⟶(−1,−2).(1, 1) \longrightarrow (1, -2), \qquad (-1, 1) \longrightarrow (-1, -2).(1,1)⟶(1,−2),(−1,1)⟶(−1,−2).

The vertex (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) stays fixed, since −2×0=0-2 \times 0 = 0−2×0=0. The domain is unchanged at all real numbers, because the input is untouched. But the outputs are now zero or negative, so the range flips to y≤0y \le 0y≤0: the V hangs below the x-axis instead of rising above it.

Here is the same V with the three constants on controls.

First, rebuild the checkpoint: step the inside shift to −3-3−3 and the outside shift to −5-5−5. Watch the vertex land at (−3,−5)(-3, -5)(−3,−5) while the expression above it reads x+3x + 3x+3: that sign flip, between the shift you set and the expression you see, is easy to miss until you try it yourself.

Then, reset the inside shift to 000 and the coefficient to 111, and set the outside shift to −4-4−4. Watching the x-axis, the V crosses it at two points, x=−4x = -4x=−4 and x=4x = 4x=4. Raise the outside shift one step at a time: the two crossings slide together, meeting at the vertex exactly when k=0k = 0k=0. Raise it further and the V clears the axis, meeting it nowhere. Two crossings, one crossing, or none: that is the whole pattern, and it is why the next section has exactly three cases.

Finally, set the outside shift back to −2-2−2 and switch the coefficient to −1-1−1. With k=−2k = -2k=−2 the vertex sits below the axis, but the V now opens downward, so its highest point is below the axis too and it never touches the axis at all. Raise the outside shift to k=2k = 2k=2: the downward-opening V’s peak rises above the axis, and now it crosses the axis at two points instead. The same three outcomes, two crossings, one, or none, still occur; only which side of the axis produces each one has swapped.

Solving absolute value equations

An absolute value equation has the variable inside the bars, as in ∣2x−3∣=7\lvert 2x - 3 \rvert = 7∣2x−3∣=7. To solve one, read the bars as distance again. The equation ∣X∣=c\lvert X \rvert = c∣X∣=c says the quantity XXX sits exactly ccc units from zero. How many numbers are that far from zero depends entirely on the sign of ccc. So the first thing to check is whether the right side is positive, zero, or negative.

Why ∣X∣=c\lvert X \rvert = c∣X∣=c splits into cases#

Everything follows from the meaning of distance, together with the fact that an absolute value is never negative.

Suppose c>0c > 0c>0. Take c=7c = 7c=7 as a concrete case first: the only numbers seven units from zero are 777 and −7-7−7, one on each side.

The same reasoning holds for any positive ccc: exactly two numbers sit that far from zero, ccc itself and its opposite −c-c−c, so

X=corX=−c.X = c \quad \text{or} \quad X = -c.X=corX=−c.

Suppose c=0c = 0c=0. The only number at distance zero from zero is zero itself, so ∣X∣=0\lvert X \rvert = 0∣X∣=0 forces X=0X = 0X=0, a single solution.

Suppose c<0c < 0c<0. Since ∣X∣≥0\lvert X \rvert \ge 0∣X∣≥0 for every XXX, a nonnegative quantity can never equal a negative number, so ∣X∣=c\lvert X \rvert = c∣X∣=c has no solution at all. This is why you check the sign of the right side before splitting into cases: a negative right side means stop, there is nothing to find.

∎

Worked example 3 Solve ∣2x−3∣=7\lvert 2x - 3 \rvert = 7∣2x−3∣=7

The right side 777 is positive, so the two-case rule applies. Set the inside equal to 777 and to −7-7−7:

2x−3=7or2x−3=−7.2x - 3 = 7 \quad \text{or} \quad 2x - 3 = -7.2x−3=7or2x−3=−7.

Solve each as an ordinary linear equation. The first gives 2x=102x = 102x=10, so x=5x = 5x=5. The second gives 2x=−42x = -42x=−4, so x=−2x = -2x=−2.

x=5orx=−2.x = 5 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -2.x=5orx=−2.

Both check: ∣2(5)−3∣=∣7∣=7\lvert 2(5) - 3 \rvert = \lvert 7 \rvert = 7∣2(5)−3∣=∣7∣=7 and ∣2(−2)−3∣=∣−7∣=7\lvert 2(-2) - 3 \rvert = \lvert -7 \rvert = 7∣2(−2)−3∣=∣−7∣=7.

Worked example 4 Solve 2∣x+1∣−3=52\lvert x + 1 \rvert - 3 = 52∣x+1∣−3=5

The bars are not alone yet, so isolate them before splitting into cases. Add 333 to both sides, then divide by 222:

2∣x+1∣=8⟹∣x+1∣=4.2\lvert x + 1 \rvert = 8 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \lvert x + 1 \rvert = 4.2∣x+1∣=8⟹∣x+1∣=4.

Now the right side is the positive number 444, so use the two-case rule on the inside:

x+1=4orx+1=−4,x + 1 = 4 \quad \text{or} \quad x + 1 = -4,x+1=4orx+1=−4,

giving x=3x = 3x=3 or x=−5x = -5x=−5. Splitting before isolating is the classic error: had you written x+1=5x + 1 = 5x+1=5 and x+1=−5x + 1 = -5x+1=−5 straight from the original equation, you would have solved the wrong equation. You would have gone wrong because the 222 and the −3-3−3 were still attached to the bars.

Worked example 5 Solve ∣3x−6∣=0\lvert 3x - 6 \rvert = 0∣3x−6∣=0 and ∣x+4∣=−2\lvert x + 4 \rvert = -2∣x+4∣=−2

These two short equations show the other two cases. First, solve ∣3x−6∣=0\lvert 3x - 6 \rvert = 0∣3x−6∣=0. Only zero has absolute value zero, so the inside must be zero:

3x−6=0⟹x=2,3x - 6 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = 2,3x−6=0⟹x=2,

a single solution. Next, solve ∣x+4∣=−2\lvert x + 4 \rvert = -2∣x+4∣=−2. The right side is negative, and an absolute value is never negative, so no number can satisfy it:

∣x+4∣≥0>−2⟹no solution.\lvert x + 4 \rvert \ge 0 > -2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{no solution}.∣x+4∣≥0>−2⟹no solution.

Reaching for the two-case rule here would have manufactured the false answers x=−6x = -6x=−6 and x=−2x = -2x=−2. Checking the sign of the right side first is what saves you from reporting them.

Absolute value inequalities

The distance picture pays off one more time, now for inequalities. Reading ∣X∣\lvert X \rvert∣X∣ as the distance from XXX to zero turns each inequality into a statement about how near or far XXX is. The two directions behave very differently.

Take ∣X∣<c\lvert X \rvert < c∣X∣<c with c>0c > 0c>0. It says XXX is less than ccc units from zero, so XXX is trapped in the band between −c-c−c and ccc. With c=5c = 5c=5, that band looks like this:

In general,

∣X∣<c⟺−c<X<c.\lvert X \rvert < c \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad -c < X < c.∣X∣<c⟺−c<X<c.

Now take ∣X∣>c\lvert X \rvert > c∣X∣>c with c>0c > 0c>0. It says XXX is more than ccc units from zero, so XXX lies beyond ccc on the right or beyond −c-c−c on the left, with nothing in between. With c=5c = 5c=5 again, the picture looks completely different from the band above:

In general,

∣X∣>c⟺X<−c  or  X>c.\lvert X \rvert > c \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad X < -c \ \text{ or } \ X > c.∣X∣>c⟺X<−c  or  X>c.

The reminders some people memorize, “less than gives one band” and “greater than gives two rays,” are just these two distance pictures in words. The non-strict versions with ≤\le≤ and ≥\ge≥ work the same way, now including the endpoints.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Thinking an absolute value can be negative. The output ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ is a distance, so it is always zero or positive. An equation like ∣x+4∣=−2\lvert x + 4 \rvert = -2∣x+4∣=−2 has no solution, and you should see that from the sign of the right side, before doing any algebra.
  • Splitting before isolating. For 2∣x+1∣−3=52\lvert x + 1 \rvert - 3 = 52∣x+1∣−3=5, get the bars alone first (∣x+1∣=4\lvert x + 1 \rvert = 4∣x+1∣=4), then split into two cases. Writing x+1=5x + 1 = 5x+1=5 or x+1=−5x + 1 = -5x+1=−5 straight from the original solves the wrong equation.
  • Forgetting the negative case. A positive right side always gives two equations, X=cX = cX=c and X=−cX = -cX=−c. Reporting only X=cX = cX=c loses half the answers.
  • Confusing the two inequality shapes, or squeezing the answer into one chain. A “less than” absolute value gives one band, −c<X<c-c < X < c−c<X<c; a “greater than” absolute value gives two separate rays, X<−cX < -cX<−c or X>cX > cX>c. For ∣x+3∣≥4\lvert x + 3 \rvert \ge 4∣x+3∣≥4, the answer is x≤−7x \le -7x≤−7 or x≥1x \ge 1x≥1; writing it as one chain like −7≥x≥1-7 \ge x \ge 1−7≥x≥1 is wrong, since that would demand xxx be at most −7-7−7 and at least 111 at the same time, which no number can do.

  • In y=a∣x−h∣+ky = a\lvert x - h \rvert + ky=a∣x−h∣+k (with a≠0a \ne 0a=0), the vertex is (h,k)(h, k)(h,k); ∣a∣\lvert a \rvert∣a∣ sets the width, and a<0a < 0a<0 flips the V to open downward.

A bit of history (optional)

Pick any point on a smooth curve. The curve has a direction there, the way a road has a heading. A corner is the exception, a place where the direction changes without warning. The V in this lesson has exactly one, at its vertex.

For most of the nineteenth century, corners were treated as rare accidents. Any curve you could draw without lifting the pencil was expected to be smooth almost everywhere. Nobody had proved that. It simply looked obvious.

Then, in Berlin in 1872, the German mathematician Karl Weierstrass showed a curve with no direction anywhere. You can trace the whole of it without lifting the pencil. Yet not one point on it has a heading, and it is worse than a corner, which at least has a slope on each side. Magnify any stretch and the roughness never settles down. Zoom in as far as you like and there is only more roughness. Later mathematicians called such curves monsters, and some of them refused to look.

Weierstrass is also the man who first fenced a number between two vertical bars, in 1841, giving absolute value the notation you have used all lesson. Both moves came from one instinct. He distrusted what a picture seemed to promise, and asked what the words underneath really said.

The V is the gentlest version of his monster. It is unbroken, and it has a direction everywhere except at a single point, where the slope jumps from −1-1−1 to +1+1+1. That point is the vertex (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), and the whole lesson is built around it.

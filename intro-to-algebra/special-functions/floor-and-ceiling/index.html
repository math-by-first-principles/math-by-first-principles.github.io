Floor and Ceiling Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra I. You can skip it.

Intuition

Fifty-three students sign up for a field trip, and each van seats 121212. Four vans hold 484848 of them, so a fifth van is needed for the last 555, even though 53÷12=4.41…53 \div 12 = 4.41\ldots53÷12=4.41… is nowhere near 555. Every leftover student still needs a seat, so you round up: ⌈53/12⌉=5\lceil 53/12 \rceil = 5⌈53/12⌉=5 vans. If instead you only care how many vans you can fill completely, you round down: ⌊53/12⌋=4\lfloor 53/12 \rfloor = 4⌊53/12⌋=4. Those two roundings are the floor and the ceiling, two exact functions built for exactly this kind of question. The floor ⌊x⌋\lfloor x \rfloor⌊x⌋ is the nearest integer at or to the left of xxx on the number line, and the ceiling ⌈x⌉\lceil x \rceil⌈x⌉ is the nearest integer at or to the right. That single picture answers every question in this lesson: what happens when xxx is already an integer, the one place people always slip (negative numbers), why the graphs turn out to be staircases, and exactly when to use a floor and when a ceiling.

Learning goals

  • Take the floor as the greatest integer at or below xxx
  • Round down on negatives, not toward zero
  • Draw the staircase with the right open and closed ends
  • Define the fractional part as x−⌊x⌋x - \lfloor x \rfloorx−⌊x⌋
  • Choose ceiling for containers and floor for full groups

What the floor and ceiling are

The floor of xxx, written ⌊x⌋\lfloor x \rfloor⌊x⌋, is the greatest integer that is less than or equal to xxx. The ceiling of xxx, written ⌈x⌉\lceil x \rceil⌈x⌉, is the least integer that is greater than or equal to xxx. The bent brackets are a memory aid: the floor’s brackets point down toward the ground, the ceiling’s point up.

Try a few. Since 3.73.73.7 sits between 333 and 444, the integers at or below it are 3,2,1,…3, 2, 1, \dots3,2,1,…, and the greatest is 333, so ⌊3.7⌋=3\lfloor 3.7 \rfloor = 3⌊3.7⌋=3. The integers at or above 3.73.73.7 are 4,5,6,…4, 5, 6, \dots4,5,6,…, and the least is 444, so ⌈3.7⌉=4\lceil 3.7 \rceil = 4⌈3.7⌉=4. When the input is already an integer, like 555, it is itself the greatest integer at or below and the least integer at or above, so ⌊5⌋=⌈5⌉=5\lfloor 5 \rfloor = \lceil 5 \rceil = 5⌊5⌋=⌈5⌉=5.

Every one of those computations used the same test: find the integer that fits snugly against xxx from below (for the floor) or from above (for the ceiling). That test is worth writing as an inequality, because it is the engine behind everything else.

Why ⌊x⌋≤x<⌊x⌋+1\lfloor x \rfloor \le x < \lfloor x \rfloor + 1⌊x⌋≤x<⌊x⌋+1#

Name the floor n=⌊x⌋n = \lfloor x \rfloorn=⌊x⌋. Two facts come straight from the definition. First, nnn is an integer with n≤xn \le xn≤x, because the floor was chosen from among the integers that are at most xxx. Second, the next integer up, n+1n + 1n+1, is bigger than nnn, so it cannot also be one of the integers at most xxx. If n+1n + 1n+1 were one of those integers, then nnn would not have been the greatest integer that is at most xxx. So n+1>xn + 1 > xn+1>x, which is the same as x<n+1x < n + 1x<n+1. Putting the two facts together,

n≤x<n+1.n \le x < n + 1.n≤x<n+1.

∎

Only one integer can do this. Consecutive integers are always a whole unit apart, so no two of them can both sit at or below xxx while staying within one unit of it. That is what makes the inequality a computation tool: find any integer kkk with k≤x<k+1k \le x < k + 1k≤x<k+1, and it has to be ⌊x⌋\lfloor x \rfloor⌊x⌋.

The same reasoning, applied to the least integer at or above xxx, gives the mirror statement for the ceiling:

⌈x⌉−1<x≤⌈x⌉.\lceil x \rceil - 1 < x \le \lceil x \rceil.⌈x⌉−1<x≤⌈x⌉.

These two inequalities also settle what happens at the boundary between the two functions. When xxx is an integer, it is both the greatest integer at or below and the least integer at or above itself, so

⌊x⌋=⌈x⌉=x(x an integer).\lfloor x \rfloor = \lceil x \rceil = x \qquad (x \text{ an integer}).⌊x⌋=⌈x⌉=x(x an integer).

When xxx is not an integer, the left inequality n≤xn \le xn≤x becomes strict, n<x<n+1n < x < n + 1n<x<n+1, so xxx lands strictly between the consecutive integers nnn and n+1n + 1n+1. The least integer at or above xxx is then n+1n + 1n+1, which gives a clean relationship between the two:

⌈x⌉=⌊x⌋+1(x not an integer).\lceil x \rceil = \lfloor x \rfloor + 1 \qquad (x \text{ not an integer}).⌈x⌉=⌊x⌋+1(x not an integer).

So the ceiling and floor are equal exactly on the integers, and exactly one apart everywhere else.

Worked example 1 Evaluate six floors and ceilings

Read each one off the number line by finding the integer that fits against the input.

For ⌊6.2⌋\lfloor 6.2 \rfloor⌊6.2⌋, the input sits between 666 and 777, and the greatest integer at or below is 666, so ⌊6.2⌋=6\lfloor 6.2 \rfloor = 6⌊6.2⌋=6. For ⌈6.2⌉\lceil 6.2 \rceil⌈6.2⌉, the least integer at or above is 777, so ⌈6.2⌉=7\lceil 6.2 \rceil = 7⌈6.2⌉=7.

For ⌊9⌋\lfloor 9 \rfloor⌊9⌋, the input is already an integer, so ⌊9⌋=⌈9⌉=9\lfloor 9 \rfloor = \lceil 9 \rceil = 9⌊9⌋=⌈9⌉=9.

For ⌈0.1⌉\lceil 0.1 \rceil⌈0.1⌉, the input is just above 000, so the least integer at or above is 111, giving ⌈0.1⌉=1\lceil 0.1 \rceil = 1⌈0.1⌉=1, while ⌊0.1⌋=0\lfloor 0.1 \rfloor = 0⌊0.1⌋=0.

⌊6.2⌋=6,⌈6.2⌉=7,⌊9⌋=9,⌈0.1⌉=1.\lfloor 6.2 \rfloor = 6, \quad \lceil 6.2 \rceil = 7, \quad \lfloor 9 \rfloor = 9, \quad \lceil 0.1 \rceil = 1.⌊6.2⌋=6,⌈6.2⌉=7,⌊9⌋=9,⌈0.1⌉=1.

Every answer is an integer, which is always true: the floor and ceiling only ever return integers, negative ones included.

Rounding down is not the same as chopping off the decimal

Everything above is easy to misread once negative numbers appear, and this is the single most common mistake with these functions. “Round down” means move to the left on the number line, toward smaller values. It does not mean “delete the part after the decimal point.”

Take x=−2.3x = -2.3x=−2.3. It sits between −3-3−3 and −2-2−2. The integers at or below −2.3-2.3−2.3 are −3,−4,−5,…-3, -4, -5, \dots−3,−4,−5,…, and the greatest of them is −3-3−3, so

⌊−2.3⌋=−3.\lfloor -2.3 \rfloor = -3.⌊−2.3⌋=−3.

The tempting answer −2-2−2 is wrong, because −2-2−2 is greater than −2.3-2.3−2.3, so −2-2−2 is not “at or below” −2.3-2.3−2.3 at all. Chopping the decimal off −2.3-2.3−2.3 would give −2-2−2, but that is rounding toward zero, not rounding down. The floor always rounds toward negative infinity. The ceiling goes the other way: the least integer at or above −2.3-2.3−2.3 is −2-2−2, so ⌈−2.3⌉=−2\lceil -2.3 \rceil = -2⌈−2.3⌉=−2. On the picture below, the floor is simply the tick to the left and the ceiling is the tick to the right.

Worked example 2 Evaluate floors and ceilings of negative inputs

Handle each one by asking which integer sits to the left (for the floor) and which sits to the right (for the ceiling).

The value −0.5-0.5−0.5 lies between −1-1−1 and 000. The greatest integer at or below is −1-1−1 and the least integer at or above is 000, so ⌊−0.5⌋=−1\lfloor -0.5 \rfloor = -1⌊−0.5⌋=−1 and ⌈−0.5⌉=0\lceil -0.5 \rceil = 0⌈−0.5⌉=0.

The value −7.2-7.2−7.2 lies between −8-8−8 and −7-7−7. Rounding down goes to −8-8−8 and rounding up goes to −7-7−7:

⌊−7.2⌋=−8,⌈−7.2⌉=−7.\lfloor -7.2 \rfloor = -8, \qquad \lceil -7.2 \rceil = -7.⌊−7.2⌋=−8,⌈−7.2⌉=−7.

For the integer input −4-4−4, both functions return it unchanged, ⌊−4⌋=⌈−4⌉=−4\lfloor -4 \rfloor = \lceil -4 \rceil = -4⌊−4⌋=⌈−4⌉=−4. Notice the pattern for the non-integers: the floor and the ceiling of −0.5-0.5−0.5 and of −7.2-7.2−7.2 are always one apart, exactly as ⌈x⌉=⌊x⌋+1\lceil x \rceil = \lfloor x \rfloor + 1⌈x⌉=⌊x⌋+1 predicted.

The graphs are staircases

To graph y=⌊x⌋y = \lfloor x \rfloory=⌊x⌋, group the inputs by the value they produce. The defining inequality says ⌊x⌋=n\lfloor x \rfloor = n⌊x⌋=n exactly when n≤x<n+1n \le x < n + 1n≤x<n+1. So on the whole interval from nnn up to (but not including) n+1n + 1n+1, the floor holds steady at the height nnn. That makes the graph a flat horizontal segment at height nnn running across that interval. The graph then jumps up by one at x=n+1x = n + 1x=n+1 to start the next segment. The result is a staircase.

Look carefully at the ends of one step. On the interval for height nnn, the left end x=nx = nx=n is included, because ⌊n⌋=n\lfloor n \rfloor = n⌊n⌋=n, so that end is a filled (closed) dot. The right end x=n+1x = n + 1x=n+1 is not included, because at x=n+1x = n + 1x=n+1 the floor has already jumped to n+1n + 1n+1, so that end is a hollow (open) dot. Every step of the floor is therefore closed on the left and open on the right.

The ceiling graph is the same staircase read the other way. Since ⌈x⌉=n\lceil x \rceil = n⌈x⌉=n exactly when n−1<x≤nn - 1 < x \le nn−1<x≤n, each ceiling step holds the height nnn across the interval that ends at x=nx = nx=n. For a ceiling step the included end is now on the right (because ⌈n⌉=n\lceil n \rceil = n⌈n⌉=n), and the excluded end is on the left. So every ceiling step is open on the left and closed on the right, the mirror image of the floor.

Both graphs pass the vertical line test (each input has one output), so they really are functions. Both accept every real number, so the domain of each is all real numbers. And every output is an integer, with each integer height actually reached, so the range of each is the integers. The steps never connect with vertical lines, because the function does not take the in-between values during a jump. The open and closed dots are what carry that information.

Worked example 3 Solve ⌊x⌋=−3\lfloor x \rfloor = -3⌊x⌋=−3 and ⌈x⌉=2\lceil x \rceil = 2⌈x⌉=2

Each equation asks for the whole strip of inputs that a single step covers, so read it straight off the defining inequality.

For ⌊x⌋=−3\lfloor x \rfloor = -3⌊x⌋=−3, the floor equals −3-3−3 exactly on the step that starts at −3-3−3:

−3≤x<−2.-3 \le x < -2.−3≤x<−2.

Every xxx from −3-3−3 (included) up to −2-2−2 (not included) has floor −3-3−3, and nothing else does.

For ⌈x⌉=2\lceil x \rceil = 2⌈x⌉=2, the ceiling equals 222 exactly on the step that ends at 222:

1<x≤2.1 < x \le 2.1<x≤2.

Here the left end 111 is excluded and the right end 222 is included, matching the open-left, closed-right shape of the ceiling staircase.

The fractional part

Subtracting the floor from a number strips away the whole part and leaves only what is left over. That leftover is the fractional part of xxx, written {x}\{x\}{x}:

{x}=x−⌊x⌋.\{x\} = x - \lfloor x \rfloor.{x}=x−⌊x⌋.

It is always at least 000 and always less than 111, and the defining inequality shows exactly why. Start from n≤x<n+1n \le x < n + 1n≤x<n+1 with n=⌊x⌋n = \lfloor x \rfloorn=⌊x⌋ and subtract nnn from every part:

0≤x−n<1,that is0≤{x}<1.0 \le x - n < 1, \qquad \text{that is} \qquad 0 \le \{x\} < 1.0≤x−n<1,that is0≤{x}<1.

For a positive number this matches intuition: {3.7}=3.7−3=0.7\{3.7\} = 3.7 - 3 = 0.7{3.7}=3.7−3=0.7, the digits after the decimal point. But watch what happens with a negative input, where the floor surprise strikes again. Because ⌊−2.3⌋=−3\lfloor -2.3 \rfloor = -3⌊−2.3⌋=−3,

{−2.3}=−2.3−(−3)=0.7.\{-2.3\} = -2.3 - (-3) = 0.7.{−2.3}=−2.3−(−3)=0.7.

The fractional part of −2.3-2.3−2.3 is 0.70.70.7, not 0.30.30.3. It has to be, since {x}\{x\}{x} can never be negative, and it measures how far xxx has climbed above the step below it. On the integers the leftover is nothing at all, {5}=5−5=0\{5\} = 5 - 5 = 0{5}=5−5=0, which is the only time the fractional part equals 000.

Worked example 4 Compute {5.8}\{5.8\}{5.8} and {−1.2}\{-1.2\}{−1.2}

Use {x}=x−⌊x⌋\{x\} = x - \lfloor x \rfloor{x}=x−⌊x⌋, taking care with the floor of the negative input.

For 5.85.85.8, the floor is 555, so the leftover is the visible decimal part:

{5.8}=5.8−⌊5.8⌋=5.8−5=0.8.\{5.8\} = 5.8 - \lfloor 5.8 \rfloor = 5.8 - 5 = 0.8.{5.8}=5.8−⌊5.8⌋=5.8−5=0.8.

For −1.2-1.2−1.2, the floor rounds down to −2-2−2, not −1-1−1, so

{−1.2}=−1.2−⌊−1.2⌋=−1.2−(−2)=0.8.\{-1.2\} = -1.2 - \lfloor -1.2 \rfloor = -1.2 - (-2) = 0.8.{−1.2}=−1.2−⌊−1.2⌋=−1.2−(−2)=0.8.

Both land in [0,1)[0, 1)[0,1) as promised. And notice that −1.2-1.2−1.2 and 5.85.85.8 have the same fractional part 0.80.80.8, which makes sense. The two numbers are exactly 777 apart, a whole number of steps, so they sit the same distance above their respective floors.

Reflecting the number line swaps left with right, so it should turn a floor into a ceiling. It does, and the exact identity is in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Where floor and ceiling show up

The reason these functions matter is counting. Whenever a real-number answer has to be turned into a whole number of things, one of the two brackets is exactly the right tool. The choice between the two brackets is never arbitrary.

Use a ceiling when every leftover still needs a full container. If 535353 students travel in vans that seat 121212, then 53÷12=4.41…53 \div 12 = 4.41\ldots53÷12=4.41… vans’ worth of seats are needed. But you cannot send a fractional van, and the 555 students left over after four full vans still need one more. So the number of vans is ⌈53/12⌉=5\lceil 53 / 12 \rceil = 5⌈53/12⌉=5. Buses for a crowd, pages for a list, and boxes for a shipment all work this way.

Use a floor when leftovers are discarded and you only count what is complete. From those same 535353 students you can fill ⌊53/12⌋=4\lfloor 53 / 12 \rfloor = 4⌊53/12⌋=4 vans completely. The amount left over is what the full groups do not use,

53−12×⌊53/12⌋=53−48=5,53 - 12 \times \lfloor 53 / 12 \rfloor = 53 - 48 = 5,53−12×⌊53/12⌋=53−48=5,

which is the same fractional-part idea from before, now scaled up by the group size. Full crates from a harvest, whole dollars from a pile of change, and complete laps around a track are all floors.

Both answers live in one picture. Lay nnn objects into rows that hold ccc each: the number of complete rows is ⌊n/c⌋\lfloor n/c \rfloor⌊n/c⌋, and the number of rows needed to hold everything, counting a partly filled last row, is ⌈n/c⌉\lceil n/c \rceil⌈n/c⌉. Try it below.

Start at 101010 dots in rows of 444. Two rows fill and two dots are left hollow, so ⌊10/4⌋=2\lfloor 10/4 \rfloor = 2⌊10/4⌋=2 while ⌈10/4⌉=3\lceil 10/4 \rceil = 3⌈10/4⌉=3. Any leftover from one dot to three would force that same extra row, since the row width is four. Now widen the rows to 555: the same 101010 dots fill two rows exactly with nothing hollow, so floor and ceiling agree at 222. Narrow the rows to 333 instead and one dot is hollow again, splitting the answers to 333 and 444. Change the row width a few more times and watch for the pattern: the two functions differ by exactly one whenever a dot is left hollow, and are equal whenever none is.

Worked example 5 Plan a field trip for 150150150 students

Each bus holds 404040 students. First find how many buses are needed. Dividing gives 150/40=3.75150 / 40 = 3.75150/40=3.75, and the 303030 students beyond three full buses still need to ride, so round up with a ceiling:

⌈150/40⌉=⌈3.75⌉=4 buses.\lceil 150 / 40 \rceil = \lceil 3.75 \rceil = 4 \text{ buses}.⌈150/40⌉=⌈3.75⌉=4 buses.

Now suppose you instead want only the buses you can fill completely. That is a floor:

⌊150/40⌋=3 full buses,\lfloor 150 / 40 \rfloor = 3 \text{ full buses},⌊150/40⌋=3 full buses,

and the students left standing are 150−40×3=30150 - 40 \times 3 = 30150−40×3=30, who fill the fourth bus only part way. The two answers differ by one whole bus, which is the usual gap between a ceiling and a floor when the division does not come out even.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Rounding a negative toward zero instead of by direction. The floor rounds toward negative infinity, so ⌊−2.3⌋=−3\lfloor -2.3 \rfloor = -3⌊−2.3⌋=−3, not −2-2−2 (dropping the decimal digits is a different operation, truncation). The ceiling rounds the other way, so ⌈−2.3⌉=−2\lceil -2.3 \rceil = -2⌈−2.3⌉=−2, not −3-3−3.
  • Misreading the staircase. There are no vertical segments joining the steps; the function jumps straight to the next height. Each floor step is closed on the left and open on the right; each ceiling step is the mirror image, open on the left and closed on the right.
  • Thinking the fractional part of a negative is just its decimal digits. Since {x}=x−⌊x⌋\{x\} = x - \lfloor x \rfloor{x}=x−⌊x⌋ and the floor of a negative rounds down, {−1.2}=0.8\{-1.2\} = 0.8{−1.2}=0.8, not 0.20.20.2. The fractional part is never negative.
  • Using a floor where a ceiling belongs. If any leftover still needs its own container, round up. Sending ⌊53/12⌋=4\lfloor 53/12 \rfloor = 4⌊53/12⌋=4 vans strands the 555 extra students; the count you want is ⌈53/12⌉=5\lceil 53/12 \rceil = 5⌈53/12⌉=5.

Key takeaways

  • The floor ⌊x⌋\lfloor x \rfloor⌊x⌋ is the greatest integer at or below xxx (nearest integer to the left); the ceiling ⌈x⌉\lceil x \rceil⌈x⌉ is the least integer at or above xxx (nearest integer to the right). They are pinned down by ⌊x⌋≤x<⌊x⌋+1\lfloor x \rfloor \le x < \lfloor x \rfloor + 1⌊x⌋≤x<⌊x⌋+1 and ⌈x⌉−1<x≤⌈x⌉\lceil x \rceil - 1 < x \le \lceil x \rceil⌈x⌉−1<x≤⌈x⌉, which is also how you solve ⌊x⌋=n\lfloor x \rfloor = n⌊x⌋=n or ⌈x⌉=n\lceil x \rceil = n⌈x⌉=n for a range of xxx.
  • On negatives the floor rounds down, so ⌊−2.3⌋=−3\lfloor -2.3 \rfloor = -3⌊−2.3⌋=−3 while ⌈−2.3⌉=−2\lceil -2.3 \rceil = -2⌈−2.3⌉=−2. Rounding toward zero is a different, wrong move.
  • Both graphs are staircases with domain all real numbers and range the integers. Floor steps are closed on the left and open on the right; ceiling steps are the mirror image.
  • The fractional part {x}=x−⌊x⌋\{x\} = x - \lfloor x \rfloor{x}=x−⌊x⌋ always satisfies 0≤{x}<10 \le \{x\} < 10≤{x}<1, and is 000 exactly on the integers.
  • For counting, a ceiling gives the number of containers needed (round up for any leftover); a floor gives the number of full groups you can make instead (leftovers discarded).

A sign-flip identity that turns a floor into a ceiling

Worked example Show that ⌈x⌉=−⌊−x⌋\lceil x \rceil = -\lfloor -x \rfloor⌈x⌉=−⌊−x⌋ and use it on ⌈4.2⌉\lceil 4.2 \rceil⌈4.2⌉

Reflecting the number line by sending xxx to −x-x−x swaps left with right, and it also swaps “at or below” with “at or above,” so it should turn a floor into a ceiling.

Let m=⌈x⌉m = \lceil x \rceilm=⌈x⌉, the least integer with m≥xm \ge xm≥x. Negating both sides of m≥xm \ge xm≥x reverses the inequality to −m≤−x-m \le -x−m≤−x. Because mmm was the least integer at or above xxx, the reflected value −m-m−m is the greatest integer at or below −x-x−x. That is precisely the floor of −x-x−x:

⌊−x⌋=−⌈x⌉.\lfloor -x \rfloor = -\lceil x \rceil.⌊−x⌋=−⌈x⌉.

Negating both sides gives the identity ⌈x⌉=−⌊−x⌋\lceil x \rceil = -\lfloor -x \rfloor⌈x⌉=−⌊−x⌋. To use it on ⌈4.2⌉\lceil 4.2 \rceil⌈4.2⌉, compute the floor of the negated input first, then flip the sign:

⌈4.2⌉=−⌊−4.2⌋=−(−5)=5.\lceil 4.2 \rceil = -\lfloor -4.2 \rfloor = -(-5) = 5.⌈4.2⌉=−⌊−4.2⌋=−(−5)=5.

That agrees with rounding 4.24.24.2 up directly, and it shows you never need a separate rule for the ceiling: a floor and two sign flips always deliver it.

A bit of history (optional)

Rounding down had a symbol of its own long before rounding up did.

In 1808 the German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss wrote [x][x][x] for the greatest integer that does not exceed xxx. A plain square bracket was enough for what he was doing, and it stuck for over a century. But it only pointed one way. Anyone who needed to round up wrote −[−x]-[-x]−[−x] instead, the sign-flip identity in this lesson’s Go Deeper section, pressed into service to cover a missing symbol.

The repair came from computing. Programs round up constantly: how many disks hold this file, how many pages a document needs once its lines are fixed, how many buses carry this many children. In 1962 Kenneth Iverson, describing a programming language of his own design, introduced ⌊x⌋\lfloor x \rfloor⌊x⌋ and ⌈x⌉\lceil x \rceil⌈x⌉ and named them the floor and the ceiling. The brackets are a small picture of what they mean: the floor’s feet sit on the ground, and the ceiling hangs down from above.

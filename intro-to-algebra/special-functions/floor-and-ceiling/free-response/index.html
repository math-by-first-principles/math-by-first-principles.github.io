    3. Testing a shortcut for the floor . Reasoning, 10 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Reasoning 10 pts

    A common shortcut says that the floor of a number can be found by simply deleting everything after the decimal point (truncating it). Test whether that shortcut always gives the same value as the floor.

    1. A

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    ⌊9.6⌋=9\lfloor 9.6 \rfloor = 9⌊9.6⌋=9 and deleting the decimal digits from 9.69.69.6 also gives 999; the two methods agree.

    Part B

    ⌊−6.7⌋=−7\lfloor -6.7 \rfloor = -7⌊−6.7⌋=−7, but deleting the decimal digits from −6.7-6.7−6.7 gives −6-6−6; the two disagree, so this single input refutes the shortcut.

    Part C

    The shortcut is safe for every x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and for every negative integer (which has no fractional part to round); it fails only for a negative NON-integer, where the floor rounds toward negative infinity but truncation rounds toward zero, sending them to different integers.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The value 9.69.69.6 lies between 999 and 101010, so ⌊9.6⌋=9\lfloor 9.6 \rfloor = 9⌊9.6⌋=9. Deleting everything after the decimal point in 9.69.69.6 also leaves 999:

    ⌊9.6⌋=9=(truncated 9.6).\lfloor 9.6 \rfloor = 9 = \text{(truncated } 9.6\text{)}.⌊9.6⌋=9=(truncated 9.6).

    For this positive input, the shortcut matches the floor.

    Part B

    The value −6.7-6.7−6.7 lies between −7-7−7 and −6-6−6, and rounding down (toward negative infinity) gives ⌊−6.7⌋=−7\lfloor -6.7 \rfloor = -7⌊−6.7⌋=−7. Deleting everything after the decimal point in −6.7-6.7−6.7 instead gives −6-6−6, since that only removes digits without changing direction:

    ⌊−6.7⌋=−7≠−6=(truncated −6.7).\lfloor -6.7 \rfloor = -7 \neq -6 = \text{(truncated } -6.7\text{)}.⌊−6.7⌋=−7=−6=(truncated −6.7).

    One disagreeing case is enough to refute the claim that the shortcut always matches the floor.

    Part C

    Rounding down and truncating are two different rules about DIRECTION. The floor always moves toward negative infinity, no matter the sign of the input. Truncating always moves toward zero, since it only deletes trailing digits.

    For a nonnegative input, both directions point the same way, so the two rules land on the same integer, exactly as in part A. A negative INTEGER has nothing after the decimal point, so neither rule moves it and they agree there too. Only a negative NON-integer splits them: rounding toward negative infinity moves it away from zero while truncation moves it toward zero, so they disagree, exactly as in part B.

    ⌊x⌋=trunc(x)  ⟺  x≥0 or x∈Z.\lfloor x \rfloor = \text{trunc}(x) \iff x \ge 0 \ \text{or} \ x \in \mathbb{Z}.⌊x⌋=trunc(x)⟺x≥0 or x∈Z.

    In one line

    For 9.69.69.6 the floor and the truncated value agree at 999, but for −6.7-6.7−6.7 the floor is −7-7−7 while truncation gives −6-6−6, a single counterexample that refutes the 'always' shortcut. The guarded truth: the floor equals truncation for every x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and every negative integer, failing only for a negative non-integer, where rounding toward negative infinity and rounding toward zero disagree.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Any fraction of a pound still costs a full pound's rate, so the weight is rounded up before multiplying by the rate:

    C(w)=5⌈w⌉.C(w) = 5 \lceil w \rceil.C(w)=5⌈w⌉.

    Part B

    Since 121212 is already an integer, it is its own least integer at or above itself, so ⌈12⌉=12\lceil 12 \rceil = 12⌈12⌉=12. The cost is then

    C(12)=5×12=60 dollars.C(12) = 5 \times 12 = 60 \text{ dollars}.C(12)=5×12=60 dollars.

    The rounding step only ever raises a weight to the next whole pound when there is a fraction to round away; an already-whole weight has nothing to round.

    Part C

    Set the cost expression equal to 505050 and solve for the rounded weight:

    5⌈w⌉=50  ⟹  ⌈w⌉=10.5 \lceil w \rceil = 50 \implies \lceil w \rceil = 10.5⌈w⌉=50⟹⌈w⌉=10.

    Apply the defining inequality n−1<w≤nn - 1 < w \le nn−1<w≤n with n=10n = 10n=10:

    9<w≤10.9 < w \le 10.9<w≤10.

    Part D

    Postage depends only on ⌈w⌉\lceil w \rceil⌈w⌉, not on www itself. On the ceiling's staircase graph, one whole horizontal step covers a full length-one interval of weights, and every weight on that step shares the same height, the same rounded pound count:

    9<w≤10  ⟹  ⌈w⌉=10  ⟹  C(w)=5×10=50.9 < w \le 10 \implies \lceil w \rceil = 10 \implies C(w) = 5\times 10 = 50.9<w≤10⟹⌈w⌉=10⟹C(w)=5×10=50.

    Since the cost is the rate times that shared height, every weight in the interval you found in part C produces the exact same charge, even though the weights themselves are different real numbers.

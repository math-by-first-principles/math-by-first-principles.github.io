Rational Functions Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra I. You can skip it.

Intuition

You divide all the time. Split a pizza of 121212 slices among xxx friends and each gets 12x\frac{12}{x}x12​ slices. Drive 100100100 miles at a speed of xxx miles per hour and the trip takes 100x\frac{100}{x}x100​ hours. In each case the output is a division, and something interesting happens at the edges. Share the pizza among more and more friends and each portion shrinks toward nothing. Now slow the drive down instead: walk the same 100100100 miles at 111, then 12\tfrac1221​, then 110\tfrac{1}{10}101​ mile per hour, and the time it takes grows without bound as the speed creeps toward 000. A rational function packages exactly this kind of rule, a polynomial divided by a polynomial, and it can never let its denominator hit zero. Near a value that would do that, the graph often does something no polynomial ever does, racing off toward a vertical line it never reaches; sometimes, though, that trouble spot turns out to be nothing worse than a single missing point. Finding where the denominator vanishes, and telling those two outcomes apart, is the heart of this lesson.

Learning goals

  • Exclude the zeros of the denominator from the domain
  • Graph 1x\tfrac{1}{x}x1​ as a hyperbola with two branches
  • Tell a vertical asymptote from a hole by whether the factor cancels
  • Compare degrees to find the horizontal asymptote
  • Read asymptotes x=hx = hx=h and y=ky = ky=k from the shifted form
  • Clear denominators, then discard extraneous candidates

What a rational function is

A rational function is a ratio of two polynomials,

f(x)=p(x)q(x),f(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)},f(x)=q(x)p(x)​,

where q(x)q(x)q(x) is not the zero polynomial (otherwise you would be dividing by nothing). The word rational comes from ratio: just as a rational number is one integer over another, a rational function is one polynomial over another. Every polynomial you have already studied is a rational function in disguise, since you can write it over the denominator 111. But the new behavior appears the moment the denominator actually contains the variable.

The simplest genuine example, and the one the whole lesson is built on, is the reciprocal function

f(x)=1x.f(x) = \frac{1}{x}.f(x)=x1​.

It sends 222 to 12\tfrac1221​, sends 12\tfrac1221​ to 222, and sends −4-4−4 to −14-\tfrac14−41​, turning each input into its reciprocal. A few more examples show the range of shapes the definition allows:

x+1x−3,2xx2−9,x2−x−6x2−9.\frac{x + 1}{x - 3}, \qquad \frac{2x}{x^2 - 9}, \qquad \frac{x^2 - x - 6}{x^2 - 9}.x−3x+1​,x2−92x​,x2−9x2−x−6​.

The rest of the lesson works out the two features that set rational functions apart from the polynomials of earlier chapters. Those two features are a domain with holes cut out of it, and a graph that runs off toward invisible guide-lines called asymptotes.

Finding the domain

A polynomial accepts every real number, but a rational function is choosier. Wherever the denominator q(x)q(x)q(x) equals zero, the rule asks you to divide by zero, and that has no answer. So the domain of a rational function is every real number except the values that make the denominator zero. To find those forbidden inputs, set the denominator equal to zero and solve.

Why dividing by zero has no value#

Division is defined through multiplication. The quotient ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ is the number that, multiplied by bbb, gives back aaa. So ab=c\frac{a}{b} = cba​=c means exactly that c⋅b=ac \cdot b = ac⋅b=a. Test what this demands when the divisor is zero.

Suppose first that a≠0a \neq 0a=0, and ask for a0\frac{a}{0}0a​. It would have to be a number ccc with c⋅0=ac \cdot 0 = ac⋅0=a. But anything times zero is zero,

c⋅0=0≠a,c \cdot 0 = 0 \neq a,c⋅0=0=a,

so no such ccc exists. There is simply no number that works.

Suppose instead that a=0a = 0a=0, and ask for 00\frac{0}{0}00​. Now the requirement c⋅0=0c \cdot 0 = 0c⋅0=0 is satisfied by every number ccc, since anything times zero is zero. A quotient that could be any number at all pins down nothing, so it cannot be assigned a single value either. Either way, dividing by zero has no sensible answer, which is exactly why every input that makes the denominator zero is thrown out of the domain.

The reciprocal function and its hyperbola

To see what a rational graph looks like, plot the reciprocal f(x)=1xf(x) = \frac{1}{x}f(x)=x1​ point by point, keeping in mind that x=0x = 0x=0 is barred from the domain:

xxx−2-2−2−1-1−1−12-\tfrac12−21​12\tfrac1221​111222
y=1xy = \tfrac{1}{x}y=x1​−12-\tfrac12−21​−1-1−1−2-2−222211112\tfrac1221​

The points fall into two separate pieces, called branches. For positive inputs both coordinates are positive, so that branch lives in the upper-right quadrant. For negative inputs both coordinates are negative, so the other branch sits in the lower-left. The two branches never join, because to pass from one to the other the curve would have to cross x=0x = 0x=0, and there is no point there. This two-branch curve is a hyperbola.

Look at what happens at the edges, described in plain words rather than symbols. As xxx grows very large in size, say x=100x = 100x=100 or x=1000x = 1000x=1000, the reciprocal 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​ becomes very small, 0.010.010.01 then 0.0010.0010.001, creeping toward zero without ever equalling it. So far out to the left and right the curve snuggles down against the x-axis. The horizontal line the graph approaches this way is a horizontal asymptote, and here it is the line y=0y = 0y=0.

Now let xxx get very close to 000, say x=0.01x = 0.01x=0.01. Then 1x=100\frac{1}{x} = 100x1​=100, and at x=0.001x = 0.001x=0.001 it is 100010001000: the closer xxx creeps to zero, the more violently 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​ shoots up (or down, for small negative xxx). The curve rises along the y-axis without bound, hugging it ever more tightly but never landing on it, since x=0x = 0x=0 is not allowed. The vertical line the graph races along like this is a vertical asymptote, and here it is the line x=0x = 0x=0.

An asymptote is any straight line that the graph approaches more and more closely as you follow it outward. It is not part of the graph; it is a guide-line that describes where the curve is headed. A rational function can have a vertical asymptote, a horizontal asymptote, or both, and reading them off the formula is the goal of the next two sections.

Vertical asymptotes and holes

The reciprocal blew up at x=0x = 0x=0 because that is where its denominator vanished. The same thing happens in general, but with one twist: not every zero of the denominator produces an asymptote. What matters is whether the troublesome factor also lives in the numerator.

Why a leftover factor blows the graph up, and a canceling one does not#

Suppose the denominator is zero at x=ax = ax=a, so it has a factor of x−ax - ax−a. Two cases split apart.

In the first case the factored denominator holds exactly one copy of x−ax - ax−a, and no copy of x−ax - ax−a divides the numerator. Then as xxx moves very close to aaa, the factor x−ax - ax−a becomes a tiny number near zero. So the whole denominator is tiny, while the numerator, with no factor of x−ax - ax−a to shrink it, settles on some nonzero value NNN. A fixed nonzero number divided by an ever-smaller number produces an ever-larger result: dividing by 0.0010.0010.001 multiplies the size by a thousand, dividing by 0.0000010.0000010.000001 by a million. So the output grows without bound in size. Because this single factor x−ax - ax−a itself flips from negative to positive as xxx crosses aaa, the denominator flips sign there too, so the output is positive on one side of x=ax = ax=a and negative on the other, and the curve races along the vertical line

x=a,x = a,x=a,

which is therefore a vertical asymptote.

In the second case x−ax - ax−a divides both, so at least one copy of it cancels between the numerator and the denominator. After cancelling everything that will cancel, the function agrees, at every input except x=ax = ax=a itself, with the simpler rational function left behind. If no copy of x−ax - ax−a remains in that reduced denominator, the simpler function has a perfectly finite value at aaa: nothing blows up, because the shrinking original denominator was matched all the way by a shrinking numerator. The only leftover damage is that x=ax = ax=a is still forbidden from the original domain, so the graph is that simpler curve with exactly one point removed, a hole. But if a copy of x−ax - ax−a is still sitting in the reduced denominator once every possible cancellation is done, then x=ax = ax=a forces the same kind of unbounded growth as before, and it is a vertical asymptote after all, not a hole. (Whether the output flips sign across x=ax = ax=a or stays the same sign on both sides then depends on how many copies of x−ax - ax−a remain, but either way the output is unbounded, so the line is still an asymptote.) The rule is clean: cancel as far as it will go, then look at what is left in the denominator. A factor still there gives an asymptote; no copy left there gives a hole.

∎

Horizontal asymptotes and end behavior

Vertical asymptotes describe what the graph does near a forbidden input. The horizontal asymptote describes the opposite edge: what height the graph settles toward as xxx runs far out to the left and right. The whole question turns on a single idea you can check by hand: when xxx is huge, a polynomial is ruled by its highest-power term.

Take x=1000x = 1000x=1000 in x2+5xx^2 + 5xx2+5x. The square term is x2=1,000,000x^2 = 1{,}000{,}000x2=1,000,000, while 5x=5,0005x = 5{,}0005x=5,000 is 200200200 times smaller and barely nudges the total. The larger xxx becomes, the more completely the top-power term dwarfs everything else. So for the purpose of the far-out behavior, you may compare a rational function’s top and bottom by their leading terms alone. Let nnn be the degree of the numerator and mmm the degree of the denominator. Three cases result.

If n<mn < mn<m, the denominator’s top power outgrows the numerator’s, so you are dividing a slower-growing quantity by a faster-growing one, and the ratio is squeezed toward zero. The horizontal asymptote is

y=0.y = 0.y=0.

If n=mn = mn=m, the top and bottom grow at the same rate, and each is dominated by its leading term. Writing the leading coefficients as aaa (top) and bbb (bottom), the ratio settles toward

y=ab.y = \frac{a}{b}.y=ba​.

If n>mn > mn>m, the numerator grows faster than the denominator, so the fraction grows without bound in size and never levels off. There is no horizontal asymptote. In the special case where the numerator is exactly one degree higher, the graph instead lines up far out with a slanted line called a slant asymptote. You find that line by dividing the polynomials, but the main fact to remember is that a top-heavy fraction has no horizontal asymptote at all.

x-intercepts and shifting the hyperbola

A fraction equals zero only when its numerator equals zero (and its denominator does not). So to find where a rational graph crosses the x-axis, set the numerator to zero and solve. Then discard any solution that also makes the denominator zero, since that input is not even in the domain. For f(x)=x−3x+1f(x) = \dfrac{x - 3}{x + 1}f(x)=x+1x−3​ the numerator is zero at x=3x = 3x=3, and since the denominator there is 3+1=4≠03 + 1 = 4 \neq 03+1=4=0, the x-intercept is (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0).

Reading a transformed reciprocal is just as direct. Every function of the form

f(x)=ax−h+k,a≠0,f(x) = \frac{a}{x - h} + k, \qquad a \neq 0,f(x)=x−ha​+k,a=0,

is the basic hyperbola 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​ shifted and scaled, using the same moves you applied to other graphs. The x−hx - hx−h in the denominator shifts the graph right by hhh, which drags the vertical asymptote from x=0x = 0x=0 over to x=hx = hx=h. The +k+k+k on the outside lifts the whole graph up by kkk, carrying the horizontal asymptote from y=0y = 0y=0 up to y=ky = ky=k. The factor aaa stretches the branches, and a negative aaa flips them across the horizontal asymptote. So the two asymptotes cross at the point (h,k)(h, k)(h,k), the new center of the hyperbola. The domain and range shift the same way: xxx can be anything but hhh, since that is where the fraction ax−h\frac{a}{x - h}x−ha​ has no value, and yyy can be anything but kkk, since adding kkk to a fraction that never equals zero can never land exactly on kkk.

Solving a rational equation

To solve an equation that has variables in a denominator, clear the fractions by multiplying every term by a common denominator. Then solve the polynomial equation that remains, and finally throw out any candidate that makes an original denominator zero. That last step is not optional. Multiplying by an expression that could be zero can introduce a false solution, an extraneous one, exactly as squaring did for radical equations.

Worked example 6 Solve two rational equations, one of which has an extraneous solution

First solve 3x+1=2x−4\dfrac{3}{x + 1} = \dfrac{2}{x - 4}x+13​=x−42​. Multiply both sides by (x+1)(x−4)(x + 1)(x - 4)(x+1)(x−4) to clear the denominators, which leaves a linear equation:

3(x−4)=2(x+1)⟹3x−12=2x+2⟹x=14.3(x - 4) = 2(x + 1) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 3x - 12 = 2x + 2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = 14.3(x−4)=2(x+1)⟹3x−12=2x+2⟹x=14.

The value x=14x = 14x=14 makes neither denominator zero, so it is genuine, and the only solution is x=14x = 14x=14.

Now solve xx−2=2x−2+3\dfrac{x}{x - 2} = \dfrac{2}{x - 2} + 3x−2x​=x−22​+3. Multiply every term by x−2x - 2x−2:

x=2+3(x−2)⟹x=3x−4⟹x=2.x = 2 + 3(x - 2) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = 3x - 4 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = 2.x=2+3(x−2)⟹x=3x−4⟹x=2.

Before celebrating, check the candidate against the original denominators. Here x=2x = 2x=2 makes x−2=0x - 2 = 0x−2=0, so it was never in the domain. The candidate is extraneous, and the equation has no solution. The multiplication step manufactured it, which is why every answer must be tested in the original equation.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Letting the numerator restrict the domain. Only the denominator can force an exclusion. For x−5x+2\dfrac{x - 5}{x + 2}x+2x−5​ the domain is every x≠−2x \neq -2x=−2; the numerator’s zero at x=5x = 5x=5 is a perfectly good input (it is the x-intercept), not a forbidden one.
  • Calling every denominator zero a vertical asymptote. A zero of the denominator is a vertical asymptote only if a copy of its factor is still left in the denominator once you cancel everything you can with the numerator. If cancelling removes the factor completely, that input is a hole instead. Always factor and cancel as far as it will go before deciding.
  • Reading the horizontal asymptote off the constant terms. End behavior is set by the highest-power terms, not the constants. For 4x2−x2x2+7\dfrac{4x^2 - x}{2x^2 + 7}2x2+74x2−x​ the asymptote is y=42=2y = \tfrac{4}{2} = 2y=24​=2, from the leading coefficients, not y=07y = \tfrac{0}{7}y=70​.
  • Expecting a horizontal asymptote when the top is heavier. If the numerator has the higher degree, there is no horizontal asymptote at all, because the fraction grows without bound as xxx gets large.
  • Forgetting to check for extraneous solutions. Multiplying an equation by a variable expression can create a solution that breaks an original denominator. Every candidate must be tested, and any that makes a denominator zero is discarded.
  • Assuming a graph can never cross a horizontal asymptote. A graph never crosses a vertical asymptote, because the function is undefined there. A horizontal asymptote only describes the far-out behavior, so a curve can cross it at ordinary xxx-values before settling toward it at the edges. The reciprocal 1x\dfrac{1}{x}x1​ happens to meet neither of its axes, but that is special to 1x\dfrac{1}{x}x1​, not a rule for every rational function.

Key takeaways

  • A rational function f(x)=p(x)q(x)f(x) = \dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)=q(x)p(x)​ is one polynomial over another. Its domain is every real number except the zeros of the denominator, because dividing by zero has no value.
  • The reciprocal 1x\dfrac{1}{x}x1​ graphs as a hyperbola with two branches. Its vertical asymptote is x=0x = 0x=0 and its horizontal asymptote is y=0y = 0y=0; the curve approaches both without touching them.
  • A zero of the denominator that still divides the denominator after cancelling everything possible with the numerator gives a vertical asymptote, where the output blows up. A zero whose factor cancels away completely gives a hole instead, a single missing point.
  • The horizontal asymptote comes from comparing degrees nnn (top) and mmm (bottom). When n<mn < mn<m the asymptote is y=0y = 0y=0; when n=mn = mn=m it is y=aby = \dfrac{a}{b}y=ba​, the ratio of leading coefficients; and when n>mn > mn>m there is no horizontal asymptote.
  • x-intercepts are the zeros of the numerator that stay in the domain. In the form ax−h+k\dfrac{a}{x - h} + kx−ha​+k with a≠0a \neq 0a=0, the asymptotes are x=hx = hx=h and y=ky = ky=k, crossing at the center (h,k)(h, k)(h,k).
  • To solve a rational equation, clear denominators, solve, and discard any extraneous candidate that makes an original denominator zero.

A bit of history (optional)

The curve y=1xy = \frac{1}{x}y=x1​ once guarded a puzzle nobody could solve. Look at the region trapped between the curve and the x-axis. Start at x=1x = 1x=1 and stop wherever you please. How much area is in there? Areas under circles and straight lines had been measured since ancient times. This region resisted every method anyone tried on it.

The answer came in the 1640s. It was found by a Flemish mathematician named Gregoire de Saint-Vincent, and it surprised everyone. He noticed that the areas grow in a strange way. Take the slab from 111 to 222. Then take the slab from 222 to 444, and then the one from 444 to 888. Each has exactly the same area as the one before it. Multiply the stopping point by a fixed number, and you always add a fixed amount of area.

That should sound familiar. Turning multiplication into addition is just what a logarithm does. A student of Saint-Vincent’s said so out loud a few years later. The area from 111 out to a number bbb is the natural logarithm of bbb. Nobody had put a logarithm into the problem. It was sitting under the curve, waiting.

So the reciprocal is not the plain example it looks like. The first rational function in this lesson is the one whose branches taught you what an asymptote is. It holds the logarithms you met chapters ago.

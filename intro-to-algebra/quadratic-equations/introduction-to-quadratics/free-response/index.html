    1. Standard form and what it means to be a root . Foundational, 11 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    Before you can classify or solve any equation, it often has to be tidied into standard form first. Separately, checking whether a specific number is a root is nothing more than careful substitution. This question asks for both skills, on two different equations.

    Part B

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    k=9k = 9k=9, since 9+6=15≠09 + 6 = 15 \neq 09+6=15=0 rules out x+6x + 6x+6, forcing 9−k=09 - k = 09−k=0.

    Part B

    x=9x = 9x=9 or x=−6x = -6x=−6.

    Part C

    A product equals 121212 in infinitely many ways, so neither factor is forced to a particular value. Zero is the only number for which a product equals it exactly when one of the factors does. The equation would have to be expanded and rewritten with 000 on one side first.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Before solving for kkk, decide which factor is even capable of being zero at x=9x = 9x=9. Evaluate the KNOWN factor first:

    9+6=15≠0.9 + 6 = 15 \neq 0.9+6=15=0.

    Since x+6x + 6x+6 is not zero at x=9x = 9x=9, the factor x−kx - kx−k must be the one that is zero there. Set it equal to 000 and solve:

    9−k=0  ⇒  k=9.9 - k = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; k = 9.9−k=0⇒k=9.

    Part B

    With k=9k = 9k=9, the equation is (x−9)(x+6)=0(x - 9)(x + 6) = 0(x−9)(x+6)=0. Apply the zero-product property to each factor:

    x−9=0orx+6=0.x - 9 = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad x + 6 = 0.x−9=0orx+6=0.

    Solving each gives the two roots:

    x=9orx=−6.x = 9 \qquad\text{or}\qquad x = -6.x=9orx=−6.

    The root x=9x = 9x=9 is the one given in the stem; x=−6x = -6x=−6 is the one this part adds.

    Part C

    Part A worked because a product of two numbers is 000 exactly when at least one of them is 000. Nothing of that kind is true of 121212:

    (1)(12)=12,(2)(6)=12,(−3)(−4)=12(1)(12) = 12, \qquad (2)(6) = 12, \qquad (-3)(-4) = 12(1)(12)=12,(2)(6)=12,(−3)(−4)=12

    Three different ways, and none of the six numbers in them is forced to be anything. So from (x−k)(x+6)=12(x - k)(x + 6) = 12(x−k)(x+6)=12 you cannot conclude that x−k=12x - k = 12x−k=12, or that x+6=12x + 6 = 12x+6=12, or anything at all about either factor on its own. The split is legal for 000 and for no other number.

    What has to happen first is that the product is expanded and every term moved to one side, so the equation reads something =0= 0=0. Only then is there a product equal to zero, and only then does the factor-by-factor step become available again.

    In one line

    Since x=9x = 9x=9 does not make x+6x + 6x+6 zero (9+6=159 + 6 = 159+6=15), it must make x−kx - kx−k zero, giving k=9k = 9k=9. The completed equation (x−9)(x+6)=0(x - 9)(x + 6) = 0(x−9)(x+6)=0 then has roots x=9x = 9x=9 and x=−6x = -6x=−6. And had the right-hand side been 121212 rather than 000, none of that would have been available: a product can equal 121212 in infinitely many ways, so the equation would first have to be expanded and rewritten with 000 on one side.

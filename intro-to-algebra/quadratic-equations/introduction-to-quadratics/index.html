Introduction to Quadratics

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

What is the side length of a square with area 252525? If the side is xxx, its area gives the equation x2=25x^2 = 25x2=25. Unlike a linear equation such as 3x+5=203x + 5 = 203x+5=20, this one squares the unknown.

Both 555 and −5-5−5 solve the equation, because 52=255^2 = 2552=25 and (−5)2=25(-5)^2 = 25(−5)2=25. Only 555 can be a side length. The equation keeps both answers; the situation tells you which one to use. We will learn two ways to solve the simplest equations with a squared term and keep track of every real solution.

  • Extend the square-root method to a squared group
  • Count 000, 111, or 222 real roots where a parabola meets the xxx-axis

What makes an equation quadratic

An equation such as x2−5x+6=0x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0x2−5x+6=0 is quadratic: its highest surviving power of xxx is 222. A quadratic equation can be written in standard form as

ax2+bx+c=0,ax^2 + bx + c = 0,ax2+bx+c=0,

where aaa, bbb, and ccc are fixed real numbers and aaa is not zero. The terms go in decreasing power, with 000 on the other side. The number aaa is the leading coefficient, bbb is the coefficient of xxx, and ccc is the constant term.

The word quadratic comes from the Latin word for square. If a=0a = 0a=0, the squared term disappears, leaving bx+c=0bx + c = 0bx+c=0. That equation is linear when bbb is not zero; if bbb is also zero, only a constant statement remains. Either way, it is not quadratic. There is no such restriction on bbb or ccc: either or both may be zero while the squared term survives.

To recognize a quadratic, first use the balancing steps you know from linear equations. Move every term to one side and collect like terms. Then check whether a squared term remains.

2x2=82x^2 = 82x2=8yesa=2, b=0, c=−8a = 2,\ b = 0,\ c = -8a=2, b=0, c=−8

The middle two rows look incomplete, yet both are quadratic. Rewriting 2x2=82x^2 = 82x2=8 as 2x2−8=02x^2 - 8 = 02x2−8=0 shows b=0b = 0b=0, and rewriting x2=7xx^2 = 7xx2=7x as x2−7x=0x^2 - 7x = 0x2−7x=0 shows c=0c = 0c=0. A missing xxx term or a missing constant is fine; a missing x2x^2x2 term is not.

An x2x^2x2 can also disappear once you simplify, even though it was there to begin with. Take x2+3x=x2+7x^2 + 3x = x^2 + 7x2+3x=x2+7. Subtract x2x^2x2 from both sides:

3x=7.3x = 7.3x=7.

The squared terms cancel completely, leaving a one-step linear equation. The equation was never quadratic; it only looked that way before you moved everything to one side. Always simplify fully before you decide.

Answer choices

Subtract x2x^2x2 from both sides of the first equation: 3x=73x = 73x=7. The squared terms cancel, leaving a linear equation with no x2x^2x2 term at all. The other three each keep a surviving x2x^2x2 term, so all three are genuinely quadratic.

Worked example 1 Put 3x2−5=x3x^2 - 5 = x3x2−5=x in standard form

The equation has an x2x^2x2 term, so it should be quadratic once it is tidied up. Move every term to the left so the right side becomes 000. Subtract xxx from both sides:

3x2−5−x=0.3x^2 - 5 - x = 0.3x2−5−x=0.

Now write the terms in order of decreasing power, the squared term first, then the xxx term, then the constant:

3x2−x−5=0.3x^2 - x - 5 = 0.3x2−x−5=0.

Reading off the coefficients gives a=3a = 3a=3, b=−1b = -1b=−1, and c=−5c = -5c=−5. Because a=3≠0a = 3 \ne 0a=3=0, the equation is genuinely quadratic.

What it means to be a solution

A solution, also called a root, is a value that makes an equation true. For a quadratic in standard form, substituting a root for xxx makes the left side equal 000.

A quadratic can have two real solutions, one real solution, or no real solution. Finding one root may leave another to find. Substitution checks a candidate; it does not by itself find every root.

Worked example 2 Check whether −3-3−3 and 111 are roots of x2+x−6=0x^2 + x - 6 = 0x2+x−6=0

To test a value, substitute it for xxx and see whether the left side lands on 000.

Start with x=−3x = -3x=−3. Squaring a negative gives a positive, so (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9:

(−3)2+(−3)−6=9−3−6=0.(-3)^2 + (-3) - 6 = 9 - 3 - 6 = 0.(−3)2+(−3)−6=9−3−6=0.

The result is 000, so x=−3x = -3x=−3 is a root. Now test x=1x = 1x=1:

(1)2+(1)−6=1+1−6=−4.(1)^2 + (1) - 6 = 1 + 1 - 6 = -4.(1)2+(1)−6=1+1−6=−4.

That is not 000, so x=1x = 1x=1 is not a root. Substitution alone cannot tell you every root, only whether a guess is one; you will meet a method later in this lesson that finds both roots directly. For now, check that x=2x = 2x=2 also works: 22+2−6=02^2 + 2 - 6 = 022+2−6=0. So this equation’s two solutions are −3-3−3 and 222.

The zero-product property

A zero factor makes a product zero: 7⋅0=07 \cdot 0 = 07⋅0=0 and 0⋅(−4)=00 \cdot (-4) = 00⋅(−4)=0. The reverse is also true: a product can equal zero only if at least one factor is zero. This is the zero-product property. The proof explains why two nonzero factors cannot give zero.

Why a product is zero only when a factor is zero#

Suppose 6B=06B = 06B=0. Multiplying both sides by the reciprocal 16\tfrac1661​ gives

16(6B)=16⋅0,\tfrac16(6B) = \tfrac16 \cdot 0,61​(6B)=61​⋅0,

so B=0B = 0B=0. The same step works with any nonzero first factor, not just 666.

Now suppose AB=0AB = 0AB=0. If A=0A = 0A=0, we already have a zero factor. If A≠0A \ne 0A=0, it has a reciprocal, so multiply both sides by 1A\tfrac1AA1​:

1A(AB)=1A⋅0.\tfrac1A(AB) = \tfrac1A \cdot 0.A1​(AB)=A1​⋅0.

The left side simplifies to BBB and the right side to 000, so B=0B = 0B=0. Thus at least one factor is zero. The reverse also holds: a zero factor makes the product zero, whatever the other factor is.

∎

The property turns a factored quadratic into two easy linear equations. Suppose an equation arrives already broken into a product of factors set equal to zero, such as

(x−2)(x+3)=0.(x - 2)(x + 3) = 0.(x−2)(x+3)=0.

The left side is a product of the two factors x−2x - 2x−2 and x+3x + 3x+3. By the zero-product property that product is 000 exactly when one of the factors is 000, so the equation splits into two:

x−2=0orx+3=0.x - 2 = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x + 3 = 0.x−2=0orx+3=0.

Each piece is a linear equation you can solve at a glance, giving x=2x = 2x=2 or x=−3x = -3x=−3. Those are the two roots. For a factor written as x−rx - rx−r, this gives a shortcut: the factor x−2x - 2x−2 is zero when x=2x = 2x=2, and the factor x+3x + 3x+3, which is x−(−3)x - (-3)x−(−3), is zero when x=−3x = -3x=−3. The number shown in the factor and the root have opposite signs, and that sign flip is the most common place to slip. The shortcut only reads off this cleanly when the coefficient of xxx is 111. For any other factor, set it equal to 000 and solve like a normal one-step or two-step equation, as the next example shows.

Factored form and standard form describe the same equation. Expanding gives

(x−2)(x+3)=x2+3x−2x−6=x2+x−6.\begin{aligned} (x - 2)(x + 3) &= x^2 + 3x - 2x - 6 \\ &= x^2 + x - 6. \end{aligned}(x−2)(x+3)​=x2+3x−2x−6=x2+x−6.​

So the roots 222 and −3-3−3 are the ones we checked earlier. Factored form makes them easy to find. The next lessons show how to turn a standard-form quadratic into factors; here the factors are already given.

The zero on the other side matters. For example, (x−2)(x+3)=6(x-2)(x+3)=6(x−2)(x+3)=6 is true at x=3x=3x=3, where the factors are 111 and 666. But it is also true at x=−4x=-4x=−4, where the factors are −6-6−6 and −1-1−1. Neither of those factors is 000 or 666. Splitting into x−2=6x-2=6x−2=6 or x+3=6x+3=6x+3=6 would lose this solution. To use the zero-product property, you need a product equal to zero.

Worked example 3 Solve (2x−1)(x+4)=0(2x - 1)(x + 4) = 0(2x−1)(x+4)=0

The left side is already a product of two factors, so apply the zero-product property and set each factor to 000:

2x−1=0orx+4=0.2x - 1 = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x + 4 = 0.2x−1=0orx+4=0.

Solve the first linear equation: 2x=12x = 12x=1, so x=12x = \tfrac{1}{2}x=21​. The second gives x=−4x = -4x=−4. The two roots are

x=12orx=−4.x = \tfrac{1}{2} \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = -4.x=21​orx=−4.

A factor with a coefficient, like 2x−12x - 12x−1, still produces a single root; you just solve for xxx as usual. Check the first root: 2(12)−1=02(\tfrac{1}{2}) - 1 = 02(21​)−1=0, so the whole product is 000, as required.

Answer choices

By the zero-product property, set each factor to 000.

x−5=0orx+2=0x - 5 = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x + 2 = 0x−5=0orx+2=0

Solving gives x=5x = 5x=5 or x=−2x = -2x=−2. Each root is the opposite sign of the number written in its factor, so x+2x + 2x+2 produces the root −2-2−2, not +2+2+2.

Solving by taking square roots

The zero-product property needs a product equal to zero. When a square equals a number, you can undo the square instead. Both 32=93^2 = 932=9 and (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9, so x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9 has two solutions: 333 and −3-3−3. Squaring loses the sign because a negative times a negative is positive.

The symbol 9\sqrt99​ means the nonnegative square root, 333. To name both solutions we write x=±9x = \pm\sqrt9x=±9​, read “plus or minus the square root of nine.”

For an equation x2=kx^2 = kx2=k, the sign of kkk decides what happens:

  • Positive kkk: x=kx = \sqrt{k}x=k​ or x=−kx = -\sqrt{k}x=−k​, two different real solutions.
  • Zero kkk: only x=0x = 0x=0 works. The two signs give the same value, a repeated root.
  • Negative kkk: no real solution, because every real square is zero or positive.

Thus, when k≥0k \ge 0k≥0, we can write the solutions together as

x=±k.x = \pm\sqrt{k}.x=±k​.

We use real numbers throughout this chapter. Imaginary numbers, which can square to negatives, come later in this course.

Worked example 4 Solve x2=49x^2 = 49x2=49, then x2=0x^2 = 0x2=0 and x2=−9x^2 = -9x2=−9

For x2=49x^2 = 49x2=49, take the square root of both sides and keep both signs:

x=±49=±7,x = \pm\sqrt{49} = \pm 7,x=±49​=±7,

so x=7x = 7x=7 or x=−7x = -7x=−7. Both check out, since 72=497^2 = 4972=49 and (−7)2=49(-7)^2 = 49(−7)2=49.

For x2=0x^2 = 0x2=0, the only number whose square is 000 is 000, so there is a single solution, x=0x = 0x=0.

For x2=−9x^2 = -9x2=−9, there is no real solution, because no real number squared can equal −9-9−9; every real square is 000 or positive. Three equations that look almost identical have two roots, one root, and none.

When the square sits on a group

The square-root method also works when a whole group is squared. First find the possible values of the group, then solve for xxx.

Worked example 5 Solve (x−3)2=16(x - 3)^2 = 16(x−3)2=16

Take the square root of both sides, keeping both signs:

x−3=±16=±4.x - 3 = \pm\sqrt{16} = \pm 4.x−3=±16​=±4.

That splits into two linear equations, x−3=4x - 3 = 4x−3=4 and x−3=−4x - 3 = -4x−3=−4. Add 333 to both sides of each:

x=7orx=−1.x = 7 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = -1.x=7orx=−1.

Check both against the original: (7−3)2=42=16(7 - 3)^2 = 4^2 = 16(7−3)2=42=16 and (−1−3)2=(−4)2=16(-1 - 3)^2 = (-4)^2 = 16(−1−3)2=(−4)2=16. The negative root x=−1x = -1x=−1 is easy to lose if you forget the ±\pm±, so write it down first, before you simplify.

The same reasoning solves (x−h)2=k(x - h)^2 = k(x−h)2=k. When k≥0k \ge 0k≥0, the group x−hx - hx−h must be one of the numbers whose square is kkk:

x−h=±k,x=h±k.\begin{aligned} x - h &= \pm\sqrt{k}, \\ x &= h \pm\sqrt{k}. \end{aligned}x−hx​=±k​,=h±k​.​

There are two values when k>0k > 0k>0, one value x=hx = hx=h when k=0k = 0k=0, and no real value when k<0k < 0k<0. The two signs apply to x−hx - hx−h; after adding hhh, the final roots need not be opposites.

Then x=−1+5=4x = -1 + 5 = 4x=−1+5=4 or x=−1−5=−6x = -1 - 5 = -6x=−1−5=−6. Dropping the shift gives the wrong pair ±5\pm 5±5, and a sign error on the negative root gives 666 instead of −6-6−6.

A first look at the graph

You know how a graph collects the solutions of an equation. For each xxx, plot the height y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c. When a≠0a \ne 0a=0, these points make a U-shaped curve called a parabola. It opens upward for positive aaa and downward for negative aaa.

A root makes ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0, so its point has height 000 and lies on the xxx-axis. The roots are the xxx-coordinates where the parabola meets the xxx-axis.

A parabola can cross the axis twice, touch it once at its lowest or highest point, or miss it entirely. Those pictures give two, one, or no real roots. Setting y=0y=0y=0 in these panels gives x2=4x^2=4x2=4, x2=0x^2=0x2=0, and x2=−4x^2=-4x2=−4: the same three cases as the square-root method. A later chapter teaches how to draw a parabola from its equation; here the picture helps you count its roots.

Check your understanding

A parabola opens upward, and its lowest point sits below the x-axis. How many real roots does its equation have?

Answer choices

If the lowest point is below the axis, the curve has to climb back up through the axis on both sides as it rises away from that low point, the same picture as y=x2−4y = x^2 - 4y=x2−4. That is two real roots. Compare the other two panels above: a lowest point exactly on the axis (like y=x2y = x^2y=x2) gives one root, and a lowest point already above the axis (like y=x2+4y = x^2 + 4y=x2+4) gives none, because the curve never comes back down to it.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the negative root. The equation x2=25x^2 = 25x2=25 has two solutions, x=5x = 5x=5 and x=−5x = -5x=−5, because both 525^252 and (−5)2(-5)^2(−5)2 equal 252525. Writing only x=5x = 5x=5 throws away half the answer. Always keep the ±\pm±.
  • Sign errors reading a root off a factor. The factor x−4x - 4x−4 is zero at x=4x = 4x=4, and the factor x+4x + 4x+4 is zero at x=−4x = -4x=−4. The root is the opposite sign of the number shown in the factor, so (x+4)(x + 4)(x+4) gives the root −4-4−4, never +4+4+4.
  • Using the zero-product property when the other side is not zero. From (x−2)(x+3)=6(x - 2)(x + 3) = 6(x−2)(x+3)=6 you may not write x−2=6x - 2 = 6x−2=6. The property only works when a product of factors is set equal to 000. Move everything to one side so it equals 000, then make sure that side is actually a product of factors before splitting it into two equations.
  • Expecting a real answer to a negative square. No real number squares to a negative, so x2=−9x^2 = -9x2=−9 has no real solution and −9\sqrt{-9}−9​ is not a real number. This is different from x2=0x^2 = 0x2=0, which has the single solution x=0x = 0x=0.
  • Assuming every quadratic has two solutions. A quadratic can have two real roots, one repeated real root, or no real roots, matching a parabola that crosses the axis twice, touches it once, or misses it entirely.
  • Confusing standard form with factored form. Standard form ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 hides the roots, while a product of factors set to 000 puts them on display. The zero-product property needs the factored form, so do not try to split x2+x−6=0x^2 + x - 6 = 0x2+x−6=0 directly.

Key takeaways

  • A quadratic equation is any equation you can write as ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 with a≠0a \ne 0a=0; the ax2ax^2ax2 term is what makes it quadratic, and bbb or ccc may be 000.
  • A root (solution) is a value that makes the expression equal 000. A quadratic has two, one, or no real solutions, and you can verify any candidate by substituting it.
  • Zero-product property: if a product is 000, some factor is 000. So (x−r)(x−s)=0(x - r)(x - s) = 0(x−r)(x−s)=0 gives x=rx = rx=r or x=sx = sx=s, reading each root as the value that makes its factor 000 (the opposite sign of the number shown).
  • Square-root method: x2=kx^2=kx2=k has two roots ±k\pm\sqrt{k}±k​ when k>0k>0k>0, one root (x=0x=0x=0) when k=0k=0k=0, and no real root when k<0k<0k<0. For a squared group, (x−h)2=k(x-h)^2=k(x−h)2=k gives x=h±kx=h\pm\sqrt{k}x=h±k​ when k≥0k\ge0k≥0. Keep both branches when k>0k>0k>0.
  • Factored form and standard form describe the same equation; expanding a product of factors returns the standard form.
  • Graphically, the parabola y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c meets the xxx-axis at the real roots, so a quadratic can have 000, 111, or 222 real roots.

A bit of history (optional)

An Egyptian papyrus preserved in Berlin holds a puzzle about two squares. In a familiar reconstruction of its damaged text, their combined area is 100100100, and one side is three quarters of the other. What are the two side lengths?

The answer is 888 and 666: three quarters of 888 is 666, and 82+62=64+36=1008^2 + 6^2 = 64 + 36 = 10082+62=64+36=100. The puzzle comes from the Berlin Papyrus, about 3,8003{,}8003,800 years old. Its surviving calculation uses square roots, long before our notation ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 existed.

There is a historical caution: parts of the papyrus are missing, so the familiar two-square wording includes modern reconstruction. It gives us a glimpse of ancient problem solving, rather than a complete page we can read without uncertainty.

Sources: Mathematical Association of America, account of ancient quadratic equations, section 1.2.1 and Scott Williams, University at Buffalo, on the Berlin Papyrus.

Introduction to Quadratics

Intuition

You have spent several chapters solving equations where the unknown appears only to the first power, like 3x+5=203x + 5 = 203x+5=20, and a straightforward one like that has exactly one answer. Many ordinary questions refuse to stay that simple. Ask for the side of a square whose area is 252525 and you are really asking what number times itself gives 252525, which is x2=25x^2 = 25x2=25. When the unknown multiplies itself and that squared term is still there once you have simplified fully, the old rules are no longer enough. This lesson is your first look at these equations, called quadratic equations, and at two direct ways to solve the simplest of them.

That square example already hints at something new: solved as pure algebra, x2=25x^2 = 25x2=25 has two answers, x=5x = 5x=5 and x=−5x = -5x=−5, since (−5)2=25(-5)^2 = 25(−5)2=25 too. Only 555 can be the length of a real square’s side, so the context throws one answer away, but the equation itself is still telling the truth about both. Watching for that gap between “every number that solves the equation” and “every number that makes sense in the problem” is part of what this lesson teaches.

Learning goals

  • Recognize ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 with a≠0a \ne 0a=0 as quadratic
  • Verify a root by substituting it back
  • Apply the zero-product property to a factored equation
  • Solve x2=kx^2 = kx2=k as x=±kx = \pm\sqrt{k}x=±k​ when k≥0k \ge 0k≥0, never dropping the sign
  • Extend the square-root method to a squared group
  • Count 000, 111, or 222 real roots where a parabola meets the xxx-axis

What makes an equation quadratic

A quadratic equation is one that can be written in the form

ax2+bx+c=0,ax^2 + bx + c = 0,ax2+bx+c=0,

where aaa, bbb, and ccc are fixed numbers and a≠0a \ne 0a=0. This is called standard form. Every term is moved to one side so the other side is 000, and the terms are written in order of decreasing power, the squared term first. The three numbers have names. We call aaa the leading coefficient, bbb the coefficient of the xxx term, and ccc the constant term.

The one requirement you cannot drop is a≠0a \ne 0a=0. The word quadratic comes from quadratus, the Latin for “squared,” and it is the ax2ax^2ax2 term that makes an equation quadratic. If aaa were 000, that term would vanish and leave bx+c=0bx + c = 0bx+c=0, an ordinary linear equation of the kind you already solve. So a≠0a \ne 0a=0 is exactly what separates a genuine quadratic from a linear equation. There is no such rule for bbb or ccc: either or both may be zero, and the equation is still quadratic as long as the x2x^2x2 term survives.

Many quadratics do not arrive in standard form. Part of the skill is recognizing one however it is dressed up, then rearranging it. To do that, move every term to the left with the same balancing steps you used on linear equations. Then collect like terms until the right side reads 000.

The middle two rows look incomplete, yet both are quadratic. Rewriting 2x2=82x^2 = 82x2=8 as 2x2−8=02x^2 - 8 = 02x2−8=0 shows b=0b = 0b=0, and rewriting x2=7xx^2 = 7xx2=7x as x2−7x=0x^2 - 7x = 0x2−7x=0 shows c=0c = 0c=0. A missing xxx term or a missing constant is fine; a missing x2x^2x2 term is not.

An x2x^2x2 can also disappear once you simplify, even though it was there to begin with. Take x2+3x=x2+7x^2 + 3x = x^2 + 7x2+3x=x2+7. Subtract x2x^2x2 from both sides:

3x=7.3x = 7.3x=7.

The squared terms cancel completely, leaving a one-step linear equation. The equation was never quadratic; it only looked that way before you moved everything to one side. Always simplify fully before you decide.

Worked example 1 Put 3x2−5=x3x^2 - 5 = x3x2−5=x in standard form

The equation has an x2x^2x2 term, so it should be quadratic once it is tidied up. Move every term to the left so the right side becomes 000. Subtract xxx from both sides:

3x2−5−x=0.3x^2 - 5 - x = 0.3x2−5−x=0.

Now write the terms in order of decreasing power, the squared term first, then the xxx term, then the constant:

3x2−x−5=0.3x^2 - x - 5 = 0.3x2−x−5=0.

Reading off the coefficients gives a=3a = 3a=3, b=−1b = -1b=−1, and c=−5c = -5c=−5. Because a=3≠0a = 3 \ne 0a=3=0, the equation is genuinely quadratic.

What it means to be a solution

A solution of an equation, also called a root, is a value of the variable that makes the equation true. For a quadratic in standard form, a root is a number you can put in place of xxx so that ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c works out to exactly 000. Nothing about that idea is new; it is the same meaning of “solution” you have used since your first linear equation. What is new is how many solutions to expect.

A linear equation like 3x+5=203x + 5 = 203x+5=20 has exactly one solution. A quadratic can have two solutions, one solution, or no real solution at all. You will see why once you have a method in hand, but hold on to the possibility from the start. Finding one root does not mean you are done, because a second one may be waiting. The surest way to test a candidate is to substitute it and check.

Worked example 2 Check whether −3-3−3 and 111 are roots of x2+x−6=0x^2 + x - 6 = 0x2+x−6=0

To test a value, substitute it for xxx and see whether the left side lands on 000.

Start with x=−3x = -3x=−3. Squaring a negative gives a positive, so (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9:

(−3)2+(−3)−6=9−3−6=0.(-3)^2 + (-3) - 6 = 9 - 3 - 6 = 0.(−3)2+(−3)−6=9−3−6=0.

The result is 000, so x=−3x = -3x=−3 is a root. Now test x=1x = 1x=1:

(1)2+(1)−6=1+1−6=−4.(1)^2 + (1) - 6 = 1 + 1 - 6 = -4.(1)2+(1)−6=1+1−6=−4.

That is not 000, so x=1x = 1x=1 is not a root. Substitution alone cannot tell you every root, only whether a guess is one; you will meet a method later in this lesson that finds both roots directly. For now, check that x=2x = 2x=2 also works: 22+2−6=02^2 + 2 - 6 = 022+2−6=0. So this equation’s two solutions are −3-3−3 and 222.

The zero-product property

Substitution tells you whether a guess is a root, but it will not hand you the roots to begin with. For that you need a method, and the first one rests on a single fact about 000 so familiar you may never have said it out loud. That fact is this: if a product of numbers equals 000, then at least one of the numbers must be 000. This is the zero-product property. Multiplying by 000 always gives 000, and nothing else does, so two nonzero numbers can never multiply to 000.

That one-sentence reason can be turned into a short formal argument using reciprocals. Read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full proof

The property turns a factored quadratic into two easy linear equations. Suppose an equation arrives already broken into a product of factors set equal to zero, such as

(x−2)(x+3)=0.(x - 2)(x + 3) = 0.(x−2)(x+3)=0.

The left side is a product of the two factors x−2x - 2x−2 and x+3x + 3x+3. By the zero-product property that product is 000 exactly when one of the factors is 000, so the equation splits into two:

x−2=0orx+3=0.x - 2 = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x + 3 = 0.x−2=0orx+3=0.

Each piece is a linear equation you can solve at a glance, giving x=2x = 2x=2 or x=−3x = -3x=−3. Those are the two roots. For a factor written as x−rx - rx−r, this gives a shortcut: the factor x−2x - 2x−2 is zero when x=2x = 2x=2, and the factor x+3x + 3x+3, which is x−(−3)x - (-3)x−(−3), is zero when x=−3x = -3x=−3. The number shown in the factor and the root have opposite signs, and that sign flip is the most common place to slip. The shortcut only reads off this cleanly when the coefficient of xxx is 111. For any other factor, set it equal to 000 and solve like a normal one-step or two-step equation, as the next example shows.

These two forms, the factored (x−2)(x+3)=0(x - 2)(x + 3) = 0(x−2)(x+3)=0 and the standard form, are the same equation wearing different clothes. Expanding the product the way you did in the chapter on expanding and factoring,

(x−2)(x+3)=x2+3x−2x−6=x2+x−6,(x - 2)(x + 3) = x^2 + 3x - 2x - 6 = x^2 + x - 6,(x−2)(x+3)=x2+3x−2x−6=x2+x−6,

so (x−2)(x+3)=0(x - 2)(x + 3) = 0(x−2)(x+3)=0 is just x2+x−6=0x^2 + x - 6 = 0x2+x−6=0 rewritten. The factored form is the useful one, because it puts the roots on display, while the expanded standard form hides them. Turning a standard-form quadratic back into a product of factors is the subject of the next few lessons. Here you are handed the factored form and asked only to finish the job.

Worked example 3 Solve (2x−1)(x+4)=0(2x - 1)(x + 4) = 0(2x−1)(x+4)=0

The left side is already a product of two factors, so apply the zero-product property and set each factor to 000:

2x−1=0orx+4=0.2x - 1 = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x + 4 = 0.2x−1=0orx+4=0.

Solve the first as a one-step linear equation: 2x=12x = 12x=1, so x=12x = \tfrac{1}{2}x=21​. The second gives x=−4x = -4x=−4. The two roots are

x=12orx=−4.x = \tfrac{1}{2} \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = -4.x=21​orx=−4.

A factor with a coefficient, like 2x−12x - 12x−1, still produces a single root; you just solve for xxx as usual. Check the first root: 2(12)−1=02(\tfrac{1}{2}) - 1 = 02(21​)−1=0, so the whole product is 000, as required.

Solve (x−5)(x+2)=0(x - 5)(x + 2) = 0(x−5)(x+2)=0.

Answer choices

By the zero-product property, set each factor to 000.

x−5=0orx+2=0x - 5 = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x + 2 = 0x−5=0orx+2=0

Solving gives x=5x = 5x=5 or x=−2x = -2x=−2. Each root is the opposite sign of the number written in its factor, so x+2x + 2x+2 produces the root −2-2−2, not +2+2+2.

Solving by taking square roots

The zero-product property needs a factored form. A second method handles quadratics with no middle bxbxbx term directly. Consider

x2=kx^2 = kx2=k

for some number kkk. You are looking for every number whose square is kkk. Square roots almost answer this, but there is a subtlety that trips up nearly everyone, and it comes from a fact about squaring you already know. A negative times a negative is positive, so a number and its opposite have the same square. For instance 32=93^2 = 932=9, and (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9 as well.

That means x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9 has two solutions, x=3x = 3x=3 and x=−3x = -3x=−3, not just the positive one. We record both at once by writing, for k≥0k \ge 0k≥0,

x=±k,x = \pm\sqrt{k},x=±k​,

read ”xxx equals plus or minus the square root of kkk.” The ±\pm± is not decoration; it captures the second root that a plain square root would hide. The condition k≥0k \ge 0k≥0 matters: how many solutions you actually get depends on the sign of kkk. When kkk is positive, there are two real square roots, k\sqrt{k}k​ and −k-\sqrt{k}−k​, so the equation has two solutions. When kkk is zero, the only number whose square is 000 is 000 itself, so there is exactly one solution, x=0x = 0x=0. In that case the two roots have merged into a single repeated one. When kkk is negative, there is no real solution at all. That is because squaring any real number, positive or negative, gives a result that is zero or positive and never negative. (Every number in this lesson is a real number; a different kind of number, covered in a later course, can square to a negative.) This is the first place you can see all three possibilities the definition promised: two roots, one root, or none.

Worked example 4 Solve x2=49x^2 = 49x2=49, then x2=0x^2 = 0x2=0 and x2=−9x^2 = -9x2=−9

For x2=49x^2 = 49x2=49, take the square root of both sides and keep both signs:

x=±49=±7,x = \pm\sqrt{49} = \pm 7,x=±49​=±7,

so x=7x = 7x=7 or x=−7x = -7x=−7. Both check out, since 72=497^2 = 4972=49 and (−7)2=49(-7)^2 = 49(−7)2=49.

For x2=0x^2 = 0x2=0, the only number whose square is 000 is 000, so there is a single solution, x=0x = 0x=0.

For x2=−9x^2 = -9x2=−9, there is no real solution, because no real number squared can equal −9-9−9; every real square is 000 or positive. Three equations that look almost identical have two roots, one root, and none.

When the square sits on a group

The same move works when the thing being squared is not xxx alone but a whole expression. An equation like

(x−h)2=k(x - h)^2 = k(x−h)2=k

is solved by taking square roots of both sides, again keeping the ±\pm±:

x−h=±k.x - h = \pm\sqrt{k}.x−h=±k​.

A single balancing step finishes the job: x=h±kx = h \pm\sqrt{k}x=h±k​. The same domain condition from before still applies, since x−hx - hx−h is just standing in for the whole squared quantity: two values when k>0k > 0k>0, the single value x=hx = hx=h when k=0k = 0k=0, and no real solution when k<0k < 0k<0. Whatever sits inside the square, treat it as one quantity, and that quantity has to be one of the numbers whose square is kkk.

Worked example 5 Solve (x−3)2=16(x - 3)^2 = 16(x−3)2=16

Take the square root of both sides, keeping both signs:

x−3=±16=±4.x - 3 = \pm\sqrt{16} = \pm 4.x−3=±16​=±4.

That splits into two linear equations, x−3=4x - 3 = 4x−3=4 and x−3=−4x - 3 = -4x−3=−4. Add 333 to both sides of each:

x=7orx=−1.x = 7 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = -1.x=7orx=−1.

Check both against the original: (7−3)2=42=16(7 - 3)^2 = 4^2 = 16(7−3)2=42=16 and (−1−3)2=(−4)2=16(-1 - 3)^2 = (-4)^2 = 16(−1−3)2=(−4)2=16. The negative root x=−1x = -1x=−1 is easy to lose if you forget the ±\pm±, so write it down first, before you simplify.

Then x=−1+5=4x = -1 + 5 = 4x=−1+5=4 or x=−1−5=−6x = -1 - 5 = -6x=−1−5=−6. Dropping the shift gives the wrong pair ±5\pm 5±5, and a sign error on the negative root gives 666 instead of −6-6−6.

A first look at the graph

Every quadratic has a picture, and it explains at a glance why the number of solutions can be two, one, or none. Take the expression ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c, and for each value of xxx plot the point whose height yyy equals that expression. The points trace a smooth U-shaped curve called a parabola, opening upward when aaa is positive and downward when aaa is negative. Solving ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 means finding the xxx values where y=0y = 0y=0, and y=0y = 0y=0 is the horizontal axis. So the real roots of a quadratic are exactly the points where its parabola meets the xxx-axis.

Same shape, three positions relative to the axis, three different answers: a parabola can meet the xxx-axis in two places, giving two roots; just touch it at its lowest (or highest) point, giving one repeated root; or float entirely above or below it, giving no real roots. That is the same three-way split you met with x2=kx^2 = kx2=k, now drawn as a picture. Drawing parabolas carefully, locating the lowest point, and reading off their features is the work of a later chapter. For now the picture is only here to make the count of solutions something you can see rather than only trust.

Check your understanding

A parabola opens upward, and its lowest point sits below the x-axis. How many real roots does its equation have?

Answer choices

If the lowest point is below the axis, the curve has to climb back up through the axis on both sides as it rises away from that low point, the same picture as y=x2−4y = x^2 - 4y=x2−4. That is two real roots. Compare the other two panels above: a lowest point exactly on the axis (like y=x2y = x^2y=x2) gives one root, and a lowest point already above the axis (like y=x2+4y = x^2 + 4y=x2+4) gives none, because the curve never comes back down to it.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the negative root. The equation x2=25x^2 = 25x2=25 has two solutions, x=5x = 5x=5 and x=−5x = -5x=−5, because both 525^252 and (−5)2(-5)^2(−5)2 equal 252525. Writing only x=5x = 5x=5 throws away half the answer. Always keep the ±\pm±.
  • Sign errors reading a root off a factor. The factor x−4x - 4x−4 is zero at x=4x = 4x=4, and the factor x+4x + 4x+4 is zero at x=−4x = -4x=−4. The root is the opposite sign of the number shown in the factor, so (x+4)(x + 4)(x+4) gives the root −4-4−4, never +4+4+4.
  • Using the zero-product property when the other side is not zero. From (x−2)(x+3)=6(x - 2)(x + 3) = 6(x−2)(x+3)=6 you may not write x−2=6x - 2 = 6x−2=6. The property only works when a product of factors is set equal to 000. Move everything to one side so it equals 000, then make sure that side is actually a product of factors before splitting it into two equations.
  • Expecting a real answer to a negative square. No real number squares to a negative, so x2=−9x^2 = -9x2=−9 has no real solution and −9\sqrt{-9}−9​ is not a real number. This is different from x2=0x^2 = 0x2=0, which has the single solution x=0x = 0x=0.
  • Assuming every quadratic has two solutions. A quadratic can have two roots, one repeated root, or none at all, matching a parabola that crosses the axis twice, touches it once, or misses it entirely.
  • Confusing standard form with factored form. Standard form ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 hides the roots, while a product of factors set to 000 puts them on display. The zero-product property needs the factored form, so do not try to split x2+x−6=0x^2 + x - 6 = 0x2+x−6=0 directly.

Key takeaways

  • A quadratic equation is any equation you can write as ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 with a≠0a \ne 0a=0; the ax2ax^2ax2 term is what makes it quadratic, and bbb or ccc may be 000.
  • A root (solution) is a value that makes the expression equal 000. A quadratic has two, one, or no real solutions, and you can verify any candidate by substituting it.
  • Zero-product property: if a product is 000, some factor is 000. So (x−r)(x−s)=0(x - r)(x - s) = 0(x−r)(x−s)=0 gives x=rx = rx=r or x=sx = sx=s, reading each root as the value that makes its factor 000 (the opposite sign of the number shown).
  • Square-root method: x2=kx^2 = kx2=k gives x=±kx = \pm\sqrt{k}x=±k​, with two roots when k>0k > 0k>0, one root (x=0x = 0x=0) when k=0k = 0k=0, and no real root when k<0k < 0k<0. For a squared group, (x−h)2=k(x - h)^2 = k(x−h)2=k gives x=h±kx = h \pm\sqrt{k}x=h±k​. Never drop the ±\pm±.
  • Factored form and standard form describe the same equation; expanding a product of factors returns the standard form.
  • Graphically, the parabola y=ax2+bx+cy = ax^2 + bx + cy=ax2+bx+c meets the xxx-axis at the real roots, so a quadratic can have 000, 111, or 222 real roots.

Why a product is zero only when a factor is zero

Why a product is zero only when a factor is zero#

Suppose AAA and BBB are numbers with A⋅B=0A \cdot B = 0A⋅B=0, and suppose the first factor is not zero, so A≠0A \ne 0A=0. Because A≠0A \ne 0A=0, it has a reciprocal 1A\tfrac{1}{A}A1​. Multiply both sides of the equation A⋅B=0A \cdot B = 0A⋅B=0 by that reciprocal:

1A⋅(A⋅B)=1A⋅0.\tfrac{1}{A} \cdot (A \cdot B) = \tfrac{1}{A} \cdot 0.A1​⋅(A⋅B)=A1​⋅0.

On the left, 1A⋅A=1\tfrac{1}{A} \cdot A = 1A1​⋅A=1, so the left side simplifies to BBB. On the right, anything times 000 is 000. So the equation collapses to B=0B = 0B=0. In words, if the first factor is not zero, the second one is forced to be zero. Running the same argument with the roles of AAA and BBB swapped shows that if B≠0B \ne 0B=0 then A=0A = 0A=0. Either way at least one factor is 000, which is the claim. The reverse direction needs no work: if one factor is already 000, the product is 000 no matter what the other factor is.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

A scribe in Egypt once set down a puzzle about two squares.

His papyrus is nearly four thousand years old, and a museum in Berlin keeps it today. The puzzle runs to three lines. Two squares together have a combined area of 100100100. The side of one of them is three quarters of the side of the other. Find both sides.

Ask why that puzzle is difficult and you arrive at the definition this lesson opened with. An area is a side multiplied by itself. So the moment you reach for the larger side, you have reached for a number that gets squared, and the simple add, subtract, multiply, or divide moves that solve a linear equation cannot undo a square by themselves. The two sides are 888 and 666, and the arithmetic confirms it, since 646464 and 363636 do make 100100100.

It is one of the oldest surviving problems of this kind, told entirely as a story about squares, recorded thousands of years before anyone had the shape ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 to pour it into. That shape is the tool you gained today: whenever a question keeps a squared term after you simplify, whether it is about a side, a second, or a dollar, it is asking the same kind of thing this puzzle asked.

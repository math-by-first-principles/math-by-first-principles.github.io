The Quadratic Formula

Intuition

Completing the square finishes any quadratic, but it makes you repeat the same steps every time. Divide, move the constant, halve the coefficient, square it, add it, and take a root. Repeating identical steps is a signal that a shortcut is hiding. Run those steps once on the general equation ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0, carrying the letters aaa, bbb, and ccc instead of specific numbers. Out comes a single formula that solves any quadratic the instant you know its three coefficients. This lesson derives that formula and shows how to use it.

Learning goals

  • Derive the quadratic formula by completing the square on ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0
  • Write a quadratic in standard form and read off aaa, bbb, ccc with their signs
  • Apply the formula, simplifying radicals and reducing the fraction to find the roots
  • Use D=b2−4acD = b^2 - 4acD=b2−4ac to predict real, repeated, or complex roots before solving

Deriving the formula by completing the square

The quadratic formula is not a rule to accept on faith. It is exactly what completing the square gives when you feed it the general equation. Every move below is one you already made in the last lesson on specific numbers, for example a=2a = 2a=2, b=5b = 5b=5, c=−3c = -3c=−3 in 2x2+5x−3=02x^2 + 5x - 3 = 02x2+5x−3=0. The only new thing is that now those numbers stay as letters all the way through.

Deriving the quadratic formula from ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0#

Start from the general quadratic equation, where aaa, bbb, and ccc are real numbers with a≠0a \ne 0a=0. If aaa were 000 there would be no x2x^2x2 term and the equation would not be quadratic at all:

ax2+bx+c=0.ax^2 + bx + c = 0.ax2+bx+c=0.

Completing the square needs the leading coefficient to be 111, so divide every term by aaa. Because a≠0a \ne 0a=0, dividing by it is allowed:

x2+bax+ca=0.x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{c}{a} = 0.x2+ab​x+ac​=0.

Move the constant term to the right side, so the left holds only the two terms that carry xxx:

x2+bax=−ca.x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}.x2+ab​x=−ac​.

Now complete the square on the left. Half of the coefficient of xxx is b2a\dfrac{b}{2a}2ab​, and squaring it gives b24a2\dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}4a2b2​. Add that constant to both sides, which keeps the equation balanced:

x2+bax+b24a2=b24a2−ca.x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a}.x2+ab​x+4a2b2​=4a2b2​−ac​.

The left side is now a perfect square. Since half of ba\tfrac{b}{a}ab​ is b2a\tfrac{b}{2a}2ab​, it factors as

(x+b2a)2=b24a2−ca.\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a}.(x+2ab​)2=4a2b2​−ac​.

Combine the right side into one fraction. The common denominator is 4a24a^24a2, and rewriting ca=4ac4a2\dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{4ac}{4a^2}ac​=4a24ac​ lets the two pieces subtract cleanly:

(x+b2a)2=b24a2−4ac4a2=b2−4ac4a2.\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{4ac}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}.(x+2ab​)2=4a2b2​−4a24ac​=4a2b2−4ac​.

Take the square root of both sides. Squaring erases a sign, so undoing it creates two possible equations, one for each sign the squared side could have had; attach a ±\pm± to capture both, and split the root over numerator and denominator. (x\sqrt{\phantom{x}}x​ itself always names one fixed value, never two: 9\sqrt{9}9​ names 333 and not −3-3−3, exactly as established in Imaginary Numbers. The two signs come from the ±\pm±, not from the radical.) Since 4a24a^24a2 is a perfect square, its root simplifies the denominator to 2a2a2a (the note after this derivation explains the one subtlety in that step):

x+b2a=±b2−4ac4a2=±b2−4ac2a.x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.x+2ab​=±4a2b2−4ac​​=±2ab2−4ac​​.

Finally subtract b2a\dfrac{b}{2a}2ab​ from both sides to isolate xxx:

x=−b2a±b2−4ac2a.x = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.x=−2ab​±2ab2−4ac​​.

Both terms on the right already share the denominator 2a2a2a, so they merge into a single fraction, and that fraction is the quadratic formula:

x=−b±b2−4ac2a.x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​.

∎

Why the square root of 4a² becomes 2a, not 2 times the absolute value of a

Strictly, 4a2=2∣a∣\sqrt{4a^2} = 2\lvert a\rvert4a2​=2∣a∣, since a square root is never negative. If aaa were negative, 2a2a2a itself would be negative, while 2∣a∣2\lvert a\rvert2∣a∣ is the positive number that squares back to 4a24a^24a2. The line right above already carries a ±\pm±, though, so swapping 2∣a∣2\lvert a\rvert2∣a∣ for 2a2a2a only swaps which sign the ±\pm± calls “plus.” It does not change which two numbers the formula produces, which is why the derivation can write the simpler 2a2a2a and still land on the correct formula.

Because the derivation never assumed anything about the real numbers aaa, bbb, and ccc beyond a≠0a \ne 0a=0, the formula holds for every quadratic equation. Whatever completing the square would have found, the formula finds too, with none of the repeated setup.

What the formula says

Here is the result on its own, worth memorizing because you will use it constantly:

ax2+bx+c=0⟹x=−b±b2−4ac2a.ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.ax2+bx+c=0⟹x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​.

The single ±\pm± packs both solutions into one line. Read with a plus it gives one root; read with a minus it gives the other. The one part people misread is the denominator: the 2a2a2a divides the entire numerator, both the −b-b−b and the b2−4ac\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}b2−4ac​ together, not just one piece of it.

To use the formula you first need the three numbers aaa, bbb, and ccc. They are the coefficients when the equation is written in standard form, with everything on one side and zero on the other:

ax2+bx+c=0.ax^2 + bx + c = 0.ax2+bx+c=0.

Here aaa is the coefficient of x2x^2x2, bbb is the coefficient of xxx, and ccc is the constant term. Read them with their signs. In 3x2−5x+2=03x^2 - 5x + 2 = 03x2−5x+2=0 the coefficients are a=3a = 3a=3, b=−5b = -5b=−5, and c=2c = 2c=2; the minus sign belongs to bbb. Getting a sign wrong here is the fastest way to a wrong answer, so it is worth a moment of care before you substitute.

The discriminant

Look again at the quantity under the square root:

D=b2−4ac.D = b^2 - 4ac.D=b2−4ac.

This is called the discriminant, and it alone decides what kind of roots a quadratic has. (If you compare it with the quantity RRR from the last lesson’s derivation, for a=1a = 1a=1 this one equals 4R4R4R. Multiplying by a positive number never flips a sign, so the same three cases below still hold.) There are exactly three cases, and each comes straight from what a square root does to a positive number, to zero, and to a negative number.

If D>0D > 0D>0, then D\sqrt{D}D​ is a nonzero real number, and the ±\pm± pulls it once above and once below −b-b−b, giving two different real roots.

If D=0D = 0D=0, then D=0\sqrt{D} = 0D​=0, and adding or subtracting 000 makes no difference, so the two roots fall together into one repeated real root, x=−b2ax = -\dfrac{b}{2a}x=−2ab​.

If D<0D < 0D<0, then D\sqrt{D}D​ is the square root of a negative number, which is imaginary. Writing D=i∣D∣\sqrt{D} = i\sqrt{\lvert D\rvert}D​=i∣D∣​ turns the answer into two complex roots, a conjugate pair −b2a±∣D∣2a i-\dfrac{b}{2a} \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{\lvert D\rvert}}{2a}\,i−2ab​±2a∣D∣​​i. Before this chapter that case simply had no answer; now that iii is available, it does.

Discriminant D=b2−4acD = b^2 - 4acD=b2−4acThe square root D\sqrt{D}D​Roots of the quadratic
D>0D > 0D>0a nonzero real numbertwo distinct real roots
D=0D = 0D=0zeroone repeated real root
D<0D < 0D<0imaginarytwo complex conjugate roots

You can name the type of roots from DDD alone, without ever finishing the solve.

Applying the formula

With the formula, the discriminant, and the imaginary unit in hand, one procedure now solves any quadratic. Put the equation in standard form, read off aaa, bbb, and ccc with their signs, substitute, and simplify.

Worked example 1 Solve 2x2+5x−3=02x^2 + 5x - 3 = 02x2+5x−3=0

The equation is already in standard form, so read off a=2a = 2a=2, b=5b = 5b=5, and c=−3c = -3c=−3, keeping the sign on ccc. Substitute into the formula, watching the double negative in −4ac-4ac−4ac:

x=−5±52−4(2)(−3)2(2)=−5±25+244.x = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{5^2 - 4(2)(-3)}}{2(2)} = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{25 + 24}}{4}.x=2(2)−5±52−4(2)(−3)​​=4−5±25+24​​.

The term −4(2)(−3)=+24-4(2)(-3) = +24−4(2)(−3)=+24 is positive because ccc was negative, so the discriminant is 25+24=4925 + 24 = 4925+24=49, a perfect square:

x=−5±494=−5±74.x = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{49}}{4} = \frac{-5 \pm 7}{4}.x=4−5±49​​=4−5±7​.

Now split the ±\pm± into its two cases:

x=−5+74=24=12,x=−5−74=−124=−3.x = \frac{-5 + 7}{4} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad x = \frac{-5 - 7}{4} = \frac{-12}{4} = -3.x=4−5+7​=42​=21​,x=4−5−7​=4−12​=−3.

The two roots are x=12x = \tfrac{1}{2}x=21​ and x=−3x = -3x=−3. When the discriminant is a perfect square, the roots come out rational, exactly the case that ordinary factoring could also have caught.

Worked example 2 Solve 3x2−6x+1=03x^2 - 6x + 1 = 03x2−6x+1=0

Read off a=3a = 3a=3, b=−6b = -6b=−6, and c=1c = 1c=1. Because b=−6b = -6b=−6, the term −b-b−b is +6+6+6, and b2=(−6)2=36b^2 = (-6)^2 = 36b2=(−6)2=36 is positive; a common slip is to write −36-36−36 here. Substitute:

x=−(−6)±(−6)2−4(3)(1)2(3)=6±36−126=6±246.x = \frac{-(-6) \pm \sqrt{(-6)^2 - 4(3)(1)}}{2(3)} = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 12}}{6} = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{24}}{6}.x=2(3)−(−6)±(−6)2−4(3)(1)​​=66±36−12​​=66±24​​.

The discriminant 242424 is not a perfect square, so the roots are irrational. Simplify the radical using 24=4×624 = 4 \times 624=4×6 and 4=2\sqrt{4} = 24​=2:

x=6±266.x = \frac{6 \pm 2\sqrt{6}}{6}.x=66±26​​.

Every term in the fraction shares a factor of 222, so divide the numerator and denominator by 222 to reduce:

x=2(3±6)6=3±63.x = \frac{2\left(3 \pm \sqrt{6}\right)}{6} = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{6}}{3}.x=62(3±6​)​=33±6​​.

The roots are x=3+63x = \dfrac{3 + \sqrt{6}}{3}x=33+6​​ and x=3−63x = \dfrac{3 - \sqrt{6}}{3}x=33−6​​, both real and irrational. Reduce only by a factor common to −b-b−b, the radical, and the denominator; here that shared factor is 222.

The formula does not stop at real roots. When the discriminant is negative, the same substitution runs straight into a square root of a negative number, which the imaginary unit iii handles.

Worked example 3 Solve x2+4x+13=0x^2 + 4x + 13 = 0x2+4x+13=0 (complex roots)

Read off a=1a = 1a=1, b=4b = 4b=4, and c=13c = 13c=13, and substitute:

x=−4±42−4(1)(13)2(1)=−4±16−522=−4±−362.x = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{4^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2(1)} = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2} = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2}.x=2(1)−4±42−4(1)(13)​​=2−4±16−52​​=2−4±−36​​.

The discriminant is −36-36−36, negative, so the roots are complex. Rewrite the root of the negative with iii, using −36=i36=6i\sqrt{-36} = i\sqrt{36} = 6i−36​=i36​=6i:

x=−4±6i2.x = \frac{-4 \pm 6i}{2}.x=2−4±6i​.

Divide both parts of the numerator by 222:

x=−42±6i2=−2±3i.x = \frac{-4}{2} \pm \frac{6i}{2} = -2 \pm 3i.x=2−4​±26i​=−2±3i.

The roots are the conjugate pair x=−2+3ix = -2 + 3ix=−2+3i and x=−2−3ix = -2 - 3ix=−2−3i. Whenever the discriminant is negative, the two roots always arrive as a conjugate pair like this, matching in real part and opposite in imaginary part.

Worked example 4 Solve 4x2−12x+9=04x^2 - 12x + 9 = 04x2−12x+9=0 (a repeated root)

Read off a=4a = 4a=4, b=−12b = -12b=−12, and c=9c = 9c=9. Substitute, being careful that −b=12-b = 12−b=12 and b2=(−12)2=144b^2 = (-12)^2 = 144b2=(−12)2=144:

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Dividing only part of the numerator by 2a2a2a. The whole numerator −b±b2−4ac-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}−b±b2−4ac​ sits over 2a2a2a. Writing −b±b2−4ac2a-b \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}−b±2ab2−4ac​​, with only the radical divided, drops the division on the −b-b−b and gives a wrong root.
  • Mishandling signs when substituting. The numerator starts with −b-b−b, the opposite of bbb: if b=−6b = -6b=−6 then −b=+6-b = +6−b=+6, not −6-6−6. Squaring always gives a nonnegative result, so b2b^2b2 is never negative. And −4ac-4ac−4ac turns positive whenever aaa and ccc have opposite signs; in 2x2+5x−3=02x^2 + 5x - 3 = 02x2+5x−3=0 the discriminant is 25−4(2)(−3)=25+24=4925 - 4(2)(-3) = 25 + 24 = 4925−4(2)(−3)=25+24=49, not 25−2425 - 2425−24.
  • Dropping the ±\pm±. The formula produces two roots, one with +++ and one with −-− (the same value twice when D=0D = 0D=0). Keeping only the plus misses a root whenever D≠0D \ne 0D=0.
  • Calling a negative discriminant “no solution.” With iii available, D<0D < 0D<0 gives a complex conjugate pair, not nothing. Write −36=6i\sqrt{-36} = 6i−36​=6i and finish.
  • Substituting before standard form. Read aaa, bbb, and ccc only after the equation is set equal to 000. Coefficients pulled from x2=3x+10x^2 = 3x + 10x2=3x+10 carry the wrong signs.

A bit of history (optional)

Look at the four in b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac and ask where it came from. Your derivation made it by hand. You put two fractions over the common denominator 4a24a^24a2, and the four survived into the answer. There is an older route that never meets a fraction at all.

Around the year 900, in India, a mathematician named Shridhara wrote down a rule for quadratics. Do not divide by aaa, it says. Multiply the whole equation by four times aaa instead. Then add the square of the original middle coefficient to both sides, and take the root.

Follow that on ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 and watch what happens. Multiplying by 4a4a4a gives 4a2x2+4abx+4ac=04a^2x^2 + 4abx + 4ac = 04a2x2+4abx+4ac=0, which is (2ax)2+2(2ax)b+4ac=0(2ax)^2 + 2(2ax)b + 4ac = 0(2ax)2+2(2ax)b+4ac=0. Move 4ac4ac4ac to the right side, then add b2b^2b2 to both sides. The left is now the exact square (2ax+b)2(2ax + b)^2(2ax+b)2, with no halves anywhere in it, and the right side is b2−4acb^2 - 4acb2−4ac. Your discriminant falls straight out of the multiplier he chose.

Both routes end on the same line, and neither is more correct than the other. The four in the discriminant is just the price of clearing the fraction, paid at the start rather than at the end.

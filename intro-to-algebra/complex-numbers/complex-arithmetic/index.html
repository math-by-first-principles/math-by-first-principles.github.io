Arithmetic with Complex Numbers Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra I. You can skip it.

Intuition

Try (2+3i)+(4−i)(2 + 3i) + (4 - i)(2+3i)+(4−i). The real parts add to 2+4=62 + 4 = 62+4=6, and the coefficients of iii add to 3+(−1)=23 + (-1) = 23+(−1)=2, so the sum is 6+2i6 + 2i6+2i. That is almost the whole lesson: treat iii like an ordinary variable. Every operation, add, subtract, multiply, and divide, lands back on a clean answer of the form a+bia + bia+bi. The one moment anything new happens is when a product creates i2i^2i2; replace it with −1-1−1 and keep going. Division looks harder, but it turns out to be the same conjugate trick you used to rationalize a denominator. That trick is aimed now at clearing iii instead of a square root.

Learning goals

  • Write a complex number in standard form a+bia + bia+bi
  • Add and subtract by combining the parts separately
  • Multiply like binomials, replacing i2i^2i2 with −1-1−1
  • Use the conjugate, since (a+bi)(a−bi)(a + bi)(a - bi)(a+bi)(a−bi) is real
  • Divide by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the conjugate
  • Square with (a+bi)2=(a2−b2)+2abi(a + bi)^2 = (a^2 - b^2) + 2abi(a+bi)2=(a2−b2)+2abi

The standard form a + bi

A complex number is a number written in the standard form

z=a+bi,z = a + bi,z=a+bi,

where aaa and bbb are real numbers and iii is the imaginary unit with i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1. The real number aaa is the real part of zzz, written Re⁡(z)\operatorname{Re}(z)Re(z), and the real number bbb is the imaginary part, written Im⁡(z)\operatorname{Im}(z)Im(z). The imaginary part is the coefficient bbb, not the term bibibi: in 2+3i2 + 3i2+3i the real part is 222 and the imaginary part is 333. A real number is the case b=0b = 0b=0, and a pure imaginary number like 3i3i3i is the case a=0a = 0a=0; standard form covers both, and everything between, in one shape.

Because the two parts play different roles, two complex numbers count as equal only when they match part for part: a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di exactly when a=ca = ca=c and b=db = db=d. That is not an extra rule bolted on; it follows from iii being non-real, a fact the next lessons lean on constantly.

The equality rule above is not just convenient, it is forced. No other way for a+bia + bia+bi and c+dic + dic+di to agree is possible once iii is not a real number. The full argument is in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Adding and subtracting

Addition and subtraction are the easiest operations, because the real and imaginary parts never interact. You simply combine them separately. For (3+i)+(1+2i)(3 + i) + (1 + 2i)(3+i)+(1+2i), that means adding 3+1=43 + 1 = 43+1=4 and 1+2=31 + 2 = 31+2=3 to get 4+3i4 + 3i4+3i.

Why (a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i#

Reorder the sum so the real terms sit together and the imaginary terms sit together:

(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(bi+di).(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (bi + di).(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(bi+di).

The two imaginary terms bibibi and dididi share the factor iii, so they combine into (b+d)i(b + d)i(b+d)i:

bi+di=(b+d)i.bi + di = (b + d)i.bi+di=(b+d)i.

Putting the pieces back together gives (a+c)+(b+d)i(a + c) + (b + d)i(a+c)+(b+d)i, already in standard form. The real part is the sum of the real parts, and the imaginary part is the sum of the imaginary parts. No two factors of iii were ever multiplied together, so i2i^2i2 never comes up in addition.

∎

So the rules are

(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i,(a+bi)−(c+di)=(a−c)+(b−d)i.\begin{aligned} (a + bi) + (c + di) &= (a + c) + (b + d)i, \\ (a + bi) - (c + di) &= (a - c) + (b - d)i. \end{aligned}(a+bi)+(c+di)(a+bi)−(c+di)​=(a+c)+(b+d)i,=(a−c)+(b−d)i.​

Subtraction works the same way once you distribute the minus sign to both parts of the second number. The single most common slip is to subtract the real parts but forget to subtract the imaginary parts. To avoid that slip, keep the whole second number inside its parentheses until the sign is distributed.

Worked example 1 Add and subtract two complex numbers

Add (3+5i)+(4−2i)(3 + 5i) + (4 - 2i)(3+5i)+(4−2i) by combining the parts separately:

(3+5i)+(4−2i)=(3+4)+(5−2)i=7+3i.(3 + 5i) + (4 - 2i) = (3 + 4) + (5 - 2)i = 7 + 3i.(3+5i)+(4−2i)=(3+4)+(5−2)i=7+3i.

Now subtract (7+2i)−(1+6i)(7 + 2i) - (1 + 6i)(7+2i)−(1+6i). Distribute the minus sign to both the 111 and the 6i6i6i:

(7+2i)−(1+6i)=(7−1)+(2−6)i=6−4i.(7 + 2i) - (1 + 6i) = (7 - 1) + (2 - 6)i = 6 - 4i.(7+2i)−(1+6i)=(7−1)+(2−6)i=6−4i.

Each answer is already in standard form, the real part first and a single imaginary term second.

There is a clean picture behind this. Suppose you plot a complex number a+bia + bia+bi as the point aaa units along a horizontal real axis and bbb units up a vertical imaginary axis. Then adding two complex numbers adds their horizontal steps and adds their vertical steps, which is exactly how you add arrows tip to tail.

Multiplying

To multiply two complex numbers, treat them as two binomials and expand with the distributive law, the same “first, outer, inner, last” you use for (x+2)(x+3)(x + 2)(x + 3)(x+2)(x+3). Try (1+i)(2+i)(1 + i)(2 + i)(1+i)(2+i): expanding gives 2+i+2i+i2=2+3i−1=1+3i2 + i + 2i + i^2 = 2 + 3i - 1 = 1 + 3i2+i+2i+i2=2+3i−1=1+3i. The one extra step is that the product of the two imaginary terms produces i2i^2i2. That is the only new thing that ever happens, and you immediately replace it with −1-1−1.

Why (a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i#

Expand the product by multiplying every term of the first factor by every term of the second:

(a+bi)(c+di)=ac+adi+bci+bd i2.(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2.(a+bi)(c+di)=ac+adi+bci+bdi2.

Three of these terms are ordinary products of real numbers times 111 or iii. The last term carries i2i^2i2, and this is the only place the imaginary unit does anything special. Replace i2i^2i2 with −1-1−1:

bd i2=bd(−1)=−bd.bd\,i^2 = bd(-1) = -bd.bdi2=bd(−1)=−bd.

Now collect the real terms, acacac and −bd-bd−bd, and the imaginary terms, adiadiadi and bcibcibci:

(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i.(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i.(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i.

The real part is ac−bdac - bdac−bd and the imaginary part is ad+bcad + bcad+bc. That −bd-bd−bd is the whole reason a product of complex numbers is interesting. It is where i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 turns a term you might expect to stay positive into one that lowers the real part instead.

∎

You do not need to memorize the final formula. It is faster and safer to expand each product by hand and replace i2i^2i2 with −1-1−1 as it appears, exactly as in the examples below. Two special cases are worth noticing: multiplying by a real number kkk scales both parts, k(a+bi)=ka+kbik(a + bi) = ka + kbik(a+bi)=ka+kbi, and multiplying by iii sends a+bia + bia+bi to i(a+bi)=ai+bi2=−b+aii(a + bi) = ai + bi^2 = -b + aii(a+bi)=ai+bi2=−b+ai. For example, i(4+2i)=4i+2i2=−2+4ii(4 + 2i) = 4i + 2i^2 = -2 + 4ii(4+2i)=4i+2i2=−2+4i.

The complex conjugate

Before dividing, we need one special product. The complex conjugate of z=a+biz = a + biz=a+bi is the number with the sign of its imaginary part flipped,

z‾=a−bi,\overline{z} = a - bi,z=a−bi,

read “z bar.” Conjugation changes only the imaginary part: the conjugate of 2+3i2 + 3i2+3i is 2−3i2 - 3i2−3i, and the conjugate of −4−i-4 - i−4−i is −4+i-4 + i−4+i. What makes the conjugate useful is what happens when you multiply a number by it: try (2+i)(2−i)=4−2i+2i−i2=4+1=5(2 + i)(2 - i) = 4 - 2i + 2i - i^2 = 4 + 1 = 5(2+i)(2−i)=4−2i+2i−i2=4+1=5, and every trace of iii is gone.

Why (a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2#

The two factors a+bia + bia+bi and a−bia - bia−bi are a sum and a difference of the same two terms, so their product is a difference of squares. That is the familiar pattern (x+y)(x−y)=x2−y2(x + y)(x - y) = x^2 - y^2(x+y)(x−y)=x2−y2, applied here with x=ax = ax=a and y=biy = biy=bi:

(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2−(bi)2.(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - (bi)^2.(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2−(bi)2.

Now expand the square (bi)2=b2i2(bi)^2 = b^2 i^2(bi)2=b2i2 and replace i2i^2i2 with −1-1−1:

a2−(bi)2=a2−b2i2=a2−b2(−1)=a2+b2.a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 - b^2 i^2 = a^2 - b^2(-1) = a^2 + b^2.a2−(bi)2=a2−b2i2=a2−b2(−1)=a2+b2.

The difference of squares became a sum of squares, because i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 turned the subtracted b2i2b^2 i^2b2i2 into an added b2b^2b2. The result a2+b2a^2 + b^2a2+b2 is a real number, and since it is a sum of two squares it is never negative. It is zero only when aaa and bbb are both zero, that is, only when z=0z = 0z=0.

∎

That is the key property: a nonzero complex number times its conjugate is a positive real number, with every trace of iii gone. Multiplying a+bia + bia+bi by a−bia - bia−bi is the surest way to turn a complex number into a real one, and it is exactly the tool that makes division possible.

Worked example 3 Conjugates and their products

The conjugate of 4−3i4 - 3i4−3i is 4+3i4 + 3i4+3i. Their product clears the imaginary part:

Dividing

A quotient of two complex numbers, such as 1+3i1+i\dfrac{1 + 3i}{1 + i}1+i1+3i​, is not yet in standard form: it has an imaginary part sitting in the denominator. Multiply top and bottom by 1−i1 - i1−i, the denominator’s conjugate:

1+3i1+i=1+3i1+i⋅1−i1−i=(1+3i)(1−i)(1+i)(1−i)=4+2i2=2+i.\frac{1 + 3i}{1 + i} = \frac{1 + 3i}{1 + i}\cdot\frac{1 - i}{1 - i} = \frac{(1 + 3i)(1 - i)}{(1 + i)(1 - i)} = \frac{4 + 2i}{2} = 2 + i.1+i1+3i​=1+i1+3i​⋅1−i1−i​=(1+i)(1−i)(1+3i)(1−i)​=24+2i​=2+i.

Multiplying by 1−i1−i=1\dfrac{1 - i}{1 - i} = 11−i1−i​=1 changes the fraction’s form without changing its value, the same conjugate move you used to rationalize a radical denominator. It works because (1+i)(1−i)=12+12=2(1 + i)(1 - i) = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2(1+i)(1−i)=12+12=2, a real number, by the conjugate property just proved, so the iii in the denominator is gone.

That is the whole method. In general, to divide a+bic+di\dfrac{a + bi}{c + di}c+dia+bi​ (with c+di≠0c + di \ne 0c+di=0), multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate c−dic - dic−di of the denominator:

a+bic+di=a+bic+di⋅c−dic−di=(a+bi)(c−di)c2+d2.\frac{a + bi}{c + di} = \frac{a + bi}{c + di}\cdot\frac{c - di}{c - di} = \frac{(a + bi)(c - di)}{c^2 + d^2}.c+dia+bi​=c+dia+bi​⋅c−dic−di​=c2+d2(a+bi)(c−di)​.

The denominator is now the real number c2+d2c^2 + d^2c2+d2, never zero here since ccc and ddd are not both zero. From there, expand the numerator with i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 and divide its real part and its imaginary part by that real denominator, exactly as above.

Powers of a complex number

Raising a complex number to a power is just repeated multiplication, so the same rules apply. The most common case is a square, which follows the square-of-a-binomial pattern (x+y)2=x2+2xy+y2(x + y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2(x+y)2=x2+2xy+y2 from earlier, again with a final i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1:

(a+bi)2=a2+2abi+(bi)2=a2+2abi+b2i2=(a2−b2)+2abi.(a + bi)^2 = a^2 + 2abi + (bi)^2 = a^2 + 2abi + b^2 i^2 = (a^2 - b^2) + 2abi.(a+bi)2=a2+2abi+(bi)2=a2+2abi+b2i2=(a2−b2)+2abi.

The real part is a2−b2a^2 - b^2a2−b2 and the imaginary part is 2ab2ab2ab.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1. A term such as −3i2-3i^2−3i2 becomes +3+3+3, not −3-3−3 and not 000. Skipping this substitution, or getting its sign wrong, is the single most frequent error in a product.
  • Combining a real part with an imaginary part. 3+2i3 + 2i3+2i does not simplify to 555 or to 5i5i5i. The two parts are different kinds of number and stay separate, just as 3+2x3 + 2x3+2x cannot be collapsed into a single term.
  • Dropping the minus sign in a subtraction. The minus sign applies to both parts of the second number, not just its real part. Distribute it before combining, and keep the second number inside its parentheses until you do.
  • Getting the conjugate product wrong. (a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2, a sum, not the a2−b2a^2 - b^2a2−b2 you would get with real numbers. The i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 turns the subtracted square into an added one.
  • Conjugating the wrong part. The conjugate of a+bia + bia+bi is a−bia - bia−bi: only the imaginary part changes sign. It is not −a+bi-a + bi−a+bi or −a−bi-a - bi−a−bi.
  • Leaving iii in the denominator. A quotient is not finished until it is written as a+bia + bia+bi. Multiply by the conjugate of the denominator, then divide each part by the resulting real number.

Key takeaways

  • A complex number in standard form is a+bia + bia+bi with real part aaa and imaginary part bbb; two are equal exactly when both parts match.
  • Add and subtract by combining the parts separately: (a+bi)±(c+di)=(a±c)+(b±d)i(a + bi) \pm (c + di) = (a \pm c) + (b \pm d)i(a+bi)±(c+di)=(a±c)+(b±d)i.
  • Multiply by expanding like binomials and replacing i2i^2i2 with −1-1−1: (a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i.
  • The conjugate of a+bia + bia+bi is a−bia - bia−bi, and (a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2, a real, nonnegative number.
  • Divide by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, the same move as rationalizing a radical: a+bic+di=(a+bi)(c−di)c2+d2\dfrac{a + bi}{c + di} = \dfrac{(a + bi)(c - di)}{c^2 + d^2}c+dia+bi​=c2+d2(a+bi)(c−di)​, for a nonzero denominator c+dic + dic+di.

Why matching real and imaginary parts is the only way to be equal

Why a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di exactly when a=ca = ca=c and b=db = db=d#

Suppose a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di but the imaginary parts differ, b≠db \ne db=d. Rearranging the equation moves the real parts to one side and the imaginary parts to the other:

a−c=di−bi=(d−b)i.a - c = di - bi = (d - b)i.a−c=di−bi=(d−b)i.

Since b≠db \ne db=d, the number d−bd - bd−b is nonzero, so both sides can be divided by it:

i=a−cd−b.i = \frac{a - c}{d - b}.i=d−ba−c​.

The right side is a ratio of real numbers, so it is real. But iii is not real: no real number squares to −1-1−1. That contradiction means the assumption b≠db \ne db=d was wrong, so b=db = db=d after all. Once the imaginary parts match, a+bi=c+dia + bi = c + dia+bi=c+di reduces to a=ca = ca=c as well. So the two complex numbers can be equal only when both parts match, exactly as the lesson states.

A bit of history (optional)

A generation before Hamilton, Wessel and later Argand had already given complex numbers a geometric meaning. They drew a+bia + bia+bi as a point in a plane, the same picture this lesson used for addition, with rules for adding and multiplying those points. What their geometry could not offer was a definition built from arithmetic alone, one that did not lean on a picture at all.

In 1837 the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton supplied exactly that. He defined a complex number as simply an ordered pair of real numbers, written (a,b)(a, b)(a,b). Those are two ordinary real numbers, kept in order, with no geometry and no picture required.

Then he laid down how pairs combine. They add slot by slot, so (a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d)(a, b) + (c, d) = (a + c, b + d)(a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d). They multiply by the rule you derived above, (a,b)(c,d)=(ac−bd,ad+bc)(a, b)(c, d) = (ac - bd, ad + bc)(a,b)(c,d)=(ac−bd,ad+bc).

Now watch the pair (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) meet itself under that product. Its first slot gives 0−10 - 10−1, and its second slot gives 0+00 + 00+0. So the answer is the pair (−1,0)(-1, 0)(−1,0), which is the real number −1-1−1.

That is the whole of it. The equation i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 stopped being a leap of faith and became a small piece of arithmetic on pairs. Hamilton built a new number system out of ordinary pairs of real numbers, just by choosing this multiplication rule. The pairs were nothing mysterious; only the rule for combining them was new. Hamilton’s definition of a product is the multiplication rule in this lesson’s takeaway, read backwards.

Completing the Square

Intuition

Factoring solves a quadratic fast when it splits into tidy whole-number pieces, but most quadratics do not split that way, and then factoring gets stuck. Take x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x. Factoring pulls out xxx, giving x(x+6)x(x + 6)x(x+6), but that is not a perfect square, and no factor pair turns it into one. One particular constant, added in, does. Completing the square is the method that finds that constant, and unlike factoring, it always works, on any quadratic. The trouble with a general equation x2+bx+c=0x^2 + bx + c = 0x2+bx+c=0 is that the unknown xxx shows up in two places, the x2x^2x2 and the bxbxbx, so you cannot isolate it directly. The fix is to gather those two terms into a single perfect square (x+something)2(x + \text{something})^2(x+something)2 plus a leftover constant. Once xxx appears only once, inside that square, taking a square root finishes the job. The same move works on any quadratic, and in the next lesson it becomes the derivation behind a single formula.

Learning goals

  • Add the square of half the linear coefficient
  • Rewrite a quadratic in completed-square form
  • Keep both signs when taking a square root of both sides
  • Sort the roots by the sign of the number under the square root
  • Divide by aaa first when the leading coefficient is not one

The constant that completes a square

Take x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x. What constant turns it into a perfect square? The picture below shows the answer. The two terms tile an L-shape, a square of area x2x^2x2 and two strips whose areas together make 6x6x6x. That L is missing one corner, and the corner that fills it is 333 by 333, area 999, because each strip already has length 333. Adding that 999 completes the figure into a full square of side x+3x + 3x+3:

x2+6x+9=(x+3)2.x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2.x2+6x+9=(x+3)2.

The picture works here because 666 is positive, so each strip has a sensible length. The pattern behind it is algebraic, and algebra also covers a negative middle coefficient, where there is no picture to draw. You already know from the squares-of-binomials lesson that squaring a sum produces a three-term pattern:

(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2.(x + p)^2 = x^2 + 2px + p^2.(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2.

Read this from right to left. Compare x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x with x2+2px+p2x^2 + 2px + p^2x2+2px+p2: the middle terms agree when 2p=62p = 62p=6, so p=3p = 3p=3, and the last term must be p2=9p^2 = 9p2=9, exactly the corner you just added. The same matching works for any linear coefficient bbb in place of 666. Compare x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx with x2+2px+p2x^2 + 2px + p^2x2+2px+p2: the middle terms agree when 2p=b2p = b2p=b, so p=b2p = \tfrac{b}{2}p=2b​, and the constant that completes the square is p2=(b2)2p^2 = \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2p2=(2b​)2, the square of half the linear coefficient.

A quick check confirms it. Squaring x+b2x + \tfrac{b}{2}x+2b​ gives x2+2⋅b2⋅x+(b2)2x^2 + 2\cdot\tfrac{b}{2}\cdot x + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+2⋅2b​⋅x+(2b​)2, and 2⋅b2=b2\cdot\tfrac{b}{2} = b2⋅2b​=b, so that is exactly x2+bx+(b2)2x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+bx+(2b​)2, the trinomial you want.

So the recipe for the missing constant is short: take the coefficient of xxx, halve it, and square the result. For x2+10xx^2 + 10xx2+10x the half is 555 and the constant is 252525, giving x2+10x+25=(x+5)2x^2 + 10x + 25 = (x + 5)^2x2+10x+25=(x+5)2. For x2−6xx^2 - 6xx2−6x the half is −3-3−3 and the constant is 999, giving x2−6x+9=(x−3)2x^2 - 6x + 9 = (x - 3)^2x2−6x+9=(x−3)2. The sign inside the binomial follows the sign of bbb, because p=b2p = \tfrac{b}{2}p=2b​ carries that sign along.

Rewriting a quadratic in completed-square form

A full quadratic x2+bx+cx^2 + bx + cx2+bx+c usually does not come with the constant (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 already in place; it comes with some other constant ccc. The fix is to add the constant you want and immediately subtract it again. Adding and subtracting the same number changes nothing, since the two moves cancel, but it lets you regroup:

x2+bx+c=x2+bx+(b2)2⏟perfect square−(b2)2+c.x^2 + bx + c = \underbrace{x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2}_{\text{perfect square}} - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + c.x2+bx+c=perfect squarex2+bx+(2b​)2​​−(2b​)2+c.

The braced part collapses to (x+b2)2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(x+2b​)2, and whatever is left over stays outside as a plain constant:

x2+bx+c=(x+b2)2+(c−(b2)2).x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + \left(c - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2\right).x2+bx+c=(x+2b​)2+(c−(2b​)2).

This is the completed-square form. It says the same thing as the original quadratic, but now xxx appears only once, inside the square. Written more generally, every quadratic with leading coefficient 111 can be put in the shape (x−h)2+k(x - h)^2 + k(x−h)2+k for some constants hhh and kkk. This is a form we will lean on again later.

Worked example 1 Rewrite x2+8x+3x^2 + 8x + 3x2+8x+3 in completed-square form

The linear coefficient is b=8b = 8b=8, so half of it is 444 and the constant that completes the square is 42=164^2 = 1642=16. Add and subtract that 161616:

x2+8x+3=(x2+8x+16)−16+3.x^2 + 8x + 3 = \left(x^2 + 8x + 16\right) - 16 + 3.x2+8x+3=(x2+8x+16)−16+3.

The parenthesized trinomial is a perfect square, (x+4)2\left(x + 4\right)^2(x+4)2, and the loose constants combine to −16+3=−13-16 + 3 = -13−16+3=−13:

x2+8x+3=(x+4)2−13.x^2 + 8x + 3 = (x + 4)^2 - 13.x2+8x+3=(x+4)2−13.

A quick check expands it back: (x+4)2−13=x2+8x+16−13=x2+8x+3(x + 4)^2 - 13 = x^2 + 8x + 16 - 13 = x^2 + 8x + 3(x+4)2−13=x2+8x+16−13=x2+8x+3, the original. The constant dropped from 333 to −13-13−13 precisely because 161616 was borrowed to build the square.

Solving a quadratic by completing the square

Rewriting is the hard part; solving is what it unlocks. To solve x2+bx+c=0x^2 + bx + c = 0x2+bx+c=0, isolate the square and then take a square root. The steps run in a fixed order.

First move the constant to the right side, so the left holds only the xxx terms:

x2+bx=−c.x^2 + bx = -c.x2+bx=−c.

Next add (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 to both sides. Adding it to just the left would change the equation; adding it to both sides keeps the balance while turning the left into a perfect square:

x2+bx+(b2)2=(b2)2−c.x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c.x2+bx+(2b​)2=(2b​)2−c.

The left side is now (x+b2)2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(x+2b​)2. Take the square root of both sides, and here is the step people forget. A positive number is the square of two different numbers, one positive and one negative, so both must be kept. That is where the ±\pm± sign comes from:

x+b2=±(b2)2−c.x + \frac{b}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c}.x+2b​=±(2b​)2−c​.

Finally subtract b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​ to isolate xxx, which gives the general result

x=−b2±(b2)2−c.x = -\frac{b}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c}.x=−2b​±(2b​)2−c​.

You do not need to memorize that line; it is faster and safer to run the four steps each time. What it shows is that completing the square always finishes a quadratic, whatever bbb and ccc are.

Worked example 2 Solve x2−6x+4=0x^2 - 6x + 4 = 0x2−6x+4=0

This quadratic has no whole-number factor pair that works, so factoring is a dead end. Complete the square instead. Move the constant across:

x2−6x=−4.x^2 - 6x = -4.x2−6x=−4.

Here b=−6b = -6b=−6, so b2=−3\tfrac{b}{2} = -32b​=−3 and (b2)2=9\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = 9(2b​)2=9. Add 999 to both sides:

x2−6x+9=−4+9=5.x^2 - 6x + 9 = -4 + 9 = 5.x2−6x+9=−4+9=5.

The left side is the perfect square (x−3)2(x - 3)^2(x−3)2, so take the square root of both sides, keeping both signs:

(x−3)2=5⇒x−3=±5.(x - 3)^2 = 5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x - 3 = \pm\sqrt{5}.(x−3)2=5⇒x−3=±5​.

Add 333 to finish:

x=3±5.x = 3 \pm \sqrt{5}.x=3±5​.

The two solutions are x=3+5x = 3 + \sqrt{5}x=3+5​ and x=3−5x = 3 - \sqrt{5}x=3−5​, both perfectly real even though neither is a whole number. This is the kind of answer a search for integer factors would never have found.

When bbb is odd, half of it is a fraction, and (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 becomes a fraction with 444 in the denominator. Nothing about the method changes; you just carry the fractions carefully.

Worked example 3 Solve x2+3x+1=0x^2 + 3x + 1 = 0x2+3x+1=0 (an odd middle coefficient)

Move the constant across, then halve the odd coefficient b=3b = 3b=3:

x2+3x=−1,b2=32,(32)2=94.x^2 + 3x = -1, \qquad \frac{b}{2} = \frac{3}{2}, \qquad \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{4}.x2+3x=−1,2b​=23​,(23​)2=49​.

Add 94\tfrac{9}{4}49​ to both sides. On the right, write −1-1−1 as −44-\tfrac{4}{4}−44​ so the fractions combine:

x2+3x+94=−1+94=−44+94=54.x^2 + 3x + \frac{9}{4} = -1 + \frac{9}{4} = -\frac{4}{4} + \frac{9}{4} = \frac{5}{4}.x2+3x+49​=−1+49​=−44​+49​=45​.

The left side is (x+32)2\left(x + \tfrac{3}{2}\right)^2(x+23​)2. Take the square root of both sides, remembering that 54=52\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{4}} = \tfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2}45​​=25​​ since the denominator 444 is a perfect square:

(x+32)2=54⇒x+32=±52.\left(x + \frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{5}{4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad x + \frac{3}{2} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}.(x+23​)2=45​⇒x+23​=±25​​.

Subtract 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​ and combine over the common denominator 222:

x=−32±52=−3±52.x = -\frac{3}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}.x=−23​±25​​=2−3±5​​.

The single fraction −3±52\dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}2−3±5​​ holds both roots at once, which is the standard way to write them.

Real, repeated, and complex roots

Every bbb and ccc you have used so far is an ordinary real number, and that stays true for the rest of this lesson. Every solve reaches the same crossroads: after isolating the square you have

(x+b2)2=R,where R=(b2)2−c.\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = R, \qquad \text{where } R = \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c.(x+2b​)2=R,where R=(2b​)2−c.

The number RRR on the right decides everything, because you are about to take its square root, and there are exactly three cases. (The next lesson gives this same quantity, scaled up, its own name, the discriminant; here it is just “the number on the right.”)

If R>0R > 0R>0, its square root is a nonzero real number, so the ±\pm± produces two different real solutions, as in x=3±5x = 3 \pm \sqrt{5}x=3±5​ above.

If R=0R = 0R=0, its square root is 000, and ±0\pm 0±0 is just 000, so the two solutions collapse into one repeated solution x=−b2x = -\tfrac{b}{2}x=−2b​. For instance x2−8x+16=0x^2 - 8x + 16 = 0x2−8x+16=0 becomes (x−4)2=0(x - 4)^2 = 0(x−4)2=0, whose only root is x=4x = 4x=4, met twice.

If R<0R < 0R<0, you are taking the square root of a negative number. Before this chapter that meant no solution, but you now have the imaginary unit iii with i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, and −1=i\sqrt{-1} = i−1​=i. So the root exists after all, and the ±\pm± produces two complex solutions, a conjugate pair x=−b2±(something) ix = -\tfrac{b}{2} \pm (\text{something})\,ix=−2b​±(something)i.

Worked example 4 Solve x2+2x+5=0x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0x2+2x+5=0 (complex roots)

Move the constant across and add (22)2=1\left(\tfrac{2}{2}\right)^2 = 1(22​)2=1 to both sides:

x2+2x=−5,x2+2x+1=−5+1=−4.x^2 + 2x = -5, \qquad x^2 + 2x + 1 = -5 + 1 = -4.x2+2x=−5,x2+2x+1=−5+1=−4.

The left side is (x+1)2(x + 1)^2(x+1)2, and the right side is negative, which is the signal that the roots are complex:

(x+1)2=−4⇒x+1=±−4.(x + 1)^2 = -4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x + 1 = \pm\sqrt{-4}.(x+1)2=−4⇒x+1=±−4​.

Rewrite the square root of the negative using iii, as you learned two lessons ago: −4=i4=2i\sqrt{-4} = i\sqrt{4} = 2i−4​=i4​=2i. Then

x+1=±2i⇒x=−1±2i.x + 1 = \pm 2i \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = -1 \pm 2i.x+1=±2i⇒x=−1±2i.

The solutions are the conjugate pair x=−1+2ix = -1 + 2ix=−1+2i and x=−1−2ix = -1 - 2ix=−1−2i. A quick check confirms the first: (−1+2i)2+2(−1+2i)+5=(1−4i+4i2)+(−2+4i)+5=(1−4i−4)−2+4i+5=0(-1 + 2i)^2 + 2(-1 + 2i) + 5 = (1 - 4i + 4i^2) + (-2 + 4i) + 5 = (1 - 4i - 4) - 2 + 4i + 5 = 0(−1+2i)2+2(−1+2i)+5=(1−4i+4i2)+(−2+4i)+5=(1−4i−4)−2+4i+5=0.

This is the payoff the chapter was built for. Back when you only had real numbers, a quadratic with R<0R < 0R<0 simply had no answer, and you had to stop. Now that iii is available, the square root of a negative number is an ordinary complex number, so every quadratic has solutions, whether real or complex. That is exactly why this chapter introduced the imaginary unit before turning to quadratics: completing the square would otherwise dead-end on any equation with R<0R < 0R<0.

When the leading coefficient is not 1

Everything so far assumed the x2x^2x2 term stood alone, with coefficient 111. When a quadratic starts with ax2ax^2ax2 for some a≠1a \ne 1a=1, the pattern x2+bx+(b2)2x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+bx+(2b​)2 no longer matches directly. The fix is to make the leading coefficient 111 first. For an equation, the cleanest way is to divide every term by aaa; because the right side is 000, dividing does not disturb it. Then complete the square on the result as usual.

Worked example 5 Solve 2x2+8x−10=02x^2 + 8x - 10 = 02x2+8x−10=0 and 3x2+6x+9=03x^2 + 6x + 9 = 03x2+6x+9=0

For 2x2+8x−10=02x^2 + 8x - 10 = 02x2+8x−10=0, divide every term by the leading coefficient 222 to make the x2x^2x2 term stand alone:

2x22+8x2−102=0⇒x2+4x−5=0.\frac{2x^2}{2} + \frac{8x}{2} - \frac{10}{2} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x^2 + 4x - 5 = 0.22x2​+28x​−210​=0⇒x2+4x−5=0.

Now complete the square. Move the constant across and add (42)2=4\left(\tfrac{4}{2}\right)^2 = 4(24​)2=4 to both sides:

x2+4x+4=5+4=9⇒(x+2)2=9.x^2 + 4x + 4 = 5 + 4 = 9 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (x + 2)^2 = 9.x2+4x+4=5+4=9⇒(x+2)2=9.

Take the root of both sides, x+2=±3x + 2 = \pm 3x+2=±3, so x=1x = 1x=1 or x=−5x = -5x=−5, two real solutions.

For 3x2+6x+9=03x^2 + 6x + 9 = 03x2+6x+9=0, divide through by 333 first:

x2+2x+3=0.x^2 + 2x + 3 = 0.x2+2x+3=0.

x2+2x+1=−3+1=−2⇒(x+1)2=−2.x^2 + 2x + 1 = -3 + 1 = -2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (x + 1)^2 = -2.x2+2x+1=−3+1=−2⇒(x+1)2=−2.

The right side is negative, so the roots are complex. Since −2=i2\sqrt{-2} = i\sqrt{2}−2​=i2​,

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 to only one side. When you are solving an equation, the completing constant must be added to both sides to keep the balance. Adding it only on the left quietly changes the equation and gives a wrong root.
  • Confusing the equation move with the expression move. When you are rewriting an expression (no equals sign), there is no other side to balance against, so you add and subtract the constant in the same line. Only an equation lets you add it to both sides.
  • Forgetting to divide by aaa first. With a leading coefficient a≠1a \ne 1a=1, you cannot complete the square on ax2+bxax^2 + bxax2+bx directly. Divide the whole equation by aaa so the x2x^2x2 term stands alone, then proceed.
  • Dropping the ±\pm±. A positive number is the square of two numbers, one positive and one negative, so x+b2=±Rx + \tfrac{b}{2} = \pm\sqrt{R}x+2b​=±R​. Writing only the positive root throws away one of the two solutions.
  • Taking the square root of a negative as if it were real. If R<0R < 0R<0, the roots are complex. Write −4=2i\sqrt{-4} = 2i−4​=2i, not 222 and not “no solution.” The negative under the root is the whole reason the answer carries an iii.
  • Halving bbb in the constant but not in the binomial (or the reverse). The completed square is (x+b2)2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(x+2b​)2, with the same b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​ inside the binomial and squared in the constant. Using bbb in one place and b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​ in the other breaks the identity.

Key takeaways

  • To complete the square on x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx, add (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2, the square of half the linear coefficient, giving x2+bx+(b2)2=(x+b2)2x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+bx+(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2.
  • Any quadratic with leading coefficient 111 rewrites in completed-square form x2+bx+c=(x+b2)2+(c−(b2)2)x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 + \left(c - \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2\right)x2+bx+c=(x+2b​)2+(c−(2b​)2), by adding and subtracting that constant.

A bit of history (optional)

The name of this method is not a figure of speech. It began as an instruction to finish a drawing.

Around the year 820, in Baghdad, a scholar called al-Khwarizmi wrote a book on solving equations. He had no symbol for xxx and no way to write an exponent, so he wrote his rules in words and justified them with a picture. For the problem we would write as x2+10x=39x^2 + 10x = 39x2+10x=39, he drew a square of side xxx and laid strips along two of its sides. The figure was one corner short of a larger square. He filled the corner in and read the answer off the finished side. That corner is the constant you added in this lesson.

The picture carried a hidden cost, and the cost outlasted the picture. A drawn square cannot have a negative side. For this equation, the algebra also allows x=−13x = -13x=−13, but a length of −13-13−13 has no place in a drawing. So the geometric method returned only the one positive root.

Europe kept the habit for centuries after the diagrams were gone. Negative roots were called false, absurd, and fictitious. A square root of a negative was refused outright. That is why a quadratic with R<0R < 0R<0 was reported as having no solution at all, rather than as having two.

Nothing in the method ever changed. What changed was which numbers people would accept as answers. Once negatives were admitted, and then complex numbers after them, the same four steps you ran here began returning both roots of every quadratic. For this equation, the second root, x=−13x = -13x=−13, is the one that particular drawing could never show.

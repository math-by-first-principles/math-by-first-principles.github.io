Completing the Square

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Factoring x2+6xx^2+6xx2+6x gives x(x+6)x(x+6)x(x+6), but it does not give a squared binomial. Adding one carefully chosen constant does: x2+6x+9=(x+3)2x^2+6x+9=(x+3)^2x2+6x+9=(x+3)2. Completing the square finds that constant. In an equation, we add it to both sides, then solve by taking square roots of both sides. This method works even when whole-number factors are hard to find, and it will lead to the quadratic formula in the next lesson.

Learning goals

  • Add the square of half the linear coefficient
  • Rewrite a quadratic in completed-square form
  • Keep both signs when taking a square root of both sides
  • Sort the roots by the sign of the number under the square root
  • Divide by aaa first when the leading coefficient is not one

The constant that completes a square

Take x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x. What constant turns it into a perfect square? The picture below shows the answer. The two terms tile an L-shape for x>0x>0x>0: a square of area x2x^2x2 and two strips whose areas together make 6x6x6x. That L is missing one corner, and the corner that fills it is 333 by 333, area 999, because each strip already has length 333. Adding that 999 completes the figure into a full square of side x+3x + 3x+3:

x2+6x+9=(x+3)2.x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2.x2+6x+9=(x+3)2.

The picture uses positive side lengths. The algebraic identity also works for negative values of xxx or a negative middle coefficient, even though those do not represent lengths in this drawing. You already know from the squares-of-binomials lesson that squaring a sum produces a three-term pattern:

(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2.(x + p)^2 = x^2 + 2px + p^2.(x+p)2=x2+2px+p2.

Read this from right to left. Compare x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x with x2+2px+p2x^2 + 2px + p^2x2+2px+p2: the middle terms agree when 2p=62p = 62p=6, so p=3p = 3p=3, and the last term must be p2=9p^2 = 9p2=9, exactly the corner you just added. The same matching works for any linear coefficient bbb in place of 666. Compare x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx with x2+2px+p2x^2 + 2px + p^2x2+2px+p2: the middle terms agree when 2p=b2p = b2p=b, so p=b2p = \tfrac{b}{2}p=2b​, and the constant that completes the square is p2=(b2)2p^2 = \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2p2=(2b​)2, the square of half the linear coefficient.

Check by expansion: the middle term of (x+b2)2(x+\tfrac b2)^2(x+2b​)2 is 2⋅b2⋅x=bx2\cdot\tfrac b2\cdot x=bx2⋅2b​⋅x=bx, and the last term is (b2)2(\tfrac b2)^2(2b​)2.

So the recipe for the missing constant is short: take the coefficient of xxx, halve it, and square the result. For x2+10xx^2 + 10xx2+10x the half is 555 and the constant is 252525, giving x2+10x+25=(x+5)2x^2 + 10x + 25 = (x + 5)^2x2+10x+25=(x+5)2. For x2−6xx^2 - 6xx2−6x the half is −3-3−3 and the constant is 999, giving x2−6x+9=(x−3)2x^2 - 6x + 9 = (x - 3)^2x2−6x+9=(x−3)2. The sign inside the binomial follows the sign of bbb, because p=b2p = \tfrac{b}{2}p=2b​ carries that sign along.

Rewriting a quadratic in completed-square form

Worked example 1 Rewrite x2+8x+3x^2 + 8x + 3x2+8x+3 in completed-square form

The linear coefficient is b=8b = 8b=8, so half of it is 444 and the constant that completes the square is 42=164^2 = 1642=16. Add and subtract that 161616:

x2+8x+3=(x2+8x+16)−16+3.x^2 + 8x + 3 = \left(x^2 + 8x + 16\right) - 16 + 3.x2+8x+3=(x2+8x+16)−16+3.

The parenthesized trinomial is a perfect square, (x+4)2\left(x + 4\right)^2(x+4)2, and the loose constants combine to −16+3=−13-16 + 3 = -13−16+3=−13:

x2+8x+3=(x+4)2−13.x^2 + 8x + 3 = (x + 4)^2 - 13.x2+8x+3=(x+4)2−13.

A quick check expands it back: (x+4)2−13=x2+8x+16−13=x2+8x+3(x + 4)^2 - 13 = x^2 + 8x + 16 - 13 = x^2 + 8x + 3(x+4)2−13=x2+8x+16−13=x2+8x+3, the original. The constant dropped from 333 to −13-13−13 precisely because 161616 was borrowed to build the square.

The example adds and subtracts the same number, so the expression keeps its value. In general, x2+bx+cx^2+bx+cx2+bx+c needs (b2)2(\tfrac b2)^2(2b​)2 to build the square. Add that number and subtract it again:

x2+bx+c=x2+bx+(b2)2⏟perfect square−(b2)2+c.x^2 + bx + c = \underbrace{x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2}_{\text{perfect square}} - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + c.x2+bx+c=perfect squarex2+bx+(2b​)2​​−(2b​)2+c.

The braced terms form (x+b2)2\left(x+\tfrac b2\right)^2(x+2b​)2. The remaining constants stay outside:

x2+bx+c=(x+b2)2+(c−(b2)2).x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + \left(c - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2\right).x2+bx+c=(x+2b​)2+(c−(2b​)2).

This is the completed-square form. It says the same thing as the original quadratic, but now xxx appears only once, inside the square. Written more generally, every quadratic with leading coefficient 111 can be put in the shape (x−h)2+k(x - h)^2 + k(x−h)2+k for some constants hhh and kkk. This is a form we will lean on again later.

Solving a quadratic by completing the square

Worked example 2 Solve x2−6x+4=0x^2 - 6x + 4 = 0x2−6x+4=0

No whole-number factor pair works here. Completing the square gives the roots without that search. Move the constant across:

x2−6x=−4.x^2 - 6x = -4.x2−6x=−4.

Here b=−6b = -6b=−6, so b2=−3\tfrac{b}{2} = -32b​=−3 and (b2)2=9\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = 9(2b​)2=9. Add 999 to both sides:

x2−6x+9=−4+9=5.x^2 - 6x + 9 = -4 + 9 = 5.x2−6x+9=−4+9=5.

The left side is the perfect square (x−3)2(x - 3)^2(x−3)2, so take the square root of both sides, keeping both signs:

(x−3)2=5⇒x−3=±5.(x - 3)^2 = 5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x - 3 = \pm\sqrt{5}.(x−3)2=5⇒x−3=±5​.

Add 333 to finish:

x=3±5.x = 3 \pm \sqrt{5}.x=3±5​.

The two solutions are x=3+5x = 3 + \sqrt{5}x=3+5​ and x=3−5x = 3 - \sqrt{5}x=3−5​, both perfectly real even though neither is a whole number. This is the kind of answer a search for integer factors would never have found.

The example uses four steps. For x2+bx+c=0x^2+bx+c=0x2+bx+c=0 with real b,cb,cb,c, the same steps give a general result.

First move the constant to the right side, so the left holds only the xxx terms:

x2+bx=−c.x^2 + bx = -c.x2+bx=−c.

Next add (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 to both sides. Adding it to just the left would change the equation; adding it to both sides keeps the balance while turning the left into a perfect square:

x2+bx+(b2)2=(b2)2−c.x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c.x2+bx+(2b​)2=(2b​)2−c.

The left side is now (x+b2)2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(x+2b​)2. Take the square root of both sides, and here is the step people forget. A positive number is the square of two different numbers, one positive and one negative, so both must be kept. That is where the ±\pm± sign comes from:

x+b2=±(b2)2−c.x + \frac{b}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c}.x+2b​=±(2b​)2−c​.

Finally subtract b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​ to isolate xxx, which gives the general result

x=−b2±(b2)2−c.x = -\frac{b}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c}.x=−2b​±(2b​)2−c​.

You do not need to memorize this result: use the four steps with the numbers in your equation. The sign of the number under the square root determines the kind of answer, as we will see below.

When bbb is odd, half of it is a fraction, and (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 becomes a fraction with 444 in the denominator. Nothing about the method changes; you just carry the fractions carefully.

Worked example 3 Solve x2+3x+1=0x^2 + 3x + 1 = 0x2+3x+1=0 (an odd middle coefficient)

Move the constant across, then halve the odd coefficient b=3b = 3b=3:

x2+3x=−1,b2=32,(32)2=94.x^2 + 3x = -1, \qquad \frac{b}{2} = \frac{3}{2}, \qquad \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{4}.x2+3x=−1,2b​=23​,(23​)2=49​.

Add 94\tfrac{9}{4}49​ to both sides. On the right, write −1-1−1 as −44-\tfrac{4}{4}−44​ so the fractions combine:

x2+3x+94=−1+94=−44+94=54.x^2 + 3x + \frac{9}{4} = -1 + \frac{9}{4} = -\frac{4}{4} + \frac{9}{4} = \frac{5}{4}.x2+3x+49​=−1+49​=−44​+49​=45​.

The left side is (x+32)2\left(x + \tfrac{3}{2}\right)^2(x+23​)2. Take the square root of both sides, remembering that 54=52\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{4}} = \tfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2}45​​=25​​ since the denominator 444 is a perfect square:

(x+32)2=54⇒x+32=±52.\left(x + \frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{5}{4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad x + \frac{3}{2} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}.(x+23​)2=45​⇒x+23​=±25​​.

Subtract 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​ and combine over the common denominator 222:

x=−32±52=−3±52.x = -\frac{3}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}.x=−23​±25​​=2−3±5​​.

The single fraction −3±52\dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}2−3±5​​ holds both roots at once, which is the standard way to write them.

Real, repeated, and complex roots

With real coefficients, completing the square gives

(x+b2)2=R,where R=(b2)2−c.\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = R, \qquad \text{where } R = \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c.(x+2b​)2=R,where R=(2b​)2−c.

The sign of RRR tells us which kind of square root to use.

If R>0R > 0R>0, its square root is a nonzero real number, so the ±\pm± produces two different real solutions, as in x=3±5x = 3 \pm \sqrt{5}x=3±5​ above.

If R=0R = 0R=0, its square root is 000, and ±0\pm 0±0 is just 000, so the two solutions collapse into one repeated solution x=−b2x = -\tfrac{b}{2}x=−2b​. For instance x2−8x+16=0x^2 - 8x + 16 = 0x2−8x+16=0 becomes (x−4)2=0(x - 4)^2 = 0(x−4)2=0, whose only root is x=4x = 4x=4, met twice.

If R<0R<0R<0, there is no real solution, since a real square cannot be negative. To describe the complex solutions, introduce the imaginary unit iii, a number satisfying i2=−1i^2=-1i2=−1. The usual rules for multiplying and squaring products still apply, so both 2i2i2i and −2i-2i−2i square to −4-4−4:

(2i)2=4i2=−4,(−2i)2=4i2=−4.(2i)^2=4i^2=-4,\qquad (-2i)^2=4i^2=-4.(2i)2=4i2=−4,(−2i)2=4i2=−4.

More generally, for a positive real number kkk,

(ik)2=i2k=−k,(−ik)2=−k.(i\sqrt k)^2=i^2k=-k,\qquad (-i\sqrt k)^2=-k.(ik​)2=i2k=−k,(−ik​)2=−k.

We write −k=ik\sqrt{-k}=i\sqrt k−k​=ik​ by convention and keep both signs when solving y2=−ky^2=-ky2=−k. Thus a negative RRR gives x=−b2±i−Rx=-\tfrac b2\pm i\sqrt{-R}x=−2b​±i−R​. A real number plus a real multiple of iii is a complex number. In −1+2i-1+2i−1+2i, −1-1−1 is the real part and 222, the coefficient of iii, is the imaginary part. The two answers form a conjugate pair: their real parts match, and their imaginary parts have opposite signs. These facts are all we need here. The chapter’s first two lessons, Imaginary Numbers and Arithmetic with Complex Numbers (both Advanced), explore them further.

Worked example 4 Solve x2+2x+5=0x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0x2+2x+5=0 (complex roots)

Move the constant across and add (22)2=1\left(\tfrac{2}{2}\right)^2 = 1(22​)2=1 to both sides:

x2+2x=−5,x2+2x+1=−5+1=−4.x^2 + 2x = -5, \qquad x^2 + 2x + 1 = -5 + 1 = -4.x2+2x=−5,x2+2x+1=−5+1=−4.

The left side is (x+1)2(x + 1)^2(x+1)2, and the right side is negative, which is the signal that the roots are complex:

(x+1)2=−4⇒x+1=±−4.(x + 1)^2 = -4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x + 1 = \pm\sqrt{-4}.(x+1)2=−4⇒x+1=±−4​.

Use the negative-root convention just stated: −4=i4=2i\sqrt{-4} = i\sqrt{4} = 2i−4​=i4​=2i. Then

x+1=±2i⇒x=−1±2i.x + 1 = \pm 2i \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = -1 \pm 2i.x+1=±2i⇒x=−1±2i.

The solutions are x=−1+2ix=-1+2ix=−1+2i and x=−1−2ix=-1-2ix=−1−2i, a conjugate pair. Both work because x+1=±2ix+1=\pm2ix+1=±2i and (±2i)2=−4(\pm2i)^2=-4(±2i)2=−4. We can also check the first in the original equation by expanding:

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 to only one side. When you are solving an equation, the completing constant must be added to both sides to keep the balance. Adding it only on the left quietly changes the equation and gives a wrong root.
  • Confusing the equation move with the expression move. When you are rewriting an expression (no equals sign), there is no other side to balance against, so you add and subtract the constant in the same line. Only an equation lets you add it to both sides.
  • Forgetting to divide by aaa first. With a leading coefficient a≠1a \ne 1a=1, the pattern used here does not apply directly to ax2+bxax^2 + bxax2+bx. Divide the whole equation by aaa so the x2x^2x2 term stands alone, then proceed.
  • Dropping the ±\pm±. A positive number is the square of two numbers, one positive and one negative, so x+b2=±Rx + \tfrac{b}{2} = \pm\sqrt{R}x+2b​=±R​. Writing only the positive root throws away one of the two solutions.
  • Taking the square root of a negative as if it were real. If R<0R < 0R<0, the roots are complex. Write −4=2i\sqrt{-4}=2i−4​=2i. Say “no real solution” if solutions are restricted to real numbers. The negative under the root is the whole reason the answer carries an iii.
  • Halving bbb in the constant but not in the binomial (or the reverse). The completed square is (x+b2)2\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(x+2b​)2, with the same b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​ inside the binomial and squared in the constant. Using bbb in one place and b2\tfrac{b}{2}2b​ in the other breaks the identity.

Key takeaways

  • To complete the square on x2+bxx^2 + bxx2+bx, add (b2)2\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2, the square of half the linear coefficient, giving x2+bx+(b2)2=(x+b2)2x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2x2+bx+(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2.
  • Any quadratic with leading coefficient 111 rewrites in completed-square form x2+bx+c=(x+b2)2+(c−(b2)2)x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 + \left(c - \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2\right)x2+bx+c=(x+2b​)2+(c−(2b​)2), by adding and subtracting that constant.

A bit of history (optional)

Completing the square was once a geometric instruction. In ninth-century Baghdad, al-Khwarizmi explained equations in words and justified his methods with drawings. One example was what we write as x2+10x=39x^2+10x=39x2+10x=39.

His rule halves 101010 to get 555, squares it to get 252525, and adds that to 393939. The new square has area 646464 and side 888, so subtracting 555 gives x=3x=3x=3. A geometric proof surrounds the original square with strips and fills the missing corners. The same added area, 252525, completes the square. Our two-strip drawing uses the same idea with a different arrangement of pieces.

The drawing explains why adding a constant works, but it also limits what counts as an answer: a side length must be positive. Modern algebra gives (x+5)2=64(x+5)^2=64(x+5)2=64, so x+5=±8x+5=\pm8x+5=±8 and x=3x=3x=3 or x=−13x=-13x=−13. The negative answer satisfies the equation, yet cannot be a side in that drawing.

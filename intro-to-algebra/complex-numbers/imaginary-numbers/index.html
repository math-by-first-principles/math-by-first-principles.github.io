Imaginary Numbers Advanced. This lesson goes beyond core Algebra I. You can skip it.

Intuition

Every number you have used so far, whether positive, negative, a fraction, or an irrational like 7\sqrt{7}7​, shares one stubborn habit: squaring it never lands below zero. A positive times a positive is positive, and a negative times a negative is also positive, so x2x^2x2 is never negative. That means a plain-looking equation like x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1 has no answer among the numbers you know. Rather than call it unsolvable, mathematicians did something bolder. They invented a brand new number whose square is −1-1−1, named it iii, and built an extended number system on top of it. This lesson introduces that number and shows how to compute with it.

Learning goals

  • Define iii by i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, and know that −i-i−i squares to −1-1−1 too
  • Reduce ini^nin using the remainder of nnn divided by four
  • Simplify −n\sqrt{-n}−n​ as ini\sqrt{n}in​, and multiply negative square roots safely
  • Solve x2=−kx^2 = -kx2=−k as x=±k ix = \pm\sqrt{k}\,ix=±k​i

Why the real numbers are not enough

You can solve x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9 because 32=93^2 = 932=9, and (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9 as well, so it has the two answers x=3x = 3x=3 and x=−3x = -3x=−3. You can even solve x2=7x^2 = 7x2=7, whose answers ±7\pm\sqrt{7}±7​ are irrational but still perfectly real. Try x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1, though, and the search fails. No real number works, and the reason is not that we have not looked hard enough. It is built into how signs behave under multiplication.

Why no real number squares to a negative number#

Take any real number aaa and look at its square a2=a×aa^2 = a \times aa2=a×a. There are only three possibilities for the sign of aaa. If aaa is positive, then a×aa \times aa×a is a positive times a positive, which is positive. If aaa is negative, then a×aa \times aa×a is a negative times a negative, and two negatives make a positive, so a2a^2a2 is again positive. If aaa is zero, then a2=0a^2 = 0a2=0.

In every case a2≥0a^2 \ge 0a2≥0, so a real square is never negative. That rules out any real solution of a2=−1a^2 = -1a2=−1, or a2=−9a^2 = -9a2=−9, or any equation that asks for a negative square. The gap is genuine and permanent, and closing it needs a number that does not sit on the real number line at all.

∎

Defining the imaginary unit

Define the imaginary unit iii to be a number whose square is −1-1−1:

i2=−1.i^2 = -1.i2=−1.

That equation is the entire definition, and everything else in this lesson follows from it. The symbol −1\sqrt{-1}−1​ is just another name for iii. That follows the same convention you already know from real numbers: 9\sqrt{9}9​ means the positive root 333, even though −3-3−3 also squares to 999. In the same way, −1\sqrt{-1}−1​ points to iii, even though (as the next paragraph shows) −i-i−i squares to −1-1−1 too.

With iii available, x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1 finally has answers. One is x=ix = ix=i, because i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 by definition. The other is x=−ix = -ix=−i, because

(−i)2=(−1)2 i2=(1)(−1)=−1(-i)^2 = (-1)^2\, i^2 = (1)(-1) = -1(−i)2=(−1)2i2=(1)(−1)=−1

as well. So x2=−1x^2 = -1x2=−1 has the two solutions x=ix = ix=i and x=−ix = -ix=−i, written together as x=±ix = \pm ix=±i, matching the same two-answer pattern you already know from x2=9x^2 = 9x2=9.

A number like 2+3i2 + 3i2+3i, combining a real number and a real multiple of iii, is called a complex number. This lesson stays with iii itself and its multiples; the arithmetic that combines the two parts of a complex number is the subject of the next lesson.

The powers of i

Since iii is a number, you can raise it to powers, and something useful happens: the powers repeat in a short cycle. Work them out one at a time, each from the one before, leaning on i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 at every step.

i1=i,i2=−1,i3=−i,i4=1.i^1 = i, \qquad i^2 = -1, \qquad i^3 = -i, \qquad i^4 = 1.i1=i,i2=−1,i3=−i,i4=1.

Why the powers of iii repeat every four steps#

Start from the definition and climb. The first power is iii itself. The second is i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, the definition. Multiply by one more factor of iii for the third power, then again for the fourth:

i3=i2⋅i=−i,i4=i2⋅i2=1.i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i, \qquad i^4 = i^2 \cdot i^2 = 1.i3=i2⋅i=−i,i4=i2⋅i2=1.

Reaching i4=1i^4 = 1i4=1 is the key event: four factors of iii multiply to 111. One factor beyond that returns to the start, since i5=i4⋅i=1⋅i=ii^5 = i^4 \cdot i = 1 \cdot i = ii5=i4⋅i=1⋅i=i. From there the same four values i,−1,−i,1i, -1, -i, 1i,−1,−i,1 repeat forever, exactly as the diagram shows.

That fact turns any power into a small one. Take i11i^{11}i11. Eleven factors of iii make two complete groups of four, worth 111 each, with three factors left over:

i11=(i4)2⋅i3=12⋅i3=i3=−i.i^{11} = (i^4)^2 \cdot i^3 = 1^2 \cdot i^3 = i^3 = -i.i11=(i4)2⋅i3=12⋅i3=i3=−i.

Only the leftover factors matter. The same idea works for any exponent nnn: write n=4q+rn = 4q + rn=4q+r, where rrr is the remainder of nnn divided by 444, one of 0,1,2,30, 1, 2, 30,1,2,3. Then

in=i4q+r=(i4)q⋅ir=1q⋅ir=ir.i^n = i^{4q + r} = (i^4)^q \cdot i^r = 1^q \cdot i^r = i^r.in=i4q+r=(i4)q⋅ir=1q⋅ir=ir.

So ini^nin depends only on rrr, the remainder of nnn divided by 444. When that remainder is 000, the power is i0=1i^0 = 1i0=1.

∎

In short, divide the exponent by 444 and keep only the remainder. A remainder of 111 gives iii, a remainder of 222 gives −1-1−1, a remainder of 333 gives −i-i−i, and a remainder of 000 gives 111.

Worked example 1 Evaluate i50i^{50}i50, i23i^{23}i23, and i100i^{100}i100

Divide each exponent by 444 and keep only the remainder.

For i50i^{50}i50, since 50=4×12+250 = 4 \times 12 + 250=4×12+2, the remainder is 222:

i50=i2=−1.i^{50} = i^2 = -1.i50=i2=−1.

For i23i^{23}i23, since 23=4×5+323 = 4 \times 5 + 323=4×5+3, the remainder is 333:

i23=i3=−i.i^{23} = i^3 = -i.i23=i3=−i.

For i100i^{100}i100, since 100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25 exactly, the remainder is 000:

i100=i0=1.i^{100} = i^0 = 1.i100=i0=1.

No matter how large the exponent, only its remainder after dividing by 444 ever matters.

Answer choices

i27=i3=−ii^{27} = i^3 = -ii27=i3=−i

The powers cycle every four, so i27i^{27}i27 matches i3i^3i3.

Square roots of negative numbers

The imaginary unit lets you take the square root of any negative number, not just −1-1−1. The rule is short:

−n=infor n>0.\sqrt{-n} = i\sqrt{n} \quad \text{for } n > 0.−n​=in​for n>0.

Here n\sqrt{n}n​ is the ordinary real square root you already know, and the factor of iii carries the negative sign. A real number multiplied by iii, such as the 3i3i3i you are about to compute, is called a pure imaginary number. These really are new numbers: 3i3i3i is not equal to any real number, because its square is (3i)2=9i2=−9(3i)^2 = 9i^2 = -9(3i)2=9i2=−9, and no real number squares to −9-9−9.

Why −n=in\sqrt{-n} = i\sqrt{n}−n​=in​#

To call something the square root of −n-n−n, it has to square to −n-n−n. Test the candidate ini\sqrt{n}in​ by squaring it:

(in)2=i2(n)2=(−1)(n)=−n.\left(i\sqrt{n}\right)^2 = i^2 \left(\sqrt{n}\right)^2 = (-1)(n) = -n.(in​)2=i2(n​)2=(−1)(n)=−n.

It works: ini\sqrt{n}in​ squares to −n-n−n, so it is a square root of −n-n−n. The step (n)2=n\left(\sqrt{n}\right)^2 = n(n​)2=n is just the meaning of a real square root, and i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1 supplies the sign. Splitting the radical as −n=−1⋅n=in\sqrt{-n} = \sqrt{-1}\cdot\sqrt{n} = i\sqrt{n}−n​=−1​⋅n​=in​ is allowed here because only one of the two factors under the roots is negative. The next section shows why that restriction is not optional.

∎

The other square root of −n-n−n is −in-i\sqrt{n}−in​, but −n\sqrt{-n}−n​ always names ini\sqrt{n}in​, the same way 9\sqrt{9}9​ always names 333 and not −3-3−3. Both roots matter when you solve an equation instead of just simplifying a radical, which is exactly what the last section of this lesson does.

Worked example 2 Simplify −9\sqrt{-9}−9​, −12\sqrt{-12}−12​, and −72\sqrt{-72}−72​

Pull out the factor of iii first, then simplify the real square root that is left.

The root −9\sqrt{-9}−9​ has a perfect square inside:

−9=i9=3i.\sqrt{-9} = i\sqrt{9} = 3i.−9​=i9​=3i.

The root −12\sqrt{-12}−12​ needs the radical simplified, using 12=4×312 = 4 \times 312=4×3 and 4=2\sqrt{4} = 24​=2:

−12=i12=i4×3=2i3.\sqrt{-12} = i\sqrt{12} = i\sqrt{4 \times 3} = 2i\sqrt{3}.−12​=i12​=i4×3​=2i3​.

The root −72\sqrt{-72}−72​ works the same way, with 72=36×272 = 36 \times 272=36×2:

−72=i72=i36×2=6i2.\sqrt{-72} = i\sqrt{72} = i\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6i\sqrt{2}.−72​=i72​=i36×2​=6i2​.

In each case the answer is a real number (or a simplified radical) times iii, that is, a pure imaginary number.

Answer choices

Factor out the iii first, then simplify the radical with 50=25×250 = 25 \times 250=25×2 and 25=5\sqrt{25} = 525​=5.

−50=i50=i25×2=5i2\sqrt{-50} = i\sqrt{50} = i\sqrt{25 \times 2} = 5i\sqrt{2}−50​=i50​=i25×2​=5i2​

The 222 has no square factor left, so 5i25i\sqrt{2}5i2​ is fully simplified.

The trap: two negative radicands

There is one place where square roots of negatives bite back, and nearly everyone is caught by it once. You have used the product rule for square roots, a⋅b=ab\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​⋅b​=ab​, to simplify radicals. That rule is valid only when aaa and bbb, the numbers under the root signs (the radicands), are not both negative. Apply it blindly when both radicands are negative and you get the wrong answer, including the wrong sign.

Watch it fail on −4⋅−9\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9}−4​⋅−9​. The correct method rewrites each square root using iii first, and only then multiplies:

−4⋅−9=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6.\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6.−4​⋅−9​=(2i)(3i)=6i2=−6.

The tempting shortcut multiplies the radicands first, and it disagrees:

−4⋅−9≠(−4)(−9)=36=6.\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} \ne \sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6.−4​⋅−9​=(−4)(−9)​=36​=6.

The two results, −6-6−6 and 666, are not equal, so the product rule genuinely breaks for two negatives. The fix is a firm habit: convert every square root of a negative into the form i  i\sqrt{\;}i​ before doing anything else. Once the factors of iii are out in the open, they multiply like any other factors, and i⋅i=i2=−1i \cdot i = i^2 = -1i⋅i=i2=−1 supplies the correct sign on its own.

Worked example 3 Evaluate −2⋅−8\sqrt{-2}\cdot\sqrt{-8}−2​⋅−8​

Convert each root to the i  i\sqrt{\;}i​ form first. Here −2=i2\sqrt{-2} = i\sqrt{2}−2​=i2​, and −8=i8=2i2\sqrt{-8} = i\sqrt{8} = 2i\sqrt{2}−8​=i8​=2i2​. Now multiply the two pure imaginary numbers:

(i2)(2i2)=2i2(2⋅2)=2(−1)(2)=−4.\left(i\sqrt{2}\right)\left(2i\sqrt{2}\right) = 2i^2\left(\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}\right) = 2(-1)(2) = -4.(i2​)(2i2​)=2i2(2​⋅2​)=2(−1)(2)=−4.

The wrong route, (−2)(−8)=16=4\sqrt{(-2)(-8)} = \sqrt{16} = 4(−2)(−8)​=16​=4, once again flips the sign. Convert first, and the answer −4-4−4 comes out right.

Check your understanding

Evaluate −4⋅−25\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-25}−4​⋅−25​.

Answer choices

Rewrite each root with iii before multiplying: −4=2i\sqrt{-4} = 2i−4​=2i and −25=5i\sqrt{-25} = 5i−25​=5i.

−4⋅−25=(2i)(5i)=10i2=−10\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-25} = (2i)(5i) = 10i^2 = -10−4​⋅−25​=(2i)(5i)=10i2=−10

Multiplying the radicands first would give 100=10\sqrt{100} = 10100​=10, the wrong sign, because both radicands are negative.

Solving x squared equals a negative

Putting the pieces together, you can now solve any equation of the form x2=−kx^2 = -kx2=−k with k>0k > 0k>0, or equally x2+k=0x^2 + k = 0x2+k=0. Take the square root of both sides, and keep both signs.

Worked example 4 Solve x2+9=0x^2 + 9 = 0x2+9=0 and x2=−20x^2 = -20x2=−20

For x2+9=0x^2 + 9 = 0x2+9=0, move the constant across to isolate the square, then take the root of both sides:

x2=−9⇒x=±−9=±3i.x^2 = -9 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = \pm\sqrt{-9} = \pm 3i.x2=−9⇒x=±−9​=±3i.

Both 3i3i3i and −3i-3i−3i check out, since (±3i)2=9i2=−9(\pm 3i)^2 = 9i^2 = -9(±3i)2=9i2=−9. For x2=−20x^2 = -20x2=−20, the root needs simplifying, with 20=4×520 = 4 \times 520=4×5:

x=±−20=±i20=±2i5.x = \pm\sqrt{-20} = \pm i\sqrt{20} = \pm 2i\sqrt{5}.x=±−20​=±i20​=±2i5​.

Every equation of this shape has two pure imaginary solutions, each the negative of the other.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Thinking i2=1i^2 = 1i2=1. By definition i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1. That single minus sign is the whole reason iii exists; a positive square would just be an ordinary real number.
  • The two-negatives radical trap. −4⋅−9=−6\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} = -6−4​⋅−9​=−6, not 666. Rewrite each root as i  i\sqrt{\;}i​ before multiplying, because a⋅b=ab\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​⋅b​=ab​ fails when both radicands are negative.
  • Writing −9=−3\sqrt{-9} = -3−9​=−3 or ±3\pm 3±3. A square root of a negative is imaginary, not a negative real: −9=3i\sqrt{-9} = 3i−9​=3i. No real number, positive or negative, squares to −9-9−9.
  • Multiplying the exponent instead of reducing it. i20i^{20}i20 is not 20i20i20i. Divide the exponent by 444 and keep the remainder: since 202020 is a multiple of 444, the remainder is 000, so i20=1i^{20} = 1i20=1.
  • Forgetting the second solution. Both iii and −i-i−i square to −1-1−1, since (−i)2=(−1)2i2=−1(-i)^2 = (-1)^2 i^2 = -1(−i)2=(−1)2i2=−1 too. So x2=−25x^2 = -25x2=−25 has both x=5ix = 5ix=5i and x=−5ix = -5ix=−5i: write ±5i\pm 5i±5i, not just one sign.

Key takeaways

  • The imaginary unit is defined by i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, equivalently i=−1i = \sqrt{-1}i=−1​. It fills the gap left by the real numbers, none of which square to a negative.
  • The powers of iii cycle every four: i1=ii^1 = ii1=i, i2=−1i^2 = -1i2=−1, i3=−ii^3 = -ii3=−i, i4=1i^4 = 1i4=1. Reduce ini^nin using the remainder of nnn divided by 444.
  • A square root of a negative simplifies as −n=in\sqrt{-n} = i\sqrt{n}−n​=in​ for n>0n > 0n>0, for example −9=3i\sqrt{-9} = 3i−9​=3i and −12=2i3\sqrt{-12} = 2i\sqrt{3}−12​=2i3​.
  • The product rule a⋅b=ab\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}a​⋅b​=ab​ fails for two negative radicands. Convert each root to i  i\sqrt{\;}i​ first: −4⋅−9=6i2=−6\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} = 6i^2 = -6−4​⋅−9​=6i2=−6, not 666.
  • A real multiple of iii, such as 3i3i3i, is a pure imaginary number, and x2=−kx^2 = -kx2=−k with k>0k > 0k>0 has the two solutions x=±k ix = \pm\sqrt{k}\,ix=±k​i. Add a real number to a pure imaginary one, as in 2+3i2 + 3i2+3i, and the result is a complex number, the subject of the next lesson.

A bit of history (optional)

The oldest square root of a negative number on record may have been quietly erased, though nobody alive can say for certain by whom.

It sits in a handbook on measuring solid shapes, written sometime in the first century. The author is usually taken to be Heron, an engineer in Alexandria, a city in Egypt. He is calculating the height of a pyramid with its top sliced off, and the working reaches a subtraction, 81−144\sqrt{81 - 144}81−144​. The larger number is the one being taken away, so the height comes out as the square root of a negative.

Every surviving copy has that subtraction reversed, 144−81\sqrt{144 - 81}144−81​ instead, and the root comes out positive and comfortable. Nobody knows whether Heron made the change or a later scribe did. Either way, the negative was read as a slip of the pen rather than a result.

That reading held for another fifteen centuries. A minus sign under a square root meant you had blundered. It could also mean the question had no answer.

This lesson asks the opposite of you. The minus sign is information, not damage. It reports that the answer is off the real number line. It also says where the imaginary unit goes: the square root of a negative nnn is ini\sqrt{n}in​.

Arithmetic Series

Intuition

In the last lesson you learned to name and reach any single term of an arithmetic sequence. Often the question is not “what is one term” but “what is the total of many terms.” A stadium section might have 181818 seats in the front row and two more in each row behind it, and you want the whole seating count. Adding the rows one at a time always works, but it is slow for a large section. There is a shortcut that a young Carl Friedrich Gauss is said to have found in seconds, and this lesson builds it, and shows exactly why it works.

Learning goals

  • Read ∑k=1nak\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k∑k=1n​ak​ as a sum with index and limits
  • Prove Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = \tfrac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}Sn​=2n(a1​+an​)​ by reversing and adding
  • Use the ddd form when the last term is unknown
  • Count n−m+1n - m + 1n−m+1 terms in a range
  • Derive 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)21 + 2 + \cdots + n = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}1+2+⋯+n=2n(n+1)​

From a sequence to a series

A sequence is an ordered list of terms. A series is what you get when you add those terms together. The two ideas are close but not the same: the sequence 2,5,8,112, 5, 8, 112,5,8,11 is a list, while the series 2+5+8+112 + 5 + 8 + 112+5+8+11 is a single number, the sum 262626.

When the terms come from an arithmetic sequence, their sum is called a finite arithmetic series. We write SnS_nSn​, the nnnth partial sum, for the sum of the first nnn terms:

Sn=a1+a2+a3+⋯+an.S_n = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots + a_n.Sn​=a1​+a2​+a3​+⋯+an​.

The subscript on SnS_nSn​ counts how many terms are being added. So S5S_5S5​ is the sum of the first five terms, and S100S_{100}S100​ is the sum of the first hundred. The terms come from a sequence with a first term a1a_1a1​ and a common difference ddd. Because of that structure, we will be able to fold this whole sum into a short formula.

Summation notation

Writing out a1+a2+⋯+ana_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_na1​+a2​+⋯+an​ every time is clumsy, so mathematicians pack it into a single symbol built from the Greek capital letter sigma, ∑\sum∑. The expression

∑k=1nak\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_kk=1∑n​ak​

is read “the sum of aka_kak​ as kkk runs from 111 to nnn,” and it is just shorthand for the same expanded sum you already know:

∑k=1nak=a1+a2+a3+⋯+an.\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots + a_n.k=1∑n​ak​=a1​+a2​+a3​+⋯+an​.

Every part of the symbol has a job:

  • The index is the letter kkk, a counter that steps through the whole numbers one at a time.
  • The lower limit is the number below the sigma, here 111, which says where the counter starts.
  • The upper limit is the number above the sigma, here nnn, which says where the counter stops.
  • The summand is the expression after the sigma, here aka_kak​, the thing being added, written once with the index inside it.

To turn a sigma back into an ordinary sum, you substitute each value of the index in turn and add the results. For instance, with the summand 2k+12k + 12k+1 and the index running from 111 to 444,

∑k=14(2k+1)=(2⋅1+1)+(2⋅2+1)+(2⋅3+1)+(2⋅4+1)=3+5+7+9=24.\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^{4} (2k + 1) &= (2\cdot 1 + 1) + (2\cdot 2 + 1) + (2\cdot 3 + 1) + (2\cdot 4 + 1) \\ &= 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 24. \end{aligned}k=1∑4​(2k+1)​=(2⋅1+1)+(2⋅2+1)+(2⋅3+1)+(2⋅4+1)=3+5+7+9=24.​

The letter chosen for the index does not matter, because it vanishes once the sum is written out: ∑k=14(2k+1)\sum_{k=1}^{4}(2k+1)∑k=14​(2k+1) and ∑i=14(2i+1)\sum_{i=1}^{4}(2i+1)∑i=14​(2i+1) are the very same number. A finite arithmetic series is simply this notation applied to an arithmetic sequence, with ak=a1+(k−1)da_k = a_1 + (k-1)dak​=a1​+(k−1)d as the summand:

Sn=∑k=1nak=a1+a2+⋯+an.S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n.Sn​=k=1∑n​ak​=a1​+a2​+⋯+an​.

Check your understanding

What is ∑k=13(3k−2)\sum_{k=1}^{3} (3k - 2)∑k=13​(3k−2)?

Answer choices

Substitute each value of the index kkk from 111 to 333 into 3k−23k - 23k−2 and add the results.

∑k=13(3k−2)=(3⋅1−2)+(3⋅2−2)+(3⋅3−2)=1+4+7=12.\sum_{k=1}^{3}(3k-2) = (3\cdot 1 - 2) + (3\cdot 2 - 2) + (3\cdot 3 - 2) = 1 + 4 + 7 = 12.∑k=13​(3k−2)=(3⋅1−2)+(3⋅2−2)+(3⋅3−2)=1+4+7=12.

The index takes every whole-number value from the lower limit to the upper limit, once each, so a sum with three values under the sigma has exactly three terms.

The reverse-and-add shortcut

Adding the terms one by one always works, but it is slow. The clever idea is to write the sum twice, once forward and once backward, and add the two copies together. Try it on a short series first, 2+5+8+11+142 + 5 + 8 + 11 + 142+5+8+11+14, by writing it above its own reverse and looking at what each column adds to.

Every column comes to the same total, 161616, because the top row is climbing by 333 each step while the bottom row is falling by that same 333. Whatever one side gains, the other side loses, so the two changes cancel and the column total never moves. That is true for any evenly spaced list, not only this one, so the same trick proves a formula that works for every arithmetic series.

Reverse and add: 2Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)2Sn​=n(a1​+an​)#

Write the series out in order, from the first term to the last:

Sn=a1+a2+a3+⋯+an−1+an.S_n = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots + a_{n-1} + a_n.Sn​=a1​+a2​+a3​+⋯+an−1​+an​.

Now write the very same sum a second time, but with the terms listed in reverse order, from the last term back to the first. The total is unchanged, because addition does not care about the order:

Sn=an+an−1+an−2+⋯+a2+a1.S_n = a_n + a_{n-1} + a_{n-2} + \cdots + a_2 + a_1.Sn​=an​+an−1​+an−2​+⋯+a2​+a1​.

Add the two lines column by column. The first column is a1+ana_1 + a_na1​+an​. Watch what happens as you move one column to the right. The top entry changes by +d+d+d (from a1a_1a1​ to a2a_2a2​), while the bottom entry changes by −d-d−d, the exact opposite (from ana_nan​ to an−1a_{n-1}an−1​). One change undoes the other, so the column total never changes, whether ddd is positive or negative. Every single column adds to the same value, a1+ana_1 + a_na1​+an​.

There are nnn columns, one for each term, so adding the two lines produces nnn copies of a1+ana_1 + a_na1​+an​:

2Sn=n (a1+an).2 S_n = n\,(a_1 + a_n).2Sn​=n(a1​+an​).

The left side is two copies of the series, so dividing both sides by 222 isolates one copy:

Sn=n(a1+an)2.S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}.Sn​=2n(a1​+an​)​.

∎

This formula has a reading worth remembering. Since a1+an2\frac{a_1 + a_n}{2}2a1​+an​​ is the average of the first and last terms, the sum equals the number of terms times that average. Evenly spaced terms balance around their middle, so replacing all of them by their average and multiplying by how many there are gives the exact total.

A second form using the common difference

Sometimes you do not know the last term ana_nan​, only the first term a1a_1a1​, the common difference ddd, and the number of terms nnn. You can still find the sum by replacing ana_nan​ with its formula from the previous lesson, an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d:

Sn=n(a1+an)2=n(a1+a1+(n−1)d)2=n2(2a1+(n−1)d).S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2} = \frac{n\big(a_1 + a_1 + (n-1)d\big)}{2} = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a_1 + (n-1)d\big).Sn​=2n(a1​+an​)​=2n(a1​+a1​+(n−1)d)​=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d).

This is the same total reached from a different starting point, so the two forms always agree; pick whichever matches the information you already have. Use Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}Sn​=2n(a1​+an​)​ when you know the first and last terms and how many there are. Use Sn=n2(2a1+(n−1)d)S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a_1 + (n-1)d\big)Sn​=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d) when you know the first term, the common difference, and the count, but not the last term.

Worked example 1 Sum the first 25 terms

Find the sum of the first 252525 terms of 3,7,11,15,…3, 7, 11, 15, \ldots3,7,11,15,…

The first term is a1=3a_1 = 3a1​=3 and the common difference is d=7−3=4d = 7 - 3 = 4d=7−3=4. You know a1a_1a1​, ddd, and n=25n = 25n=25, but not the last term, so reach for the form that does not need ana_nan​:

S25=252(2⋅3+(25−1)⋅4)=252(6+96)=252⋅102.S_{25} = \frac{25}{2}\big(2\cdot 3 + (25 - 1)\cdot 4\big) = \frac{25}{2}(6 + 96) = \frac{25}{2}\cdot 102.S25​=225​(2⋅3+(25−1)⋅4)=225​(6+96)=225​⋅102.

Since 102÷2=51102 \div 2 = 51102÷2=51,

S25=25×51=1275.S_{25} = 25 \times 51 = 1275.S25​=25×51=1275.

You can confirm this with the other form by first finding the last term. The 252525th term is a25=3+(25−1)⋅4=3+96=99a_{25} = 3 + (25 - 1)\cdot 4 = 3 + 96 = 99a25​=3+(25−1)⋅4=3+96=99, so

S25=25(3+99)2=25×1022=1275.S_{25} = \frac{25(3 + 99)}{2} = \frac{25 \times 102}{2} = 1275.S25​=225(3+99)​=225×102​=1275.

Both routes give 127512751275, which is a good habit for catching slips.

Nothing about either formula requires ddd to be positive. A decreasing sequence, where ddd is negative, adds up the very same way.

Check your understanding

An arithmetic series has first term a1=50a_1 = 50a1​=50, common difference d=−4d = -4d=−4, and n=20n = 20n=20 terms. What is S20S_{20}S20​?

Answer choices

Use Sn=n2(2a1+(n−1)d)S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a_1 + (n-1)d\big)Sn​=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d) with a1=50a_1 = 50a1​=50, d=−4d = -4d=−4, and n=20n = 20n=20.

S20=202(2(50)+19(−4))=10(100−76)=10(24)=240.S_{20} = \frac{20}{2}\big(2(50) + 19(-4)\big) = 10(100 - 76) = 10(24) = 240.S20​=220​(2(50)+19(−4))=10(100−76)=10(24)=240.

The formula works the same way whether ddd is positive or negative. A negative ddd just means each term is smaller than the one before it. In this series the terms shrink from 505050 and eventually turn negative, so those later terms reduce the total instead of adding to it.

Worked example 2 The sum 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + 100

Add all the whole numbers from 111 to 100100100.

This is an arithmetic series with first term a1=1a_1 = 1a1​=1, last term a100=100a_{100} = 100a100​=100, and n=100n = 100n=100 terms. Both the first and last terms are known, so use

S100=n(a1+an)2=100 (1+100)2.S_{100} = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2} = \frac{100\,(1 + 100)}{2}.S100​=2n(a1​+an​)​=2100(1+100)​.

The first and last terms add to 101101101, and there are 100100100 of them, so

S100=100×1012=50×101=5050.S_{100} = \frac{100 \times 101}{2} = 50 \times 101 = 5050.S100​=2100×101​=50×101=5050.

The pairing view says the same thing: match 111 with 100100100, 222 with 999999, 333 with 989898, and so on down the line. That makes 505050 pairs, each adding to 101101101, and 50×101=505050 \times 101 = 505050×101=5050.

Nothing about that argument depends on stopping at 100100100. For any positive whole number nnn, the sum 1+2+⋯+n1 + 2 + \cdots + n1+2+⋯+n is an arithmetic series with first term a1=1a_1 = 1a1​=1, last term an=na_n = nan​=n, and nnn terms, so

1+2+⋯+n=n(1+n)2=n(n+1)2.1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(1 + n)}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.1+2+⋯+n=2n(1+n)​=2n(n+1)​.

This single formula covers every whole-number sum at once, from 1+2+⋯+1001 + 2 + \cdots + 1001+2+⋯+100 down to 1+2+⋯+51 + 2 + \cdots + 51+2+⋯+5, or up to any nnn you choose.

An arithmetic series has first term a1=5a_1 = 5a1​=5, last term an=95a_n = 95an​=95, and n=10n = 10n=10 terms. What is the sum S10S_{10}S10​?

Answer choices

You know the first term, the last term, and how many terms there are, so use Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}Sn​=2n(a1​+an​)​ with n=10n = 10n=10.

S10=10(5+95)2=10×1002=500S_{10} = \frac{10(5 + 95)}{2} = \frac{10 \times 100}{2} = 500S10​=210(5+95)​=210×100​=500

The first and last terms average to 505050, and ten terms averaging 505050 total 500500500.

Summing a range of terms

Not every series starts at the first term. To add, say, the 101010th through the 303030th terms, notice that any run of consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence is itself an arithmetic series. So the same formula applies, as long as you use that run’s own first term, its own last term, and the right count. The number of terms from position mmm to position nnn is n−m+1n - m + 1n−m+1, not n−mn - mn−m, because both endpoints are counted.

A range sum can also be found by subtracting one partial sum from another, a different route to the same number and a good way to catch a mistake. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Worked example 3 Sum the 10th through 30th terms

For the arithmetic sequence with a1=4a_1 = 4a1​=4 and d=3d = 3d=3, find the sum of the terms from the 101010th through the 303030th.

Start with the explicit formula for a single term, an=a1+(n−1)d=4+3(n−1)a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d = 4 + 3(n - 1)an​=a1​+(n−1)d=4+3(n−1). The two end terms of the range are

a10=4+3(10−1)=4+27=31,a30=4+3(30−1)=4+87=91.a_{10} = 4 + 3(10 - 1) = 4 + 27 = 31, \qquad a_{30} = 4 + 3(30 - 1) = 4 + 87 = 91.a10​=4+3(10−1)=4+27=31,a30​=4+3(30−1)=4+87=91.

Count the terms carefully. From position 101010 to position 303030 there are 30−10+1=2130 - 10 + 1 = 2130−10+1=21 terms, because both ends are included. This range is its own arithmetic series with first term 313131, last term 919191, and 212121 terms:

S=21(31+91)2=21×1222=21×61=1281.S = \frac{21(31 + 91)}{2} = \frac{21 \times 122}{2} = 21 \times 61 = 1281.S=221(31+91)​=221×122​=21×61=1281.

The sum of the terms from the 101010th through the 303030th is 128112811281.

Check your understanding

How many terms are there from the 777th term through the 191919th term of a sequence, inclusive?

Answer choices

Both endpoints are included, so the count is n−m+1=19−7+1=13n - m + 1 = 19 - 7 + 1 = 13n−m+1=19−7+1=13, not the plain difference 19−7=1219 - 7 = 1219−7=12. Dropping the +1+1+1 leaves out one whole term, the one at the far end.

Series in the real world

Worked example 4 Seats in a section

A theater section has 121212 rows. The front row holds 181818 seats, and each row behind it holds 222 more seats than the row in front. How many seats are in the whole section?

The seat counts by row form an arithmetic sequence with a1=18a_1 = 18a1​=18 and d=2d = 2d=2, and the section total is the arithmetic series of those 121212 row counts. First find the back row, the 121212th term:

a12=18+(12−1)⋅2=18+22=40.a_{12} = 18 + (12 - 1)\cdot 2 = 18 + 22 = 40.a12​=18+(12−1)⋅2=18+22=40.

The rows run from 181818 seats up to 404040 seats, and there are 121212 of them, so the total is

S12=12(18+40)2=12×582=6×58=348 seats.S_{12} = \frac{12(18 + 40)}{2} = \frac{12 \times 58}{2} = 6 \times 58 = 348 \text{ seats}.S12​=212(18+40)​=212×58​=6×58=348 seats.

Adding the twelve row counts one at a time would give the same 348348348, but the series formula reaches it in a single step. The very same method totals stacked logs, the rows of a stadium, or a saving plan that grows by a fixed amount each period.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing a sequence with a series. A sequence is the list a1,a2,a3,…a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldotsa1​,a2​,a3​,…; a series is its sum a1+a2+a3+⋯a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdotsa1​+a2​+a3​+⋯. “Find the 101010th term” asks about the sequence, while “find the sum of the first 101010 terms” asks about the series.
  • Miscounting the terms in a range. The number of terms from position mmm to position nnn is n−m+1n - m + 1n−m+1, not n−mn - mn−m. Dropping the +1+1+1 loses one whole term: from the 101010th to the 303030th there are 212121 terms, not 202020.
  • Forgetting to divide by two. The sum is n(a1+an)2\frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}2n(a1​+an​)​, not n(a1+an)n(a_1 + a_n)n(a1​+an​). The division by 222 is there because reverse-and-add counts the whole series twice before you halve it.
  • Choosing the less convenient form. Finding ana_nan​ first and then using Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}Sn​=2n(a1​+an​)​ is not wrong when you know a1a_1a1​, ddd, and nnn, but it adds an extra step. Going straight to Sn=n2(2a1+(n−1)d)S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a_1 + (n-1)d\big)Sn​=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d) saves the trip.
  • Mixing up the count with the total. In “the sum of the first 121212 terms is 348348348,” the number 121212 is the count nnn and 348348348 is the sum SnS_nSn​. Swapping them, treating the total as if it were the number of terms, is a common slip in word problems.

Key takeaways

  • A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence; the finite arithmetic series is Sn=a1+a2+⋯+anS_n = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_nSn​=a1​+a2​+⋯+an​.
  • Summation notation writes it compactly: ∑k=1nak\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k∑k=1n​ak​ means a1+a2+⋯+ana_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_na1​+a2​+⋯+an​, where kkk is the index, 111 and nnn are the lower and upper limits, and aka_kak​ is the summand.
  • Reverse and add proves 2Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)2Sn​=n(a1​+an​), so Sn=n(a1+an)2S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}Sn​=2n(a1​+an​)​, the number of terms times the average of the first and last.
  • Substituting an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d gives the equivalent form Sn=n2(2a1+(n−1)d)S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a_1 + (n-1)d\big)Sn​=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d), which is handy when the last term is unknown.
  • The number of terms from position mmm to position nnn is n−m+1n - m + 1n−m+1. That range is its own arithmetic series, so its sum is the count times the average of its own first and last terms.
  • The famous case 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)21 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}1+2+⋯+n=2n(n+1)​ follows at once, since a1=1a_1 = 1a1​=1 and an=na_n = nan​=n.

Checking a range sum by subtracting partial sums

There is a second way to sum a range of terms: subtract one partial sum from another. The terms from position mmm to position nnn are everything up to term nnn with everything up to term m−1m - 1m−1 removed, so their total is Sn−Sm−1S_n - S_{m-1}Sn​−Sm−1​.

For the arithmetic sequence with a1=4a_1 = 4a1​=4 and d=3d = 3d=3, sum the terms from the 101010th through the 303030th again, this time by subtraction. The sum of the first 303030 terms is S30=30(4+91)2=1425S_{30} = \frac{30(4 + 91)}{2} = 1425S30​=230(4+91)​=1425, and the sum of the first 999 terms is S9=9(4+28)2=144S_{9} = \frac{9(4 + 28)}{2} = 144S9​=29(4+28)​=144, using a9=4+3(9−1)=28a_9 = 4 + 3(9 - 1) = 28a9​=4+3(9−1)=28. Taking one from the other strips off terms 111 through 999 and leaves exactly terms 101010 through 303030:

S30−S9=1425−144=1281.S_{30} - S_{9} = 1425 - 144 = 1281.S30​−S9​=1425−144=1281.

This matches the 128112811281 found directly in Worked Example 3, which is a useful way to catch a mistake: a range sum found two different ways should always agree.

A bit of history (optional)

A teacher who wants an hour of quiet sets the class a very long addition. That, the story goes, is how the shortcut in this lesson was found.

The boy was Carl Friedrich Gauss, later one of the great mathematicians, and he was about nine years old. His schoolmaster in Brunswick, a German town, told the class to add a long run of evenly spaced numbers. Gauss laid his slate on the table almost at once, with a single number written on it.

What had he noticed? Take the list from one to a hundred, the version usually attached to the story. The smallest number and the largest add to a hundred and one. So do the second smallest and the second largest, because one climbs by exactly as much as the other falls. Fifty such pairs, each worth a hundred and one, come to five thousand and fifty. He never had to add anything a hundred times.

Treat the details of that afternoon gently. The earliest published account appeared in 1856, the year after Gauss died. It does not say which numbers the schoolmaster chose. Later tellers supplied them, and different tellers supply different ones. The pairing survives every version, though, and it is exactly what the reverse-and-add proof performs. Writing the series above its own reverse builds all the pairs at once. That is why every column in that proof carries the same total.

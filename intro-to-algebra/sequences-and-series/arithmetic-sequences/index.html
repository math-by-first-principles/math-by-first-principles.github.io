Arithmetic Sequences

Intuition

Some patterns grow by the same fixed amount every step. A theater’s front row has 181818 seats, the next row has 202020, the next 222222, always two more than the row before. You could keep adding 222 to find any row’s seat count, but listing all the way to row 500500500 to find how many seats it holds is not realistic. What you really want is a single formula that jumps straight to any position. Building that formula, and understanding exactly why it works, is what this lesson is about.

Learning goals

  • Index a sequence's terms by position
  • Find the common difference from consecutive terms
  • Apply an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d for any term
  • Write the recursive rule beside the explicit one
  • Recover ddd from two terms with ak=am+(k−m)da_k = a_m + (k-m)dak​=am​+(k−m)d
  • Solve for nnn to test whether a value appears

Naming the terms of a sequence

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers. Order is part of what a sequence is: the list 2,5,8,112, 5, 8, 112,5,8,11 is a different sequence from 11,8,5,211, 8, 5, 211,8,5,2, even though the same four numbers appear. Each number in the list is called a term.

Because the order matters, we label the terms by their position. We write a1a_1a1​ for the first term, a2a_2a2​ for the second, a3a_3a3​ for the third, and in general ana_nan​ for the term in position nnn. The small number written below the line is the subscript, and it is just the position counter, not a multiplication. For the sequence 2,5,8,112, 5, 8, 112,5,8,11,

a1=2,a2=5,a3=8,a4=11.a_1 = 2, \qquad a_2 = 5, \qquad a_3 = 8, \qquad a_4 = 11.a1​=2,a2​=5,a3​=8,a4​=11.

Another way to see this: a sequence is a function whose inputs are the positions 1,2,3,…1, 2, 3, \ldots1,2,3,… and whose outputs are the terms. Instead of writing f(1)f(1)f(1), f(2)f(2)f(2), f(3)f(3)f(3) the way we did for ordinary functions, we write a1a_1a1​, a2a_2a2​, a3a_3a3​. The subscript notation is only a compact way to write “the output at position nnn.” The position nnn is always a counting number (1,2,3,…1, 2, 3, \ldots1,2,3,…), never a fraction or a negative, because there is no “term number one-half.”

What makes a sequence arithmetic

An arithmetic sequence is a sequence in which you get each term by adding the same fixed number to the term before it. That fixed number is called the common difference, written ddd. It is the gap between one term and the next, and in an arithmetic sequence that gap never changes.

To find ddd, subtract any term from the one that follows it:

d=a2−a1=a3−a2=a4−a3=⋯d = a_2 - a_1 = a_3 - a_2 = a_4 - a_3 = \cdotsd=a2​−a1​=a3​−a2​=a4​−a3​=⋯

Take 3,7,11,15,193, 7, 11, 15, 193,7,11,15,19. The differences are 7−3=47 - 3 = 47−3=4, then 11−7=411 - 7 = 411−7=4, then 15−11=415 - 11 = 415−11=4, then 19−15=419 - 15 = 419−15=4. Every gap is 444, so this is arithmetic with d=4d = 4d=4. The common difference can be negative, which makes the sequence decrease: 20,17,14,1120, 17, 14, 1120,17,14,11 has d=17−20=−3d = 17 - 20 = -3d=17−20=−3, since each term is three less than the one before. It can also be a fraction or a decimal. The common difference can even be zero: then every term is the same and the sequence is constant, which still counts as arithmetic (it just never changes).

To test whether a sequence is arithmetic, check that all the consecutive differences agree. The sequence 2,4,8,162, 4, 8, 162,4,8,16 is not arithmetic: the first gap is 4−2=24 - 2 = 24−2=2 but the second is 8−4=48 - 4 = 48−4=4, and a single mismatched gap is enough to rule it out.

A formula for the nth term

Adding ddd over and over does reach any term, but it is slow. To find the 100100100th term you would add ddd ninety-nine times. There is a shortcut, and the picture above already shows why it works. To land on a later term you take a whole number of equal jumps from the first term, so you only need to count the jumps.

The nnnth term is an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d#

Start at the first term and add the common difference one step at a time. The second term is the first term plus one ddd:

a2=a1+d.a_2 = a_1 + d.a2​=a1​+d.

The third term is the second plus another ddd, so it is the first term plus two ddd‘s:

a3=a2+d=(a1+d)+d=a1+2d.a_3 = a_2 + d = (a_1 + d) + d = a_1 + 2d.a3​=a2​+d=(a1​+d)+d=a1​+2d.

The fourth term adds one more ddd, which makes three ddd‘s in all:

a4=a3+d=a1+3d.a_4 = a_3 + d = a_1 + 3d.a4​=a3​+d=a1​+3d.

The number of ddd‘s added is always one less than the position of the term: the second term carries one ddd, the third carries two, the fourth carries three. That is because reaching the nnnth term from the first takes n−1n - 1n−1 single steps, each adding ddd: one step lands you on the second term, two steps on the third, and so on up to the nnnth. Adding n−1n - 1n−1 copies of ddd to the first term gives

an=a1+(n−1) d.a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,d.an​=a1​+(n−1)d.

This closed formula reaches any term straight from its position nnn, with no need to build the terms before it.

∎

Two numbers generate the entire sequence: the first term a1a_1a1​ tells you where it starts, and the common difference ddd tells you the size and direction of each step. Any term, at any position the sequence reaches, follows from just those two numbers.

Worked example 1 Find the 15th term

Find the 151515th term of the sequence 4,11,18,25,…4, 11, 18, 25, \ldots4,11,18,25,…

First read off the two numbers that define the sequence. The first term is a1=4a_1 = 4a1​=4. The common difference is the gap between consecutive terms, 11−4=711 - 4 = 711−4=7, so d=7d = 7d=7.

Now use the formula with n=15n = 15n=15. The number of steps is n−1=14n - 1 = 14n−1=14:

a15=a1+(15−1)d=4+14×7.a_{15} = a_1 + (15 - 1)d = 4 + 14 \times 7.a15​=a1​+(15−1)d=4+14×7.

Work out the product before the sum, 14×7=9814 \times 7 = 9814×7=98, then add:

a15=4+98=102.a_{15} = 4 + 98 = 102.a15​=4+98=102.

So the 151515th term is 102102102. Notice you never had to list the first fourteen terms.

Check your understanding

An arithmetic sequence has first term a1=40a_1 = 40a1​=40 and common difference d=−3d = -3d=−3. What is the 101010th term a10a_{10}a10​?

Answer choices

Use an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d with a1=40a_1 = 40a1​=40, d=−3d = -3d=−3, and n=10n = 10n=10, so there are n−1=9n - 1 = 9n−1=9 steps.

a10=40+9×(−3)=40−27=13a_{10} = 40 + 9 \times (-3) = 40 - 27 = 13a10​=40+9×(−3)=40−27=13

Because ddd is negative the terms decrease, so the tenth term 131313 is smaller than the first term 404040.

The recursive rule

The formula an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d is called the explicit (or closed) form: it gives a term directly from its position. There is a second way to describe the same sequence, called the recursive form. It says how each term is built from the one right before it:

an=an−1+dfor n≥2,a_n = a_{n-1} + d \qquad \text{for } n \ge 2,an​=an−1​+dfor n≥2,

together with a stated first term a1a_1a1​. Read aloud, it says exactly what “arithmetic” means: each term is the previous term plus the common difference. It also matches how many real processes actually work (you deposit 252525 more dollars onto last month’s balance, not recompute the total from scratch). Its one drawback: to reach a far-off term you must climb through every term in between. The explicit formula skips straight to any position; the recursive rule only describes the next step.

Climbing through the terms is worth doing once by hand, because it is what makes the n−1n - 1n−1 stop being an arbitrary detail. Try it below: the figure is a single step on a number line, and you control both halves of it, the number it starts from and the number added to it.

Set the start to −5-5−5 and the step to 333. The first move lands you on −2-2−2; restart from there and repeat three more times to land on 111, then 444, then 777. That is four steps in total to go from a1a_1a1​ to a5a_5a5​, exactly the n−1n - 1n−1 in the formula, since standing on the first term costs no steps at all. Now start over at 777 and use a step of −3-3−3 instead: four steps of −3-3−3 retrace the same route back down to −5-5−5, which is why a negative common difference runs a sequence downward without changing how the steps are counted.

The same counting argument works between any two terms, not only from the first. To get from the mmmth term to the kkkth term you take k−mk - mk−m steps of size ddd, so

ak=am+(k−m) d.a_k = a_m + (k - m)\,d.ak​=am​+(k−m)d.

This version is what you reach for when you know two terms and want to recover the common difference.

Worked example 2 Find ddd and a1a_1a1​ from two terms

In an arithmetic sequence the third term is a3=20a_3 = 20a3​=20 and the seventh term is a7=44a_7 = 44a7​=44. Find the common difference and the first term.

Between the third term and the seventh term there are 7−3=47 - 3 = 47−3=4 steps, each of size ddd. Using ak=am+(k−m)da_k = a_m + (k - m)dak​=am​+(k−m)d,

a7=a3+(7−3)d⟹44=20+4d.a_7 = a_3 + (7 - 3)d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 44 = 20 + 4d.a7​=a3​+(7−3)d⟹44=20+4d.

Solve for ddd by subtracting 202020 and dividing by 444:

4d=24⟹d=6.4d = 24 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = 6.4d=24⟹d=6.

Now back up from the third term to the first. The first term is two steps below the third, so a1=a3−2da_1 = a_3 - 2da1​=a3​−2d:

a1=20−2×6=20−12=8.a_1 = 20 - 2 \times 6 = 20 - 12 = 8.a1​=20−2×6=20−12=8.

The sequence is 8,14,20,26,32,38,44,…8, 14, 20, 26, 32, 38, 44, \ldots8,14,20,26,32,38,44,…, and a quick check confirms a3=20a_3 = 20a3​=20 and a7=44a_7 = 44a7​=44.

Check your understanding

An arithmetic sequence has a2=9a_2 = 9a2​=9 and a5=21a_5 = 21a5​=21. What is the common difference ddd?

Answer choices

There are 5−2=35 - 2 = 35−2=3 steps between the two terms. Using ak=am+(k−m)da_k = a_m + (k-m)dak​=am​+(k−m)d:

21=9+3d21 = 9 + 3d21=9+3d

so 3d=123d = 123d=12 and d=4d = 4d=4. (Dividing the gap 21−9=1221 - 9 = 1221−9=12 by the wrong number of steps is the usual slip here.)

Working backward: which term is it?

The explicit formula also answers the reverse question. Instead of asking “what is term number nnn,” you can ask “which term equals a given value,” and you find out by solving for nnn. Because nnn must be a counting number, this also tells you whether a value appears in the sequence at all.

Worked example 3 Which term equals a given value?

For the sequence 5,8,11,14,…5, 8, 11, 14, \ldots5,8,11,14,…, decide which term equals 989898, and whether 100100100 ever appears.

Here a1=5a_1 = 5a1​=5 and d=3d = 3d=3, so the explicit formula is an=5+(n−1) 3a_n = 5 + (n - 1)\,3an​=5+(n−1)3. Set it equal to 989898 and solve for nnn:

5+3(n−1)=98⟹3(n−1)=93⟹n−1=31.5 + 3(n - 1) = 98 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3(n - 1) = 93 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n - 1 = 31.5+3(n−1)=98⟹3(n−1)=93⟹n−1=31.

So n=32n = 32n=32, which is a whole number, and 989898 is the 323232nd term.

Now try 100100100 the same way:

5+3(n−1)=100⟹3(n−1)=95⟹n−1=953.5 + 3(n - 1) = 100 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3(n - 1) = 95 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n - 1 = \tfrac{95}{3}.5+3(n−1)=100⟹3(n−1)=95⟹n−1=395​.

Since 953\tfrac{95}{3}395​ is not a whole number, there is no position nnn that lands on 100100100, so 100100100 is not a term of this sequence. A value belongs to the sequence only when solving for nnn gives a positive whole number.

Check your understanding

For the sequence 2,6,10,14,…2, 6, 10, 14, \ldots2,6,10,14,…, is 100100100 a term? If so, which position?

Answer choices

Solve 2+4(n−1)=1002 + 4(n-1) = 1002+4(n−1)=100: this gives 4(n−1)=984(n-1) = 984(n−1)=98, so n−1=24.5n - 1 = 24.5n−1=24.5. Since n−1n - 1n−1 is not a whole number, 100100100 never appears in this sequence. (Its neighbors are 989898, the 25th term, and 102102102, the 26th.)

A real-world pattern

Worked example 4 Seats in a theater

A theater has 181818 seats in its front row, and each row behind it holds 222 more seats than the row in front. How many seats are in row 121212, and which row is the first to hold 545454 seats?

The seat counts by row form an arithmetic sequence with first term a1=18a_1 = 18a1​=18 (the front row) and common difference d=2d = 2d=2. The number of seats in row nnn is

an=18+(n−1) 2.a_n = 18 + (n - 1)\,2.an​=18+(n−1)2.

For row 121212, put n=12n = 12n=12, so there are n−1=11n - 1 = 11n−1=11 steps:

a12=18+11×2=18+22=40 seats.a_{12} = 18 + 11 \times 2 = 18 + 22 = 40 \text{ seats}.a12​=18+11×2=18+22=40 seats.

To find the row with 545454 seats, set an=54a_n = 54an​=54 and solve for nnn:

18+2(n−1)=54⟹2(n−1)=36⟹n−1=18.18 + 2(n - 1) = 54 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2(n - 1) = 36 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n - 1 = 18.18+2(n−1)=54⟹2(n−1)=36⟹n−1=18.

So n=19n = 19n=19, and row 191919 is the one with 545454 seats. Plugging in a position and solving for one are the two moves this lesson built, and together they answer both questions.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using nnn instead of n−1n - 1n−1 steps. The nnnth term adds the common difference n−1n - 1n−1 times, not nnn times. The first term already sits at position 111 with zero steps taken, so an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d. Writing a1+nda_1 + nda1​+nd overshoots by one full step.
  • Subtracting the difference the wrong way. The common difference is a later term minus an earlier term, d=a2−a1d = a_2 - a_1d=a2​−a1​. Reversing it flips the sign, turning a rising sequence into a falling one.
  • Losing the sign of a negative ddd. When the sequence decreases, ddd is negative, and (n−1)d(n-1)d(n−1)d must stay negative through the whole computation. It helps to keep the negative in parentheses, as in 40+9×(−3)40 + 9 \times (-3)40+9×(−3).
  • Assuming every value appears. Solving an=Va_n = Van​=V tells you the position only if nnn comes out a positive whole number. If nnn is a fraction, the value simply is not a term of the sequence.
  • Confusing the position with the term. In “the 121212th term is 404040,” the number 121212 is the position nnn and 404040 is the term ana_nan​. Feeding 404040 back in as if it were the position is a common slip in word problems.

Key takeaways

  • A sequence is an ordered list of terms a1,a2,a3,…a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldotsa1​,a2​,a3​,…, where the subscript nnn is the position; it is a function whose inputs are the counting numbers.
  • A sequence is arithmetic when consecutive terms differ by a constant common difference d=a2−a1=a3−a2=⋯d = a_2 - a_1 = a_3 - a_2 = \cdotsd=a2​−a1​=a3​−a2​=⋯, which may be positive, negative, zero, or fractional.
  • The explicit formula is an=a1+(n−1)da_n = a_1 + (n-1)dan​=a1​+(n−1)d: start at a1a_1a1​ and take n−1n - 1n−1 steps of size ddd.
  • The recursive rule is an=an−1+da_n = a_{n-1} + dan​=an−1​+d with a1a_1a1​ given, describing one step at a time.
  • Between any two terms, ak=am+(k−m)da_k = a_m + (k - m)dak​=am​+(k−m)d, which recovers ddd from two known terms.
  • To find which term equals a value VVV, solve an=Va_n = Van​=V for nnn; the value is in the sequence only if nnn is a positive whole number.

A bit of history (optional)

A hundred loaves of bread must be divided among five workers, with shares that climb from smallest to largest by a fixed amount. One more condition pins the shares down exactly: the two smallest shares together must equal one-seventh of the three largest shares together. How much bread does every worker receive?

That question is remarkably old. It sits in the Rhind Papyrus, a scroll of practical arithmetic from ancient Egypt. A scribe named Ahmes copied the surviving scroll out around 1650 BCE, working from an older roll that is now lost. Its problems are practical ones: grain to measure, wages to pay, and food or goods to divide unevenly by a stated rule.

Ahmes had no symbol for an unknown quantity and no formula for a distant term, yet he worked the shares out and solved it anyway. Notice how little information the problem actually hands you once you see its structure: the size of one share, and the gap from each share to the next. Every remaining share follows from those two numbers, which are exactly what this lesson calls the first term and the common difference. Thirty-six centuries of better notation have changed how we write that pair down, but never added a third number to it.

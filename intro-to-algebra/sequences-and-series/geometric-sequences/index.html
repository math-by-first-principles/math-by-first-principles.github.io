Geometric Sequences

Intuition

A single bacterium in a warm dish splits into two, then each of those splits again, so the count runs 1,2,4,8,161, 2, 4, 8, 161,2,4,8,16, doubling on every round. A car worth 20,000 dollars loses a fifth of its value each year, so its price slides to 16,00016{,}00016,000, then 12,80012{,}80012,800, shrinking by the same factor annually. A ball dropped on a hard floor rebounds to a fixed fraction of its last height on every bounce. None of these lists is arithmetic, because the terms do not change by a fixed amount; they change by a fixed multiple. A pattern in which each term is the one before it times a constant is a geometric sequence. Such a sequence is the natural partner to the arithmetic sequence you just met. This lesson builds the formula that jumps straight to any term, and shows exactly why it works.

Learning goals

  • Divide consecutive terms to find the common ratio
  • Apply an=a1rn−1a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}an​=a1​rn−1 for any term
  • Move between terms with ak=amrk−ma_k = a_m r^{k-m}ak​=am​rk−m
  • Test membership by solving for nnn, or with a logarithm
  • Read the ratio for growth, decay, alternation or constancy
  • Exclude r=0r = 0r=0, since no term is ever zero

What makes a sequence geometric

In an arithmetic sequence you reach the next term by adding a fixed number, the common difference ddd. In a geometric sequence you reach it by multiplying by a fixed number. That fixed multiplier is the common ratio, written rrr. To find it, divide any term by the term before it, and in a geometric sequence that quotient is always the same:

r=a2a1=a3a2=a4a3=⋯r = \frac{a_2}{a_1} = \frac{a_3}{a_2} = \frac{a_4}{a_3} = \cdotsr=a1​a2​​=a2​a3​​=a3​a4​​=⋯

Take 3,6,12,24,483, 6, 12, 24, 483,6,12,24,48. Dividing each term by the one before it gives 63=2\tfrac{6}{3} = 236​=2, 126=2\tfrac{12}{6} = 2612​=2, 2412=2\tfrac{24}{12} = 21224​=2, and 4824=2\tfrac{48}{24} = 22448​=2. Every ratio is 222, so the sequence is geometric with r=2r = 2r=2. This is the mirror image of the arithmetic test, where you subtract to look for a constant difference. Here the differences are 3,6,12,243, 6, 12, 243,6,12,24, which are nowhere near constant, so the list is not arithmetic. Multiplying by a constant is a completely different engine from adding one: with r=2r = 2r=2 here it makes the terms grow faster and faster.

The common ratio can be almost any number, but the ratio test itself rules out two values. The first term a1a_1a1​ must be nonzero, since finding rrr starts by dividing by a1a_1a1​. And rrr itself cannot be 000: if it were, every term after the first would become 000, and the very next ratio, a3a2\tfrac{a_3}{a_2}a2​a3​​, would be 00\tfrac{0}{0}00​, which is undefined. A ratio test that breaks after one step is not a constant ratio. Together these mean no term of a geometric sequence is ever zero: starting away from zero and only ever multiplying by a nonzero rrr can never land on zero.

Beyond that, rrr can be almost anything, and its value decides the shape of the sequence. When rrr is greater than 111 the terms grow in size, and when rrr is between 000 and 111 they shrink toward zero. When rrr is negative the terms flip sign back and forth, and when rrr is exactly 111 every term is the same.

To test whether a sequence is geometric, check that all the consecutive ratios agree. The sequence 2,4,6,82, 4, 6, 82,4,6,8 is not geometric: the first ratio is 42=2\tfrac{4}{2} = 224​=2 but the second is 64=1.5\tfrac{6}{4} = 1.546​=1.5, and a single mismatch disqualifies it. That list is arithmetic instead, since it is the differences that stay constant there.

A formula for the nth term

Multiplying by rrr over and over reaches any term, but it is slow: to find the 202020th term you would multiply nineteen times. The picture above already shows the shortcut. To land on a later term you apply the ratio a whole number of times from the first term, so you only need to count the multiplications.

The nnnth term is an=a1rn−1a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}an​=a1​rn−1#

Start at the first term and multiply by the common ratio one step at a time. The second term is the first term times one factor of rrr:

a2=a1r.a_2 = a_1 r.a2​=a1​r.

The third term is the second times another rrr, which makes two factors of rrr on the first term:

a3=a2r=(a1r) r=a1r2.a_3 = a_2 r = (a_1 r)\,r = a_1 r^2.a3​=a2​r=(a1​r)r=a1​r2.

The fourth term multiplies once more, for three factors in all:

a4=a3r=a1r3.a_4 = a_3 r = a_1 r^3.a4​=a3​r=a1​r3.

A pattern is now plain. The number of factors of rrr is always one less than the position of the term. The second term carries one, the third carries two, and the fourth carries three. The reason is easy to say in words. To travel from the first term to the nnnth term you multiply exactly n−1n - 1n−1 times, and every multiplication is by rrr. That count is right because those multiplications land you on the second term, then the third, and so on up to the nnnth. Applying n−1n - 1n−1 factors of rrr to the first term gives

an=a1rn−1.a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}.an​=a1​rn−1.

This closed formula reaches any term straight from its position nnn, with no need to build the terms before it.

∎

The formula has a clean meaning: start at a1a_1a1​ and apply the ratio n−1n - 1n−1 times. Just as an arithmetic sequence is pinned down by its first term and common difference, a geometric sequence is pinned down by just two numbers, the first term a1a_1a1​ and the common ratio rrr.

Worked example 1 Find the 8th term

Find the 888th term of the sequence 3,6,12,24,…3, 6, 12, 24, \ldots3,6,12,24,…

First read off the two numbers that define the sequence. The first term is a1=3a_1 = 3a1​=3. The common ratio is any term divided by the one before it, 63=2\tfrac{6}{3} = 236​=2, so r=2r = 2r=2.

Now use the formula with n=8n = 8n=8. The number of multiplications is n−1=7n - 1 = 7n−1=7:

a8=a1r8−1=3⋅27.a_8 = a_1 r^{8-1} = 3 \cdot 2^{7}.a8​=a1​r8−1=3⋅27.

Work out the power before the product, 27=1282^7 = 12827=128, then multiply:

a8=3⋅128=384.a_8 = 3 \cdot 128 = 384.a8​=3⋅128=384.

So the 888th term is 384384384. Notice you never had to list the first seven terms.

The recursive rule and moving between terms

The formula an=a1rn−1a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}an​=a1​rn−1 is the explicit (or closed) form, because it gives a term directly from its position. The same sequence also has a recursive form, which describes one step at a time:

an=r an−1for n≥2,a_n = r\,a_{n-1} \qquad \text{for } n \ge 2,an​=ran−1​for n≥2,

together with a stated first term a1a_1a1​. Read aloud, it is the definition itself: each term is rrr times the term before it. The recursive rule often matches a real process most honestly (this year’s balance is last year’s balance times the growth factor). But to reach a far-off term with the recursive rule, you must climb through every term in between. The explicit formula is the tool for jumping ahead; the recursive rule is the tool for describing the step.

The multiplying works between any two terms, not only from the first. When k>mk > mk>m, getting from the mmmth term to the later kkkth term takes exactly k−mk - mk−m multiplications by rrr, so

ak=am rk−m.a_k = a_m\,r^{k-m}.ak​=am​rk−m.

The same formula still holds when k<mk < mk<m, moving from a later term back to an earlier one, but then “multiply k−mk - mk−m times” stops being the right picture, since k−mk - mk−m is negative. Moving backward divides by rrr once per step instead, and the negative exponent in rk−mr^{k-m}rk−m records exactly that: it is r−1r^{-1}r−1, which is 1r\tfrac{1}{r}r1​, raised to the m−km - km−k steps you are moving back. Either way, the formula ak=amrk−ma_k = a_m r^{k-m}ak​=am​rk−m is correct for any two positions mmm and kkk, and it is the version to reach for when you know two terms and want to recover the ratio or a distant term.

Worked example 2 Recover the ratio and first term

In a geometric sequence the second term is a2=6a_2 = 6a2​=6 and the fifth term is a5=48a_5 = 48a5​=48. Find the common ratio and the first term.

Between the second term and the fifth there are 5−2=35 - 2 = 35−2=3 multiplications by rrr. Using ak=amrk−ma_k = a_m r^{k-m}ak​=am​rk−m,

a5=a2 r5−2⟹48=6 r3.a_5 = a_2\,r^{5-2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 48 = 6\,r^{3}.a5​=a2​r5−2⟹48=6r3.

Divide both sides by 666 to isolate the power, then take the cube root:

r3=8⟹r=2.r^{3} = 8 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = 2.r3=8⟹r=2.

A cube root has just one real value, so r=2r = 2r=2 is settled. Now back up one step from the second term to the first, dividing by rrr because a geometric sequence multiplies going forward:

a1=a2r=62=3.a_1 = \frac{a_2}{r} = \frac{6}{2} = 3.a1​=ra2​​=26​=3.

The sequence is 3,6,12,24,48,…3, 6, 12, 24, 48, \ldots3,6,12,24,48,…, and a quick check confirms a2=6a_2 = 6a2​=6 and a5=48a_5 = 48a5​=48.

One warning about recovering rrr. In that example the two known terms sat an odd number of positions apart, so r3=8r^3 = 8r3=8 had a single real solution. When the two terms are an even number of positions apart, the equation for rrr is an even power, and an even power hides a sign. From a1=3a_1 = 3a1​=3 and a3=12a_3 = 12a3​=12, for instance, you get r2=123=4r^2 = \tfrac{12}{3} = 4r2=312​=4, which is solved by both r=2r = 2r=2 and r=−2r = -2r=−2. The first gives 3,6,12,243, 6, 12, 243,6,12,24 and the second gives 3,−6,12,−243, -6, 12, -243,−6,12,−24, and both genuinely have a3=12a_3 = 12a3​=12. With only those two terms you cannot tell which sequence is meant. To settle it you need one more fact, such as a term that fixes the sign or a statement that the ratio is positive.

Testing membership and solving for n

The explicit formula answers the reverse question too. Instead of asking “what is term number nnn,” you can ask “which term equals a given value,” and you find out by solving for nnn. Because nnn must be a counting number, this also tells you whether a value appears in the sequence at all.

Worked example 3 Is a value a term?

For the sequence 3,6,12,24,…3, 6, 12, 24, \ldots3,6,12,24,…, decide whether 768768768 is a term, and whether 300300300 ever appears.

Here a1=3a_1 = 3a1​=3 and r=2r = 2r=2, so the explicit formula is an=3⋅2n−1a_n = 3 \cdot 2^{n-1}an​=3⋅2n−1. Set it equal to 768768768 and isolate the power:

3⋅2n−1=768⟹2n−1=256.3 \cdot 2^{n-1} = 768 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2^{n-1} = 256.3⋅2n−1=768⟹2n−1=256.

Now match powers of 222. Since 256=28256 = 2^{8}256=28, the exponents must agree, so n−1=8n - 1 = 8n−1=8 and n=9n = 9n=9. That is a whole number, so 768768768 is the 999th term.

Try 300300300 the same way:

3⋅2n−1=300⟹2n−1=100.3 \cdot 2^{n-1} = 300 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2^{n-1} = 100.3⋅2n−1=300⟹2n−1=100.

But 100100100 is not a power of 222: it falls between 26=642^6 = 6426=64 and 27=1282^7 = 12827=128, so no whole number exponent works. Therefore 300300300 is not a term of this sequence. A value belongs only when solving for nnn gives a positive whole number.

Matching powers is quickest when the target is a clean multiple of a power of rrr. When it is not, you can still solve a1rn−1=Va_1 r^{n-1} = Va1​rn−1=V by isolating the power and taking a logarithm, the tool from the logarithms lesson. Dividing gives rn−1=Va1r^{n-1} = \tfrac{V}{a_1}rn−1=a1​V​, and a base-rrr logarithm brings the exponent down to the ground:

n−1=log⁡r ⁣(Va1)⟹n=1+log⁡r ⁣(Va1).n - 1 = \log_{r}\!\left(\frac{V}{a_1}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = 1 + \log_{r}\!\left(\frac{V}{a_1}\right).n−1=logr​(a1​V​)⟹n=1+logr​(a1​V​).

This logarithm route applies only when the ratio is positive, with r>0r > 0r>0, r≠1r \ne 1r=1, and Va1>0\tfrac{V}{a_1} > 0a1​V​>0, since a base-rrr logarithm makes sense only then. When r<0r < 0r<0 the terms alternate in sign, so there is no base-rrr logarithm to take. For a negative ratio, test membership by matching powers of ∣r∣\lvert r \rvert∣r∣ and checking that the sign comes out right.

If Va1\tfrac{V}{a_1}a1​V​ is a recognizable power of rrr, the logarithm is a whole number (for 2n−1=1282^{n-1} = 1282n−1=128 it is log⁡2(128)=7\log_{2}(128) = 7log2​(128)=7). If it is not, change of base evaluates it, log⁡r(x)=log⁡(x)log⁡(r)\log_{r}(x) = \tfrac{\log(x)}{\log(r)}logr​(x)=log(r)log(x)​, and a non-whole result confirms the value is not a term. The same setup answers a threshold question, such as “which is the first term past a given value.” Solve the inequality for nnn and keep its direction straight: dividing by log⁡(r)\log(r)log(r) flips the inequality whenever 0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1, since log⁡(r)\log(r)log(r) is then negative. The bouncing-ball example below works through this carefully.

How the common ratio shapes the sequence

Everything about the long-run behavior of a geometric sequence is decided by the ratio rrr. Four cases cover it, and the positive starting value 161616 keeps the table simple. With a negative starting term, read every row as a statement about size instead: the sign of each term then also depends on the sign of a1a_1a1​.

Common ratio rrrWhat the terms doExample starting at 161616
r>1r > 1r>1grow in size without boundr=2r = 2r=2 gives 16,32,64,128,…16, 32, 64, 128, \ldots16,32,64,128,…
0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1shrink toward 000, never reaching itr=12r = \tfrac{1}{2}r=21​ gives 16,8,4,2,…16, 8, 4, 2, \ldots16,8,4,2,…
r<0r < 0r<0alternate in signr=−2r = -2r=−2 gives 16,−32,64,−128,…16, -32, 64, -128, \ldots16,−32,64,−128,…
r=1r = 1r=1stay constant16,16,16,16,…16, 16, 16, 16, \ldots16,16,16,16,…

Two of these deserve a closer look. When 0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1, each term is a fraction of the one before it, so the terms fall toward zero and get arbitrarily small. Even so, no term is ever exactly zero, since you only ever multiply a nonzero start by a nonzero ratio. This is exactly the exponential decay you saw with a base between 000 and 111. When r<0r < 0r<0, multiplying by a negative number flips the sign every step, so the terms swing positive, negative, positive, negative. The sizes of those terms still follow the powers of ∣r∣\lvert r \rvert∣r∣: r=−2r = -2r=−2 still grows in size, 16,−32,64,−128,…16, -32, 64, -128, \ldots16,−32,64,−128,…, while r=−12r = -\tfrac{1}{2}r=−21​ still shrinks toward zero, 16,−8,4,−2,…16, -8, 4, -2, \ldots16,−8,4,−2,…. Only the sign is new; the size behavior is the same r>1r > 1r>1 versus 0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1 story as before. A negative ratio does not mean the sequence decreases; it means it alternates.

Because ∣r∣=13\lvert r \rvert = \tfrac{1}{3}∣r∣=31​ is between 000 and 111, the sizes shrink toward zero, not grow, even though the ratio itself is negative.

Geometric sequences around us

Geometric sequences describe any quantity that is repeatedly scaled by the same factor, the same idea behind the doubling bacteria and the shrinking car value from the opening. A radioactive sample that loses half its atoms in each half-life has r=12r = \tfrac{1}{2}r=21​, a decay toward zero it never quite reaches. A savings balance left at a fixed annual rate compounded once a year is multiplied by the same growth factor each year, so the yearly balances form a geometric sequence: at a rate of 555 percent the factor is r=1.05r = 1.05r=1.05, and the balance after n−1n - 1n−1 years is a1rn−1a_1 r^{n-1}a1​rn−1. That is exactly the compound-interest pattern from the earlier lesson. A ball that rebounds to a fixed fraction of its height on each bounce is the same idea with 0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1.

Worked example 4 A bouncing ball

A ball is dropped and each bounce rebounds to 0.60.60.6 of the previous rebound height. The first rebound reaches 300300300 cm. How high is the 444th rebound, and after how many bounces does the rebound first fall below 202020 cm?

The rebound heights form a geometric sequence with first term a1=300a_1 = 300a1​=300 and common ratio r=0.6r = 0.6r=0.6, a decay because 0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1. The 444th rebound uses n−1=3n - 1 = 3n−1=3 multiplications:

a4=300⋅0.63=300⋅0.216=64.8 cm.a_4 = 300 \cdot 0.6^{3} = 300 \cdot 0.216 = 64.8 \text{ cm}.a4​=300⋅0.63=300⋅0.216=64.8 cm.

For the second question, ask when the rebound height drops below 202020 cm. That means 300⋅0.6 n−1<20300 \cdot 0.6^{\,n-1} < 20300⋅0.6n−1<20, so first isolate the power:

0.6 n−1<20300=115.0.6^{\,n-1} < \frac{20}{300} = \frac{1}{15}.0.6n−1<30020​=151​.

Take a logarithm of both sides and use the power law. Because log⁡(0.6)\log(0.6)log(0.6) is negative, dividing by it reverses the inequality:

(n−1)log⁡(0.6)<log⁡ ⁣(115)⟹n−1>log⁡(1/15)log⁡(0.6)=−1.176−0.222≈5.30.(n-1)\log(0.6) < \log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{15}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n - 1 > \frac{\log(1/15)}{\log(0.6)} = \frac{-1.176}{-0.222} \approx 5.30.(n−1)log(0.6)<log(151​)⟹n−1>log(0.6)log(1/15)​=−0.222−1.176​≈5.30.

Checking confirms it: the 666th rebound is 300⋅0.65≈23.3300 \cdot 0.6^{5} \approx 23.3300⋅0.65≈23.3 cm, still above 202020, while the 777th is 300⋅0.66≈14.0300 \cdot 0.6^{6} \approx 14.0300⋅0.66≈14.0 cm, the first below 202020. The ball keeps bouncing forever in theory, each rebound a fixed fraction of the last, but the heights shrink toward zero.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding when you should multiply. A geometric sequence multiplies by a constant ratio; an arithmetic sequence adds a constant difference. Find rrr by dividing consecutive terms, not by subtracting them. In 3,6,12,243, 6, 12, 243,6,12,24 the ratio is 222, even though the differences 3,6,123, 6, 123,6,12 keep changing.
  • Using the exponent nnn instead of n−1n - 1n−1. The nnnth term applies the ratio n−1n - 1n−1 times, so an=a1rn−1a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}an​=a1​rn−1. Writing a1rna_1 r^{n}a1​rn multiplies by one extra factor of rrr and overshoots the term.
  • Losing a sign when recovering rrr. Two terms an even number of positions apart give an even power of rrr, and r2=4r^2 = 4r2=4 has both r=2r = 2r=2 and r=−2r = -2r=−2 as solutions. Pin the sign with extra information (an adjacent term, a term an odd distance away, or a stated sign) before committing to one ratio.
  • Reading the ratio upside down. The ratio is a term divided by the one before it, r=a2a1r = \tfrac{a_2}{a_1}r=a1​a2​​, not a1a2\tfrac{a_1}{a_2}a2​a1​​. Dividing the wrong way gives the reciprocal ratio.
  • Thinking a negative ratio means the sequence shrinks. A negative rrr makes the terms alternate in sign, not decrease. The sizes still grow when ∣r∣>1\lvert r \rvert > 1∣r∣>1; only the signs flip.
  • Expecting a decaying sequence to reach zero. With 0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1 the terms fall toward zero and get as small as you like. No term is ever exactly zero, though, because you only ever multiply a nonzero start by a nonzero ratio.

Key takeaways

  • A sequence is geometric when consecutive terms have a constant common ratio r=a2a1=a3a2=⋯r = \tfrac{a_2}{a_1} = \tfrac{a_3}{a_2} = \cdotsr=a1​a2​​=a2​a3​​=⋯, found by dividing a term by the one before it. Each term is the previous term times rrr.
  • The explicit formula is an=a1rn−1a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}an​=a1​rn−1: start at a1a_1a1​ and multiply by rrr a total of n−1n - 1n−1 times. Two numbers, a1a_1a1​ and rrr, fix the whole sequence.
  • The recursive rule is an=r an−1a_n = r\,a_{n-1}an​=ran−1​ with a1a_1a1​ given, and between any two terms ak=amrk−ma_k = a_m r^{k-m}ak​=am​rk−m, which recovers rrr or a distant term.
  • To test whether a value VVV is a term, solve a1rn−1=Va_1 r^{n-1} = Va1​rn−1=V; it belongs only if nnn is a positive whole number. Match powers when you can, or, for a positive ratio (r>0r > 0r>0, r≠1r \ne 1r=1), take a logarithm, n=1+log⁡r ⁣(Va1)n = 1 + \log_{r}\!\left(\tfrac{V}{a_1}\right)n=1+logr​(a1​V​).
  • The ratio sets the behavior: r>1r > 1r>1 grows in size, 0<r<10 < r < 10<r<1 decays toward zero, r<0r < 0r<0 alternates in sign, and r=1r = 1r=1 is constant. Both r=0r = 0r=0 and a1=0a_1 = 0a1​=0 are not allowed, and no term is ever zero.
  • Geometric sequences model doubling populations, radioactive halving, rebound heights, and compound interest, wherever a quantity is repeatedly scaled by the same factor.

A bit of history (optional)

A gift can be enormous and still sound modest, so long as it is described one step at a time.

An old legend makes the point with a chessboard. A king, delighted by a newly invented game, offers its inventor any reward he cares to name. The inventor asks for wheat: one grain on the first square, two grains on the second, four on the third. Every square after that doubles the square before it. Sixty-four squares in all, and a request the king believes he can settle out of pocket.

He cannot. The grain counts form a geometric sequence with first term 111 and common ratio 222, so the square in position nnn holds 2n−12^{n-1}2n−1 grains. The run begins gently at one, two, four, eight, sixteen. It does not stay gentle for long. Halfway along the board a single square already wants more than two billion grains. The last square alone wants more than nine billion billion, which is beyond any harvest ever gathered.

The legend has travelled a long way, through Persian and Indian retellings, and nobody can date it. What it demonstrates is exact. A ratio only slightly above one looks harmless for a few terms and then overwhelms everything, because each term multiplies rather than adds.

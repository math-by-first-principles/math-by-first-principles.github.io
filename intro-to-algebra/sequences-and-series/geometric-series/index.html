Geometric Series

Intuition

The previous lesson ended on a cliffhanger. A king promised the inventor of chess one grain of wheat on the first square, two on the second, four on the third. The doubling ran across all sixty-four squares, and we found each square’s count but never added them up. This lesson adds them, and the grand total turns out to be astonishingly large. Adding the terms of a geometric sequence gives a geometric series, and two surprises are waiting. A finite geometric series, even a long one, folds into a single short formula instead of a marathon of additions. And an infinite geometric series, when its ratio is small enough, can add infinitely many positive numbers and still land on a finite total. That idea finally answers Zeno’s ancient paradox of the runner who must cross half the track, then half of what is left, and half again, forever.

From a sequence to its sum

An ordered list like 3,6,12,24,483, 6, 12, 24, 483,6,12,24,48 is a geometric sequence. Join its terms with plus signs and you get a geometric series, a single number:

3+6+12+24+48=93.3 + 6 + 12 + 24 + 48 = 93.3+6+12+24+48=93.

As with an arithmetic series, write SnS_nSn​ for the sum of the first nnn terms of a geometric sequence. Using the explicit term ak=a1rk−1a_k = a_1 r^{k-1}ak​=a1​rk−1 from the last lesson, every term is the first term times a power of the ratio:

Sn=a1+a2+⋯+an=a1+a1r+a1r2+⋯+a1rn−1.S_n = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n = a_1 + a_1 r + a_1 r^2 + \cdots + a_1 r^{n-1}.Sn​=a1​+a2​+⋯+an​=a1​+a1​r+a1​r2+⋯+a1​rn−1.

In the summation notation from the arithmetic-series lesson, the same sum is

Sn=∑k=1na1rk−1.S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_1 r^{k-1}.Sn​=k=1∑n​a1​rk−1.

The only thing new compared with an arithmetic series is what sits between the terms: a constant multiplier rrr instead of a constant added difference. That single change forces a different summing trick, because the reverse-and-add pairing no longer produces equal columns when the terms are scaled rather than stepped.

A formula for the finite sum

There is a clever move that collapses the whole sum. Multiply the series by the ratio rrr and subtract the result from the original. Because multiplying by rrr shifts every term over by one position, almost everything cancels.

See it first with numbers. Take the six-term sum S=3+6+12+24+48+96S = 3 + 6 + 12 + 24 + 48 + 96S=3+6+12+24+48+96, built from the ratio-222 sequence. Multiply every term by 222 and line the result up under the original so matching terms sit in the same column:

S  =  3+6+12+24+48+962S  =  3+6+12+24+48+96+192\begin{aligned} S \;&=\; 3 + 6 + 12 + 24 + 48 + 96 \\[2pt] 2S \;&=\; \phantom{3 + {}} 6 + 12 + 24 + 48 + 96 + 192 \end{aligned}S2S​=3+6+12+24+48+96=3+6+12+24+48+96+192​

Every term from 666 through 969696 sits in both rows, so subtracting the second row from the first cancels every one of them. Only the leading 333 from the top row and the trailing 192192192 from the bottom row are left over:

S−2S=3−192=−189,soS=189.S - 2S = 3 - 192 = -189, \qquad\text{so}\qquad S = 189.S−2S=3−192=−189,soS=189.

That matches adding the six terms directly. The general proof below is the same cancellation, written so it works for any first term, ratio, and number of terms.

The finite sum is Sn=a1(1−rn)1−rS_n = \frac{a_1(1 - r^{n})}{1 - r}Sn​=1−ra1​(1−rn)​ for r≠1r \ne 1r=1#

Write the sum of the first nnn terms in full, each term the one before it times rrr:

Sn=a1+a1r+a1r2+⋯+a1rn−1.S_n = a_1 + a_1 r + a_1 r^2 + \cdots + a_1 r^{n-1}.Sn​=a1​+a1​r+a1​r2+⋯+a1​rn−1.

Now multiply the whole line by the common ratio rrr. Every term picks up one more factor of rrr, so the entire list shifts one place to the right:

rSn=a1r+a1r2+a1r3+⋯+a1rn.r S_n = a_1 r + a_1 r^2 + a_1 r^3 + \cdots + a_1 r^{n}.rSn​=a1​r+a1​r2+a1​r3+⋯+a1​rn.

Subtract the second line from the first and watch what lines up. The a1ra_1 ra1​r in the top line cancels the a1ra_1 ra1​r in the bottom line, the a1r2a_1 r^2a1​r2 cancels the a1r2a_1 r^2a1​r2, and so on all the way down. Every middle term appears in both sums, so every one of them cancels. Only two survivors are left. One is the a1a_1a1​ at the front of the top line, which has nothing beneath it. The other is the −a1rn-a_1 r^{n}−a1​rn at the end of the bottom line, which has nothing above it:

Sn−rSn=a1−a1rn.S_n - r S_n = a_1 - a_1 r^{n}.Sn​−rSn​=a1​−a1​rn.

Factor each side. The left side has the common factor SnS_nSn​, and the right side has the common factor a1a_1a1​:

Sn(1−r)=a1(1−rn).S_n(1 - r) = a_1(1 - r^{n}).Sn​(1−r)=a1​(1−rn).

As long as r≠1r \ne 1r=1 the factor 1−r1 - r1−r is not zero, so divide both sides by it to free SnS_nSn​:

Sn=a1(1−rn)1−r.S_n = \frac{a_1(1 - r^{n})}{1 - r}.Sn​=1−ra1​(1−rn)​.

∎

Check your understanding

In the proof above, multiplying SnS_nSn​ by rrr and subtracting leaves only two surviving terms, a1a_1a1​ and −a1rn-a_1 r^{n}−a1​rn. Why does every other term cancel?

Answer choices

Multiplying by rrr shifts every term over by one position, so a1r,a1r2,…,a1rn−1a_1 r, a_1 r^2, \ldots, a_1 r^{n-1}a1​r,a1​r2,…,a1​rn−1 show up in both the top row (SnS_nSn​) and the bottom row (rSnrS_nrSn​). Subtracting cancels each shared term, leaving only the un-matched a1a_1a1​ at the front and −a1rn-a_1 r^{n}−a1​rn at the back. This cancellation works for any ratio, not just one with ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1: that condition is a separate fact about small ratios that this finite-sum proof does not depend on. Both rows have nnn terms, so a mismatched term count is not the reason, and every term (not just a1a_1a1​) gets multiplied by rrr.

The one ratio this formula cannot handle is r=1r = 1r=1, because then 1−r=01 - r = 01−r=0 and dividing by it is illegal. That case needs no formula at all. When r=1r = 1r=1 every term equals a1a_1a1​, so the series is just a1a_1a1​ added to itself nnn times:

Sn=a1+a1+⋯+a1⏟n terms=n a1.S_n = \underbrace{a_1 + a_1 + \cdots + a_1}_{n \text{ terms}} = n\,a_1.Sn​=n termsa1​+a1​+⋯+a1​​​=na1​.

When the ratio is larger than 111 it is tidier to avoid the negative quantities in the fraction. Multiplying the top and bottom by −1-1−1 flips both differences and gives an equal form built from positive parts:

Sn=a1(rn−1)r−1.S_n = \frac{a_1(r^{n} - 1)}{r - 1}.Sn​=r−1a1​(rn−1)​.

The two versions are the same number, so reach for whichever form keeps the denominator positive: the first when r<1r < 1r<1 (so 1−r>01 - r > 01−r>0), the second when r>1r > 1r>1 (so r−1>0r - 1 > 0r−1>0).

Worked example 1 Sum the first 4 terms

Find the sum of the first 444 terms of the geometric series 81+27+9+3+⋯81 + 27 + 9 + 3 + \cdots81+27+9+3+⋯

Read off the two numbers that define it. The first term is a1=81a_1 = 81a1​=81, and the ratio is any term divided by the one before it, 2781=13\tfrac{27}{81} = \tfrac138127​=31​, so r=13r = \tfrac13r=31​. You want n=4n = 4n=4 terms. Since r<1r < 1r<1, use the form with 1−r1 - r1−r in the denominator:

S4=a1(1−r4)1−r=81(1−(13)4)1−13.S_4 = \frac{a_1(1 - r^{4})}{1 - r} = \frac{81\left(1 - \left(\tfrac13\right)^{4}\right)}{1 - \tfrac13}.S4​=1−ra1​(1−r4)​=1−31​81(1−(31​)4)​.

Work out the power first, (13)4=181\left(\tfrac13\right)^{4} = \tfrac{1}{81}(31​)4=811​, then finish the arithmetic:

S4=81(1−181)23=81×808123=8023=120.S_4 = \frac{81\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{81}\right)}{\tfrac23} = \frac{81 \times \tfrac{80}{81}}{\tfrac23} = \frac{80}{\tfrac23} = 120.S4​=32​81(1−811​)​=32​81×8180​​=32​80​=120.

Adding the four terms directly, 81+27+9+381 + 27 + 9 + 381+27+9+3, also gives 120120120, a good check that the formula does the same job in a single step.

Worked example 2 The chessboard grand total

Total the wheat on the chessboard from the previous lesson: one grain on the first square, two on the second, four on the third. Each square’s count doubles the one before it, across all 646464 squares.

The grain counts are the geometric sequence 1,2,4,8,…1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots1,2,4,8,…, with first term a1=1a_1 = 1a1​=1 and ratio r=2r = 2r=2, and the last square (the 646464th) holds 2632^{63}263 grains. The grand total is the finite geometric series

S64=1+2+4+⋯+263.S_{64} = 1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 2^{63}.S64​=1+2+4+⋯+263.

With a1=1a_1 = 1a1​=1, r=2r = 2r=2, and n=64n = 64n=64, the r>1r > 1r>1 form keeps everything positive:

S64=a1(rn−1)r−1=1 (264−1)2−1=264−1.S_{64} = \frac{a_1(r^{n} - 1)}{r - 1} = \frac{1\,(2^{64} - 1)}{2 - 1} = 2^{64} - 1.S64​=r−1a1​(rn−1)​=2−11(264−1)​=264−1.

That is 18,446,744,073,709,551,61518{,}446{,}744{,}073{,}709{,}551{,}61518,446,744,073,709,551,615 grains, more than eighteen quintillion, far more wheat than has ever been harvested in all of history. Notice how the formula crushes a sum of 646464 terms into a single subtraction. The whole board comes to exactly one less than 2642^{64}264, the value of the square that would have come next.

Adding infinitely many terms

A geometric pattern can keep going forever, so it is natural to ask what happens if you try to add all of its terms, out to infinity. This is exactly the situation behind Zeno’s ancient puzzle: a runner must cross half the track, then half of what is left, then half of that, and so on forever, an endless list of shrinking distances. For most ratios, adding forever is dull: the running total never settles on one number, so there is no finite sum to report. But when the ratio is small in size, something remarkable happens. Infinitely many positive pieces can add up to a single finite value, exactly what lets Zeno’s runner finish the race after all.

This is Zeno’s runner too: each remaining stretch of track is half of what is left, and although the list of stretches never ends, they tile the whole track exactly, so the runner does finish the race after a finite distance.

The finite formula already shows why the same thing happens for any ratio with ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, not only r=12r = \tfrac12r=21​. Look again at the sum of the first nnn terms:

Sn=a1(1−rn)1−r.S_n = \frac{a_1(1 - r^{n})}{1 - r}.Sn​=1−ra1​(1−rn)​.

The only part that changes as you pile on more terms is the power rnr^{n}rn in the numerator. Suppose rrr lies strictly between −1-1−1 and 111, that is ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1. Then every multiplication by rrr lands you on a number smaller in size than before, because multiplying by something less than 111 in absolute value always shrinks it. Keep multiplying and rnr^{n}rn marches toward zero. For r=12r = \tfrac12r=21​ the powers run 12,14,18,116,…\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac{1}{16}, \ldots21​,41​,81​,161​,…, each half the last and all heading to nothing.

rnr^{n}rn never actually reaches 000 after any finite number of terms, but it can be made as close to 000 as you like by adding enough terms. So the numerator 1−rn1 - r^{n}1−rn gets closer and closer to 111, and the partial sum SnS_nSn​ gets closer and closer to a fixed value:

S=a11−r.S = \frac{a_1}{1 - r}.S=1−ra1​​.

This is the sum of the infinite geometric series. When the partial sums approach one fixed number like this, the series is said to converge to that number; when they do not, it diverges. The tail you are dropping, all the terms past the nnnth, is itself a tiny geometric series that keeps shrinking, so the more terms you include, the closer the total sits to a11−r\frac{a_1}{1 - r}1−ra1​​. When rrr is positive, every term shares a1a_1a1​‘s sign, and the running total moves steadily toward that value without ever crossing it. When rrr is negative, the terms alternate in sign and the running total lands just above and just below the value in turn. Those overshoots shrink to nothing, so the running total still closes in on that value.

There is a second route to the same infinite-sum formula: multiply the full sum by rrr and subtract, instead of reasoning about the finite partial sums the way the paragraphs above just did. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See another way to reach this formula

This shortcut only makes sense when the sum SSS really is a finite number to begin with, which is exactly the case ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1.

Everything here hinges on that condition. If ∣r∣≥1|r| \ge 1∣r∣≥1 the terms do not shrink. When r=1r = 1r=1 you are adding a1a_1a1​ to itself forever and the total moves further from zero without bound (upward if a1a_1a1​ is positive, downward if a1a_1a1​ is negative). When r>1r > 1r>1 the terms grow in size, so the total moves further from zero even faster. When r=−1r = -1r=−1 the running sum flips between a1a_1a1​ and 000 and never settles anywhere. When r<−1r < -1r<−1 the terms grow in size while flipping sign, so the running total swings between larger and larger positive and negative values and never settles either. In every one of these cases there is no finite sum, and the formula a11−r\tfrac{a_1}{1 - r}1−ra1​​ does not apply. The rule to memorize is short: an infinite geometric series has a finite sum exactly when ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1.

Worked example 3 Two infinite sums

Find the sum of each infinite geometric series.

(a) 8+4+2+1+12+⋯8 + 4 + 2 + 1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots8+4+2+1+21​+⋯

The first term is a1=8a_1 = 8a1​=8, and each term is half the one before it, so r=12r = \tfrac12r=21​. Because ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, the series has a finite sum:

S=a11−r=81−12=812=16.S = \frac{a_1}{1 - r} = \frac{8}{1 - \tfrac12} = \frac{8}{\tfrac12} = 16.S=1−ra1​​=1−21​8​=21​8​=16.

The partial sums 8,12,14,15,15.5,…8, 12, 14, 15, 15.5, \ldots8,12,14,15,15.5,… stay just under 161616, and they get closer and closer to 161616 the more terms you add. The infinite sum is the number they are closing in on, so the answer is exactly 161616.

(b) 12−6+3−32+⋯12 - 6 + 3 - \tfrac32 + \cdots12−6+3−23​+⋯

Here a1=12a_1 = 12a1​=12, and each term is −12-\tfrac12−21​ times the one before it, so r=−12r = -\tfrac12r=−21​. The sign does not stop it from converging, since ∣r∣=12<1|r| = \tfrac12 < 1∣r∣=21​<1; it only makes the terms alternate. Carry the sign into the formula:

S=121−(−12)=1232=8.S = \frac{12}{1 - \left(-\tfrac12\right)} = \frac{12}{\tfrac32} = 8.S=1−(−21​)12​=23​12​=8.

With a1a_1a1​ positive here, the negative ratio pulls the sum below the first term. The same formula lands the answer regardless of the signs involved, though whether a negative ratio pulls the sum up or down in general also depends on the sign of a1a_1a1​.

Repeating decimals are geometric series

A repeating decimal such as 0.7777…0.7777\ldots0.7777… hides an infinite geometric series, which is why every repeating decimal is secretly an exact fraction. Splitting the decimal into its place values lines the series up, and the infinite-sum formula turns it back into a fraction.

Worked example 4 Repeating decimals to fractions

Write each repeating decimal as an exact fraction.

(a) 0.7‾=0.7777…0.\overline{7} = 0.7777\ldots0.7=0.7777…

Break the decimal into place values. The digit 777 sits in the tenths place, then the hundredths, then the thousandths, so

0.7‾=710+7100+71000+⋯0.\overline{7} = \frac{7}{10} + \frac{7}{100} + \frac{7}{1000} + \cdots0.7=107​+1007​+10007​+⋯

This is an infinite geometric series with first term a1=710a_1 = \tfrac{7}{10}a1​=107​ and ratio r=110r = \tfrac{1}{10}r=101​, since each place is a tenth of the one before it. Because ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, apply the formula:

0.7‾=7/101−1/10=7/109/10=79.0.\overline{7} = \frac{7/10}{1 - 1/10} = \frac{7/10}{9/10} = \frac{7}{9}.0.7=1−1/107/10​=9/107/10​=97​.

(b) 0.48‾=0.484848…0.\overline{48} = 0.484848\ldots0.48=0.484848…

When the repeating block is two digits long, each copy is a hundredth of the last. So for a two-digit block, the first term is the block over 100100100 and the ratio is 1100\tfrac{1}{100}1001​:

0.48‾=48100+4810000+⋯=48/1001−1/100=48/10099/100=4899=1633.0.\overline{48} = \frac{48}{100} + \frac{48}{10000} + \cdots = \frac{48/100}{1 - 1/100} = \frac{48/100}{99/100} = \frac{48}{99} = \frac{16}{33}.0.48=10048​+1000048​+⋯=1−1/10048/100​=99/10048/100​=9948​=3316​.

The pattern is worth keeping: a one-digit repeat divides by 999, and a two-digit repeat by 999999. Each result then reduces to lowest terms, here from 4899\tfrac{48}{99}9948​ down to 1633\tfrac{16}{33}3316​.

Worked example 5 A bouncing ball

A ball is dropped from a height of 121212 m. On each bounce it rebounds to half of its previous height. If it keeps bouncing, what is the total vertical distance it travels?

The ball first falls the full 121212 m. After that, every bounce carries it up to some peak and then back down the same distance, so each bounce past the drop contributes twice its peak height. The peak heights form a geometric sequence starting at the first rebound, 12⋅12=612 \cdot \tfrac12 = 612⋅21​=6 m, and halving each time: 6,3,32,…6, 3, \tfrac32, \ldots6,3,23​,…. The total up-and-down distance from all the bounces is an infinite geometric series with a1=6a_1 = 6a1​=6 and r=12r = \tfrac12r=21​, doubled to count both directions:

2(6+3+32+⋯ )=2⋅61−12=2⋅12=24 m.2\left(6 + 3 + \tfrac32 + \cdots\right) = 2 \cdot \frac{6}{1 - \tfrac12} = 2 \cdot 12 = 24 \text{ m}.2(6+3+23​+⋯)=2⋅1−21​6​=2⋅12=24 m.

Add the first drop, which happened before any bounce:

12+24=36 m.12 + 24 = 36 \text{ m}.12+24=36 m.

The ball bounces infinitely many times in this idealized model, yet the rebounds shrink fast enough that the whole journey adds up to a finite 363636 m.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using the arithmetic sum formula. A geometric sum is a1(1−rn)1−r\frac{a_1(1 - r^{n})}{1 - r}1−ra1​(1−rn)​, with the ratio raised to a power. Do not reach for n(a1+an)2\frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}2n(a1​+an​)​: that averages evenly spaced terms, and geometric terms are not evenly spaced.
  • Dividing by zero when r=1r = 1r=1. The finite formula has 1−r1 - r1−r in the denominator, so it is undefined at r=1r = 1r=1. If every term is the same, the sum is just n a1n\,a_1na1​. Check for r=1r = 1r=1 before you divide.
  • Summing to infinity when ∣r∣≥1|r| \ge 1∣r∣≥1. The infinite-sum formula a11−r\frac{a_1}{1 - r}1−ra1​​ works only when ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1. If the ratio is 111 or larger in size, the terms stop shrinking toward zero, so the running total never settles on one number (its size grows past every bound, or, for a negative ratio, it swings back and forth forever), and there is no finite answer to give.
  • Losing the sign of a negative ratio. A ratio like r=−12r = -\tfrac12r=−21​ still has ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, so the series converges, but the sign belongs in the formula: a11−(−1/2)=a13/2\frac{a_1}{1 - (-1/2)} = \frac{a_1}{3/2}1−(−1/2)a1​​=3/2a1​​, not a11/2\frac{a_1}{1/2}1/2a1​​. Substitute rrr with its sign attached.
  • Reading the first term of a repeating decimal wrong. For 0.7‾0.\overline{7}0.7 the first term is 710\frac{7}{10}107​ and the ratio is 110\frac{1}{10}101​, not 777 and 110\frac{1}{10}101​. Line the series up as 710+7100+⋯\frac{7}{10} + \frac{7}{100} + \cdots107​+1007​+⋯ before you read off a1a_1a1​ and rrr.
  • Confusing the term count with the ratio. In a1(1−rn)1−r\frac{a_1(1 - r^{n})}{1 - r}1−ra1​(1−rn)​ the exponent nnn counts the terms while rrr is the multiplier. Feeding the count in where rrr goes, or the ratio in where nnn goes, scrambles the answer.

Key takeaways

  • A geometric series is the sum of the terms of a geometric sequence, Sn=a1+a1r+⋯+a1rn−1=∑k=1na1rk−1S_n = a_1 + a_1 r + \cdots + a_1 r^{n-1} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_1 r^{k-1}Sn​=a1​+a1​r+⋯+a1​rn−1=∑k=1n​a1​rk−1.
  • The finite sum for r≠1r \ne 1r=1 is Sn=a1(1−rn)1−rS_n = \frac{a_1(1 - r^{n})}{1 - r}Sn​=1−ra1​(1−rn)​, equivalently a1(rn−1)r−1\frac{a_1(r^{n} - 1)}{r - 1}r−1a1​(rn−1)​, proved by multiplying the series by rrr and subtracting so the middle terms cancel. When r=1r = 1r=1 the sum is simply Sn=n a1S_n = n\,a_1Sn​=na1​.
  • An infinite geometric series with ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 converges to the finite sum S=a11−rS = \frac{a_1}{1 - r}S=1−ra1​​, because rnr^{n}rn shrinks toward zero as more terms are added, so the dropped tail stops changing the total.
  • When ∣r∣≥1|r| \ge 1∣r∣≥1 the terms do not shrink toward zero, so the infinite series diverges: it has no finite sum.
  • A repeating decimal is an infinite geometric series in disguise: 0.7‾=7/101−1/10=790.\overline{7} = \frac{7/10}{1 - 1/10} = \frac{7}{9}0.7=1−1/107/10​=97​, which is how any repeating decimal becomes an exact fraction.

A second way to reach the infinite-sum formula

Here is a second way to reach S=a11−rS = \dfrac{a_1}{1 - r}S=1−ra1​​, working with the infinite sum directly instead of through the finite partial sums.

See it first with numbers, using a series that converges: a1=3a_1 = 3a1​=3 and r=16r = \tfrac16r=61​, so S=3+12+112+⋯S = 3 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac{1}{12} + \cdotsS=3+21​+121​+⋯. Multiply every term by r=16r = \tfrac16r=61​ and line the result up under the original so matching terms sit in the same column:

S  =  3+12+112+⋯16S  =  3+12+112+⋯\begin{aligned} S \;&=\; 3 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac{1}{12} + \cdots \\[2pt] \tfrac16 S \;&=\; \phantom{3 + {}} \tfrac12 + \tfrac{1}{12} + \cdots \end{aligned}S61​S​=3+21​+121​+⋯=3+21​+121​+⋯​

Every term from 12\tfrac1221​ onward sits in both rows, so subtracting the second row from the first cancels all of them, leaving only the leading 333:

S−16S=3,so56S=3,soS=185=3.6.S - \tfrac16 S = 3, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \tfrac56 S = 3, \qquad\text{so}\qquad S = \tfrac{18}{5} = 3.6.S−61​S=3,so65​S=3,soS=518​=3.6.

That matches the infinite-sum formula: S=a11−r=31−1/6=35/6=185S = \dfrac{a_1}{1 - r} = \dfrac{3}{1 - 1/6} = \dfrac{3}{5/6} = \dfrac{18}{5}S=1−ra1​​=1−1/63​=5/63​=518​.

The same move works for any ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1. Call the full infinite sum SSS, then multiply it by rrr, which shifts every term one place to the right:

S=a1+a1r+a1r2+⋯ ,rS=a1r+a1r2+a1r3+⋯S = a_1 + a_1 r + a_1 r^2 + \cdots, \qquad r S = a_1 r + a_1 r^2 + a_1 r^3 + \cdotsS=a1​+a1​r+a1​r2+⋯,rS=a1​r+a1​r2+a1​r3+⋯

The second sum is exactly the first with its leading a1a_1a1​ removed, so rSr SrS is S−a1S - a_1S−a1​. Solving that relation gives the same formula:

rS=S−a1  ⟹  S(1−r)=a1  ⟹  S=a11−r.r S = S - a_1 \;\Longrightarrow\; S(1 - r) = a_1 \;\Longrightarrow\; S = \frac{a_1}{1 - r}.rS=S−a1​⟹S(1−r)=a1​⟹S=1−ra1​​.

This shortcut only makes sense when the sum SSS really is a finite number to begin with, which is exactly the case ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1.

A bit of history (optional)

Most rules of area the Greeks trusted were built for straight edges: rectangles, triangles, and shapes you can cut into those. So what is the area of a region with a curved side?

Archimedes settled that question for one curve, the parabola, working in Syracuse, a Greek city, in the third century BCE. His method was to fill the region with triangles. One large triangle takes most of it. The gaps left over take smaller triangles, the gaps after those take smaller ones still, and the process never ends.

The pattern hidden in those triangles is what rescues the calculation. Each new batch of triangles contributes exactly a quarter of the batch before it. So the running total is one, plus a quarter, plus a sixteenth, and on for ever. That is a geometric series with ratio 14\tfrac1441​. Archimedes proved it comes to 43\tfrac4334​ of that first triangle. It is an exact value, not an estimate.

He would never have written the sum the way you just saw it. Greek mathematicians distrusted the idea of literally adding up an endless list, so he reached 43\tfrac4334​ instead by a longer route that stayed finite the whole way, comparing areas directly rather than summing an infinite series. The number he was chasing is nevertheless yours. Put a1=1a_1 = 1a1​=1 and r=14r = \tfrac14r=41​ into a11−r\tfrac{a_1}{1 - r}1−ra1​​ and out comes 43\tfrac4334​. He measured a region bounded by a curve, nineteen centuries before calculus existed.

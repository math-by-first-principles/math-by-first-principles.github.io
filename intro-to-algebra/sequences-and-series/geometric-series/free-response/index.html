    Part A

    rS=S−a1rS = S - a_1rS=S−a1​.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Multiply the sum term by term by rrr.

    rS=a1r+a1r2+a1r3+⋯ .rS = a_1 r + a_1 r^{2} + a_1 r^{3} + \cdots.rS=a1​r+a1​r2+a1​r3+⋯.

    Every term on the right is exactly one term of the original sum, shifted over by one position, so this list is the original sum SSS with its leading a1a_1a1​ removed.

    rS=S−a1.rS = S - a_1.rS=S−a1​.

    Part B

    Starting from rS=S−a1rS = S - a_1rS=S−a1​, move every term with SSS to one side.

    S−rS=a1.S - rS = a_1.S−rS=a1​.

    S(1−r)=a1⟹S=a11−r.S(1-r) = a_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = \frac{a_1}{1-r}.S(1−r)=a1​⟹S=1−ra1​​.

    Part C

    Look again at the opening line of part A: 'let SSS denote the sum.' That phrasing quietly assumes such a number exists, ready to be multiplied and subtracted from like any other. Nothing in the multiply-and-subtract step ever questions that assumption; it just carries SSS through the algebra as if it were already a settled quantity.

    Whether SSS actually is such a number is precisely the question ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1 answers. When ∣r∣<1|r| < 1∣r∣<1, the running total does settle on one value as more terms are added, so SSS genuinely exists and the algebra describes something real.

    When ∣r∣≥1|r| \ge 1∣r∣≥1, no such settled value exists: the running total either grows without bound or refuses to approach any one number. Naming that non-existent quantity SSS and doing algebra on it does not summon it into being. With r=2r = 2r=2, for instance, the very same steps would claim

    S=a11−2=−a1,S = \frac{a_1}{1-2} = -a_1,S=1−2a1​​=−a1​,

    a negative number for a sum of terms that only ever grow, which shows the derivation never checked whether its own opening assumption held.

Systems with More Variables

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

You can already solve two equations in two unknowns. Three unknowns need a third equation, but not a new method: remove one variable from the system, and three unknowns drop to two, the familiar ground of the last three lessons. Solve that two-variable system, then work backward to find the variable you set aside. Picture each equation as a flat sheet, a plane, stretching out in space. In the ordinary case, three planes meet at a single point, the one triple (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z) that satisfies all three equations at once.

Learning goals

  • Write ax+by+cz=dax + by + cz = dax+by+cz=d and solve for ordered triples
  • Eliminate the same variable from two different pairs
  • Reduce to a two-variable system, then back-substitute
  • Explain why each round removes an unknown and terminates
  • Interpret a shared point, line, or plane as solutions
  • Read a false or always-true leftover as the special cases

From two unknowns to three

Take the system

x+y+z=6,x−y+z=2,x+y−z=0.\begin{gathered} x + y + z = 6, \\ x - y + z = 2, \\ x + y - z = 0. \end{gathered}x+y+z=6,x−y+z=2,x+y−z=0.​

The triple (1,2,3)(1, 2, 3)(1,2,3) solves it, because 1+2+3=61 + 2 + 3 = 61+2+3=6, 1−2+3=21 - 2 + 3 = 21−2+3=2, and 1+2−3=01 + 2 - 3 = 01+2−3=0 are all true. A solution is now three numbers, one for each letter.

An equation like these, written in the standard form

ax+by+cz=d,ax + by + cz = d,ax+by+cz=d,

is a linear equation in three variables: aaa, bbb, ccc, and ddd are numbers, aaa, bbb, and ccc are not all zero, and each variable appears only to the first power. Its solutions are ordered triples (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z). A system of three linear equations asks for the triple that satisfies all three at the same time. Pinning down three unknowns takes three equations with independent left sides: no left side can be formed by adding multiples of the other two. When one can, the system has either no solution or infinitely many, as the special cases below show.

Elimination, from two lessons ago, still removes a variable however many other letters ride along. The new idea is organizational: eliminate the same variable from two different pairs of equations, leaving two equations in the same two unknowns.

The reduction strategy step by step

  1. Choose a variable to eliminate, often one that already has matching or opposite coefficients somewhere.
  2. Eliminate it from one pair of equations by adding or subtracting (scaling first if needed). This leaves one equation in the other two unknowns.
  3. Eliminate the same variable from a different pair, one that uses the equation you have not touched yet. This gives a second equation in the same two unknowns.
  4. Solve that two-variable system by substitution or elimination.
  5. Back-substitute the two values into an original equation with a nonzero coefficient on the eliminated variable, and solve for it.
  6. Check the triple in all three original equations, not just the ones you combined.

Step 3 is the one people get wrong: eliminate zzz from one pair but yyy from the other, and the two leftover equations still involve three unknowns. If an equation loses all its variables, use the special cases below.

Why the reduction works and always finishes

Each replacement is reversible, just as in the elimination lesson.

Why eliminating a variable preserves the solution set, and why two rounds finish it#

Use x+y+z=9x+y+z=9x+y+z=9, 2x+y+z=122x+y+z=122x+y+z=12, and x+2y+3z=20x+2y+3z=20x+2y+3z=20. Keep the first equation. Subtract twice the first from the second, and the first from the third:

−y−z=−6,y+2z=11.\begin{aligned} -y-z&=-6,\\ y+2z&=11. \end{aligned}−y−zy+2z​=−6,=11.​

Adding twice the kept equation back to the first reduced equation recovers the original second equation, and adding the kept equation to the other recovers the original third. Each reduced equation is a combination of the original equations, so every triple that solves those three satisfies it. No triple was gained or lost. Adding the two reduced equations gives z=5z=5z=5; then y=1y=1y=1, and the kept equation gives x=3x=3x=3.

Nothing depended on these coefficients. Choose a variable with a nonzero coefficient and keep an equation containing it. Subtract suitable multiples of that equation from the other two to cancel the variable; adding the same multiples back undoes each step, so the solutions are unchanged. Solve the two remaining equations, then use the kept equation’s nonzero coefficient to recover the eliminated variable. Three unknowns become two, then one: two rounds finish the one-solution case.

The other endings finish too. A false statement means no solution. An always-true statement adds no condition and can be set aside. If the reduction finishes with no false statement at any step, a variable that was never eliminated and is missing from all the remaining equations is free to vary.

∎

Larger systems shrink the same way: four equations in four unknowns become three in at most three, and so on, with the same endings.

Each elimination step keeps exactly the same solutions, and the process finishes for any finite number of unknowns. The full argument is in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Check your understanding

Why does the reduction always come to an end, instead of running forever, no matter which three-variable system you start with?

Answer choices

Each elimination round removes a variable from the remaining problem: three can become two, then one. There are only finitely many variables. The process reaches equations that can be solved directly, free variables, or a contradiction; redundant equations can be set aside. Which variable you eliminate is a choice, not a rule, and a three-variable system can also have no solution or infinitely many, not only one.

A three-variable word problem

Three-variable systems fit problems with three unknowns and three facts, such as recovering prices from three receipts, where each receipt is one equation.

Worked example 2 Unknown prices from three receipts

At a snack stand the popcorn, pretzels, and drinks each have a fixed price. Three customers pay:

  • one popcorn, one pretzel, and one drink cost 777 dollars,
  • two popcorns, one pretzel, and three drinks cost 111111 dollars,
  • one popcorn, two pretzels, and four drinks cost 141414 dollars.

Find the price of each item.

Let ppp, ttt, and ddd be the price in dollars of a popcorn, a pretzel, and a drink. Each receipt becomes one equation:

(1) p+t+d=7,(2) 2p+t+3d=11,(3) p+2t+4d=14.\begin{gathered} (1)\ p + t + d = 7, \\ (2)\ 2p + t + 3d = 11, \\ (3)\ p + 2t + 4d = 14. \end{gathered}(1) p+t+d=7,(2) 2p+t+3d=11,(3) p+2t+4d=14.​

Eliminate ppp, whose coefficient is 111 in the first equation. Subtract twice equation (1)(1)(1) from equation (2)(2)(2):

(2p+t+3d)−2(p+t+d)=11−14,−t+d=−3.(A)\begin{gathered} (2p + t + 3d) - 2(p + t + d) \\ = 11 - 14, \\ -t + d = -3. \quad (A) \end{gathered}(2p+t+3d)−2(p+t+d)=11−14,−t+d=−3.(A)​

Eliminate the same variable ppp from the untouched equation (3)(3)(3) by subtracting equation (1)(1)(1):

(p+2t+4d)−(p+t+d)=14−7,t+3d=7.(B)\begin{gathered} (p + 2t + 4d) - (p + t + d) = 14 - 7, \\ t + 3d = 7. \quad (B) \end{gathered}(p+2t+4d)−(p+t+d)=14−7,t+3d=7.(B)​

Add (A)(A)(A) and (B)(B)(B) to cancel ttt:

(−t+d)+(t+3d)=−3+7,4d=4,d=1.\begin{gathered} (-t + d) + (t + 3d) = -3 + 7, \\ 4d = 4, \\ d = 1. \end{gathered}(−t+d)+(t+3d)=−3+7,4d=4,d=1.​

From (A)(A)(A), −t+1=−3-t + 1 = -3−t+1=−3, so t=4t = 4t=4. Back-substitute into equation (1)(1)(1): p+4+1=7p + 4 + 1 = 7p+4+1=7, so p=2p = 2p=2.

The geometry: three planes in space

With two variables, a linear equation draws a line on a flat page. With three, ax+by+cz=dax + by + cz = dax+by+cz=d draws a flat sheet stretching out forever in space, a plane. A system of three equations is three planes, and a solution triple is a point lying on all three at once. In the one-solution case, the three planes meet at exactly one point.

Check your understanding

One system's three planes share exactly one point. A second system is x+y+z=7x+y+z=7x+y+z=7, 2x+2y+2z=142x+2y+2z=142x+2y+2z=14, and 3x+3y+3z=213x+3y+3z=213x+3y+3z=21. A third system's planes share exactly one line. Which description of their solution sets is correct?

Answer choices

A point shared by all three planes gives a triple satisfying every equation. Exactly one shared point means exactly one solution.

In the second system, dividing the last two equations by 222 and 333 gives the first equation. All three therefore describe the plane x+y+z=7x+y+z=7x+y+z=7.

Every point on that plane solves the second system, including (7,0,0)(7,0,0)(7,0,0) and (0,7,0)(0,7,0)(0,7,0). Every point on the third system's shared line solves all three of its equations. Both systems have infinitely many triples, but their solution sets have different shapes.

Two planes can also be parallel. Compare x+y+z=3x+y+z=3x+y+z=3 with 2x+2y+2z=82x+2y+2z=82x+2y+2z=8. Dividing the second by 222 gives x+y+z=4x+y+z=4x+y+z=4, which cannot hold at the same point as x+y+z=3x+y+z=3x+y+z=3: same variable coefficients, different constant, so the planes are distinct and parallel. (With 666 instead of 888, the two equations would describe the same plane.) As with lines in the elimination lesson, two planes are parallel or the same exactly when one equation’s variable coefficients are a nonzero multiple of the other’s; otherwise they cross along a line.

If the planes have no common point, the system is inconsistent and has no solution. That happens when two of them are distinct parallel planes, or when all three cross in pairs without one point common to all three. In the second case, the three intersection lines are distinct and parallel, like the vertical edges of a triangular prism with each side face extended into a whole plane.

Any two of these lines lie in one of the planes and cannot meet, because a meeting point would lie on all three planes, so they are parallel.

The planes x=0x=0x=0, y=0y=0y=0, and x+y=1x+y=1x+y=1 meet in pairs along three parallel lines you can write out and check. The Go deeper section at the end of this lesson does that, then proves that any three distinct planes meeting in pairs with no common point have parallel intersection lines.

See why the lines must be parallel

If the three planes share a whole line, or coincide as one plane, the system has infinitely many solutions. Elimination tells the cases apart: a false statement means no common point (Worked Example 3), and 0=00=00=0 with no contradiction left means infinitely many (Worked Example 4).

With two equations in two unknowns, 0=00 = 00=0 always meant infinitely many solutions. With three equations it means only that one equation added nothing new, and another can still contradict the rest: in x+y+z=1x + y + z = 1x+y+z=1, 2x+2y+2z=22x + 2y + 2z = 22x+2y+2z=2, x+y+z=5x + y + z = 5x+y+z=5, the first two collapse to 0=00 = 00=0, but the third against the first leaves 0=40 = 40=4, so there is no solution.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Canceling different variables, or dropping an equation. Cancel the same variable from both pairs, or the two leftover equations will not share the same two unknowns. Use all three original equations in two different pairs: reusing one pair twice throws away a fact and can turn a solvable system into a false “infinitely many.”
  • Stopping after two variables. Back-substitute to find the third, state the full triple (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z), and check it in all three originals; an arithmetic slip can pass the combined equations and still fail an original.
  • Bookkeeping slips. Scaling an equation multiplies every term, and subtracting an equation flips every sign inside it. Number your equations and keep like terms in columns.
  • Misreading a vanished system. A leftover 0=k0 = k0=k with kkk not zero means no solution. A leftover 0=00 = 00=0 means only that one equation added no new condition; with three equations, the survivors can still give no solution. Neither says a variable equals zero.

Key takeaways

  • A linear equation in three variables is ax+by+cz=dax + by + cz = dax+by+cz=d, with aaa, bbb, and ccc not all zero. Its solutions are ordered triples (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z), and it takes three independent equations to pin three unknowns down.
  • Eliminate the same variable from two different pairs to get a two-variable system, solve it, back-substitute, and check the triple in all three originals.
  • Each elimination preserves the solution set, and each round removes one unknown, so the reduction is valid and terminates.
  • A plane is the graph of one linear equation in three variables with at least one nonzero variable coefficient. Exactly one shared point means one solution; no common point means no solution; a shared line or a shared plane means infinitely many solutions.
  • No solution follows from a false statement such as 0=40=40=4. An always-true equation such as 0=00=00=0 is redundant; the surviving equations decide between infinitely many solutions and none.

Why three pairwise plane intersections can be parallel

Pairwise intersections of three planes with no common point#

Three distinct planes that meet in pairs but share no common point have parallel intersection lines.

Take the planes x=0x=0x=0, y=0y=0y=0, and x+y=1x+y=1x+y=1. They meet in pairs along the lines (0,0,z)(0,0,z)(0,0,z), (0,1,z)(0,1,z)(0,1,z), and (1,0,z)(1,0,z)(1,0,z), with zzz any real number; each varies only in its third coordinate, so they are parallel. No two of them meet: a common point would satisfy all three equations, but the first two give x+y=0x+y=0x+y=0 while the third requires x+y=1x+y=1x+y=1.

The same reasoning works for any three distinct planes that meet in pairs with no point common to all three. Any two intersection lines lie in one plane (the first plane’s intersections with the other two both lie in the first plane, for example). A point on both would lie on all three planes, which is impossible, so the lines are distinct and do not meet. Distinct lines in one plane that do not meet are parallel.

∎

Why elimination works and finishes for any number of equations, not just three

Why elimination preserves solutions and terminates for a system of any size#

Start with four equations:

w+x+y+z=10,2w+x+y+z=14,w+2x+3y+2z=18,w+2x+2y+3z=17.\begin{aligned} w+x+y+z&=10,\\ 2w+x+y+z&=14,\\ w+2x+3y+2z&=18,\\ w+2x+2y+3z&=17. \end{aligned}w+x+y+z2w+x+y+zw+2x+3y+2zw+2x+2y+3z​=10,=14,=18,=17.​

Keep the first equation; subtract twice it from the second and once from each of the last two:

−x−y−z=−6(A),x+2y+z=8(B),x+y+2z=7(C).\begin{aligned} -x-y-z&=-6 &&(A),\\ x+2y+z&=8 &&(B),\\ x+y+2z&=7 &&(C). \end{aligned}−x−y−zx+2y+zx+y+2z​=−6=8=7​​(A),(B),(C).​

Keep (A)(A)(A) and add it to (B)(B)(B) and (C)(C)(C), eliminating xxx. This leaves y=2y=2y=2 and z=1z=1z=1. Set aside y=2y=2y=2 for back-substitution; the last equation, z=1z=1z=1, is solved. Four equations have become three, then two, then one.

Work backward: (A)(A)(A) gives −x−2−1=−6-x-2-1=-6−x−2−1=−6, so x=3x=3x=3, and the first original gives w=4w=4w=4. The four original left sides at (4,3,2,1)(4,3,2,1)(4,3,2,1) are 101010, 141414, 181818, and 171717, as required. Every move reverses (subtract (A)(A)(A) from y=2y=2y=2 and z=1z=1z=1 to get back (B)(B)(B) and (C)(C)(C), then add back the multiples of the first original), so no solution was lost or gained.

For nnn equations in nnn unknowns, choose a variable with a nonzero coefficient in one of the remaining equations, keep that equation for back-substitution, and use it to eliminate the variable from all the others; each replacement can be undone. Along the way, discard an always-true equation, and stop with no solution if an equation becomes false. A variable not yet eliminated that is missing from all the remaining equations needs no elimination: choose a value for it for now and keep that choice for back-substitution.

Every elimination removes a variable from the remaining problem, and there were only nnn variables, so there are at most nnn elimination steps. If the process ends with no contradiction, use any free choices and work backward through the kept equations. Each has a nonzero coefficient on the variable it recovers, so every step is possible. Nothing in the argument depends on the system having four equations.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

A circuit with several branches poses a puzzle: how much electric current travels through each wire? The current splits at junctions, and the branches affect one another. Solving each branch in isolation does not settle the whole network.

In 1845, Gustav Kirchhoff announced two laws for such circuits while studying at the University of Königsberg in Prussia, in the city now called Kaliningrad. He was about twenty-one. One law balances the currents entering and leaving a junction. The other balances voltage changes around a closed loop.

For circuits of fixed resistors and known voltage sources, those laws give a linear system for the unknown currents, reduced by elimination just like a three-variable problem. Kirchhoff’s work extended Ohm’s earlier results to networks with several loops, so a complicated arrangement of wires could be treated as one connected calculation.

MacTutor’s biography of Kirchhoff describes his student work and the two laws.

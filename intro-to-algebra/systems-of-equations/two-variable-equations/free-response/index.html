    1. Pairs that pass, and equations built to order . Foundational, 12 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    A pair either satisfies a two-variable equation or it does not, and one substitution settles it. The reverse job is less familiar: start from the pair you want, and produce equations that accept it.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Decide which of (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0), (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) and (2,−3)(2, -3)(2,−3) are solutions of 3x−2y=123x - 2y = 123x−2y=12. Show the substitution behind each verdict.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Write two linear equations in two variables, neither obtained from the other by multiplying through, that both have (5,−2)(5, -2)(5,−2) among their solutions. Verify each by substitution.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      How many linear equations in two variables have (5,−2)(5, -2)(5,−2) among their solutions? Support your answer, and state exactly what knowing that one pair pins down about the numbers aaa, bbb and ccc in ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Both halves of this question run on the same one-line test, taken in opposite directions: forwards you are handed an equation and asked about a pair, backwards you are handed a pair and asked for an equation.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Do not hunt for an equation that happens to fit. Pick whatever two coefficients you like, substitute the pair you were given, and read off the only constant that can then sit on the right.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Substitute the pair into the general form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c and look at what is left: one relation among three unknown numbers. Ask how many of those three you are still free to choose afterwards.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0) and (2,−3)(2, -3)(2,−3) are solutions. (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) is not: it makes the left side −12-12−12.

    Part B

    For example x+y=3x + y = 3x+y=3 and 2x+y=82x + y = 82x+y=8. Substituting x=5x = 5x=5 and y=−2y = -2y=−2 gives 333 and 888, matching the right sides.

    • any equation ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c whose numbers satisfy 5a−2b=c5a - 2b = c5a−2b=c qualifies, for instance x−y=7x - y = 7x−y=7 or 3x+4y=73x + 4y = 73x+4y=7; two answers count as different here so long as neither is a multiple of the other

    Part C

    Infinitely many. The pair imposes the single condition 5a−2b=c5a - 2b = c5a−2b=c, so aaa and bbb may be chosen freely, so long as they are not both zero, and ccc is then forced.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Put the first number of each pair in for xxx and the second in for yyy, then compare the left side with 121212.

    For (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0):

    3(4)−2(0)=12−0=12.3(4) - 2(0) = 12 - 0 = 12.3(4)−2(0)=12−0=12.

    The two sides agree, so this pair is a solution. Notice that the term 2y2y2y did not disappear when yyy was zero; it contributed 000 and was still subtracted.

    For (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6):

    3(0)−2(6)=0−12=−12.3(0) - 2(6) = 0 - 12 = -12.3(0)−2(6)=0−12=−12.

    That is not 121212, so this pair is not a solution.

    For (2,−3)(2, -3)(2,−3):

    3(2)−2(−3)=6+6=12.3(2) - 2(-3) = 6 + 6 = 12.3(2)−2(−3)=6+6=12.

    Subtracting a negative adds, which is where this one is won or lost. The two sides agree, so it is a solution.

    Part B

    Choose the two coefficients first and let the constant fall out of the pair you were given.

    Take x+yx + yx+y on the left. Substituting the pair fixes what has to be on the right:

    5+(−2)=3,5 + (-2) = 3,5+(−2)=3,

    so x+y=3x + y = 3x+y=3 has (5,−2)(5, -2)(5,−2) among its solutions. Take 2x+y2x + y2x+y instead and the same substitution gives

    2(5)+(−2)=8,2(5) + (-2) = 8,2(5)+(−2)=8,

    so 2x+y=82x + y = 82x+y=8 works too. Neither is a multiple of the other: doubling the first gives 2x+2y=62x + 2y = 62x+2y=6, which is not the second.

    Each verification is the ordinary test from part A. The pair goes in, and the left side has to come out equal to the right.

    Part C

    Substituting the pair into ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c turns the requirement into one condition on the three numbers:

    5a−2b=c.5a - 2b = c.5a−2b=c.

    Read that as a recipe rather than an obstacle. Choose any aaa and bbb you like, provided they are not both zero, and the condition hands back the only constant that will do. Every such choice produces an equation with the pair among its solutions, and choices that are not proportional to one another produce equations that are not multiples of one another, so there is no end to them.

    The condition runs the other way too: if ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c has the pair as a solution, substituting gives exactly that same relation, so no equation with this solution escapes it.

    So one known solution pins down nothing about aaa and bbb separately, and pins ccc down only once aaa and bbb have been settled. One pair is one condition on three numbers, which is why part B had so much room in it.

    In one line

    (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0) and (2,−3)(2, -3)(2,−3) solve 3x−2y=123x - 2y = 123x−2y=12 while (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) gives −12-12−12; equations such as x+y=3x + y = 3x+y=3 and 2x+y=82x + y = 82x+y=8 both accept (5,−2)(5, -2)(5,−2); and infinitely many equations accept it, because that one pair imposes only the single condition 5a−2b=c5a - 2b = c5a−2b=c on ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c.

    Another way: Test a pair by generating its partner instead

    When several pairs are to be tested against one equation, rearranging once can beat substituting each time. Solve 3x−2y=123x - 2y = 123x−2y=12 for yyy:

    y=3x−122.y = \frac{3x - 12}{2}.y=23x−12​.

    A pair now passes exactly when this formula, fed the pair's first number, hands back its second. At x=2x = 2x=2 it returns 6−122=−3\frac{6 - 12}{2} = -326−12​=−3, which is the second coordinate of (2,−3)(2, -3)(2,−3), so that pair passes. At x=0x = 0x=0 it returns −6-6−6, and the pair on offer there had 666.

    When it is worth it When one equation has to be checked against a whole list of pairs, since the rearrangement is done once and reused. It also hands you the correct partner for any first coordinate, which a bare pass or fail never does.

    2. Two numbers, two ways round, and a rearrangement . Foundational, 12 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    Naomi is working with 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3. She reports that (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) and (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) both solve it, 'since a solution just needs the right two numbers', and then, to produce more solutions on demand, she rearranges the equation into y=3−4xy = 3 - 4xy=3−4x. Her verdict and her rearrangement both need checking.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Test each of Naomi's two pairs in 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3, reporting a verdict for each, and say what her stated reason leaves out.

      Find and correct the error Say which line first goes wrong, why it is wrong, and then do it correctly. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Use Naomi's formula y=3−4xy = 3 - 4xy=3−4x at x=0x = 0x=0 and at x=2x = 2x=2, and test each pair it produces in 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3. Then rearrange 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3 yourself to give yyy in terms of xxx.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain what a correct rearrangement of 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3 guarantees. Say what it promises about every pair it hands back, and what it promises about solutions that might otherwise have been missed.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing in this question is settled by looking at an equation beside a pair and judging whether they seem to match. Every claim here is decided by putting numbers into the original equation and comparing the two sides.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

      Work out what job each of the two numbers is doing before computing anything. One of them is multiplied by 444 and the other is subtracted, so exchanging which is which is not a harmless relabelling.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask the formula two separate questions. Can it ever hand you a pair that fails the original equation, and can the original equation have a solution the formula never reaches? Neither answer implies the other.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) solves it, and (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) gives 181818, so it does not. Her reason leaves out the order: the first number is the value of xxx and the second the value of yyy, so the two pairs make different claims.

    Part B

    Her formula gives (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3) and (2,−5)(2, -5)(2,−5), which make the left side −3-3−3 and 131313, so neither is a solution. The rearrangement is y=4x−3y = 4x - 3y=4x−3.

    Part C

    Both directions. Every pair it hands back solves the original equation, because each step of the rearrangement can be undone; and no solution escapes it, because feeding in the first coordinate of any solution returns that solution's own second coordinate.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Test each pair separately, first number for xxx and second for yyy.

    For (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5):

    4(2)−5=8−5=3.4(2) - 5 = 8 - 5 = 3.4(2)−5=8−5=3.

    The left side matches the right, so this pair is a solution.

    For (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2):

    4(5)−2=20−2=18.4(5) - 2 = 20 - 2 = 18.4(5)−2=20−2=18.

    That is not 333, so this pair is not a solution.

    Naomi's reason treats a pair as a bag holding two numbers. It is not: it is an ordered pair, and the order is what says which number does which job. Here the two numbers enter through different terms, one multiplied by 444 and the other subtracted, so exchanging them changes the arithmetic entirely. That is why a solution is written with brackets and a comma and not as a pair of loose values.

    Part B

    Take the pairs her formula produces, then test them where it matters, in the original equation.

    At x=0x = 0x=0 her formula gives y=3y = 3y=3, and at x=2x = 2x=2 it gives y=3−8=−5y = 3 - 8 = -5y=3−8=−5. Testing both:

    4(0)−3=−3,4(2)−(−5)=13.4(0) - 3 = -3, \qquad 4(2) - (-5) = 13.4(0)−3=−3,4(2)−(−5)=13.

    Neither is 333, so the formula is not producing solutions.

    Rearrange the equation properly. Add yyy to both sides, then subtract 333 from both sides:

    4x−y=3⇒4x=y+3⇒y=4x−3.4x - y = 3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 4x = y + 3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = 4x - 3.4x−y=3⇒4x=y+3⇒y=4x−3.

    Check it at the same two values. At x=0x = 0x=0 it gives (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3), and 4(0)−(−3)=34(0) - (-3) = 34(0)−(−3)=3. At x=2x = 2x=2 it gives (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5), the pair that passed in part A.

    One test would have been thin. The two formulas agree at x=34x = \tfrac{3}{4}x=43​ and nowhere else, so a single unlucky choice of xxx would have produced a genuine solution from her formula and hidden the fault. Two values settle it.

    Part C

    A generating formula is worth having only if it is faithful in both directions, and each direction has its own reason.

    Nothing it produces is spurious. The steps from 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3 to y=4x−3y = 4x - 3y=4x−3 are adding yyy to both sides and subtracting 333 from both sides, and each is undone by the opposite move. So a pair built by the formula satisfies the original equation:

    4x−(4x−3)=3.4x - (4x - 3) = 3.4x−(4x−3)=3.

    The left side collapses to 333 whatever xxx was chosen, which is the promise in full.

    Nothing is missed either. Suppose some pair solves 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3. Feeding that pair's first coordinate into the formula returns 4x−34x - 34x−3, and the original equation says that this is exactly the pair's second coordinate. So the formula reproduces every solution there is, one for each choice of the first coordinate, which is also why the solutions never run out.

    Two directions, two guarantees: nothing spurious comes in, nothing genuine is left out. A formula with only the first property would be safe but incomplete, and one with only the second would hand you pairs you still had to check one at a time.

    In one line

    (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) solves 4x−y=34x - y = 34x−y=3 and (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) does not, because a solution is an ordered pair and the order decides which number is xxx. The formula y=3−4xy = 3 - 4xy=3−4x produces (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3) and (2,−5)(2, -5)(2,−5), which give −3-3−3 and 131313 rather than 333; the correct rearrangement is y=4x−3y = 4x - 3y=4x−3, and it is faithful in both directions, producing only solutions and missing none.

    3. One afternoon's takings, and every way it could have happened . Application, 13 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 13 pts

    A stall sells muffins at 333 dollars each and cartons of juice at 222 dollars each, and nothing else. One afternoon it takes exactly 242424 dollars.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write an equation in two variables that records the afternoon's takings, and say what each letter counts.

      Model the situation Name your unknown first, then write every other quantity in terms of that one letter. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Find every pair of whole numbers of muffins and cartons that the equation allows, and show why the search can be stopped rather than continued indefinitely.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The equation has infinitely many solutions, and part B found only a handful. Check the pairs (2.5, 8.25)(2.5,\, 8.25)(2.5,8.25) and (10,−3)(10, -3)(10,−3) in the equation, then explain what does the cutting down, and say what the takings alone can and cannot tell the stall about how many muffins were sold.

      Carry your own answer forward Judge the cutting down against whatever list of whole-number possibilities you drew up in part B. The credit here is for naming what does the cutting and for saying what is left undecided, not for the length of that list.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Prices multiply counts, so decide first which of those two roles each letter is going to play. Everything after that, including the hunt for whole-number possibilities, follows from the one equation you write down.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Step through the possible numbers of one item, starting from the smallest. Two things bring the search to an end: the money runs out, and some of the amounts left over cannot be paid for in exact cartons.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Both of the pairs you are handed pass the equation. So ask what else an answer to this question has to satisfy, and where that extra requirement is written down, because it is certainly not in the equation.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    3m+2j=243m + 2j = 243m+2j=24, where mmm is the number of muffins sold and jjj is the number of juice cartons sold.

    Part B

    Five afternoons fit: 000 muffins with 121212 cartons, 222 with 999, 444 with 666, 666 with 333, and 888 with 000.

    Part C

    Both pairs satisfy the equation, so the arithmetic does not reject them: the situation does, since a count must be a whole number and cannot be negative. The takings narrow the afternoon to the short list of part B and cannot choose between its entries.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Two quantities are unknown, so give each a letter, and be precise that the letter counts items rather than naming a price. Let mmm be the number of muffins sold and jjj the number of juice cartons sold.

    Muffins bring in 333 dollars each, contributing 3m3m3m dollars, and cartons bring in 222 dollars each, contributing 2j2j2j dollars. Together those make up the whole afternoon:

    3m+2j=24.3m + 2j = 24.3m+2j=24.

    The coefficients are the prices and the letters are the counts. Swapping those two roles is the usual slip, and it produces an equation that says nothing about the afternoon.

    Part B

    Work through the muffin counts in order, since each one leaves a definite amount for the juice.

    Rearranging shows what is left once the muffins are paid for:

    2j=24−3m.2j = 24 - 3m.2j=24−3m.

    The muffins cannot bring in more than the whole takings, so 3m≤243m \le 243m≤24 and the muffin count is at most 888. That is what stops the search: past 888 muffins the carton count would have to be negative.

    Inside that range, the money left over has to be an exact number of 222 dollar cartons, so 24−3m24 - 3m24−3m must be even. Since 242424 is even, that happens exactly when 3m3m3m is even, and so exactly when mmm is even. Testing the even counts:

    m=0,2,4,6,8givej=12,9,6,3,0.m = 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 \quad \text{give} \quad j = 12, 9, 6, 3, 0.m=0,2,4,6,8givej=12,9,6,3,0.

    An odd muffin count leaves an odd number of dollars, which no whole number of cartons can make up. So exactly five afternoons are possible.

    Part C

    Check both pairs first, because the point of this part depends on their passing.

    3(2.5)+2(8.25)=7.5+16.5=24,3(2.5) + 2(8.25) = 7.5 + 16.5 = 24,3(2.5)+2(8.25)=7.5+16.5=24,

    3(10)+2(−3)=30−6=24.3(10) + 2(-3) = 30 - 6 = 24.3(10)+2(−3)=30−6=24.

    Both are genuine solutions of the equation. What rules them out is not the equation but the situation: half a muffin cannot be sold, and a stall cannot sell a negative number of cartons. The counts have to be whole numbers, and they cannot be negative.

    Two different things are therefore at work. The equation is one condition, and by itself it leaves a whole line's worth of pairs. The situation carries restrictions the equation knows nothing about, and those cut the endless family down to the afternoons of part B.

Part C 4

  • Argues from a construction that produces such an equation from a free choice of numbers, rather than from a list of examples, which could never establish that there is no end to them. 3
  • States the single relation between aaa, bbb and ccc that the known pair imposes, and says which of the three is left with no freedom once the others are chosen. 1

2. Two numbers, two ways round, and a rearrangement 12 points

Part A 4

  • Evaluates both pairs, in each case putting the first number in for xxx and the second in for yyy, and compares each left side with the right. 2
  • Explains what the ordering of a pair is doing, rather than only recording that the two pairs came out differently. 2

Part B 4

  • Tests each pair the formula produces in the original equation, rather than back in the formula that produced it. 1
  • Rearranges by moving each term across the equals sign with its sign corrected, and reaches a formula whose output can be checked. 2
  • Says what the tests establish about the formula the pairs came from, rather than leaving two arithmetic results side by side. 1

Part C 4

  • Treats the two directions separately, with a reason for each: why nothing the formula produces can fail the original equation, and why no solution of the original equation can escape the formula. 3
  • Says what would be lost if only one of the two directions held. 1

3. One afternoon's takings, and every way it could have happened 13 points

Part A 4

  • Assigns a letter to each unknown COUNT and states in words which item each letter counts. 2
  • Multiplies each count by its own price and sets the sum of those two amounts equal to the afternoon's takings. 2

Part B 5

  • Works through the possible values of one of the two counts in order, rather than collecting pairs as they happen to be spotted. 2
  • Rules out the values that leave an amount the other item's price cannot make up exactly, and gives the reason rather than the surviving list alone. 2
  • Reports each possibility as a pair of counts with the items named, so it is clear which number counts which. 1

Part C 4

  • Substitutes both of the given pairs into the equation rather than dismissing them on sight. 1
  • Attributes the cutting down to restrictions the situation carries, and names those restrictions, rather than attributing it to the equation. 2
  • States what the takings alone leave undecided about the afternoon. 1

4. The same equation in different clothes 13 points

Part A 4

  • Names the single operation that carries one equation to the other and states that it can be undone, rather than checking pairs. 2
  • Draws both directions out of that operation, so that no solution of either equation can fail the other, and says what a finite list of tests can and cannot establish. 2

Part B 5

  • Produces one specific pair and says which of the two equations it was chosen to fail. 2
  • Evaluates that pair in BOTH equations, so the disagreement is shown rather than asserted. 2
  • States what a single shared pair does establish, without inflating it into a claim about the two collections of solutions. 1

Part C 4

  • Uses a known solution of one equation to force the value in a single line, rather than trying candidate values one at a time. 2
  • Closes both halves: rules out the other values AND shows that the surviving value reproduces the whole collection of solutions, not merely the pair used to find it. 2

5. What the word solution now has to mean 16 points

Part A 4

  • Says what a single solution consists of in each case, not only how many there are. 2
  • Settles the last question by testing what a solution of a two-variable equation is required to supply, and produces the solution that the stated value of xxx belongs to. 2

Part B 5

  • Computes each halfway pair coordinate by coordinate and substitutes it into the equation, rather than assuming it inherits the property from the pairs it came from. 2
  • Explains why the halving can always be applied again, and draws from that a conclusion about the gaps between plotted solutions. 3

Part C 3

  • Separates the two claims and matches the table's evidence to one of them, saying why the other gets no evidence from it. 2
  • Describes a concrete thing that could no longer be done with a drawn line if the unsupported direction failed. 1

Part D 4

  • States the standard a complete answer has to meet here: it describes every solution rather than exhibiting one. 1
  • Judges both responses against that standard, saying for each what it gets right and what it gets wrong, and does not treat the two as the same kind of failure. 3

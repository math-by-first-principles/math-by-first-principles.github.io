Equations with Two Variables

Intuition

Every equation you have solved so far had one unknown and, usually, one answer: 2x+3=112x + 3 = 112x+3=11 forces x=4x = 4x=4 and nothing else. But most real situations tie two unknowns together at once. “Two adult tickets and three child tickets cost 26 dollars” is a single fact linking two prices, and it does not pin down either price by itself. Adult tickets at 7 dollars and child tickets at 4 dollars fit, since 2(7)+3(4)=262(7) + 3(4) = 262(7)+3(4)=26. So do adult tickets at 10 dollars and child tickets at 2 dollars, since 2(10)+3(2)=262(10) + 3(2) = 262(10)+3(2)=26 just as well. One total fits many price pairs. An equation with two variables captures exactly this kind of statement. Instead of one solution it has a whole family of them, one for each way the total can be split. The striking part is that plotting those solutions on the coordinate plane lines them up into a perfectly straight line. That line is the picture the rest of this chapter is built on, because solving a system means finding where two such lines meet.

Learning goals

  • Write a linear equation in two variables as ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c
  • Test an ordered pair by substituting in the right order
  • Generate solutions by solving for one variable and choosing values
  • Recognize the graph as the whole solution set, a line
  • Explain why a second equation is needed to fix one pair

What a two-variable equation is

A linear equation in two variables is an equation that can be arranged into the standard form

ax+by=c,ax + by = c,ax+by=c,

where aaa, bbb, and ccc are numbers and aaa and bbb are not both zero. The two variables are usually called xxx and yyy. Each one appears only to the first power: never squared, never multiplied by the other, never under a root, and never in a denominator. That restriction is what keeps the equation linear, exactly as it did for the one-variable equations of the last chapter.

These are linear equations in two variables:

2x+3y=12,x−y=4,y=3x−1.2x + 3y = 12, \qquad x - y = 4, \qquad y = 3x - 1.2x+3y=12,x−y=4,y=3x−1.

The last one does not look like the standard form at first, but subtracting 3x3x3x from both sides turns it into −3x+y=−1-3x + y = -1−3x+y=−1, which fits the pattern. These are not linear:

xy=6(the variables are multiplied),x2+y=5(x is squared).xy = 6 \quad (\text{the variables are multiplied}), \qquad x^2 + y = 5 \quad (x \text{ is squared}).xy=6(the variables are multiplied),x2+y=5(x is squared).

A one-variable linear equation such as 2x=62x = 62x=6 locks its variable to a single value. With two variables in a single equation, one number can no longer settle both. So instead of asking for the value of xxx we look for pairs of values, one for xxx and one for yyy, that fit together.

When is a pair a solution?

A solution of a two-variable equation is an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y). It counts as a solution when putting the first number in for xxx and the second for yyy makes the equation true. You already have the tool to test one: substitution, the same check you used for one-variable equations.

Take 2x+3y=122x + 3y = 122x+3y=12 and test the pair (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2). Substitute x=3x = 3x=3 and y=2y = 2y=2 into the left side:

2(3)+3(2)=6+6=12.2(3) + 3(2) = 6 + 6 = 12.2(3)+3(2)=6+6=12.

The left side equals 121212, matching the right, so (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) is a solution. Now test (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1):

2(1)+3(1)=2+3=5,2(1) + 3(1) = 2 + 3 = 5,2(1)+3(1)=2+3=5,

which is not 121212, so (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) is not a solution.

The word ordered matters as much here as it did when you plotted points. The pair (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) is a different claim from (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2): it puts x=2x = 2x=2 and y=3y = 3y=3, giving 2(2)+3(3)=4+9=132(2) + 3(3) = 4 + 9 = 132(2)+3(3)=4+9=13, not 121212. So (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) solves the equation while (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) does not, even though they use the same two numbers. Always feed the first coordinate to xxx and the second to yyy.

3(2)+2(3)=6+6=12.True, so (2,3) is a solution.3(2) + 2(3) = 6 + 6 = 12. \quad \text{True, so } (2, 3) \text{ is a solution.}3(2)+2(3)=6+6=12.True, so (2,3) is a solution.

3(4)+2(1)=12+2=14.Not 12, so (4,1) is not a solution.3(4) + 2(1) = 12 + 2 = 14. \quad \text{Not } 12, \text{ so } (4, 1) \text{ is not a solution.}3(4)+2(1)=12+2=14.Not 12, so (4,1) is not a solution.

3(0)+2(6)=0+12=12.True, so (0,6) is a solution.3(0) + 2(6) = 0 + 12 = 12. \quad \text{True, so } (0, 6) \text{ is a solution.}3(0)+2(6)=0+12=12.True, so (0,6) is a solution.

Two of the three pairs check out. Notice that (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) works even though x=0x = 0x=0: the term 3x3x3x becomes 000, but it is still there and still counted. A zero coordinate never lets you drop a term.

Which ordered pair is a solution of 2x+y=102x + y = 102x+y=10?

Answer choices

Substitute each pair, first number for xxx and second for yyy, and look for a left side equal to 101010.

2(3)+4=6+4=102(3) + 4 = 6 + 4 = 102(3)+4=6+4=10

So (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4) works. The others give 2(4)+3=112(4) + 3 = 112(4)+3=11, 2(2)+5=92(2) + 5 = 92(2)+5=9, and 2(1)+9=112(1) + 9 = 112(1)+9=11, none equal to 101010.

Why there are infinitely many solutions

A single two-variable equation does not have one solution or a handful. It has infinitely many, and there is a simple procedure that produces them on demand. Solve the equation for one variable, then feed in any value you like for the other.

Take 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6. Solving for yyy takes one move, subtracting 2x2x2x from both sides:

y=6−2x.y = 6 - 2x.y=6−2x.

Now yyy is written directly in terms of xxx. Choose any number for xxx, and this formula hands back the matching yyy, so the pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) solves the equation. Choose x=1x = 1x=1 and you get y=6−2=4y = 6 - 2 = 4y=6−2=4, the pair (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4). Because you can pick xxx to be any number at all, and every pick produces a solution, there is no end to them.

The same idea works for any linear equation in two variables, not just this one, as long as the variable you solve for has a nonzero coefficient. That is true of every equation in this lesson.

The same solve-and-substitute reasoning proves it for the general equation ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c, including the one boundary case it has to handle. You can read the full argument in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the general argument

One equation, then, is nowhere near enough to pin down two unknowns. A single equation narrows the possibilities from every pair in the plane down to a single line’s worth of them, but no further. That is exactly why the next lessons bring in a second equation.

A neat way to keep track of these solutions is a table. Solve for one variable, list a few values of the other, and compute. Using y=6−2xy = 6 - 2xy=6−2x:

xxxy=6−2xy = 6 - 2xy=6−2xSolution (x,y)(x, y)(x,y)
−1-1−1888(−1,8)(-1, 8)(−1,8)
000666(0,6)(0, 6)(0,6)
111444(1,4)(1, 4)(1,4)
222222(2,2)(2, 2)(2,2)
333000(3,0)(3, 0)(3,0)

Every row is a genuine solution, and you could extend the table forever in either direction. Notice a pattern already: each time xxx goes up by 111, yyy drops by 222. Hold on to that, because it is what makes the graph a straight line.

Worked example 2 Fill in the missing coordinate for 3x+2y=183x + 2y = 183x+2y=18

When one coordinate of a solution is known, substitute it and solve the one-variable equation that remains for the other coordinate.

Find the solution with x=4x = 4x=4. Put x=4x = 4x=4 into 3x+2y=183x + 2y = 183x+2y=18:

3(4)+2y=18,12+2y=18,2y=6,y=3.3(4) + 2y = 18, \qquad 12 + 2y = 18, \qquad 2y = 6, \qquad y = 3.3(4)+2y=18,12+2y=18,2y=6,y=3.

So (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3) is a solution. Find the solution with y=0y = 0y=0. Put y=0y = 0y=0:

3x+2(0)=18,3x=18,x=6.3x + 2(0) = 18, \qquad 3x = 18, \qquad x = 6.3x+2(0)=18,3x=18,x=6.

So (6,0)(6, 0)(6,0) is a solution. Each known coordinate turns the two-variable equation into an ordinary one-variable equation, which you already know how to solve.

Check your understanding

Rewriting 4x+y=94x + y = 94x+y=9 to give yyy in terms of xxx, what do you get?

Answer choices

Solve for yyy by removing everything else from its side. Only 4x4x4x is added to yyy, so subtract 4x4x4x from both sides.

y=9−4xy = 9 - 4xy=9−4x

Subtracting the same 4x4x4x from both sides keeps the equation true. On the yyy side it cancels; on the 999 side it lands as −4x-4x−4x, not +4x+4x+4x.

The solutions form a straight line

Solutions are pairs (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), and pairs are points on the coordinate plane, so a natural question is what the whole collection of solutions looks like once plotted. Take the solutions of 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6 from the table and plot the four that fit on the grid.

The points do not scatter. They fall on one straight line, and this happens for every linear equation, which is where the name linear comes from. The reason is the pattern you spotted in the table: each step of 111 to the right in xxx drops yyy by the same 222. So from any plotted point the next one sits one across and two down, every time. Equal steps across paired with equal steps down trace a straight path and never bend. (That steady rate of change is what the graphing chapter will call the line’s slope; here we need only the fact that the solutions line up.)

The line is the complete picture of the solution set, and this goes both ways. Every solution of the equation is one of the points on the line. Every point on the line, including the ones between the whole-number dots, is a solution. So “the graph of 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6” and “the set of all pairs that solve 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6” name the very same thing. To draw the line you only need two solutions, since two distinct points determine a line. But plotting a third is a good check: if it does not land on the same line, one of your pairs is wrong.

Worked example 3 Solutions of 2x+3y=122x + 3y = 122x+3y=12 for chosen values of xxx

Solve for yyy first, so each value of xxx is quick to evaluate. Subtract 2x2x2x and divide by 333:

y=12−2x3.y = \frac{12 - 2x}{3}.y=312−2x​.

At x=0x = 0x=0:

y=12−03=4,the pair (0,4).y = \frac{12 - 0}{3} = 4, \qquad \text{the pair } (0, 4).y=312−0​=4,the pair (0,4).

At x=3x = 3x=3:

y=12−63=2,the pair (3,2).y = \frac{12 - 6}{3} = 2, \qquad \text{the pair } (3, 2).y=312−6​=2,the pair (3,2).

At x=1x = 1x=1:

y=12−23=103,the pair (1,103).y = \frac{12 - 2}{3} = \frac{10}{3}, \qquad \text{the pair } \left(1, \tfrac{10}{3}\right).y=312−2​=310​,the pair (1,310​).

The first two pairs land on whole-number grid points, but the third does not, and it is every bit as valid a solution. Solutions of a linear equation are not required to be whole numbers. Between any two of the tidy grid points, the line is packed with fractional solutions like this one.

Check your understanding

Using y=12−2x3y = \dfrac{12 - 2x}{3}y=312−2x​ for the equation 2x+3y=122x + 3y = 122x+3y=12, which pair is the solution when x=−3x = -3x=−3?

Check your understanding

Which of these points lies on the graph of x+y=5x + y = 5x+y=5?

Answer choices

A point lies on the graph exactly when its coordinates solve the equation, so test each pair in x+y=5x + y = 5x+y=5.

2+3=52 + 3 = 52+3=5

So (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) is on the line. The others give 1+3=41 + 3 = 41+3=4, 4+2=64 + 2 = 64+2=6, and 3+3=63 + 3 = 63+3=6, so none of them lie on it.

The figure below is that checkpoint, left open. The line drawn on it is x+y=5x + y = 5x+y=5, which is the same rule written as y=−x+5y = -x + 5y=−x+5. The hollow marker is a pair you can move anywhere on the grid. It starts at (1,3)(1, 3)(1,3), the first option, and the sentence under the figure does the substitution out loud. Walk the marker through (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2), (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) and (3,3)(3, 3)(3,3) and watch which one lands.

What is worth noticing is not that one of the four works. It is that the marker landing on the line and the substitution checking out are the same event, every single time. You can also see them happen together rather than being told they agree. Send the marker somewhere the checkpoint never mentioned, (5,0)(5, 0)(5,0) or (−1,6)(-1, 6)(−1,6), and the rule holds there too. The line is not a summary of the four tidy pairs, it is the complete list of them.

Then change the line itself. Move the rise and the run and the marker stays exactly where you put it, now testing the same pair against a different equation. That is the other half of the same idea: a pair is not a solution on its own, it is a solution of something.

Looking ahead: two equations, one point

A single two-variable equation leaves a whole line of possibilities open. To single out one specific pair, you need a second equation, a second condition the pair must also satisfy at the same time. Two such equations considered together are called a system of equations, and that is the subject of the rest of this chapter.

The graph makes the goal vivid. Each equation is a line, so a pair that solves both must lie on both lines at once, which means it sits wherever the two lines cross. Two different lines usually cross at exactly one point; the coming lessons cover the two exceptions, lines that never cross and lines that lie one on top of the other. Consider the pairs solving x+y=6x + y = 6x+y=6 and, separately, the pairs solving x−y=2x - y = 2x−y=2. The single pair (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2) lies on both lines, because 4+2=64 + 2 = 64+2=6 and 4−2=24 - 2 = 24−2=2 are both true, so it is the one pair that solves the system.

Check your understanding

Which pair solves both x+y=7x + y = 7x+y=7 and x−y=1x - y = 1x−y=1 at the same time?

Answer choices

A solution of the system has to make BOTH equations true. Check (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3):

4+3=7and4−3=14 + 3 = 7 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 4 - 3 = 14+3=7and4−3=1

Both hold, so (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3) solves the system. (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) solves x+y=7x + y = 7x+y=7 but not x−y=1x - y = 1x−y=1, since 5−2=35 - 2 = 35−2=3. (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1) solves x−y=1x - y = 1x−y=1 but not x+y=7x + y = 7x+y=7, since 2+1=32 + 1 = 32+1=3. (6,6)(6, 6)(6,6) solves neither.

Reading a crossing point off a graph is quick to picture but hard to do exactly, especially when the answer is a fraction. So the coming lessons develop algebra that finds the crossing point without drawing anything: substitution and elimination. Both rest on the idea you built here, that a two-variable equation stands for a line’s worth of solution pairs. Both also rest on the second part of that idea, that where a system’s two lines cross, the shared pair is its answer.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Writing a pair in the wrong order. A solution is an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y). For 2x+3y=122x + 3y = 122x+3y=12 the pair (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) works but (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) does not, because 2(2)+3(3)=132(2) + 3(3) = 132(2)+3(3)=13. Always put the first number in for xxx and the second for yyy.
  • Expecting a single answer. A single two-variable equation has infinitely many solutions, not one. It stands for an entire line of pairs, so “solve it” means describe that line, not find one point.
  • Thinking solutions must be whole numbers. For an equation solved for yyy like the ones here, any number you choose for xxx gives a solution, so between the tidy grid points sit fractional pairs like (1,103)\left(1, \tfrac{10}{3}\right)(1,310​). They count too.
  • Dropping a term when a coordinate is zero. In 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6 the pair (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) is a solution because 2(0)+6=62(0) + 6 = 62(0)+6=6. The 2x2x2x term does not disappear just because x=0x = 0x=0; it becomes 000 and is still added in.
  • Sign slips when solving for a variable. Solving 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6 for yyy gives y=6−2xy = 6 - 2xy=6−2x, not y=6+2xy = 6 + 2xy=6+2x. Subtracting 2x2x2x from both sides is what changes its sign: it cancels on the yyy side and lands as −2x-2x−2x on the other.
  • Drawing a line through a single point. One point does not fix a line. Plot at least two solutions, and use a third as a check that they truly line up.

Key takeaways

  • A linear equation in two variables can be written ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c, with xxx and yyy each only to the first power. A solution is an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that makes it true.
  • Test a pair by substitution, keeping the order: the first number replaces xxx, the second replaces yyy.
  • One such equation has infinitely many solutions. Solve for one variable, for example y=6−2xy = 6 - 2xy=6−2x, then choose any value of the other to generate solution pairs, and collect them in a table.
  • Plotting all the solutions traces a straight line. The graph of the equation is its solution set: every point on the line is a solution and every solution is on the line. Two distinct points are enough to draw it.
  • One equation fixes a whole line of pairs. A second equation adds another condition, and where the two lines cross is the solution of a system of equations, the coming lessons’ focus. (Two lines can also never cross, or coincide entirely; the next lessons handle both.)

Why every linear equation in two variables has infinitely many solutions

The reasoning earlier in this lesson used the specific equation 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6. Here is why the same solve-and-substitute method works for every linear equation in two variables, including the one case where the equation only pins down xxx.

Why ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c has infinitely many solutions#

Start with ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c, and suppose b≠0b \neq 0b=0. Solve for yyy: subtract axaxax from both sides and then divide by bbb, which is allowed precisely because bbb is not zero,

y=c−axb.y = \frac{c - ax}{b}.y=bc−ax​.

Now pick any number whatsoever for xxx. The right-hand side is then a definite arithmetic expression, so it evaluates to exactly one number, and that number is the matching yyy. By construction the pair (x,c−axb)\left(x, \frac{c - ax}{b}\right)(x,bc−ax​) satisfies the original equation, because we obtained yyy from it. There are infinitely many numbers to choose from for xxx: the whole numbers 0,1,2,…0, 1, 2, \ldots0,1,2,… alone are already endless, and each different choice of xxx produces a different pair. So the equation has infinitely many solutions.

If instead b=0b = 0b=0, the same reasoning works with the roles of xxx and yyy swapped, since aaa cannot also be zero. An equation such as x=3x = 3x=3 is exactly this case: it has no yyy term to solve for, so instead pick any number whatsoever for yyy, and x=3x = 3x=3 still holds. The solutions are the pairs (3,y)(3, y)(3,y) for every yyy, which is once again infinitely many, and they plot as a vertical line.

A bit of history (optional)

Ancient Indian astronomers had a counting problem. Two planets circle the sky at different speeds. If they set out together, how many days pass before they line up again?

The question becomes a single equation with two unknowns. One unknown is the number of days. The other is the number of extra full turns the faster planet makes. Every pair of numbers that fits the equation answers the question, and there are endlessly many such pairs. You have just seen why. But the astronomers could not use most of them. The question reached them in whole units, so many days elapsed and so many turns completed, and an answer had to arrive in the same terms.

So they wanted the answers built from whole numbers only. Aryabhata, an astronomer working in India around the year 499499499, set out a method for finding them. Later writers named it the kuttaka, the pulverizer. It grinds the numbers down in stages until the answer is small enough to read. It is one of the first general recipes for a problem with more unknowns than given facts.

Notice the shape of what they did. One equation left them a whole line of pairs. They narrowed it by adding a demand of their own, that the answers be whole. This chapter narrows it the other way. It hands you a second equation instead, and when the two lines cross once, the two facts together cut the line down to that single point.

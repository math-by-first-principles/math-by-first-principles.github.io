Word Problems with Systems

Intuition

In the Linear Equations chapter you solved word problems by pinning every quantity to a single letter. That works, but it can force an awkward rearrangement: to keep one variable you had to write a second quantity as something like 30−n30 - n30−n. Systems give you a cleaner option. When a problem hides two unknowns, give each its own letter and turn each fact the problem states into its own equation. Substitution or elimination then finishes the job, exactly as it did in the last two lessons.

Learning goals

  • Name two unknowns with units and turn each independent fact into an equation
  • Set up value problems by counting items and totaling worth
  • Organize a mixture's amount, strength and content in a table, then write its two equations
  • Apply d=rtd = rtd=rt per leg, with a current changing the rate
  • Find break-even where cost equals revenue

Number and relationship problems

Here is a situation with two numbers unknown, and two separate facts connecting them. Name the two numbers, write one equation per fact, and solve.

Worked example 1 Two numbers with a sum and a multiple relationship

The sum of two numbers is 272727, and the larger is 333 more than twice the smaller. Find the two numbers.

Name the two unknowns. Let xxx be the smaller number and yyy the larger number. The problem states two facts, so expect two equations.

The first fact is the sum:

x+y=27.x + y = 27.x+y=27.

The second fact is the relationship, “the larger is 333 more than twice the smaller”:

y=2x+3.y = 2x + 3.y=2x+3.

The second equation is already solved for yyy, so substitution is the easy route. Replace yyy in the first equation with 2x+32x + 32x+3:

x+(2x+3)=27.x + (2x + 3) = 27.x+(2x+3)=27.

Solve the one-variable equation that remains:

3x+3=27,3x=24,x=8.3x + 3 = 27, \qquad 3x = 24, \qquad x = 8.3x+3=27,3x=24,x=8.

Back-substitute to recover yyy:

y=2(8)+3=19.y = 2(8) + 3 = 19.y=2(8)+3=19.

Interpret and check against the words. The smaller number is 888 and the larger is 191919. Their sum is 8+19=278 + 19 = 278+19=27, and 191919 really is 333 more than twice 888, since 2×8+3=192 \times 8 + 3 = 192×8+3=19. The two numbers are 888 and 191919.

Check your understanding

Two numbers have a sum of 303030 and a difference of 888. If xxx is the larger and yyy is the smaller, which system matches the words?

Answer choices

The sum is 303030, so x+y=30x + y = 30x+y=30. The difference, larger minus smaller, is 888, and since xxx is the larger that is x−y=8x - y = 8x−y=8.

x+y=30,x−y=8x + y = 30, \qquad x - y = 8x+y=30,x−y=8

Writing y−x=8y - x = 8y−x=8 would make the smaller exceed the larger, which the words rule out. Adding the correct pair gives 2x=382x = 382x=38, so x=19x = 19x=19 and y=11y = 11y=11.

Two unknowns, two conditions

You just named two unknowns and wrote one equation per fact. That pattern is the general idea: a system of two equations, one equation for each independent fact. Solving it is work you already know, the substitution and elimination from the last two lessons; the new skill was the translation you just carried out twice.

The method is the same every time, whatever the story is about.

  1. Name the two unknowns. Decide exactly which two quantities the problem asks about, and give each its own letter. Write down what each letter means, units included, as in “let aaa be the number of adult tickets and ccc the number of child tickets.”
  2. Translate each condition into an equation. A well-posed problem states two facts. Turn each one into an equation in your two letters. You should finish with two equations, not one.
  3. Solve the system. Use substitution when a variable is already isolated or easy to isolate, and elimination when both equations sit in standard form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c. Either route returns the same pair.
  4. Interpret and check. Put the pair back into the words of the problem, confirm it answers the actual question, and state it with units.

Steps 1 and 4 are the ones most often rushed. Naming both unknowns, with units, is what keeps the two equations straight. Checking against the words, not just against your own equations, is what catches a translation that came out backwards.

Why two conditions are exactly enough

Worked Example 1 used two equations, not one. A single equation in two unknowns cannot find them both. The picture below shows why, and the proof below it says why the final check has to be against the words.

Why two independent conditions determine two unknowns#

One equation, x+y=6x + y = 6x+y=6, is true for many pairs, the picture above marks three of them: (1,5)(1, 5)(1,5), (2,4)(2, 4)(2,4), and (3,3)(3, 3)(3,3). A second, different equation is a second line, and two non-parallel lines cross at exactly one point, here (2,4)(2, 4)(2,4): the only pair making both equations true at once.

The word “independent” is doing real work. A fact that only restates the first gives the very same line, so nothing new is pinned down. A fact that contradicts the first, same steepness but a different total, gives a distinct but parallel line that never crosses the first at all.

The final check has to go back to the words, not just your own two equations. Solving a system only tests whether the algebra was done correctly; it says nothing about whether the equations were a faithful translation of the problem. Worked Example 1’s check reread the original sentences, the sum and the relationship, rather than stopping at the equations x+y=27x + y = 27x+y=27 and y=2x+3y = 2x + 3y=2x+3.

∎

See what happens to the two lines when a second fact only repeats or contradicts the first, instead of saying something new. Then watch a translation slip that passes a perfect check of the equations but fails the original words.

Money and value problems

Coins, bills, tickets, and stamps all share a shape. There are two facts: a count (how many items) and a total value (what they are worth together). The count equation just adds the numbers of items; the value equation multiplies each count by its unit price before adding. Work in a single unit, all cents or all dollars, so nothing is mixed.

Worked example 2 A ticket problem: counts and total value

A theater sold 200200200 tickets one night and collected 244024402440 dollars. Adult tickets cost 151515 dollars and child tickets cost 888 dollars. How many of each were sold?

Name the two unknowns. Let aaa be the number of adult tickets and ccc the number of child tickets.

The first fact counts the tickets:

a+c=200.a + c = 200.a+c=200.

The second fact is the money, each ticket contributing its price:

15a+8c=2440.15a + 8c = 2440.15a+8c=2440.

Both equations are in standard form, so eliminate a variable. Multiply the count equation by 888 so the ccc terms match:

8a+8c=1600.8a + 8c = 1600.8a+8c=1600.

Subtract this from the value equation to cancel ccc:

(15a+8c)−(8a+8c)=2440−1600,7a=840,a=120.(15a + 8c) - (8a + 8c) = 2440 - 1600, \qquad 7a = 840, \qquad a = 120.(15a+8c)−(8a+8c)=2440−1600,7a=840,a=120.

Back-substitute into the count equation:

120+c=200,c=80.120 + c = 200, \qquad c = 80.120+c=200,c=80.

Interpret and check. The theater sold 120120120 adult tickets and 808080 child tickets. The counts add to 120+80=200120 + 80 = 200120+80=200, and the money comes to 15(120)+8(80)=1800+640=244015(120) + 8(80) = 1800 + 640 = 244015(120)+8(80)=1800+640=2440 dollars, matching both facts. So 120120120 adult tickets and 808080 child tickets.

Check your understanding

A piggy bank holds nnn nickels and ddd dimes, 323232 coins in all, worth 250250250 cents. A nickel is 555 cents and a dime is 101010 cents. Which system models this?

Answer choices

One equation counts the coins and one totals their value in cents. There are 323232 coins, so n+d=32n + d = 32n+d=32. Each nickel is worth 555 cents and each dime 101010 cents, so the value is 5n+10d=2505n + 10d = 2505n+10d=250.

n+d=32,5n+10d=250n + d = 32, \qquad 5n + 10d = 250n+d=32,5n+10d=250

The last choice swaps the coin values, paying a nickel 101010 cents and a dime 555, which reverses the answer.

Mixture and blend problems

A mixture problem combines two ingredients of different strengths or prices into one batch. Again there are two facts: the total amount and the total content (of acid, salt, or value). A small table, one row per ingredient plus a row for the mixture, keeps the bookkeeping straight.

Worked example 3 A mixture problem: blending two acid solutions

A chemist blends a 20%20\%20% acid solution with a 50%50\%50% acid solution to make 121212 liters of a 30%30\%30% acid solution. How many liters of each go into the blend?

Name the two unknowns. Let xxx be the liters of 20%20\%20% solution and yyy the liters of 50%50\%50% solution. A table keeps the amount and the content straight:

20%20\%20% acidxxx0.200.200.200.20x0.20x0.20x
50%50\%50% acidyyy0.500.500.500.50y0.50y0.50y
Mixture1212120.300.300.303.63.63.6

The Liters column gives the first fact, the total volume:

x+y=12.x + y = 12.x+y=12.

The Liters of acid column gives the second, the total acid content. The finished 121212 liters is 30%30\%30% acid, so 0.30×12=3.60.30 \times 12 = 3.60.30×12=3.6:

0.20x+0.50y=3.6.0.20x + 0.50y = 3.6.0.20x+0.50y=3.6.

Solve the volume equation for xxx and substitute. From x=12−yx = 12 - yx=12−y:

0.20(12−y)+0.50y=3.6.0.20(12 - y) + 0.50y = 3.6.0.20(12−y)+0.50y=3.6.

Distribute and solve:

2.4−0.20y+0.50y=3.6,0.30y=1.2,y=4.2.4 - 0.20y + 0.50y = 3.6, \qquad 0.30y = 1.2, \qquad y = 4.2.4−0.20y+0.50y=3.6,0.30y=1.2,y=4.

Then x=12−4=8x = 12 - 4 = 8x=12−4=8.

Interpret and check. Use 888 liters of the 20%20\%20% solution and 444 liters of the 50%50\%50% solution. The volumes add to 8+4=128 + 4 = 128+4=12 liters, and the acid comes to 0.20(8)+0.50(4)=1.6+2.0=3.60.20(8) + 0.50(4) = 1.6 + 2.0 = 3.60.20(8)+0.50(4)=1.6+2.0=3.6 liters, which is 30%30\%30% of 121212. So 888 liters of 20%20\%20% and 444 liters of 50%50\%50%.

Check your understanding

A mixer blends a 10%10\%10% juice concentrate with a 40%40\%40% juice concentrate to make 999 liters of a 30%30\%30% mixture. If xxx is the liters of the 10%10\%10% concentrate and yyy the liters of the 40%40\%40% concentrate, which system models this?

Answer choices

One equation totals the liters, the other totals the pure juice. There are 999 liters altogether, so x+y=9x + y = 9x+y=9. The pure juice comes to 30%30\%30% of that 999 liters, so 0.10x+0.40y=0.30(9)0.10x + 0.40y = 0.30(9)0.10x+0.40y=0.30(9).

x+y=9,0.10x+0.40y=0.30(9)x + y = 9, \qquad 0.10x + 0.40y = 0.30(9)x+y=9,0.10x+0.40y=0.30(9)

The second choice sets the juice content equal to 999 liters, confusing the total volume with the total content.

Distance problems: rate times time

Distance problems rest on one relationship, distance equals rate times time, written d=rtd = rtd=rt. Two unknowns appear when a moving thing has both its own speed and the help or hindrance of a current or wind. Going downstream a current adds to the boat’s speed; going upstream it subtracts. That single difference turns one trip into two equations.

Worked example 4 A current problem: boat speed and stream speed

A motorboat travels 363636 miles downstream in 222 hours, then returns the same 363636 miles upstream in 333 hours. Find the speed of the boat in still water and the speed of the current.

Name the two unknowns. Let bbb be the boat’s speed in still water and ccc the speed of the current, both in miles per hour.

Going downstream the current helps, so the speed over the ground is b+cb + cb+c; going upstream the current fights the boat, so the speed is b−cb - cb−c. Each leg gives a distance-equals-rate-times-time equation. Downstream, 363636 miles in 222 hours:

2(b+c)=36,b+c=18.2(b + c) = 36, \qquad b + c = 18.2(b+c)=36,b+c=18.

Upstream, 363636 miles in 333 hours:

3(b−c)=36,b−c=12.3(b - c) = 36, \qquad b - c = 12.3(b−c)=36,b−c=12.

The ccc terms are already opposites, so add the two equations to eliminate ccc:

(b+c)+(b−c)=18+12,2b=30,b=15.(b + c) + (b - c) = 18 + 12, \qquad 2b = 30, \qquad b = 15.(b+c)+(b−c)=18+12,2b=30,b=15.

Back-substitute into b+c=18b + c = 18b+c=18:

15+c=18,c=3.15 + c = 18, \qquad c = 3.15+c=18,c=3.

Interpret and check. The boat makes 151515 mph in still water and the current runs at 333 mph. Downstream the speed is 15+3=1815 + 3 = 1815+3=18 mph, covering 18×2=3618 \times 2 = 3618×2=36 miles; upstream it is 15−3=1215 - 3 = 1215−3=12 mph, covering 12×3=3612 \times 3 = 3612×3=36 miles. Both legs check, so the boat is 151515 mph and the current is 333 mph.

Check your understanding

Solving a boat-and-current problem you reach b+c=20b + c = 20b+c=20 and b−c=14b - c = 14b−c=14, where bbb is the boat's still-water speed and ccc is the current. What is the current's speed?

Answer choices

Subtract the second equation from the first to eliminate bbb; the bbb terms cancel and the ccc terms double.

(b+c)−(b−c)=20−14,2c=6,c=3(b + c) - (b - c) = 20 - 14, \qquad 2c = 6, \qquad c = 3(b+c)−(b−c)=20−14,2c=6,c=3

So the current is 333 mph, and adding the equations gives 2b=342b = 342b=34, so b=17b = 17b=17. Reading 171717 as the current confuses it with the boat's speed.

Break-even: where cost meets revenue

A business has a cost that grows with how many items it makes and a revenue that grows with how many it sells. Break-even is the number of items where the two are equal, so the company neither loses nor gains. Because cost and revenue are two lines, break-even is exactly the point where they cross, the picture that opened this chapter.

Worked example 5 A break-even problem: cost meets revenue

A small studio makes phone cases. It spends 120012001200 dollars in fixed costs plus 444 dollars in materials per case, and sells each case for 101010 dollars. How many cases must it sell to break even, and what are the cost and revenue there?

Name the two quantities. Let xxx be the number of cases and yyy the number of dollars.

The cost is the fixed amount plus 444 dollars per case:

y=4x+1200.y = 4x + 1200.y=4x+1200.

The revenue is 101010 dollars per case:

y=10x.y = 10x.y=10x.

Break-even is the point that lies on both lines, where cost and revenue are the same yyy. Both equations are solved for yyy, so set the two expressions equal:

10x=4x+1200.10x = 4x + 1200.10x=4x+1200.

Solve:

6x=1200,x=200.6x = 1200, \qquad x = 200.6x=1200,x=200.

Then y=10(200)=2000y = 10(200) = 2000y=10(200)=2000.

Interpret and check. The studio breaks even at 200200200 cases, where cost and revenue are both 200020002000 dollars. The cost is 4(200)+1200=20004(200) + 1200 = 20004(200)+1200=2000 dollars and the revenue is 10(200)=200010(200) = 200010(200)=2000 dollars, equal as required. Selling fewer than 200200200 cases loses money, and selling more turns a profit.

Check your understanding

A vendor spends 606060 dollars on a stand plus 333 dollars per item, and sells each item for 888 dollars. Which equation gives the break-even number of items xxx?

Answer choices

Cost is the fixed amount plus 333 dollars per item, 3x+603x + 603x+60; revenue is 888 dollars per item, 8x8x8x. Break-even sets them equal.

3x+60=8x⟹60=5x⟹x=123x + 60 = 8x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad 60 = 5x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad x = 123x+60=8x⟹60=5x⟹x=12

At 121212 items, cost and revenue are both 3(12)+60=8(12)=963(12) + 60 = 8(12) = 963(12)+60=8(12)=96 dollars.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Writing only one equation for two unknowns. Two unknowns need two conditions. A single equation leaves an entire line of pairs still fitting, so read the problem again and find the second fact before you start solving.
  • Mislabeling which quantity is which. Decide before you translate whether xxx is the larger or the smaller, the adults or the children, and hold it fixed. Swapping midway can reverse a subtraction or attach the wrong price, percentage, or rate to a variable, quietly wrecking the answer.
  • Blurring the count and the value. In a coin or ticket problem, the count equation and the value equation are different facts. Do not add coins to cents. Multiply each count by its unit price, and keep every term in one unit.
  • Mixing amount with content in a mixture. The volumes add to the batch volume; the acid, salt, or value adds to the batch content. Keep every term in one equation in the same unit, liters in one equation and liters of acid in the other. Otherwise the equation stops matching the fact it was meant to state.
  • Checking only against your own equations. Substituting the pair into your equations confirms the arithmetic, not the translation. Read the answer back into the sentences, and reject a count of people or coins that comes out negative or fractional.

Key takeaways

  • Model a two-unknown situation with a system. Give each unknown its own letter and units, translate each independent fact into an equation, and solve by substitution or elimination. Two unknowns need two independent equations. One alone leaves a whole line of possible pairs; a second, genuinely new condition is the line that crosses it at the single answer.
  • Value problems count the items and total their worth, count times unit price. Mixture problems total the amount and the content separately; a table keeps them apart. Distance problems apply d=rtd = rtd=rt to each leg, letting a current or wind make the rate b+cb + cb+c one way and b−cb - cb−c the other. Break-even sets cost equal to revenue, the point where the two lines cross.
  • Always check against the original words, not just your equations, and report the quantity the problem actually asked for, with units.

When a second fact isn't really new

If the second equation is only the first in disguise, a multiple of it, the two describe the same line. Then nothing new is fixed, and the problem has infinitely many answers. If the second contradicts the first, the same steepness but a different total, the lines are parallel and no pair satisfies both. A well-posed problem avoids both traps, handing you two facts that are neither identical nor contradictory.

A translation can go wrong in a way neither of those cases catches. Suppose a story says the larger number exceeds the smaller by 555, and a slip translates that as the smaller minus the larger. Solving that system flawlessly still reaches a pair that satisfies both written equations while contradicting the sentence the problem actually stated. The only way to catch it is to read the answer back against the original words: does the larger number really exceed the smaller by 555? Checking against the equations tests only the arithmetic; checking against the words tests the translation.

A bit of history (optional)

Why have math books dressed their problems in little stories for so long? The habit is older than the printed page.

Around the year 800800800, an English scholar named Alcuin taught at the court of Charlemagne, the king who ruled much of western Europe. A set of about fifty puzzles is credited to him, under a title that translates as Problems to Sharpen the Young. A trader must spend exactly a hundred coins on exactly a hundred animals. Grain has to be carried across a desert by a camel that eats some of the load on the way. A man must ferry a wolf, a goat and a cabbage across a river without ever leaving the wrong pair alone together.

These are not all the same kind of puzzle underneath. The wolf, the goat and the cabbage hide no equation at all; that puzzle is solved by finding a safe crossing order, not by naming unknowns. The animal-buying puzzle and the camel-and-grain puzzle are closer cousins of algebra, but harder ones than the systems in this lesson. Each hides several unknown counts at once, or a quantity that shrinks as it travels. What the three puzzles share is not their mathematics but the habit behind them. Each dresses a puzzle in a story and asks the reader to see the structure hiding inside it.

That is the real point of Alcuin’s title. None of these puzzles is really about the animals or the cabbage. Each one trains a reader to look past the story and find what kind of problem it actually is. The value, mixture and distance problems of this lesson are dressed in different clothes for the same reason. Naming each unknown and turning each stated fact into its own equation is how you see through the costume to the system underneath.

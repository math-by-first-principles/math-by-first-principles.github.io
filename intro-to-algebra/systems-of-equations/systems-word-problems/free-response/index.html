    1. A rack of two wheels and three . Foundational, 12 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    A repair shop keeps only bicycles and tricycles on one rack. There are 242424 machines on the rack altogether, standing on 555555 wheels. A bicycle has 222 wheels and a tricycle has 333.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Name the two unknown quantities, each with its own letter and with what it counts, and write the two equations the description of the rack gives. Do not solve them yet.

      Model the situation Name your unknown first, then write every other quantity in terms of that one letter. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Solve the system and say how many bicycles and how many tricycles are on the rack. Test your pair against both of the stated facts.

      Carry your own answer forward Solve the system you wrote in part A, whatever it turned out to be, and test the pair it gives against the description of the rack. The credit here is for reducing the two equations to one, for solving correctly, and for testing the pair against both stated facts, not for reaching one particular pair.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Either fact on its own leaves the question unanswered. Give a different pair of whole numbers that fits the machine count but not the wheel count, and explain what the wheel count supplies that the machine count cannot.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Count what is unknown before you count anything else. Two different quantities are being asked about here, and at this stage each of them deserves a letter of its own rather than being squeezed into one.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

    Let bbb be the number of bicycles and ttt the number of tricycles. Counting machines gives b+t=24b + t = 24b+t=24, and counting wheels gives 2b+3t=552b + 3t = 552b+3t=55.

    • any letters, provided each is said to be a NUMBER OF machines of one kind, for instance xxx bicycles and yyy tricycles
    • 3t+2b=553t + 2b = 553t+2b=55, written in the other order

    Part B

    171717 bicycles and 777 tricycles.

    • b=17b = 17b=17 and t=7t = 7t=7

    Part C

    For example 202020 bicycles and 444 tricycles: the machines come to 20+4=2420 + 4 = 2420+4=24, as required, but the wheels come to 525252, not 555555. The machine count on its own admits 252525 whole-number pairs, and the wheel count is what selects one of them.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Two quantities are unknown, the number of bicycles and the number of tricycles, so each gets a letter of its own: let bbb be the number of bicycles and ttt the number of tricycles, both counts of machines.

    The description states two separate facts, so expect two equations. The first counts machines, where a bicycle and a tricycle each contribute exactly one:

    b+t=24.b + t = 24.b+t=24.

    The second counts wheels, where a bicycle contributes 222 and a tricycle contributes 333:

    2b+3t=55.2b + 3t = 55.2b+3t=55.

    The two equations count different things, machines in one and wheels in the other, and that is what makes them two conditions rather than one condition written twice.

    Part B

    The machine equation is the easier one to rearrange, so use it to write one letter in terms of the other:

    b=24−t.b = 24 - t.b=24−t.

    Put that into the wheel equation, which then has a single unknown in it:

    2(24−t)+3t=55,48−2t+3t=55,48+t=55,t=7.2(24 - t) + 3t = 55, \qquad 48 - 2t + 3t = 55, \qquad 48 + t = 55, \qquad t = 7.2(24−t)+3t=55,48−2t+3t=55,48+t=55,t=7.

    Back-substitute into the machine equation: b=24−7=17b = 24 - 7 = 17b=24−7=17.

    Now test the pair against both facts as they were stated, not just against the equation it was substituted into. Machines: 17+7=2417 + 7 = 2417+7=24. Wheels: 2(17)+3(7)=34+21=552(17) + 3(7) = 34 + 21 = 552(17)+3(7)=34+21=55. Both hold, so the rack holds 171717 bicycles and 777 tricycles.

    Part C

    Take any whole number of bicycles from 000 to 242424 and let the tricycles make the count up to 242424. Every one of those 252525 pairs satisfies the machine count. For instance 202020 bicycles and 444 tricycles gives 20+4=2420 + 4 = 2420+4=24 machines, yet its wheels come to 2(20)+3(4)=40+12=522(20) + 3(4) = 40 + 12 = 522(20)+3(4)=40+12=52, three short of the 555555 counted on the rack.

    So the machine count alone cannot answer the question: it leaves 252525 candidates standing. What the wheel count adds is a second, different measurement of the same rack. Writing t=24−bt = 24 - bt=24−b, the wheels of a candidate pair come to

    2b+3(24−b)=72−b.2b + 3(24 - b) = 72 - b.2b+3(24−b)=72−b.

    That expression takes a different value for every one of the 252525 candidates, running from 727272 wheels when there are no bicycles down to 484848 when they all are. Exactly one candidate therefore carries 555555 wheels, which is why the second fact narrows 252525 possibilities to one and why a problem about two unknown counts needs two conditions rather than one.

    In one line

    The rack holds 171717 bicycles and 777 tricycles, from b+t=24b + t = 24b+t=24 and 2b+3t=552b + 3t = 552b+3t=55. The machine count on its own is satisfied by 252525 whole-number pairs, from no bicycles up to 242424 of them; such a pair carries 72−b72 - b72−b wheels, a different total for every one of the 252525, so the wheel count is exactly what reduces those candidates to one.

    Another way: Start from an all-bicycle rack and swap

    If all 242424 machines were bicycles the rack would stand on 2×24=482 \times 24 = 482×24=48 wheels, which is short of the 555555 counted:

    55−48=7.55 - 48 = 7.55−48=7.

    Swapping one bicycle for one tricycle leaves the machine count alone and adds exactly one wheel, so seven swaps close the gap: 777 tricycles and 171717 bicycles.

    When it is worth it When one kind of item is exchanged for another at a fixed cost per exchange, this counts the exchanges directly and needs no algebra. It stops being easy the moment the amounts can be fractions, or the two conditions are not a plain count and a plain total.

    2. Two teas and a target price . Application, 15 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Application 15 pts

    A shop blends two teas. Its house tea costs 121212 dollars per kilogram and its jasmine tea costs 202020 dollars per kilogram. Every batch it makes weighs 101010 kilograms, and a batch is priced by what the leaves in it cost.

    1. A
      Part A.

      The manager orders a batch priced at 151515 dollars per kilogram. Find how many kilograms of each tea it contains.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 5 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      The manager now orders a batch priced at 222222 dollars per kilogram. Do the same work for this order, report what the algebra returns, and say whether the shop can fill it.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Which batch prices, in dollars per kilogram, can this shop hit with these two teas? Give the whole range, and argue both that nothing outside it is possible and that everything inside it is.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      A blending problem balances two different things at once: how much stuff there is, and what that stuff is worth. Decide which sentence measures which before you write a single equation.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

      A price per kilogram is not a total. Multiply it by the weight of the batch to get the money the whole batch is worth, and set the value equation against that figure.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Get the cost of a batch down to an expression in just one of the two masses. Then ask how large and how small that expression can be as the mass runs across the values a real batch allows.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Let xxx be the kilograms of house tea in the batch and yyy the kilograms of jasmine tea. One fact fixes the weight of the batch:

    x+y=10.x + y = 10.x+y=10.

    The other fact is money, and the price given is per kilogram, so turn it into the cost of the whole batch first: 101010 kilograms at 151515 dollars each is 150150150 dollars. Each tea contributes its own cost:

    12x+20y=150.12x + 20y = 150.12x+20y=150.

    12(10−y)+20y=150,120+8y=150,8y=30,y=3.75,12(10 - y) + 20y = 150, \qquad 120 + 8y = 150, \qquad 8y = 30, \qquad y = 3.75,12(10−y)+20y=150,120+8y=150,8y=30,y=3.75,

    and then x=10−3.75=6.25x = 10 - 3.75 = 6.25x=10−3.75=6.25.     Part B

    Only the target changes. Ten kilograms at 222222 dollars each is 220220220 dollars, so the system is

    x+y=10,12x+20y=220.x + y = 10, \qquad 12x + 20y = 220.x+y=10,12x+20y=220.

Find the numbers, show that a second choice of two clues gives the same pair, and decide whether a fourth clue saying the two numbers differ by 777 could be printed alongside them.

    The answer

    The numbers are 181818 and 121212, and any two of the three clues return them. The proposed fourth clue cannot be printed: 181818 and 121212 differ by 666, so no pair would satisfy all four and the puzzle would have no answer.

    With xxx the larger and yyy the smaller, the sum clue and the difference clue give

    x+y=30,x=y+6,x + y = 30, \qquad x = y + 6,x+y=30,x=y+6,

    so y+6+y=30y + 6 + y = 30y+6+y=30, giving y=12y = 12y=12 and x=18x = 18x=18.

    A second choice of two clues, the difference clue with the tripling clue, gives x=y+6x = y + 6x=y+6 and 3y=x+183y = x + 183y=x+18, so

    3y=y+6+18,2y=24,y=12,3y = y + 6 + 18, \qquad 2y = 24, \qquad y = 12,3y=y+6+18,2y=24,y=12,

    and x=18x = 18x=18 again. The third clue checks out on the pair as well, since 3(12)=363(12) = 363(12)=36 and 18+18=3618 + 18 = 3618+18=36.

    The proposed fourth clue fails on that pair: the numbers differ by 18−12=618 - 12 = 618−12=6, not 777. So no pair could satisfy all four clues, and printing it would leave the puzzle with no answer.

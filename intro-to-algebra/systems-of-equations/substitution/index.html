Solving Systems by Substitution

Intuition

You can already solve an equation with one unknown: 3x+1=73x + 1 = 73x+1=7 marches straight to x=2x = 2x=2. A system hands you two equations and two unknowns at once, and neither equation settles anything on its own. Substitution is the trick that turns the hard problem back into the easy one. If one equation tells you that yyy equals some expression in xxx, you can erase yyy from the other equation and write that expression in its place. Suddenly the second equation has a single unknown left, and you are back on familiar ground. Solve it, then feed the answer back to recover the variable you set aside. The whole method is one idea: trade a variable for something equal to it.

Learning goals

  • Replace a variable with an equal expression to leave one unknown
  • Choose a coefficient of 111 or −1-1−1 to avoid fractions
  • Substitute into the other equation, then back-substitute
  • Read the pair as the crossing point of two lines
  • Interpret a cancelled variable as parallel lines or one line
  • Check both coordinates in both original equations

Turning two unknowns into one

A system of two linear equations presents two equations that must hold at the same time, and asks for the pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that satisfies both. In the last lesson you saw the picture: each equation draws a line, and the one pair solving both sits exactly where the two lines cross. Reading that crossing point off a graph is quick to picture but hard to do exactly, especially when the coordinates are fractions. Substitution finds it precisely, and it needs nothing beyond the equation solving from the Linear Equations chapter.

The method rests on a single principle you have used since your very first equation: equals may be replaced by equals. If one equation tells you that y=7−2xy = 7 - 2xy=7−2x, then yyy and 7−2x7 - 2x7−2x are two names for the same number. So anywhere yyy appears you may write 7−2x7 - 2x7−2x instead without changing what is true.

Watch it clear an entire variable out of the way. Take

y=x+1,2x+y=7.y = x + 1, \qquad 2x + y = 7.y=x+1,2x+y=7.

The first equation says yyy is the same as x+1x + 1x+1. Replace yyy in the second equation with x+1x + 1x+1:

2x+(x+1)=7.2x + (x + 1) = 7.2x+(x+1)=7.

The yyy is gone. What remains has only xxx in it, and you already know how to finish:

3x+1=7,3x=6,x=2.3x + 1 = 7, \qquad 3x = 6, \qquad x = 2.3x+1=7,3x=6,x=2.

Then recover yyy from the first equation: y=x+1=3y = x + 1 = 3y=x+1=3. The solution is (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3), the very point where these two lines cross.

The substitution method step by step

The example above followed a fixed routine, and every system starts with the same moves. (If the variable terms cancel completely in step 3, there is no second coordinate to find, and step 4 does not happen. That case gets its own section below.)

  1. Solve one equation for one variable. Pick a variable that is easy to isolate, ideally one whose coefficient is 111 or −1-1−1 so no fractions appear. This gives an expression for that variable in terms of the other.
  2. Substitute into the other equation. Replace that variable, in the equation you did not use in step 1, with the expression you found. The result is a single equation in one variable.
  3. Solve the one-variable equation. Use the ordinary methods from the Linear Equations chapter.
  4. Back-substitute. Put that value into the expression from step 1 to get the second variable.
  5. Check. Substitute the pair into both original equations. A genuine solution satisfies each one.

Steps 2 and 5 are the two most often skipped. Substituting into the other equation is what makes progress, and checking in both originals is what catches a slip.

Worked example 1 Solve x−2y=1x - 2y = 1x−2y=1 and 3x+y=173x + y = 173x+y=17

Neither equation is solved for a variable yet, so start with step 1. In x−2y=1x - 2y = 1x−2y=1 the variable xxx has coefficient 111, so isolating it is clean. Add 2y2y2y to both sides:

x=1+2y.x = 1 + 2y.x=1+2y.

Substitute this expression for xxx into the other equation, 3x+y=173x + y = 173x+y=17:

3(1+2y)+y=17.3(1 + 2y) + y = 17.3(1+2y)+y=17.

Now there is only yyy to find. Distribute and collect:

3+6y+y=17,3+7y=17,7y=14,y=2.3 + 6y + y = 17, \qquad 3 + 7y = 17, \qquad 7y = 14, \qquad y = 2.3+6y+y=17,3+7y=17,7y=14,y=2.

Back-substitute y=2y = 2y=2 into x=1+2yx = 1 + 2yx=1+2y:

x=1+2(2)=5.x = 1 + 2(2) = 5.x=1+2(2)=5.

So the candidate solution is (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2). Check it in both original equations, not just the one you solved:

x−2y=5−2(2)=1 ✓,3x+y=3(5)+2=17 ✓.x - 2y = 5 - 2(2) = 1 \ \checkmark, \qquad 3x + y = 3(5) + 2 = 17 \ \checkmark.x−2y=5−2(2)=1 ✓,3x+y=3(5)+2=17 ✓.

Both hold, so (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) is the solution.

In the system y=x−2y = x - 2y=x−2 and 3x+y=103x + y = 103x+y=10, what do you get right after substituting for yyy in the second equation?

Answer choices

The first equation says yyy is the same as x−2x - 2x−2, so replace yyy in 3x+y=103x + y = 103x+y=10 with x−2x - 2x−2. That is the other equation, not the one you solved for yyy.

3x+(x−2)=103x + (x - 2) = 103x+(x−2)=10

From here 4x−2=104x - 2 = 104x−2=10, so 4x=124x = 124x=12 and x=3x = 3x=3. Back-substitute x=3x = 3x=3 into y=x−2y = x - 2y=x−2 to get y=1y = 1y=1, so the solution is (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1).

Check your understanding

Solve the system x+y=8x + y = 8x+y=8 and y=x−2y = x - 2y=x−2 by substitution. What is the full solution pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y)?

Answer choices

The second equation already gives yyy in terms of xxx, so substitute it into the first equation:

x+(x−2)=8x + (x - 2) = 8x+(x−2)=8

This gives 2x−2=82x - 2 = 82x−2=8, so 2x=102x = 102x=10 and x=5x = 5x=5. Back-substitute into y=x−2y = x - 2y=x−2 to get y=3y = 3y=3. Check both originals: 5+3=85 + 3 = 85+3=8, and 3=5−23 = 5 - 23=5−2. Both hold, so the solution is (5,3)(5, 3)(5,3), not just the xxx-value.

3=6−3 ✓3 = 6 - 3 \ \checkmark3=6−3 ✓

Why substitution works

Substitution can feel almost too easy, so it is worth seeing exactly why the pair it produces is guaranteed to be the solution of the system. The argument below also shows that no solutions are gained and none are lost.

Why substitution never gains or loses a solution#

A system asks for every pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that makes both equations true. Substitution swaps the system for one smaller equation, so it is worth checking that the swap keeps exactly the same pairs. Take the opening example again: y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 and 2x+y=72x + y = 72x+y=7.

Solving the first equation for yyy did not remove or add any pairs. Every step used to get y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 can be undone, so a pair satisfies that equation exactly when its yyy-coordinate equals x+1x + 1x+1.

Now look only at pairs with y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1. For any one of them, yyy and x+1x + 1x+1 are just two names for the same number. So writing x+1x + 1x+1 in place of yyy inside the second equation cannot change whether that pair satisfies it: you are only replacing a quantity with an equal one. That is why 2x+y=72x + y = 72x+y=7 and 2x+(x+1)=72x + (x + 1) = 72x+(x+1)=7 are true for exactly the same pairs of this kind, for any starting equations, not just these two.

Put the two facts together. A pair solves the system exactly when y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 and 2x+(x+1)=72x + (x + 1) = 72x+(x+1)=7 are both true. The second condition pins down xxx, and the first then hands you yyy. So substitution trades the system for one equivalent equation. Every xxx that solves it gives one solution of the system, and every solution of the system comes from exactly one such xxx. Nothing is gained, and nothing is lost.

This also explains what the graph does. When the substituted equation pins down one value of xxx, the two lines meet at one point. When it collapses to a false statement such as 1=41 = 41=4, no xxx works, so the lines are parallel. When it collapses to something always true such as 4=44 = 44=4, every xxx on the first line works, so the two equations describe the same line.

∎

Choosing what to solve for

Every system yields to substitution, but a smart first choice saves you from fractions. Scan the four coefficients and look for a variable whose coefficient is 111 or −1-1−1. A coefficient of 111 needs only addition or subtraction to isolate; a coefficient of −1-1−1 needs one extra sign flip. Neither ever divides by anything other than 111, so no denominator appears. If no coefficient is 111 or −1-1−1, any choice works; you will just carry a fraction through the arithmetic.

Worked example 2 Solve 3x+y=63x + y = 63x+y=6 and 5x+2y=115x + 2y = 115x+2y=11

Look for the easiest variable to isolate. In 3x+y=63x + y = 63x+y=6 the variable yyy has coefficient 111, so solve that equation for yyy:

y=6−3x.y = 6 - 3x.y=6−3x.

Substitute into the other equation, 5x+2y=115x + 2y = 115x+2y=11:

5x+2(6−3x)=11.5x + 2(6 - 3x) = 11.5x+2(6−3x)=11.

Distribute and collect the xxx terms:

5x+12−6x=11,−x+12=11,−x=−1,x=1.5x + 12 - 6x = 11, \qquad -x + 12 = 11, \qquad -x = -1, \qquad x = 1.5x+12−6x=11,−x+12=11,−x=−1,x=1.

Back-substitute x=1x = 1x=1 into y=6−3xy = 6 - 3xy=6−3x:

y=6−3(1)=3.y = 6 - 3(1) = 3.y=6−3(1)=3.

The solution is (1,3)(1, 3)(1,3). A quick check confirms it: 3(1)+3=63(1) + 3 = 63(1)+3=6 and 5(1)+2(3)=115(1) + 2(3) = 115(1)+2(3)=11, both true. Had we instead solved 5x+2y=115x + 2y = 115x+2y=11 for xxx, we would have divided by 555 and dragged a fraction through every line. Choosing the coefficient of 111 kept the numbers whole.

When the variable disappears: no solution and infinitely many

Usually the substituted equation pins one variable to a single value. Once in a while the variable terms cancel completely and you are left with a bare numerical statement instead. That is not a dead end; it is the system telling you how the two lines lie.

Worked example 3 A system with no solution

Solve

y=2x+1,y=2x+4.y = 2x + 1, \qquad y = 2x + 4.y=2x+1,y=2x+4.

Both equations are already solved for yyy, so set the two expressions for yyy equal (each equals yyy, so they equal each other):

2x+1=2x+4.2x + 1 = 2x + 4.2x+1=2x+4.

Subtract 2x2x2x from both sides, and every xxx vanishes:

1=4.1 = 4.1=4.

This is false: no value of xxx can make 111 equal 444. So there is no pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) satisfying both equations, and the system has no solution. Geometrically, both lines climb 222 units for every step right but start at different heights, so they are parallel and never cross.

Worked example 4 A system with infinitely many solutions

Solve

y=3x−2,6x−2y=4.y = 3x - 2, \qquad 6x - 2y = 4.y=3x−2,6x−2y=4.

Substitute y=3x−2y = 3x - 2y=3x−2 into the second equation:

6x−2(3x−2)=4.6x - 2(3x - 2) = 4.6x−2(3x−2)=4.

Distribute carefully, watching the sign on the −2-2−2:

6x−6x+4=4,4=4.6x - 6x + 4 = 4, \qquad 4 = 4.6x−6x+4=4,4=4.

Every xxx has cancelled and the statement left over is always true. That means every pair on the first line already satisfies the second, so the system has infinitely many solutions. In fact the second equation is the first one in disguise: rearranging y=3x−2y = 3x - 2y=3x−2 gives 3x−y=23x - y = 23x−y=2, and doubling that gives 6x−2y=46x - 2y = 46x−2y=4. The two equations describe the same line, so its every point, written (x, 3x−2)(x,\, 3x - 2)(x,3x−2), is a solution.

Check your understanding

Solving a system by substitution, you arrive at 0=70 = 70=7. What does this tell you?

Answer choices

When the variable cancels and leaves a false numerical statement, no pair can satisfy both equations at once.

0=7 is false  ⇒  no solution0 = 7 \text{ is false} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{no solution}0=7 is false⇒no solution

The lines are parallel and never meet. Read it as no solution, not as x=0x = 0x=0.

Check your understanding

Solving a system by substitution, you arrive at 0=00 = 00=0. What does this tell you?

Answer choices

When the variable cancels and leaves a statement that is always true, every pair on the first line also satisfies the second.

0=0 is always true  ⇒  infinitely many solutions0 = 0 \text{ is always true} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{infinitely many solutions}0=0 is always true⇒infinitely many solutions

The two equations describe the same line. Contrast this with 0=70 = 70=7: a false statement means no solution, an always-true one means infinitely many.

Seeing the answer as a crossing point

Substitution never draws a picture, but it is worth remembering that the pair it returns is exactly the point the two lines share. Below are the lines from the opening example, y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 and 2x+y=72x + y = 72x+y=7. Substitution replaced yyy in the second equation and returned (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3), and that is precisely where the two lines cross.

The algebra and the picture agree, which is the point. Substitution is just a way to compute the crossing point without the guesswork of reading it off a grid. The method also stays exact even when the answer is a fraction that no drawing could pin down.

Check your understanding

Substitution turns the system y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1 and x+y=8x + y = 8x+y=8 into the pair (3,5)(3, 5)(3,5). If both lines were graphed, what does the point (3,5)(3, 5)(3,5) represent?

Answer choices

A system's solution is not just a pair of numbers to report; it is the one point that lies on both lines at once, so on the graph it is exactly where the two lines cross. A point on only one line would satisfy just one equation, not both.

3+5=8 ✓,5=2(3)−1 ✓3 + 5 = 8 \ \checkmark, \qquad 5 = 2(3) - 1 \ \checkmark3+5=8 ✓,5=2(3)−1 ✓

The figure below opens on that same system, y=x+1y = x + 1y=x+1 as the solid line and y=−2x+7y = -2x + 7y=−2x+7 as the dashed one. The figure reports the crossing point, when there is one, as you move either line. Substitution and the picture are answering the same question, so the coordinates under the figure should always be the pair substitution would return.

Use it to make the two special cases from earlier in this lesson concrete, because they can look like nothing on paper. Set Rise B and Run B to match Rise A and Run A, a rise of 111 over a run of 111. Now the dashed line has the same slope as the solid one, and the dot disappears. The lines are parallel, which is what a false statement like 7=17 = 17=1 was telling you. Next also lower Crosses B until it matches Crosses A, at 111. The dashed line now sits exactly on top of the solid one. The sentence changes to say every point is a solution, which is what an always-true statement like 4=44 = 44=4 was telling you. Look closely at what changed. The parallel case still shows two separate lines side by side. The matching case shows only one, because the dashed line has landed exactly on the solid one.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Stopping after one variable. Finding x=2x = 2x=2 is only half the answer. Back-substitute to get yyy, then state the full pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) and check it. A system’s solution is a pair, not a single number.
  • Substituting back into the same equation. If you solve the first equation for yyy and then plug that expression into the first equation again, every term cancels. You are left with something like 6=66 = 66=6, true but useless. Always substitute into the other equation.
  • Dropping the parentheses. When you replace yyy with 2x+12x + 12x+1 in 3x−y=53x - y = 53x−y=5, wrap it up: 3x−(2x+1)=53x - (2x + 1) = 53x−(2x+1)=5. The minus sign must reach both terms, giving 3x−2x−1=53x - 2x - 1 = 53x−2x−1=5. Without the parentheses you would wrongly write 3x−2x+13x - 2x + 13x−2x+1 and flip a sign.
  • Sign slips when isolating a variable. Solving 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6 for yyy gives y=6−2xy = 6 - 2xy=6−2x, not y=6+2xy = 6 + 2xy=6+2x. A term that crosses the equals sign changes sign.
  • Misreading a vanished variable. If substitution ends in 0=50 = 50=5, the answer is “no solution”, and if it ends in 0=00 = 00=0, the answer is “infinitely many solutions”. Neither one means x=0x = 0x=0.

Key takeaways

  • Substitution turns a two-variable system into a one-variable equation by replacing a variable with an equal expression, resting on the rule that equals may replace equals.
  • The steps: solve one equation for a variable (choose a coefficient of 111 or −1-1−1 to avoid fractions). Then substitute into the other equation, solve, back-substitute, and check the pair in both originals.
  • The pair you find is the exact point where the two lines cross, obtained without any graphing.
  • If the variable cancels and leaves a false statement like 1=41 = 41=4, the lines are parallel and there is no solution. If it leaves an always-true statement like 4=44 = 44=4, the equations are the same line and there are infinitely many solutions.
  • Never call a system done until you have both coordinates and have verified them in each equation.

A bit of history (optional)

For most of its history, algebra was written out in sentences. No letters stood for numbers. A writer called the unknown “the thing”, and gave each step of a solution as a line of prose. Take the thing, add five to it, and so on. Historians call this rhetorical algebra.

Now try this lesson’s method in that style. Replacing one unknown by an expression equal to it meant rewriting an entire paragraph. Then you had to read the new paragraph closely to see what had changed. The move you now make in one line was a page of careful writing.

Symbols arrived in stages, and one important piece was a decision about which letters to use. Rene Descartes, a French philosopher and mathematician, popularized this one in a book published in 163716371637. Letters from the front of the alphabet, aaa, bbb and ccc, would stand for quantities already known. Letters from the back, xxx, yyy and zzz, would stand for the quantities being hunted. That convention has held ever since, which is why the systems in this lesson are written in xxx and yyy.

The choice did more than tidy the page. Once an unknown was a single symbol, you could lift it out of one equation and set it into another. Substitution stopped being a paragraph of prose. It became one line of algebra, short enough to check at a glance.

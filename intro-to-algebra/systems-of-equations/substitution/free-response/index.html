    1. Two unknowns, and the letter that leaves first . Foundational, 7 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 7 pts

    Neither equation of a system settles anything on its own, and an equation with two letters in it cannot be marched to an answer. Substitution gets rid of one letter so that the ordinary one-unknown solving can start. Here the system is y=4−2xy = 4 - 2xy=4−2x together with 3x+2y=53x + 2y = 53x+2y=5, and one of the two equations is already reporting what yyy is.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Replace yyy in the equation that still contains both letters, writing that substitution down before simplifying it. Then simplify what you wrote until only one term in xxx is left.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Solve the equation you produced, recover the other coordinate, and test the finished pair in both of the original equations.

      Carry your own answer forward Carry on from the one-variable equation you wrote in part A, whatever it came to. The marks here are for recovering the second coordinate from the isolation and for testing the finished pair in both original equations, not for landing on a particular pair.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 2 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The equation you solved in part A had only xxx in it, so a pair of equations had become one. Say what became of the equation y=4−2xy = 4 - 2xy=4−2x in the course of the work: whether its content was spent, set aside, or used more than once, and point to where each use appears.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 2 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing here needs a method you have not met. The only genuinely new move is writing an expression in the place where a letter used to stand, and everything after that is one-unknown equation solving.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      A single number is not a solution of a system, since the system asked which PAIR makes both statements true. Once one coordinate is known, the equation that was already solved for the other letter delivers it in one evaluation.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Count how many times the already-solved equation gets touched between the first line of the work and the finished pair. It is more than once, and the occasions do different jobs.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Substituting gives 3x+2(4−2x)=53x + 2(4 - 2x) = 53x+2(4−2x)=5, which collects to −x+8=5-x + 8 = 5−x+8=5, that is −x=−3-x = -3−x=−3.

    • 8−x=58 - x = 58−x=5 and x−8=−5x - 8 = -5x−8=−5 are the same line collected differently; what matters is that the 222 reached both terms of the bracket

    Part B

    (3,−2)(3, -2)(3,−2). It fits both originals: 4−2(3)=−24 - 2(3) = -24−2(3)=−2 and 3(3)+2(−2)=53(3) + 2(-2) = 53(3)+2(−2)=5.

    Part C

    It was used twice and set aside at neither stage. First to erase yyy from the other equation, which is what left a single unknown standing, and then again to turn the value of xxx into the value of yyy. The equation consumed by the substitution is the other one.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The equation y=4−2xy = 4 - 2xy=4−2x says that yyy and 4−2x4 - 2x4−2x are two names for one number, so wherever yyy stands, 4−2x4 - 2x4−2x may stand instead. The place worth doing that is the equation that still has both letters in it:

    3x+2y=5 ⟶ 3x+2(4−2x)=5.3x + 2y = 5 \ \longrightarrow \ 3x + 2(4 - 2x) = 5.3x+2y=5 ⟶ 3x+2(4−2x)=5.

    Keep the bracket. The coefficient 222 multiplies the whole expression that arrived, not just its first term, and dropping the bracket would quietly leave the −2x-2x−2x unmultiplied. Distribute and collect:

    3x+8−4x=5,−x+8=5,−x=−3.3x + 8 - 4x = 5, \qquad -x + 8 = 5, \qquad -x = -3.3x+8−4x=5,−x+8=5,−x=−3.

    The letter yyy has gone, and what is left is an equation of the kind you could already solve before this lesson began.

    Part B

    Divide both sides of −x=−3-x = -3−x=−3 by −1-1−1:

    x=3.x = 3.x=3.

    A number is not yet a solution of a system, because the system asked for a pair. Feed the value back into the equation that was already solved for yyy:

    y=4−2(3)=−2.y = 4 - 2(3) = -2.y=4−2(3)=−2.

    Now test the candidate (3,−2)(3, -2)(3,−2) in the two equations as they were given, not in anything derived along the way:

    4−2(3)=−2,3(3)+2(−2)=9−4=5.4 - 2(3) = -2, \qquad 3(3) + 2(-2) = 9 - 4 = 5.4−2(3)=−2,3(3)+2(−2)=9−4=5.

    Both hold, so (3,−2)(3, -2)(3,−2) is the solution.

    Part C

    Follow the isolation through the work and count the places it does something.

    Its first job is the substitution itself. Because yyy and 4−2x4 - 2x4−2x are the same number, writing one where the other stood turns

    3x+2y=5into3x+2(4−2x)=5,3x + 2y = 5 \quad \text{into} \quad 3x + 2(4 - 2x) = 5,3x+2y=5into3x+2(4−2x)=5,

    and it is this exchange, not any solving, that removes a letter. The equation that gets used up here is 3x+2y=53x + 2y = 53x+2y=5: after the exchange it has become a statement about xxx alone.

    Its second job comes after the value of xxx is known. The isolation is still a true statement about the solution, so it converts that value into the other coordinate:

    y=4−2(3)=−2.y = 4 - 2(3) = -2.y=4−2(3)=−2.

    So the two equations are not treated alike. One is transformed into a one-unknown equation and is finished with; the other survives the whole calculation as a rule for producing yyy from xxx, which is exactly why substituting into it, rather than into its partner, would make no progress at all.

    In one line

    Substituting gives 3x+2(4−2x)=53x + 2(4 - 2x) = 53x+2(4−2x)=5, which collects to −x=−3-x = -3−x=−3, so x=3x = 3x=3 and y=4−2(3)=−2y = 4 - 2(3) = -2y=4−2(3)=−2: the solution is (3,−2)(3, -2)(3,−2), and it fits both original equations. The isolation y=4−2xy = 4 - 2xy=4−2x is used twice, once to remove yyy from the other equation and once afterwards to produce the second coordinate.

    2. Reading the four coefficients before solving anything . Foundational, 10 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    A system of two equations offers four possible openings, one for each letter in each equation. All four describe the same system, so none of them can change what the answer turns out to be. What they do not share is how much arithmetic they cost, and that is decided by the coefficients before a single line is written.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Take the system 4x+3y=184x + 3y = 184x+3y=18 together with x+5y=13x + 5y = 13x+5y=13. Without solving anything, name the one isolation that brings in no fraction at that step, and state the feature of the coefficients you read in order to decide.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Carry out the isolation you named, finish the system, and test the pair in both equations.

      Carry your own answer forward Use the isolation you named in part A. If you would now choose a different one, say so and work with that instead: the marks here are for substituting into the equation the expression did not come from and finishing the work cleanly.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Now read the system 2x+3y=172x + 3y = 172x+3y=17 together with 5x−2y=145x - 2y = 145x−2y=14 the same way. No opening there is free of fractions, so say which are the cheapest and what makes them cheapest, carry one of them through to the pair, and say whether the fractions met on the way could have changed the pair you arrive at.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Look at the four numbers standing in front of the letters before committing to anything. One kind of coefficient makes the opening move a subtraction, and every other kind makes it a division.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      The expression goes into the equation it did not come from, brackets and all. What comes out is an ordinary one-unknown equation, and the isolation is still waiting to hand you the other coordinate.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      When every opening divides by something, the useful question is what it divides BY. Compare the divisors, then remember that an equation carrying a denominator can be multiplied through to clear it before anything else happens.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Isolate xxx in x+5y=13x + 5y = 13x+5y=13, giving x=13−5yx = 13 - 5yx=13−5y. It is the only letter in the system whose coefficient is 111 or −1-1−1, so isolating it takes a subtraction alone and never a division.

    Part B

    (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2). Both hold: 4(3)+3(2)=184(3) + 3(2) = 184(3)+3(2)=18 and 3+5(2)=133 + 5(2) = 133+5(2)=13.

    Part C

    Two of the four openings divide by 222, the smallest divisor on offer, so either is cheapest; taking yyy from 5x−2y=145x - 2y = 145x−2y=14 leads to (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3). The fractions belong to the route and not to the answer: every route replaces a quantity by an equal one, so all four end at the same pair.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    List the four openings and ask what each one would have to divide by.

    In 4x+3y=184x + 3y = 184x+3y=18, isolating xxx divides by 444 and isolating yyy divides by 333. In x+5y=13x + 5y = 13x+5y=13, isolating yyy divides by 555. The fourth is different:

    x+5y=13 ⟶ x=13−5y.x + 5y = 13 \ \longrightarrow \ x = 13 - 5y.x+5y=13 ⟶ x=13−5y.

    The letter xxx already stands alone in that equation apart from what is added to it, so getting it by itself takes one subtraction and no division at all. That is the feature to scan for: a coefficient of 111 or −1-1−1 on some letter in some equation. When one exists, isolating that letter keeps every number in the next line a whole number.

    Part B

    The isolated expression goes into the equation it did not come from:

    4(13−5y)+3y=18.4(13 - 5y) + 3y = 18.4(13−5y)+3y=18.

    Distribute and collect, and notice that every number on the way is whole:

    52−20y+3y=18,−17y=−34,y=2.52 - 20y + 3y = 18, \qquad -17y = -34, \qquad y = 2.52−20y+3y=18,−17y=−34,y=2.

    Back into the isolation for the other coordinate:

    x=13−5(2)=3.x = 13 - 5(2) = 3.x=13−5(2)=3.

    Test (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) in the two equations as given:

    4(3)+3(2)=12+6=18,3+5(2)=13.4(3) + 3(2) = 12 + 6 = 18, \qquad 3 + 5(2) = 13.4(3)+3(2)=12+6=18,3+5(2)=13.

    Part C

    Scan the four openings for their divisors first. Isolating xxx in 2x+3y=172x + 3y = 172x+3y=17 divides by 222; isolating yyy there divides by 333; isolating xxx in 5x−2y=145x - 2y = 145x−2y=14 divides by 555; isolating yyy there divides by 222. So no opening is free, and two of them are tied for the smallest divisor.

    Take yyy from 5x−2y=145x - 2y = 145x−2y=14:

    2y=5x−14,y=5x−142.2y = 5x - 14, \qquad y = \frac{5x - 14}{2}.2y=5x−14,y=25x−14​.

    Substitute into the other equation and clear the denominator at once, by multiplying every term by 222:

    2x+3⋅5x−142=17,4x+3(5x−14)=34.2x + 3 \cdot \frac{5x - 14}{2} = 17, \qquad 4x + 3(5x - 14) = 34.2x+3⋅25x−14​=17,4x+3(5x−14)=34.

    Now the arithmetic is whole again:

    4x+15x−42=34,19x=76,x=4,4x + 15x - 42 = 34, \qquad 19x = 76, \qquad x = 4,4x+15x−42=34,19x=76,x=4,

    and the isolation returns the other coordinate, y=5(4)−142=3y = \frac{5(4) - 14}{2} = 3y=25(4)−14​=3. Test (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3) in the equations as given: 2(4)+3(3)=172(4) + 3(3) = 172(4)+3(3)=17 and 5(4)−2(3)=145(4) - 2(3) = 145(4)−2(3)=14.

    The last question is the one worth thinking about. Every opening does the same thing: it writes one quantity in place of another quantity equal to it, which cannot change which pairs make the original two statements true. So the choice sets how heavy the arithmetic is and nothing else. Notice that the answer here is a pair of whole numbers even though the route was full of halves: the fractions were a property of the path, not of the solution.

    In one line

    In 4x+3y=184x + 3y = 184x+3y=18 and x+5y=13x + 5y = 13x+5y=13 the only fraction-free opening is x=13−5yx = 13 - 5yx=13−5y, and it gives (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2). The choice of opening sets the arithmetic, never the answer.

    Another way: Substitute a multiple of the letter and never write a fraction

    When no coefficient is 111 or −1-1−1, the fraction can be avoided altogether by substituting for a MULTIPLE of a letter rather than for the letter itself. From 5x−2y=145x - 2y = 145x−2y=14, without dividing anything,

    2y=5x−14.2y = 5x - 14.2y=5x−14.

    The other equation carries 3y3y3y, so double it first to make a 6y6y6y appear, which is three lots of 2y2y2y:

    2x+3y=17 ⟶ 4x+6y=34,4x+3(5x−14)=34.2x + 3y = 17 \ \longrightarrow \ 4x + 6y = 34, \qquad 4x + 3(5x - 14) = 34.2x+3y=17 ⟶ 4x+6y=34,4x+3(5x−14)=34.

    That is the same line reached above, arrived at without a denominator ever being written.

    When it is worth it When every isolation would divide, and you would rather multiply a whole equation once than carry a fraction through several lines. It costs one extra multiplication and buys whole numbers throughout.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Read the line as a record of an exchange. The bracket is what took the place of a letter, and the isolation was for yyy, so one of the original equations must have been equivalent to

    y=2x−5.y = 2x - 5.y=2x−5.

    Put in the requested form, that is 2x−y=52x - y = 52x−y=5.

    Everything outside the bracket belonged to the equation that received the expression. Restoring yyy where the bracket stands, and leaving its other terms untouched, gives

    4x+3y=25.4x + 3y = 25.4x+3y=25.

    So the system was 2x−y=52x - y = 52x−y=5 together with 4x+3y=254x + 3y = 254x+3y=25, and the recorded line is what the second becomes once the first has been used on it.

    Part B

    Expand the bracket and collect:

    4x+6x−15=25,10x=40,x=4.4x + 6x - 15 = 25, \qquad 10x = 40, \qquad x = 4.4x+6x−15=25,10x=40,x=4.

    The second coordinate comes from the isolation the bracket recorded, not from a fresh calculation:

    y=2(4)−5=3.y = 2(4) - 5 = 3.y=2(4)−5=3.

    Test (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3) in both recovered equations:

    The answer

    The pair fits 3x+2y=103x + 2y = 103x+2y=10 but gives 47\frac{4}{7}74​ in 5x−y=85x - y = 85x−y=8; the faulty line is the isolation, which should read y=5x−8y = 5x - 8y=5x−8, and the solution is (2,2)(2, 2)(2,2).

    Test the pair as given. In 3x+2y=103x + 2y = 103x+2y=10:

    3⋅67+2⋅267=187+527=707=10,3 \cdot \frac{6}{7} + 2 \cdot \frac{26}{7} = \frac{18}{7} + \frac{52}{7} = \frac{70}{7} = 10,3⋅76​+2⋅726​=718​+752​=770​=10,

    which holds. In 5x−y=85x - y = 85x−y=8:

    5⋅67−267=307−267=47,5 \cdot \frac{6}{7} - \frac{26}{7} = \frac{30}{7} - \frac{26}{7} = \frac{4}{7},5⋅76​−726​=730​−726​=74​,

    1. Cancel on sight . Foundational, 9 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 9 pts

    Some systems arrive ready to be combined: one variable's coefficients are already opposites, or already equal, so a single addition or subtraction removes it and no multiplying is needed. Handle one system of each kind, then say what in the coefficients forced the choice of operation.

    2. B

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Work one column at a time. Combining two equations does arithmetic on the two coefficients standing in that column, and the variable leaves only if that arithmetic lands on zero.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

    The answer

    Part A

    (x,y)=(4,−3)(x, y) = (4, -3)(x,y)=(4,−3). Both originals hold: 2(4)+5(−3)=−72(4) + 5(-3) = -72(4)+5(−3)=−7 and 3(4)−5(−3)=273(4) - 5(-3) = 273(4)−5(−3)=27.

    Part B

    (x,y)=(−2,3)(x, y) = (-2, 3)(x,y)=(−2,3). Both originals hold: 4(−2)+3(3)=14(-2) + 3(3) = 14(−2)+3(3)=1 and 4(−2)+7(3)=134(-2) + 7(3) = 134(−2)+7(3)=13.

    Part C

    Adding removes a variable exactly when its two coefficients are opposites, since they must sum to zero; subtracting removes it exactly when they are equal, since they must differ by zero. If neither holds, multiply one or both equations by a nonzero constant first to create one of those two situations.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Look down the yyy column. The coefficients are +5+5+5 and −5-5−5, opposites, so adding the two equations sends those terms to zero:

    (2x+5y)+(3x−5y)=−7+27.(2x + 5y) + (3x - 5y) = -7 + 27.(2x+5y)+(3x−5y)=−7+27.

    What survives has a single unknown:

    5x=20,x=4.5x = 20, \qquad x = 4.5x=20,x=4.

    Back-substitute into 2x+5y=−72x + 5y = -72x+5y=−7:

    8+5y=−7,5y=−15,y=−3.8 + 5y = -7, \qquad 5y = -15, \qquad y = -3.8+5y=−7,5y=−15,y=−3.

    The test has to include the equation you did NOT back-substitute into, because that is the one your value of yyy has never been measured against: 3(4)−5(−3)=12+15=273(4) - 5(-3) = 12 + 15 = 273(4)−5(−3)=12+15=27, and 2(4)+5(−3)=8−15=−72(4) + 5(-3) = 8 - 15 = -72(4)+5(−3)=8−15=−7.     Part B

    This time the xxx column holds +4x+4x+4x in both equations. They are equal, not opposite, so adding would produce 8x8x8x and remove nothing. Subtracting is what cancels them.     (4x+7y)−(4x+3y)=13−1,4y=12,y=3.(4x + 7y) - (4x + 3y) = 13 - 1, \qquad 4y = 12, \qquad y = 3.(4x+7y)−(4x+3y)=13−1,4y=12,y=3.

    Part C

    Combining the equations does arithmetic on the two coefficients sitting in a column, so the variable leaves exactly when that arithmetic lands on zero. Write the two coefficients as b1b_1b1​ and b2b_2b2​.

    Adding produces the coefficient b1+b2b_1 + b_2b1​+b2​, so

    b1+b2=0  ⟺  b2=−b1.b_1 + b_2 = 0 \iff b_2 = -b_1.b1​+b2​=0⟺b2​=−b1​.

    Read left to right, opposite coefficients make adding work. Read right to left, if adding worked then the coefficients summed to zero, so they were opposites and nothing weaker would have done. The test holds both ways.

    Subtracting produces b1−b2b_1 - b_2b1​−b2​, so

    b1−b2=0  ⟺  b2=b1,b_1 - b_2 = 0 \iff b_2 = b_1,b1​−b2​=0⟺b2​=b1​,

    and by the same two readings, subtracting removes a variable exactly when its coefficients are equal.

    That is what decided both systems above. One offered a column of +5+5+5 and −5-5−5, opposites, so adding was the move; the other offered a column of 444 and 444, equal, so subtracting was.

    A system offering neither is not stuck. Multiplying an equation through by a nonzero constant rescales its coefficients without changing which pairs satisfy it, so you can manufacture an opposite pair or an equal pair and then combine.

    In one line

    2x+5y=−72x + 5y = -72x+5y=−7 with 3x−5y=273x - 5y = 273x−5y=27 gives (4,−3)(4, -3)(4,−3), reached by adding, because that system's yyy coefficients are opposites; 4x+3y=14x + 3y = 14x+3y=1 with 4x+7y=134x + 7y = 134x+7y=13 gives (−2,3)(-2, 3)(−2,3), reached by subtracting, because its xxx coefficients are equal. Adding removes a variable exactly when its coefficients sum to zero and subtracting exactly when they are equal, and when neither holds you first multiply an equation through by a nonzero constant to create one of those two situations.

    Another way: Turn every subtraction into an addition

    Subtracting an equation is where signs get lost, because the minus sign has to reach every term inside it. You can retire that risk by multiplying the equation through by −1-1−1 first and then adding. For 4x+3y=14x + 3y = 14x+3y=1 and 4x+7y=134x + 7y = 134x+7y=13, scale the first by −1-1−1:

    −4x−3y=−1.-4x - 3y = -1.−4x−3y=−1.

    The xxx coefficients are now −4-4−4 and 444, opposites, and adding gives 4y=124y = 124y=12 with no subtraction anywhere in the work.

    The two routes are the same arithmetic. The difference is that the sign flip happens once, on a line of its own, where you can see it.

    When it is worth it Whenever the matched coefficients are equal rather than opposite, and especially when the equation you would otherwise subtract already carries negative terms.

    What this shows is that the relationship runs one way only. Every solution of the system satisfies the combination, as the previous part confirmed, but a pair satisfying the combination need not solve the system. A single combined equation is therefore weaker than the system it was built from: information has been thrown away.

    That is exactly why elimination does not replace the system with one equation. It replaces one of the two and keeps the other, so the pair of equations together still pins down the same solutions.

    Part C

    Put the hypothesis into usable form first. Saying that (x0,y0)(x_0, y_0)(x0​,y0​) satisfies both equations means the two statements

    a1x0+b1y0=c1,a2x0+b2y0=c2a_1x_0 + b_1y_0 = c_1, \qquad a_2x_0 + b_2y_0 = c_2a1​x0​+b1​y0​=c1​,a2​x0​+b2​y0​=c2​

    m(a1x0+b1y0)=mc1,n(a2x0+b2y0)=nc2,m(a_1x_0 + b_1y_0) = mc_1, \qquad n(a_2x_0 + b_2y_0) = nc_2,m(a1​x0​+b1​y0​)=mc1​,n(a2​x0​+b2​y0​)=nc2​,

    whatever numbers mmm and nnn are, negative ones and zero included.

    Now the addition property of equality: adding equals to equals leaves equals. Adding the left sides of those two true statements and the right sides of them:

    m(a1x0+b1y0)+n(a2x0+b2y0)=mc1+nc2.m(a_1x_0 + b_1y_0) + n(a_2x_0 + b_2y_0) = mc_1 + nc_2.m(a1​x0​+b1​y0​)+n(a2​x0​+b2​y0​)=mc1​+nc2​.

    Another way: Undo the trade on numbers before trusting it in letters

    The claim that the trade can be undone is easier to believe once you have undone one.     (5x+6y)−3(x+3y)=26−3(7),2x−3y=5.(5x + 6y) - 3(x + 3y) = 26 - 3(7), \qquad 2x - 3y = 5.(5x+6y)−3(x+3y)=26−3(7),2x−3y=5.

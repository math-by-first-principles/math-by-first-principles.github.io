Solving Systems by Elimination

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Substitution removes a variable by replacing it with an equal expression. Elimination removes one by combining equations. Adding two true equations gives another true equation: equal quantities added to equal quantities give equal totals. If one equation contains +y+y+y and the other −y-y−y, adding them cancels yyy and leaves a one-variable equation you already know how to solve. When the coefficients do not match, you can first multiply an equation by a nonzero constant to arrange the cancellation.

Learning goals

  • Add or subtract equations so one variable cancels
  • Scale one or both equations to match the coefficients
  • Justify the method from the properties of equality
  • Back-substitute for the second variable, then check both
  • Distinguish no solution from infinitely many by reading a false or always-true leftover
  • Prefer elimination in standard form, substitution when one is isolated

Adding equations to cancel a variable

A system of two linear equations asks for the pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that satisfies both equations at the same time. Elimination finds it by combining the two equations so that one variable cancels. Start with a system whose coefficients are already arranged for it:

x+y=5,x−y=1.\begin{gathered} x + y = 5, \\ x - y = 1. \end{gathered}x+y=5,x−y=1.​

The yyy terms, +y+y+y and −y-y−y, are opposites. Add the left sides and add the right sides:

(x+y)+(x−y)=5+1.(x + y) + (x - y) = 5 + 1.(x+y)+(x−y)=5+1.

The yyy terms sum to zero, leaving only xxx:

2x=6,x=3.\begin{gathered} 2x = 6, \\ x = 3. \end{gathered}2x=6,x=3.​

Recover the other variable by putting x=3x = 3x=3 back into either original equation, say x+y=5x + y = 5x+y=5:

3+y=5,y=2.\begin{gathered} 3 + y = 5, \\ y = 2. \end{gathered}3+y=5,y=2.​

Check (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) in both equations, as with substitution: 3+2=53 + 2 = 53+2=5 and 3−2=13 - 2 = 13−2=1. Both are true, so (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) solves the system.

Adding cancels a variable whenever its coefficients are opposites, because opposite numbers sum to zero. If the coefficients are equal, subtracting one equation from the other cancels it instead. The remaining question is what to do when the coefficients are neither opposite nor equal.

The elimination method step by step

  1. Line up the equations in standard form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c, so like terms sit in columns.
  2. Match a variable’s coefficients. Pick the variable to eliminate, then multiply one or both equations by nonzero constants so its coefficients become opposites (to add) or equal (to subtract).
  3. Add or subtract the equations to remove that variable, leaving one equation in one unknown.
  4. Solve the one-variable equation.
  5. Back-substitute the value into an original equation to find the other variable.
  6. Check the pair in both original equations.

If both variables cancel in step 3, read the leftover statement as described later in the lesson. If a variable is already absent from one equation, solve that one-variable equation directly.

Worked example 1 Solve 2x+y=72x + y = 72x+y=7 and 3x−y=83x - y = 83x−y=8

The first equation has +y+y+y and the second has −y-y−y, so step 2 needs no multiplying. Adding the equations eliminates yyy:

(2x+y)+(3x−y)=7+8.(2x + y) + (3x - y) = 7 + 8.(2x+y)+(3x−y)=7+8.

The yyy terms cancel and the xxx terms combine:

5x=15,x=3.\begin{gathered} 5x = 15, \\ x = 3. \end{gathered}5x=15,x=3.​

Back-substitute x=3x = 3x=3 into an original equation. Using 2x+y=72x + y = 72x+y=7:

2(3)+y=7,6+y=7,y=1.\begin{gathered} 2(3) + y = 7, \\ 6 + y = 7, \\ y = 1. \end{gathered}2(3)+y=7,6+y=7,y=1.​

So the candidate is (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1). Check it in both equations, not just the one you used:

2(3)+1=7 ✓,3(3)−1=8 ✓.\begin{gathered} 2(3) + 1 = 7 \ \checkmark, \\ 3(3) - 1 = 8 \ \checkmark. \end{gathered}2(3)+1=7 ✓,3(3)−1=8 ✓.​

Both hold, so the solution is (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1).

Stacking the equations shows the whole method: like terms sit in columns, and a column of opposite terms adds to zero (a column of equal terms cancels on subtracting). When that column is not already in place, multiply one or both equations first to create it.

Why adding equations keeps every solution

The combined equation alone is usually not enough: it gives one coordinate, and a kept original equation recovers the other. Together, the kept equation and the combined equation have exactly the same solutions as the original pair.

Why combining the equations preserves the solution set#

Take x+2y=10x+2y=10x+2y=10 and 2x−y=52x-y=52x−y=5. The pair (4,3)(4,3)(4,3) satisfies both: 4+2(3)=104+2(3)=104+2(3)=10 and 2(4)−3=52(4)-3=52(4)−3=5. Keep the first equation and replace the second by the first plus twice the second:

(x+2y)+2(2x−y)=10+2(5),5x=20.\begin{gathered} (x+2y)+2(2x-y)=10+2(5),\\ 5x=20. \end{gathered}(x+2y)+2(2x−y)=10+2(5),5x=20.​

The original solution (4,3)(4,3)(4,3) satisfies the combination too: 5(4)=205(4)=205(4)=20. To reverse the move, subtract the kept equation, then divide by 222:

5x−(x+2y)=20−10,4x−2y=10,2x−y=5.\begin{gathered} 5x-(x+2y)=20-10,\\ 4x-2y=10,\\ 2x-y=5. \end{gathered}5x−(x+2y)=20−10,4x−2y=10,2x−y=5.​

That recovers the second original. The new system gives x=4x=4x=4, then y=3y=3y=3, the same pair.

For the general move, keep the first equation and replace the second by mmm times the first plus nnn times the second, with n≠0n\neq0n=0. A pair satisfying the originals satisfies the combination, by the multiplication property of equality and the addition property of equality: adding equal quantities to equal quantities gives equal totals. If L1=R1L_1=R_1L1​=R1​ and L2=R2L_2=R_2L2​=R2​, then

L1+L2=R1+R2.L_1+L_2=R_1+R_2.L1​+L2​=R1​+R2​.

To reverse the move, subtract mmm times the kept equation from the combination, then divide by nnn. That recovers the second equation, so every pair satisfying the new system also satisfies both originals. Nothing depended on the numbers in the example; the nonzero multiplier nnn is what makes the reversal possible. Multiplying by zero would erase the replaced equation’s information.

So the new system has lost no solutions and gained none. The same check works for any replacement: if each new equation is a combination of the originals and each original can be rebuilt from the new ones, the two systems have the same solutions. Systems with exactly the same solutions are called equivalent systems.

∎

Matching the coefficients: multiply, then eliminate

Most systems do not hand you opposite coefficients, so you create them. Multiplying an entire equation by a nonzero constant gives an equation with the same solutions, by the multiplication property of equality, and with rescaled coefficients you can aim at a match.

If one coefficient of a variable is a multiple of the other, multiplying just one equation lines them up. When neither nonzero integer coefficient divides the other, multiply both equations to reach a common multiple of their absolute values; the least common multiple keeps the numbers small. Give the matched coefficients opposite signs and add, or equal signs and subtract.

Worked example 2 Multiply one equation: solve x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11 and 3x−y=53x - y = 53x−y=5

Eliminate yyy. Doubling the second equation turns its −y-y−y into −2y-2y−2y, the opposite of the +2y+2y+2y in the first. Multiply every term of 3x−y=53x - y = 53x−y=5 by 222:

6x−2y=10.6x - 2y = 10.6x−2y=10.

Now add this to the first equation, x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11:

(x+2y)+(6x−2y)=11+10.(x + 2y) + (6x - 2y) = 11 + 10.(x+2y)+(6x−2y)=11+10.

The yyy terms cancel:

7x=21,x=3.\begin{gathered} 7x = 21, \\ x = 3. \end{gathered}7x=21,x=3.​

Back-substitute x=3x = 3x=3 into x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11:

3+2y=11,2y=8,y=4.\begin{gathered} 3 + 2y = 11, \\ 2y = 8, \\ y = 4. \end{gathered}3+2y=11,2y=8,y=4.​

The solution is (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4). Check the second equation too: 3(3)−4=53(3) - 4 = 53(3)−4=5. Only one equation needed multiplying, because doubling the yyy coefficient −1-1−1 gives −2-2−2, the opposite of the first equation’s 222.

Worked example 3 Multiply both equations: solve 2x+3y=72x + 3y = 72x+3y=7 and 3x+2y=83x + 2y = 83x+2y=8

Eliminate xxx. Its coefficients are 222 and 333, and neither divides the other, so scale both to their least common multiple, 666. Multiply the first equation by 333 and the second by 222:

6x+9y=21,6x+4y=16.\begin{gathered} 6x + 9y = 21, \\ 6x + 4y = 16. \end{gathered}6x+9y=21,6x+4y=16.​

Both now carry 6x6x6x. The coefficients are equal, so subtract the second equation from the first, flipping every sign in the second:

(6x+9y)−(6x+4y)=21−16,5y=5,y=1.\begin{gathered} (6x + 9y) - (6x + 4y) = 21 - 16, \\ 5y = 5, \\ y = 1. \end{gathered}(6x+9y)−(6x+4y)=21−16,5y=5,y=1.​

Back-substitute y=1y = 1y=1 into 2x+3y=72x + 3y = 72x+3y=7:

2x+3(1)=7,2x=4,x=2.\begin{gathered} 2x + 3(1) = 7, \\ 2x = 4, \\ x = 2. \end{gathered}2x+3(1)=7,2x=4,x=2.​

The solution is (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1), and 3(2)+2(1)=83(2) + 2(1) = 83(2)+2(1)=8 confirms the second equation. Multiplying the second equation by −2-2−2 instead, to get −6x−4y=−16-6x - 4y = -16−6x−4y=−16, and adding gives the same result.

When a variable cancels to a number: no solution and infinitely many

Sometimes both variables cancel together and leave a bare numerical statement. As with substitution, that statement tells you how the two lines lie.

Worked example 4 A system with no solution

Solve

2x+y=5,4x+2y=3.\begin{gathered} 2x + y = 5, \\ 4x + 2y = 3. \end{gathered}2x+y=5,4x+2y=3.​

Eliminate xxx. Multiply the first equation by −2-2−2 so its 2x2x2x becomes −4x-4x−4x, opposite the 4x4x4x below:

−4x−2y=−10.-4x - 2y = -10.−4x−2y=−10.

Add this to 4x+2y=34x + 2y = 34x+2y=3:

(−4x−2y)+(4x+2y)=−10+3.(-4x - 2y) + (4x + 2y) = -10 + 3.(−4x−2y)+(4x+2y)=−10+3.

Both variables cancel at once, leaving

0=−7.0 = -7.0=−7.

This is false for every xxx and yyy, so the system has no solution. The two lines have the same steepness but different heights, so they are parallel and never meet.

Worked example 5 A system with infinitely many solutions

Solve

x−3y=2,2x−6y=4.\begin{gathered} x - 3y = 2, \\ 2x - 6y = 4. \end{gathered}x−3y=2,2x−6y=4.​

Eliminate xxx by doubling the first equation so its xxx becomes 2x2x2x:

2x−6y=4.2x - 6y = 4.2x−6y=4.

This is now identical to the second equation, so subtracting them gives

0=0.0 = 0.0=0.

The leftover statement is always true. The second equation is just the first multiplied by 222, so every pair solving the first already solves the second: the two describe the same line, and the system has infinitely many solutions. Each point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) with x−3y=2x - 3y = 2x−3y=2 is a solution.

These examples give a quick coefficient test in standard form. If the second left side is a nonzero multiple of the first, elimination cancels both variables. The same multiple on the right side leaves 0=00=00=0 (one line); a different right side leaves a false statement (parallel lines). If the left sides are not multiples, elimination leaves a nonzero coefficient and exactly one solution pair (lines crossing once).

For example, take x+2y=5x+2y=5x+2y=5 and 3x+4y=113x+4y=113x+4y=11. Multiply the first equation by 333 to match the xxx coefficients, then subtract the second:

(3x+6y)−(3x+4y)=15−11,2y=4,y=2.\begin{gathered} (3x+6y)-(3x+4y)=15-11,\\ 2y=4,\qquad y=2. \end{gathered}(3x+6y)−(3x+4y)=15−11,2y=4,y=2.​

The kept first equation gives x+4=5x+4=5x+4=5, so x=1x=1x=1, and the pair checks: 1+2(2)=51+2(2)=51+2(2)=5 and 3(1)+4(2)=113(1)+4(2)=113(1)+4(2)=11. After matching xxx, the yyy coefficients 666 and 444 differ, and that difference is what fixes one value of yyy.

The same reasoning works for any two linear equations whose left sides are not multiples, each with at least one nonzero variable coefficient. When both xxx coefficients are nonzero, scale to match them; the yyy coefficients then differ, so subtraction leaves a nonzero coefficient of yyy, which fixes yyy, and the kept equation fixes xxx. If only one equation contains xxx, the other fixes yyy directly, then the first fixes xxx. If neither contains xxx, both left sides are multiples of yyy, the case already covered. In the nonmultiple case, these reversible steps give exactly one solution pair.

Substitution or elimination?

Both methods agree on the solutions. Choose the one that needs less rearranging.

  • Elimination is convenient when both equations are in standard form, especially when coefficients already match or are easy multiples.
  • Substitution is convenient when a variable is already isolated, as in y=3x−2y=3x-2y=3x−2, or has coefficient 111 or −1-1−1.

Check your understanding

For y=2x−1y=2x-1y=2x−1 and 3x+4y=183x+4y=183x+4y=18, which first step needs the least rearranging?

Answer choices

Substitution uses the expression already equal to yyy, so no rearrangement is needed.

3x+4(2x−1)=183x+4(2x-1)=183x+4(2x−1)=18

This gives 11x=2211x=2211x=22, hence x=2x=2x=2 and y=3y=3y=3. Elimination would work after putting both equations in standard form, but substitution uses the isolated variable immediately.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding when you should subtract, or the reverse. Adding cancels a variable only when its coefficients are opposites; subtracting cancels it only when they are equal. Check the signs before you combine.
  • Not multiplying every term on both sides. Multiplying x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11 by 222 gives 2x+4y=222x + 4y = 222x+4y=22, not 2x+4y=112x + 4y = 112x+4y=11. Scaling only one side, or forgetting the constant, changes the equation into a different one.
  • Sign slips when subtracting. Subtracting an equation flips every sign inside it. So (3x−y)−(x−y)(3x - y) - (x - y)(3x−y)−(x−y) is 2x2x2x, not 2x−2y2x - 2y2x−2y.
  • Misreading a vanished variable. If both variables cancel and leave 0=80 = 80=8, the answer is no solution; if they leave 0=00 = 00=0, it is infinitely many solutions. Neither one means x=0x = 0x=0.
  • Stopping at one variable. Back-substitute for the other, state the full pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), and check it in both equations.

Key takeaways

  • Elimination combines equations to cancel a variable. Keep an original equation for back-substitution.
  • Addition cancels opposite coefficients; subtraction cancels equal coefficients. Multiply every term by a nonzero constant first when needed. For integer coefficients, a least common multiple can help.
  • Solve for the surviving variable, back-substitute for the other, and check the pair in both originals.
  • Equivalent systems have exactly the same solutions. Keep one original beside the combined equation, with a nonzero multiplier on the equation being replaced, so the move is reversible: the originals can be rebuilt from the new system. More generally, replacing equations by combinations keeps the same solutions whenever every original can be rebuilt from the new equations.
  • No solution follows from a false leftover such as 0=80=80=8. Infinitely many solutions follows from 0=00=00=0 when reducing two linear equations in two variables.
  • Choose elimination for convenient matching coefficients and substitution for an isolated variable.

A bit of history (optional)

A farmer has three grades of grain. Three bundles of the best grade, two of the middle, and one of the poorest yield a known amount. Two other mixtures yield two other amounts. How much grain does one bundle of each grade contain?

This problem appears in The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, a Chinese handbook from roughly two thousand years ago. Its eighth chapter, Fangcheng, treats systems of equations. The numbers for each equation are arranged in a column. Scaling and subtracting columns removes unknowns one at a time, then working backward recovers their values. The bookkeeping differs from ours, but the cancellation is recognizable.

The modern name, Gaussian elimination, honors Carl Friedrich Gauss, who in the early 1800s used related calculations in astronomy, including work on the minor planet Pallas. He called it common elimination. His special notation for the repeated calculations helped attach his name to a technique whose roots were much older.

MacTutor describes the Chinese handbook. Joseph Grcar’s history of elimination traces how Gauss’s name became attached to the method.

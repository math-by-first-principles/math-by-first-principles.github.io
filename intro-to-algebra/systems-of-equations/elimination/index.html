Solving Systems by Elimination

Intuition

You just learned substitution, which clears a variable by trading it for an equal expression. Elimination reaches the same answer from a different angle, and it is often quicker. The idea is simple: two true equations can be added, and the sum is still true. Line the equations up so their like terms sit in columns. If one variable appears as +y+y+y in the first equation and −y-y−y in the second, adding the two makes that variable vanish on the spot. What is left has a single unknown, and you are back on familiar ground. When the coefficients are not already neat opposites, you first multiply an equation by a constant to arrange them, then add. That one move, combining equations to cancel a variable, is the whole method.

Learning goals

  • Add or subtract equations so one variable cancels
  • Scale one or both equations to match the coefficients
  • Justify the method from the properties of equality
  • Back-substitute for the second variable, then check both
  • Read 0=k0 = k0=k as no solution and 0=00 = 00=0 as infinitely many
  • Prefer elimination in standard form, substitution when one is isolated

Adding equations to cancel a variable

A system of two linear equations asks for the pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that satisfies both equations at the same time. Substitution found that pair by isolating a variable and replacing it. Elimination finds it by combining the two equations so that one variable cancels outright.

Start with a system whose coefficients are already arranged for it:

x+y=5,x−y=1.x + y = 5, \qquad x - y = 1.x+y=5,x−y=1.

Look at the yyy terms. The first equation has +y+y+y, the second has −y-y−y, and those are opposites. So if you add the equations column by column, the yyy terms sum to zero and drop out. Add the left sides, add the right sides:

(x+y)+(x−y)=5+1.(x + y) + (x - y) = 5 + 1.(x+y)+(x−y)=5+1.

The +y+y+y and −y-y−y cancel, and what remains has only xxx in it:

2x=6,x=3.2x = 6, \qquad x = 3.2x=6,x=3.

One variable is solved already. Recover the other by putting x=3x = 3x=3 back into either original equation, say x+y=5x + y = 5x+y=5:

3+y=5,y=2.3 + y = 5, \qquad y = 2.3+y=5,y=2.

The candidate solution is (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2). Check it in both equations, exactly as you did with substitution: 3+2=53 + 2 = 53+2=5 and 3−2=13 - 2 = 13−2=1, both true. So (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) solves the system.

The move that did the work was adding two equations to cancel a variable. It succeeds whenever a variable’s coefficients are opposites, because opposite numbers sum to zero. If instead the coefficients are equal, subtracting one equation from the other cancels the variable just as well. Adding and subtracting are the two faces of elimination. The only remaining question is what to do when the coefficients are neither opposite nor equal.

The elimination method step by step

Every system yields to the same routine.

  1. Line up the equations in standard form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c, so like terms sit in columns.
  2. Match a variable’s coefficients. Pick the variable to eliminate, then multiply one or both equations by nonzero constants so its coefficients become opposites (to add) or equal (to subtract).
  3. Add or subtract the equations to remove that variable, leaving one equation in one unknown.
  4. Solve the one-variable equation.
  5. Back-substitute the value into an original equation to find the other variable.
  6. Check the pair in both original equations.

When a variable already has opposite coefficients, step 2 costs nothing and you go straight to adding.

Worked example 1 Solve 2x+y=72x + y = 72x+y=7 and 3x−y=83x - y = 83x−y=8

Scan the columns for a variable ready to cancel. The first equation has +y+y+y and the second has −y-y−y, opposites, so adding the equations eliminates yyy with no setup:

(2x+y)+(3x−y)=7+8.(2x + y) + (3x - y) = 7 + 8.(2x+y)+(3x−y)=7+8.

The yyy terms cancel and the xxx terms combine:

5x=15,x=3.5x = 15, \qquad x = 3.5x=15,x=3.

Back-substitute x=3x = 3x=3 into an original equation. Using 2x+y=72x + y = 72x+y=7:

2(3)+y=7,6+y=7,y=1.2(3) + y = 7, \qquad 6 + y = 7, \qquad y = 1.2(3)+y=7,6+y=7,y=1.

So the candidate is (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1). Check it in both equations, not just the one you used:

2(3)+1=7 ✓,3(3)−1=8 ✓.2(3) + 1 = 7 \ \checkmark, \qquad 3(3) - 1 = 8 \ \checkmark.2(3)+1=7 ✓,3(3)−1=8 ✓.

Both hold, so the solution is (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1).

Answer choices

The yyy terms are +4y+4y+4y and −4y-4y−4y, opposites, so adding the equations cancels yyy.

(3x+4y)+(5x−4y)=10+6  ⇒  8x=16(3x + 4y) + (5x - 4y) = 10 + 6 \;\Rightarrow\; 8x = 16(3x+4y)+(5x−4y)=10+6⇒8x=16

That leaves 8x=168x = 168x=16, so x=2x = 2x=2. Then back-substitute to find y=1y = 1y=1.

Why adding equations keeps every solution

Elimination keeps one of the two equations and replaces the other with a combined equation, then solves that smaller system. It is worth seeing why this is trustworthy: the new system is made of the equation you kept and the combined equation. That new system has exactly the same solution pairs as the original, so no common solution is lost and none is invented.

Two properties of equality make this safe, and you already used them without naming them above. Add two true statements and the sum is still true: 2=22 = 22=2 and 3=33 = 33=3 give 2+3=2+32 + 3 = 2 + 32+3=2+3, no matter what numbers sit on each side. So if a pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) makes both original equations true, adding the equations keeps that pair true in the sum. Multiply a true equation by any nonzero number and it stays true the same way, which is why scaling an equation before adding never changes its solutions. Both moves can be undone (subtract the same equation back off, or divide by the same nonzero number), and a step that can be undone cannot manufacture a solution that was not there before, or erase one that was.

The full argument shows this both ways at once: every pair that solves the original two equations survives into the combined equation, and every pair that solves the kept equation and the combined equation also solves the original second equation, so the two systems have exactly the same solution set. Matching the coefficients: multiply, then eliminate

Most systems do not hand you opposite coefficients. You create them. Multiplying an entire equation by a nonzero constant produces an equation with the same solutions, by the multiplication property of equality. The new equation carries rescaled coefficients, and you can aim those at a match.

Two situations arise. Sometimes one variable’s coefficient in one equation is a multiple of its coefficient in the other, so multiplying just one equation lines them up. When neither coefficient is a multiple of the other, multiply both equations, scaling the chosen variable’s coefficients up to their least common multiple. Make one of those coefficients positive and the other negative, so they cancel on adding.

Worked example 2 Multiply one equation: solve x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11 and 3x−y=53x - y = 53x−y=5

Aim to eliminate yyy. The first equation has +2y+2y+2y and the second has −y-y−y. Doubling the second turns its −y-y−y into −2y-2y−2y, the opposite of the +2y+2y+2y above it. Multiply every term of 3x−y=53x - y = 53x−y=5 by 222:

6x−2y=10.6x - 2y = 10.6x−2y=10.

Now add this to the first equation, x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11:

(x+2y)+(6x−2y)=11+10.(x + 2y) + (6x - 2y) = 11 + 10.(x+2y)+(6x−2y)=11+10.

The yyy terms cancel:

7x=21,x=3.7x = 21, \qquad x = 3.7x=21,x=3.

Back-substitute x=3x = 3x=3 into x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11:

3+2y=11,2y=8,y=4.3 + 2y = 11, \qquad 2y = 8, \qquad y = 4.3+2y=11,2y=8,y=4.

The solution is (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4). Check the second equation too: 3(3)−4=9−4=53(3) - 4 = 9 - 4 = 53(3)−4=9−4=5, correct. Notice that only one equation needed multiplying, because the coefficient 222 was already a multiple of the 111 on the −y-y−y.

Check your understanding

You want to solve 3x+2y=73x + 2y = 73x+2y=7 and x+y=3x + y = 3x+y=3 by elimination. If you multiply the second equation by −2-2−2 and add it to the first, which variable is eliminated?

Answer choices

Multiply every term of x+y=3x + y = 3x+y=3 by −2-2−2, giving −2x−2y=−6-2x - 2y = -6−2x−2y=−6. Add it to 3x+2y=73x + 2y = 73x+2y=7.

(3x+2y)+(−2x−2y)=7+(−6)  ⇒  x=1(3x + 2y) + (-2x - 2y) = 7 + (-6) \;\Rightarrow\; x = 1(3x+2y)+(−2x−2y)=7+(−6)⇒x=1

The +2y+2y+2y and −2y-2y−2y cancel, so yyy is eliminated and x=1x = 1x=1 is left.

When neither coefficient divides the other, you scale both equations. Aim the variable you are eliminating at the least common multiple of its two coefficients.

Worked example 3 Multiply both equations: solve 2x+3y=72x + 3y = 72x+3y=7 and 3x+2y=83x + 2y = 83x+2y=8

Eliminate xxx. Its coefficients are 222 and 333, and neither divides the other, so scale both up to their least common multiple, 666. Multiply the first equation by 333 and the second by 222:

6x+9y=21,6x+4y=16.6x + 9y = 21, \qquad 6x + 4y = 16.6x+9y=21,6x+4y=16.

Both now carry 6x6x6x. Since the xxx coefficients are equal rather than opposite, subtract the second equation from the first so the 6x6x6x terms cancel. Subtracting flips every sign in the second equation:

(6x+9y)−(6x+4y)=21−16,5y=5,y=1.(6x + 9y) - (6x + 4y) = 21 - 16, \qquad 5y = 5, \qquad y = 1.(6x+9y)−(6x+4y)=21−16,5y=5,y=1.

Back-substitute y=1y = 1y=1 into 2x+3y=72x + 3y = 72x+3y=7:

2x+3(1)=7,2x=4,x=2.2x + 3(1) = 7, \qquad 2x = 4, \qquad x = 2.2x+3(1)=7,2x=4,x=2.

The solution is (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1), and 3(2)+2(1)=83(2) + 2(1) = 83(2)+2(1)=8 confirms the second equation. You could instead have multiplied the second equation by −2-2−2 to get −6x−4y=−16-6x - 4y = -16−6x−4y=−16 and then added; the result is identical. Making coefficients equal and subtracting, or opposite and adding, are two routes to the same cancellation.

Back-substitute into x+2y=8x + 2y = 8x+2y=8: 2+2y=82 + 2y = 82+2y=8, so y=3y = 3y=3. Check both originals: 2+2(3)=82 + 2(3) = 82+2(3)=8 and 3(2)−3=33(2) - 3 = 33(2)−3=3, both true, so the solution is (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3).

When a variable cancels to a number: no solution and infinitely many

Usually elimination leaves a one-variable equation that pins a value down. Once in a while both variables cancel together and you are left with a bare numerical statement. As with substitution, that is not a dead end; it is the system telling you how the two lines lie.

Worked example 4 A system with no solution

Solve

2x+y=5,4x+2y=3.2x + y = 5, \qquad 4x + 2y = 3.2x+y=5,4x+2y=3.

Eliminate xxx. Multiply the first equation by −2-2−2 so its 2x2x2x becomes −4x-4x−4x, opposite the 4x4x4x below:

−4x−2y=−10.-4x - 2y = -10.−4x−2y=−10.

Add this to 4x+2y=34x + 2y = 34x+2y=3:

(−4x−2y)+(4x+2y)=−10+3.(-4x - 2y) + (4x + 2y) = -10 + 3.(−4x−2y)+(4x+2y)=−10+3.

Both variables cancel at once, and what is left is

0=−7.0 = -7.0=−7.

This is false: no values of xxx and yyy can make 000 equal −7-7−7. So the system has no solution. The two lines have the same steepness but different heights, so they are parallel and never meet.

Worked example 5 A system with infinitely many solutions

Solve

x−3y=2,2x−6y=4.x - 3y = 2, \qquad 2x - 6y = 4.x−3y=2,2x−6y=4.

Eliminate xxx by doubling the first equation so its xxx becomes 2x2x2x:

2x−6y=4.2x - 6y = 4.2x−6y=4.

This is now identical to the second equation, so subtracting them gives

0=0.0 = 0.0=0.

Every term has cancelled and the statement left over is always true. That means every pair solving the first equation already solves the second, so the system has infinitely many solutions. The second equation is just the first multiplied by 222, so the two describe the same line. Each point on that line, written (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) with x−3y=2x - 3y = 2x−3y=2, is a solution.

Every point on the shared line solves both equations. Contrast this with 0=k0 = k0=k for k≠0k \neq 0k=0, which is false and means no solution instead.

Substitution or elimination?

Both methods reach the same outcome for a given system: the same pair when it has exactly one solution, and agreement on no solution or infinitely many when it does not. So the choice between them is about convenience, not correctness. Reach for elimination when both equations are already in standard form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c. Elimination fits especially well when a variable shares a coefficient or an easy multiple across the two equations, so a single add or subtract clears it. Reach for substitution when one equation already has a variable isolated, like y=3x−2y = 3x - 2y=3x−2, because you can drop that expression straight into the other equation. Many systems accept either method comfortably; with a little practice you will glance at a system and pick the route that needs fewer steps.

Seeing the cancellation

The whole method comes down to lining up like terms and adding so that a column cancels. Here is the first worked example laid out that way. The yyy column holds +y+y+y directly above −y-y−y, opposite terms that sum to zero, so adding the two equations erases yyy and leaves a single equation in xxx.

Every elimination problem is this picture. When the canceling column is not already in place, you multiply one or both equations first to force it there, then add.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding when you should subtract, or the reverse. Adding cancels a variable only when its coefficients are opposites; subtracting cancels it only when they are equal. Check the signs before you combine, or you will keep the variable you meant to remove.
  • Forgetting to multiply every term. Scaling an equation multiplies the whole thing, both variable terms and the constant. Multiplying x+2y=11x + 2y = 11x+2y=11 by 222 gives 2x+4y=222x + 4y = 222x+4y=22, not 2x+4y=112x + 4y = 112x+4y=11. A forgotten constant quietly breaks the answer.
  • Multiplying only one side. Multiplying an equation means multiplying both sides. Scale only the left side and you have changed the equation into a different one.
  • Sign slips when subtracting. Subtracting an equation flips every sign inside it. So (6x+9y)−(6x+4y)(6x + 9y) - (6x + 4y)(6x+9y)−(6x+4y) is 5y5y5y, and (3x−y)−(x−y)(3x - y) - (x - y)(3x−y)−(x−y) is 2x2x2x, not 2x−2y2x - 2y2x−2y. Distribute the minus to every term.
  • Misreading a vanished variable. If both variables cancel and leave 0=80 = 80=8, the answer is no solution; if they leave 0=00 = 00=0, it is infinitely many solutions. Neither one means x=0x = 0x=0.
  • Stopping at one variable. Elimination gives you one coordinate. Back-substitute for the other, state the full pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), and check it in both equations before calling the system solved.

Key takeaways

  • Elimination solves a system by combining the two equations so that one variable cancels, turning the system into a single one-variable equation.
  • Add when a variable’s coefficients are opposites; subtract when they are equal. If they are neither, multiply one or both equations by nonzero constants first to force a match, scaling to the least common multiple of the coefficients.
  • After eliminating, solve for the surviving variable, back-substitute into an original equation, and check the pair in both.
  • Elimination is valid because of the addition and multiplication properties of equality: any pair solving both equations also solves any scaled sum of them. The step is also reversible, so no solution is lost or invented.
  • A leftover 0=k0 = k0=k with kkk not zero means no solution (parallel lines); a leftover 0=00 = 00=0 means infinitely many (the same line).
  • Prefer elimination when both equations are in standard form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c, and substitution when a variable is already isolated.

Why combining the equations preserves the solution set

Why combining the equations preserves the solution set#

A system asks for every pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that makes both equations true at once, and that collection of pairs is its solution set. Two properties of equality do all the work. The addition property says that if L1=R1L_1 = R_1L1​=R1​ and L2=R2L_2 = R_2L2​=R2​ are both true, then adding them gives another true statement, L1+L2=R1+R2L_1 + L_2 = R_1 + R_2L1​+L2​=R1​+R2​. The multiplication property says that if L=RL = RL=R is true, then kL=kRkL = kRkL=kR is true for any number kkk. Scaling an equation and adding two equations are exactly the moves elimination uses.

First, no common solution is lost. Elimination keeps one original equation, say the first, and replaces the second with a combined equation built as mmm times the first plus nnn times the second. Suppose a pair (x0,y0)(x_0, y_0)(x0​,y0​) solves the original system, so it makes both equations true. By the multiplication property, scaling the first equation by mmm and the second by nnn keeps each true for this pair. By the addition property, their sum is then true for this pair as well. That sum is exactly the combined equation, so the pair satisfies it. The pair also still satisfies the kept first equation, so it solves the new system made of the first equation and the combined equation. Every solution of the original system survives into the new one.

Second, no new common solution is invented. The replacement is reversible. The combined equation is mmm times the first plus nnn times the second. Subtracting mmm times the first equation from that combined equation, and then dividing by nnn, gives back the second equation exactly. This undoing is valid because the multiplier nnn on the second equation is not zero. That is guaranteed, since you never eliminate by multiplying an equation by zero, which would only erase it. So from the new system, the first equation together with the combined equation, you can rebuild the original second equation. Any pair that solves the new system therefore also satisfies the second equation, and so it solves the original system too.

The new system is the first equation paired with the combined equation. The two directions together show that this new system has exactly the same solution set as the original. Elimination therefore trades the system for an equivalent one in which a variable is already gone. This also explains the unusual outcomes. When the combined equation still carries a variable, it pins that variable to a single value and the system has one solution. When both variables cancel and leave a false statement such as 0=80 = 80=8, no pair can satisfy the equations, so the lines are parallel and there is no solution. When both cancel and leave a statement that is always true such as 0=00 = 00=0, the second equation was a multiple of the first all along. The two equations therefore describe the same line, and every point on that line solves the system.

A bit of history (optional)

A farmer brings in three grades of grain. Three bundles of the best grade, two of the middle and one of the poorest together fill a known measure. Two other mixtures fill two other known measures. How much does one bundle of each grade hold?

That problem is about two thousand years old. It comes from a Chinese classic, The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. The book gathers hundreds of questions from daily life, and the methods that answer them. Its eighth chapter is called Fangcheng, and every problem in it is a system of linear equations.

The method given there is the one you have just learned. The numbers of each equation go into a column of their own, set side by side. Then one column is scaled and taken away from the next. You pick the scaling so that a single unknown falls away. Repeat, and the unknowns vanish one at a time until one stands alone. Work backwards to recover the rest.

Nothing there needs the equals sign, or the letter xxx, or even the habit of writing an equation on one line. None of them existed yet. The bookkeeping was different. The move was the same as yours.

Today the routine usually carries the name of Carl Friedrich Gauss. This German mathematician used it in the early 1800s to track a newly found minor planet. He never claimed to have invented it. The name was attached long after his death. When you scale one equation and subtract it from another to knock out a variable, you are running a method eighteen centuries older than its name.

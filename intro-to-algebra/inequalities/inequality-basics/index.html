Inequality Basics

Intuition

Most of the equations you have solved so far settle on one exact value. The equation 2x+1=72x + 1 = 72x+1=7 has the one answer x=3x = 3x=3. But plenty of real conditions are not that exact. A bridge posts a limit of 121212 tons or less. A game needs at least 222 players. A thermometer reads somewhere below zero. None of these names one number. Each allows many numbers at once, not a single answer. An inequality is the tool for writing a condition like that. This lesson is about what inequalities say and how to picture their solutions, plus the handful of rules for rearranging them. The rules look almost exactly like the balance moves for equations, with a single famous exception you will meet in the middle.

Learning goals

  • Compare two quantities with <<<, >>>, ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥
  • Describe a solution set as a range of values
  • Show a solution in words, on a line, and as an interval
  • Bracket an included endpoint and parenthesize an excluded one
  • Reverse the direction when scaling by a negative number
  • Read a compound a<x<ba < x < ba<x<b as a band between bounds

The four inequality symbols

An inequality compares two quantities that need not be equal. In place of the equals sign it uses one of four symbols.

SymbolRead asMeaning
<<<”less than”strictly smaller, not equal
>>>”greater than”strictly larger, not equal
≤\le≤”less than or equal to”, “at most”smaller, or exactly equal
≥\ge≥”greater than or equal to”, “at least”larger, or exactly equal

So 5<85 < 85<8 says five is less than eight, and 8>58 > 58>5 says eight is greater than five. Those are the same fact read from opposite ends. The symbol always opens toward the larger amount and narrows to a point at the smaller one. So the pointed end aims at the smaller number: in 5<85 < 85<8 the point sits on the 555.

That two-ended reading is worth stating as a rule, because it lets you turn any inequality around. Take 7>x7 > x7>x: swap the two sides and reverse the symbol, and you get x<7x < 7x<7, the same fact read from the other end. You will use this often to move a variable to the left. In general, the statements a≤ba \le ba≤b and b≥ab \ge ab≥a say exactly the same thing: ”aaa is at most bbb” is the same claim as ”bbb is at least aaa.”

The two symbols with a bar underneath, ≤\le≤ and ≥\ge≥, allow equality as well as the strict comparison. The statement x≤6x \le 6x≤6 is true when xxx is 666 and also true when xxx is anything below 666. We call <<< and >>> strict inequalities, because they exclude the boundary, and ≤\le≤ and ≥\ge≥ inclusive (or non-strict), because they let it in. That one bar is the whole difference between admitting the boundary number and shutting it out.

Every number that makes an inequality true is part of its solution set. Ask which numbers satisfy x>3x > 3x>3: the value 444 works, and so do 555, 3.13.13.1, 3.00013.00013.0001, and a million. Every number to the right of 333 qualifies, so this solution set holds infinitely many numbers. That is why we describe the answer as a range and draw it, rather than listing it.

Worked example 1 Turn each phrase into an inequality

Let nnn be the number in question. Match the wording to the symbol, watching for whether the boundary is allowed.

“A rider must be at least 484848 inches tall” allows exactly 484848 and anything taller, so it includes the boundary:

n≥48.n \ge 48.n≥48.

“You may spend at most 202020 dollars” allows exactly 202020 and anything less, again including the boundary:

n≤20.n \le 20.n≤20.

“There are more than 222 players” excludes 222 itself, since “more than” is strict:

n>2.n > 2.n>2.

Finally, rewrite 15≥n15 \ge n15≥n with the variable on the left. Swap the sides and reverse the symbol:

15≥n⟺n≤15.15 \ge n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n \le 15.15≥n⟺n≤15.

Both forms say ”nnn is at most 151515.”

Picturing the solution on a number line

A number line turns a solution set into a picture you can take in at a glance. Locate the boundary number from the inequality, decide whether the boundary itself belongs, and shade the direction that holds the rest of the solutions. Three decisions capture every case.

The circle at the boundary records whether the boundary is a solution. Draw an open (hollow) circle for a strict inequality, <<< or >>>, because the boundary is excluded. Draw a filled (closed) circle for an inclusive inequality, ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥, because the boundary is included. The shaded ray then runs in the direction of the solutions: to the right for a “greater” relation (>>> or ≥\ge≥), toward the larger numbers. The ray runs to the left for a “less” relation (<<< or ≤\le≤). The arrowhead means the solutions run on forever that way.

Here is the graph of x>2x > 2x>2. The circle on 222 is open, because 222 is not a solution, and the shading runs right across every number larger than 222.

The only thing that changes for x≥2x \ge 2x≥2 is the circle: it becomes filled, because now 222 belongs to the solution. Switching between a strict and an inclusive relation never moves the shading, it only opens or closes the boundary circle.

That last sentence is the one to test rather than take on trust, and the figure below is built for it. Its three controls are exactly the three decisions described above. “Endpoint” sets where the boundary sits, “Side” picks which way to shade, and “Endpoint is” switches the boundary in or out.

Set “Endpoint” to 222 and “Side” to right, and you have the graph of x>2x > 2x>2 from the picture above. Now switch “Endpoint is” between out and in, and watch the whole figure. The ray does not move a pixel, the sign in the readout changes from >>> to ≥\ge≥, and the circle fills in. One decision, one thing on the screen, and it is the decision students most often lose. Then set “Endpoint” to −1-1−1, “Side” to left, and “Endpoint is” to in: that is the graph the next worked example asks for before you read it.

Worked example 2 Graph x≤−1x \le -1x≤−1 and describe its parts

The symbol is ≤\le≤, which is inclusive, so the boundary −1-1−1 is a solution and gets a filled circle. It is a “less” relation, so the solutions are the numbers below −1-1−1, which lie to the left. Shade left from the filled circle.

So the picture is a filled dot on −1-1−1 with a ray heading left. In words it reads ”xxx is at most −1-1−1,” and the boundary −1-1−1 is part of the answer.

Interval notation

Drawing a picture every time is slow, so there is a compact written shorthand for the same solution set, called interval notation. An interval is written as its left and right bounds inside a pair of fences, with the kind of fence recording whether each bound is included.

A square bracket includes the endpoint, matching a filled circle and an inclusive symbol. A parenthesis excludes the endpoint, matching an open circle and a strict symbol. So 2≤x≤52 \le x \le 52≤x≤5 becomes [2,5][2, 5][2,5], while 2<x<52 < x < 52<x<5 becomes (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5). The two can be mixed: 2≤x<52 \le x < 52≤x<5 is the half-open interval [2,5)[2, 5)[2,5), closed on the left and open on the right.

When the solution runs on forever in one direction, we mark that end with the infinity symbol, ∞\infty∞ for the right and −∞-\infty−∞ for the left. Here is the one firm rule: infinity always takes a parenthesis, never a bracket. Infinity is not a number, so there is no endpoint to reach or to include; the parenthesis records that the interval is open and unbounded. That is why x>2x > 2x>2 is written (2,∞)(2, \infty)(2,∞) and x≤−1x \le -1x≤−1 is written (−∞,−1](-\infty, -1](−∞,−1], with a bracket on the real endpoint and a parenthesis at infinity. The table lines up all three ways of saying the same thing.

InequalityIn wordsNumber lineInterval
x>2x > 2x>2greater than 222open circle at 222, shade right(2,∞)(2, \infty)(2,∞)
x≥2x \ge 2x≥2at least 222filled circle at 222, shade right[2,∞)[2, \infty)[2,∞)
x<2x < 2x<2less than 222open circle at 222, shade left(−∞,2)(-\infty, 2)(−∞,2)
x≤2x \le 2x≤2at most 222filled circle at 222, shade left(−∞,2](-\infty, 2](−∞,2]
−1<x≤3-1 < x \le 3−1<x≤3above −1-1−1 and at most 333open at −1-1−1, filled at 333, shade between(−1,3](-1, 3](−1,3]

One caution about reading. Written on its own, a symbol pair like (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) could be an interval on a line or a point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) in the plane from the last chapter. The surrounding words tell you which is meant: an interval names a set of numbers on one axis, while a coordinate point names one location in the plane.

Worked example 3 Move between symbols, number line, and interval

Take the inequality x≥−2x \ge -2x≥−2. It is inclusive, so the boundary −2-2−2 belongs, and it is a “greater” relation, so the solutions run right. On a number line that is a filled circle on −2-2−2 with a ray to the right. In interval notation the real endpoint −2-2−2 is included, so it takes a bracket, and the right end runs to infinity, which always takes a parenthesis:

x≥−2⟺[−2,∞).x \ge -2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad [-2, \infty).x≥−2⟺[−2,∞).

Now run the translation the other way, starting from the interval (−∞,4)(-\infty, 4)(−∞,4). The left end is −∞-\infty−∞, so the set is unbounded below, and the right endpoint 444 carries a parenthesis, so 444 is excluded. That is exactly the strict “less” relation

(−∞,4)⟺x<4,(-\infty, 4) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x < 4,(−∞,4)⟺x<4,

an open circle on 444 with the shading running left. Reading the fences carefully is the whole skill: a bracket is a filled circle, a parenthesis is an open circle, and an infinity end is always open.

The left end is unbounded, so it takes −∞-\infty−∞ with a parenthesis, and the included endpoint 555 takes a bracket. A parenthesis on 555 would wrongly exclude it, and the intervals starting at 555 describe x≥5x \ge 5x≥5 or x>5x > 5x>5 instead.

The rules for changing an inequality

To rearrange an inequality without breaking it, you need to know which moves keep the statement true. Three of the rules match your equation habits exactly, and one carries a twist. In each case the reason is visible on the number line, where a<ba < ba<b simply means aaa sits to the left of bbb.

Adding or subtracting the same quantity from both sides keeps the direction. Sliding both numbers the same distance in the same direction does not change which one is on the left. Starting from the true statement 3<73 < 73<7 and adding 444 to each side gives 7<117 < 117<11, still true, and subtracting 101010 from each side of 3<73 < 73<7 gives −7<−3-7 < -3−7<−3, also true. The order survives every shift.

Multiplying or dividing both sides by a positive number keeps the direction. Scaling by a positive factor stretches or shrinks every distance from zero but never carries a number across zero, so the left-right order holds. From 3<73 < 73<7, multiplying both sides by 222 gives 6<146 < 146<14, and dividing 6<146 < 146<14 by 222 returns 3<73 < 73<7. Both stay true.

Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the direction. This is the twist, and it is the single most error-prone fact about inequalities, so it deserves a careful look. Take the true statement 3<53 < 53<5 and multiply both sides by −1-1−1. The left becomes −3-3−3 and the right becomes −5-5−5. If the symbol stayed put we would be claiming −3<−5-3 < -5−3<−5, but that is false. On the number line −5-5−5 lies to the left of −3-3−3, so −3-3−3 is the larger number. The true statement is −3>−5-3 > -5−3>−5, with the symbol flipped.

Why multiplying by a negative reverses the inequality#

Try it with numbers first: 4<104 < 104<10, and multiply both sides by the positive number 222. The difference 10−4=610 - 4 = 610−4=6 is positive. Multiplying that difference by 222 gives 2×6=122 \times 6 = 122×6=12, still positive, and 2×62 \times 62×6 is the same amount as 2×10−2×42 \times 10 - 2 \times 42×10−2×4, so

20−8=12.20 - 8 = 12.20−8=12.

So 202020 is larger than 888: multiplying 4<104 < 104<10 by the positive number 222 keeps the order, giving 8<208 < 208<20. Now negate each side: 888 becomes −8-8−8 and 202020 becomes −20-20−20, and −8>−20-8 > -20−8>−20. Multiplying 4<104 < 104<10 by the negative number −2-2−2 gave −8>−20-8 > -20−8>−20, with the symbol reversed.

Nothing above depended on the numbers 444, 101010, and 222. The same three moves work for any a<ba < ba<b and any positive kkk. The difference b−ab - ab−a is positive, so multiplying it by kkk gives k(b−a)>0k(b - a) > 0k(b−a)>0, which distributes to

kb−ka>0.kb - ka > 0.kb−ka>0.

So kbkbkb is larger than kakaka: multiplying a<ba < ba<b by the positive number kkk keeps the order, exactly as the earlier rule said. Now take the opposite of each side. Taking opposites sends kakaka to −ka-ka−ka and kbkbkb to −kb-kb−kb, and the positive difference kb−kakb - kakb−ka equals (−ka)−(−kb)(-ka) - (-kb)(−ka)−(−kb). So (−ka)−(−kb)(-ka) - (-kb)(−ka)−(−kb) is positive too, which means

−ka>−kb.-ka > -kb.−ka>−kb.

Multiplying a<ba < ba<b by the negative number −k-k−k gives −ka>−kb-ka > -kb−ka>−kb, with the symbol reversed. The same argument works with ≤\le≤ in place of <<<, giving ≥\ge≥: the boundary case a=ba = ba=b turns into −ka=−kb-ka = -kb−ka=−kb either way.

Dividing by a negative number reverses the direction for the same reason. Dividing by −k-k−k is the same as multiplying by the positive number 1k\tfrac{1}{k}k1​ and then by −1-1−1. Only that last step flips anything.

∎

Transitivity chains comparisons together. If a<ba < ba<b and b<cb < cb<c, then a<ca < ca<c. On the line, aaa is left of bbb and bbb is left of ccc, so aaa must be left of ccc. A quick check: since 2<52 < 52<5 and 5<95 < 95<9, transitivity gives 2<92 < 92<9, which is plainly true. You will use this soon to check whether a chain like a<x<ba < x < ba<x<b can describe any numbers at all.

These rules are enough to take a short step on an inequality, the same way you take one step on an equation. For x+3<7x + 3 < 7x+3<7, subtract 333 from both sides, and since subtraction never disturbs the symbol, x<4x < 4x<4. For −2x<6-2x < 6−2x<6, divide both sides by −2-2−2, and because −2-2−2 is negative you reverse the symbol, giving x>−3x > -3x>−3. Carrying such a problem through several steps in a reliable order is the job of the next lesson. Here the point is only that each individual move is governed by the rules above.

Worked example 4 Apply one rule to an inequality

Start with −13x≤2-\tfrac{1}{3}x \le 2−31​x≤2. The variable is multiplied by the negative number −13-\tfrac{1}{3}−31​, so to isolate xxx you multiply both sides by −3-3−3. Multiplying by a negative reverses the direction, so ≤\le≤ turns into ≥\ge≥:

−3⋅(−13x)≥−3⋅2⟹x≥−6.-3 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{1}{3}x\right) \ge -3 \cdot 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x \ge -6.−3⋅(−31​x)≥−3⋅2⟹x≥−6.

The flip is the entire lesson of this step. To confirm the direction, test one value against the original inequality. Try x=0x = 0x=0, which should satisfy x≥−6x \ge -6x≥−6: the original gives −13(0)=0-\tfrac{1}{3}(0) = 0−31​(0)=0, and 0≤20 \le 20≤2 is true. Try x=−9x = -9x=−9, which should fail x≥−6x \ge -6x≥−6: the original gives −13(−9)=3-\tfrac{1}{3}(-9) = 3−31​(−9)=3, and 3≤23 \le 23≤2 is false, exactly as predicted. The solution set is [−6,∞)[-6, \infty)[−6,∞).

Compound inequalities

Sometimes a value is squeezed from both sides at once, larger than one number and smaller than another. A compound inequality writes both bounds in a single line. The notation a<x<ba < x < ba<x<b means x>ax > ax>a and x<bx < bx<b at the same time, so xxx lies strictly between aaa and bbb. Read it as two claims joined by “and”: xxx is above aaa, and xxx is below bbb.

Both symbols in the chain point the same way: never one “less than” symbol and one “greater than” symbol. Use <<< or ≤\le≤ throughout, or use >>> or ≥\ge≥ throughout; that is the syntax. But writing it correctly is not enough to guarantee it describes any numbers. If x>ax > ax>a and x<bx < bx<b are both true, transitivity forces a<ba < ba<b: the left bound must be smaller than the right bound. We write −2<x≤3-2 < x \le 3−2<x≤3, which reads left to right as ”−2-2−2 is less than xxx, and xxx is at most 333,” and it makes sense because −2<3-2 < 3−2<3. Now look at 3<x<−23 < x < -23<x<−2: both symbols point the same way, so the syntax looks fine, but the numbers do not cooperate. It asks for a number above 333 and below −2-2−2, and since 333 is not less than −2-2−2, nothing satisfies both parts at once. Check the numbers, not just the arrows, before trusting a compound inequality.

Each end keeps its own circle: an open circle where the bound is strict and a filled circle where it is inclusive. On the number line the solution is the band between the two boundaries rather than a ray running off to one side. In interval notation the same band is just the pair of bounds in one set of fences, each fence chosen by its own symbol. So −2<x≤3-2 < x \le 3−2<x≤3, open on the left and closed on the right, is the half-open interval (−2,3](-2, 3](−2,3].

Worked example 5 A compound inequality in three forms

Read the statement ”xxx is at least −1-1−1 and less than 444.” “At least −1-1−1” is inclusive, so the left bound −1-1−1 is included; “less than 444” is strict, so the right bound 444 is excluded. Written as one line with the smaller bound on the left, that is

−1≤x<4.-1 \le x < 4.−1≤x<4.

On the number line this is a filled circle on −1-1−1, an open circle on 444, and a shaded band between them. In interval notation the left endpoint is included, so it takes a bracket, and the right endpoint is excluded, so it takes a parenthesis:

−1≤x<4⟺[−1,4).-1 \le x < 4 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad [-1, 4).−1≤x<4⟺[−1,4).

Test a value to make sure both parts hold. Is x=−1x = -1x=−1 a solution? The left part −1≤−1-1 \le -1−1≤−1 is true and the right part −1<4-1 < 4−1<4 is true, so −1-1−1 is in, matching the filled circle. Is x=4x = 4x=4 a solution? The right part 4<44 < 44<4 is false, so 444 is out, matching the open circle. The value x=0x = 0x=0 sits comfortably inside, since −1≤0-1 \le 0−1≤0 and 0<40 < 40<4 both hold.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to reverse the direction after a negative. Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number flips the symbol. Turning −2x<6-2x < 6−2x<6 into x<−3x < -3x<−3 is wrong; the correct result is x>−3x > -3x>−3. This is the single most common inequality error.
  • Reversing when you should not. The flip happens only for multiplying or dividing by a negative. Adding or subtracting a number, even a negative one, never flips the symbol, and a negative sitting elsewhere in the problem is irrelevant. Only the sign of the number you multiply or divide both sides by matters.
  • Mixing up open and filled circles. A strict symbol (<<< or >>>) gets an open circle, because the boundary is excluded. An inclusive symbol (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥) gets a filled circle, because the boundary is included. The circle is the only thing that separates x>2x > 2x>2 from x≥2x \ge 2x≥2.
  • Putting a bracket next to infinity. Infinity is not a number, so it never gets a bracket. Write (2,∞)(2, \infty)(2,∞) and (−∞,5](-\infty, 5](−∞,5], always with a parenthesis on the infinite end.
  • Reading a comparison backward. The symbol points at the smaller number, so −3>−5-3 > -5−3>−5 is correct even though 555 is bigger than 333. Among negatives, the number closer to zero is the larger one.
  • Trusting the arrows without checking the numbers. Both symbols in a<x<ba < x < ba<x<b must point the same way, but that alone does not guarantee a solution. The chain also needs a<ba < ba<b: something like 3<x<−23 < x < -23<x<−2 has matching arrows but no solutions at all, since 333 is not less than −2-2−2.

Key takeaways

  • An inequality compares two quantities with <<< (less than), >>> (greater than), ≤\le≤ (at most), or ≥\ge≥ (at least). The bar under ≤\le≤ and ≥\ge≥ includes the boundary; <<< and >>> exclude it. The statements a≤ba \le ba≤b and b≥ab \ge ab≥a are the same.
  • A solution set is every value that makes the inequality true, almost always a whole range rather than a single number.
  • Show a solution set three matching ways: in words, on a number line, and in interval notation. On the line, use an open circle for <<< or >>>, a filled circle for ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥, and shade toward the solutions. In the interval, a bracket marks an included endpoint, a parenthesis marks an excluded one, and ±∞\pm\infty±∞ always takes a parenthesis.
  • Rules for rearranging: adding or subtracting the same amount, and multiplying or dividing by a positive number, keep the direction. Multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the direction, because taking opposites swaps the order of numbers on the line. Transitivity links comparisons: a<ba < ba<b and b<cb < cb<c give a<ca < ca<c.
  • A compound inequality a<x<ba < x < ba<x<b means x>ax > ax>a and x<bx < bx<b at once, a band between the bounds, with each end open or closed on its own symbol.

A bit of history (optional)

Look again at the four signs in this lesson. Two of them are the other two with a line drawn underneath.

That line has a history of its own. The plain signs <<< and >>> reached print in 163116311631. They appear in an algebra book of Thomas Harriot’s, published ten years after the English mathematician died. For most of the century that followed, nobody had a settled mark for “smaller, or else exactly equal”. A writer set down the comparison, then explained the exception in ordinary words.

The mathematician John Wallis tried a version in 167016701670, drawing the extra line above the sign instead of below. It did not catch on. The form close to the one you have been using came from Pierre Bouguer, a French scientist who taught navigation and measured the shape of the planet. Around 173417341734 he printed the signs with a bar underneath to mark “or equal to,” and a line under the sign is the idea that stuck.

It is a tiny addition, and a surprising amount rides on it. A bridge posted at twelve tons either admits a twelve ton truck or turns it away. Nothing else in the notice settles that question.

You have been drawing that bar all lesson without calling it that. The filled circle on the number line is the bar, and so is the square bracket in an interval. Three notations, one repeated decision: does the boundary belong to the answer?

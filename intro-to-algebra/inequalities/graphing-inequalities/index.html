Graphing Inequalities

Intuition

In the last two lessons a linear inequality had a single variable, and its solution was a piece of the number line, a ray or a band. Give the inequality a second variable, as in y>2x+1y > 2x + 1y>2x+1 or 3x+2y≤63x + 2y \le 63x+2y≤6, and the solution gains a dimension. A solution is any pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that makes the statement true, and there are now whole rows and columns of such pairs. Plotted together they fill an entire region of the coordinate plane, a flat sheet called a half-plane that lies to one side of a line. So graphing a two-variable inequality comes down to two questions. Which line forms the edge of the region, and which side of that line do the solutions live on?

Learning goals

  • Draw the boundary dashed for strict and solid for inclusive
  • Choose an off-boundary test point and shade the half-plane it proves true
  • Recognize x>cx > cx>c as a vertical boundary and y≤cy \le cy≤c as horizontal
  • Check whether a given point, including one on the boundary, is a solution
  • Keep only the overlap for a system, the feasible region

The boundary line and the two half-planes

Every two-variable linear inequality has a matching equation, the one you get by replacing its inequality symbol with an equals sign. The graph of that equation is a straight line, and this line is the boundary of the solution region. It earns the name honestly: it is the exact border between the pairs that satisfy the inequality and the pairs that do not. Replacing >>> with === in y>2x+1y > 2x + 1y>2x+1 gives y=2x+1y = 2x + 1y=2x+1, a line you can graph directly from its slope and intercept.

A single line splits the plane into two pieces, one on each side, and each piece is a half-plane. The boundary is the crease between them. The solutions of the inequality are exactly one of those two half-planes, never a mix of the two. That clean split between the two half-planes is what makes graphing an inequality quick. To see why an entire side either works or fails together, look at what the inequality is really measuring.

Why one whole side satisfies the inequality#

Put the inequality in the form y>mx+by > mx + by>mx+b, solving for yyy first if it is not already there. Pick any input xxx. On the boundary line the height at that input is exactly mx+bmx + bmx+b. A point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) sitting at that same input lies above the line when its height yyy is greater than mx+bmx + bmx+b, and below the line when yyy is less. For instance, at x=1x = 1x=1 the line y=2x+1y = 2x + 1y=2x+1 has height 2(1)+1=32(1) + 1 = 32(1)+1=3: the point (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4) sits above the line because 4>34 > 34>3, and the point (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2) sits below it because 2<32 < 32<3.

So the quantity

y−(mx+b),y - (mx + b),y−(mx+b),

the signed vertical gap between the point and the line, is positive at every point above the line. That gap is zero at every point on the line, and negative at every point below it. The inequality y>mx+by > mx + by>mx+b is nothing more than the statement that this gap is positive, which is true at every single point of the upper half-plane and false at every single point of the lower one. The boundary line is the only place where the verdict switches: the sign of y−(mx+b)y - (mx + b)y−(mx+b) cannot change without passing through zero, and that quantity reaches zero only on the line. So you cannot move from a solution to a non-solution without crossing the boundary, which is exactly why testing one point settles its whole side at once. A vertical boundary x=cx = cx=c behaves the same way through the horizontal gap x−cx - cx−c, and a standard-form inequality Ax+By≤CAx + By \le CAx+By≤C through the quantity Ax+By−CAx + By - CAx+By−C. Each of those is again a single number that is zero on the line and one fixed sign on each side.

∎

Picking the side with a test point

The proof hands you a shortcut. Because a whole side shares one verdict, you never have to reason about “above” or “below” yourself. Choose any point that is not on the boundary, substitute its coordinates into the original inequality, and check whether the statement comes out true. If it is true, that point is a solution, so shade the entire side it sits on. If it is false, the solutions are on the other side, so shade that one instead. A single substitution decides everything.

The handiest test point is almost always the origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), because setting x=0x = 0x=0 and y=0y = 0y=0 collapses most of the arithmetic to nothing. The one time you cannot use the origin is when the boundary passes through it. Then (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) sits on the line itself, not on either side of it. Substituting it still gives a true or false statement, but that result only confirms whether the boundary is included, which the symbol already told you, and it cannot tell you which half-plane to shade. In that case pick another easy off-line point, such as (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0) or (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1).

One more decision fixes how you draw the boundary. If the inequality is strict, <<< or >>>, the points on the line are not solutions. Then draw the boundary as a dashed line to show that the line is excluded. If the inequality is inclusive, ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥, the points on the line do satisfy it. Then draw the boundary solid to show that the line is part of the answer. You read this straight off the symbol, exactly like the open or filled circle you used on the number line.

Worked example 1 Graph y>2x+1y > 2x + 1y>2x+1

Start with the boundary. Replacing >>> with === gives y=2x+1y = 2x + 1y=2x+1, a line with slope 222 and yyy-intercept (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1). Plot (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1), step up 222 and right 111 to (1,3)(1, 3)(1,3), and draw the line. The symbol is strict, so make it dashed: the points on the line are not part of the solution.

Now pick the side. Test the origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), which is not on the line, in the original inequality:

0>2(0)+1⟹0>1,0 > 2(0) + 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0 > 1,0>2(0)+1⟹0>1,

which is false. The origin is not a solution, so the solutions lie on the other side. Shade the half-plane that does not contain the origin, the region above the line.

The finished graph is a dashed line with the region above it shaded. As a check, the point (−2,0)(-2, 0)(−2,0) is in the shaded region, and the original reads 0>2(−2)+1=−30 > 2(-2) + 1 = -30>2(−2)+1=−3, which is true. The unshaded point (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0), by contrast, gives 0>2(2)+1=50 > 2(2) + 1 = 50>2(2)+1=5, and 0>50 > 50>5 is false.

Worked example 2 Graph 3x+2y≤63x + 2y \le 63x+2y≤6

The boundary is 3x+2y=63x + 2y = 63x+2y=6, quickest from its intercepts. Setting y=0y = 0y=0 gives 3x=63x = 63x=6, so x=2x = 2x=2 and the xxx-intercept is (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0). Setting x=0x = 0x=0 gives 2y=62y = 62y=6, so y=3y = 3y=3 and the yyy-intercept is (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3). Draw the line through those two points. The symbol is ≤\le≤, which is inclusive, so make the line solid: the points on it are solutions too.

Test the origin in the original inequality:

3(0)+2(0)≤6⟹0≤6,3(0) + 2(0) \le 6 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0 \le 6,3(0)+2(0)≤6⟹0≤6,

which is true. The origin is a solution, so shade the side that contains it, the region below the line.

Notice you never had to solve for yyy or decide for yourself what “below” means. The test point did that work. As a check, the origin we tested is shaded. A point on the far side such as (4,4)(4, 4)(4,4) gives 3(4)+2(4)=203(4) + 2(4) = 203(4)+2(4)=20, and 20≤620 \le 620≤6 is false, so that point is correctly left unshaded.

To graph y≥−x+4y \ge -x + 4y≥−x+4, how should you draw the boundary line, and does the origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) lie in the shaded region?

Answer choices

The symbol is ≥\ge≥, which is inclusive, so the boundary is drawn solid. Now test the origin in the inequality.

0≥−(0)+4  ⇒  0≥40 \ge -(0) + 4 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 \ge 40≥−(0)+4⇒0≥4

That is false, so the origin is not a solution and the shaded region is the side that does not contain it. A solid boundary with the origin unshaded is the matching pair.

Horizontal and vertical boundaries

Two special inequalities have boundaries that run straight across or straight up and down, yet nothing about the method changes. An inequality in xxx alone, such as x>−2x > -2x>−2, has the vertical boundary x=−2x = -2x=−2. An inequality in yyy alone, such as y≤1y \le 1y≤1, has the horizontal boundary y=1y = 1y=1. Draw the boundary dashed or solid from the symbol as always, then test a point.

For x>−2x > -2x>−2 the boundary is the vertical line two units left of the yyy-axis, drawn dashed because the symbol is strict. Testing the origin gives 0>−20 > -20>−2, which is true, so shade the side holding the origin, everything to the right of the line.

For y≤1y \le 1y≤1 the boundary is the horizontal line one unit above the xxx-axis, drawn solid because ≤\le≤ is inclusive. Testing the origin gives 0≤10 \le 10≤1, which is true, so shade the side holding the origin, everything on or below the line.

There is a quick sense-check for the shading. The statement x>−2x > -2x>−2 says ”xxx is greater than −2-2−2,” and greater xxx lies to the right, so the region opens rightward. In the same way y≤1y \le 1y≤1 says ”yyy is at most 111,” and smaller yyy lies downward, so the region opens downward. The test point confirms it, but the words already point the right way.

Check your understanding

To graph x>0x > 0x>0, how should you draw the boundary, why can't you use the origin as the test point, and which side should you shade?

Answer choices

The symbol is strict, so the boundary x=0x = 0x=0 is dashed. That boundary is the yyy-axis itself, so the origin sits on it and cannot decide a side. Pick another easy point such as (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0).

1>0 (true)1 > 0 \ \text{(true)}1>0 (true)

That point is a solution, so shade the half-plane to the right of the yyy-axis, where xxx is positive.

Checking whether a point is a solution

Reading a graph in the other direction matters just as much: given a point, is it in the solution region? Substitute its coordinates into the inequality and see whether the statement is true. If it is, the point is a solution and lies in the shaded region; if not, it lies outside. The only case that needs care is a point landing exactly on the boundary line. There the inequality becomes an equality, so the point counts as a solution only when the boundary is solid (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥). The point is not a solution when the boundary is dashed (<<< or >>>).

Worked example 3 Test three points against y≥2x−1y \ge 2x - 1y≥2x−1

Check each point by substituting it into y≥2x−1y \ge 2x - 1y≥2x−1 and judging the result.

The point (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4): the right side is 2(3)−1=52(3) - 1 = 52(3)−1=5, so the statement is 4≥54 \ge 54≥5, which is false. This point is not a solution.

The point (−1,2)(-1, 2)(−1,2): the right side is 2(−1)−1=−32(-1) - 1 = -32(−1)−1=−3, so the statement is 2≥−32 \ge -32≥−3, which is true. This point is a solution.

The point (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3): the right side is 2(2)−1=32(2) - 1 = 32(2)−1=3, so the statement is 3≥33 \ge 33≥3, which is true. This point sits exactly on the boundary line, and because the symbol is ≥\ge≥ the boundary is solid and its points count, so (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) is a solution.

(3,4) is false,(−1,2) is true,(2,3) is true on a solid edge.(3, 4)\text{ is false}, \qquad (-1, 2)\text{ is true}, \qquad (2, 3)\text{ is true on a solid edge}.(3,4) is false,(−1,2) is true,(2,3) is true on a solid edge.

Had the inequality been the strict y>2x−1y > 2x - 1y>2x−1, the point (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) would give 3>33 > 33>3, which is false, and it would not be a solution. The boundary is the one place where the dashed-or-solid distinction changes the answer.

Check your understanding

Is the point (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) a solution of 3x−y<53x - y < 53x−y<5?

Answer choices

Substitute x=1x = 1x=1 and y=−2y = -2y=−2 into the left side 3x−y3x - y3x−y.

3(1)−(−2)=3+2=5,so the statement is 5<53(1) - (-2) = 3 + 2 = 5, \quad\text{so the statement is } 5 < 53(1)−(−2)=3+2=5,so the statement is 5<5

That is false, so the point is not a solution. In fact (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) lands exactly on the boundary 3x−y=53x - y = 53x−y=5, and since the symbol is the strict <<<, that boundary is dashed and its points are excluded.

Systems of linear inequalities

A system of linear inequalities asks for the points that satisfy several inequalities at the same time. Each inequality on its own shades a half-plane. A point that works for the whole system has to lie in every one of those half-planes at once. The solution of the system is therefore the region where all the shaded half-planes overlap. That common region is called the feasible region, and every point inside it satisfies every inequality in the system.

Graph a system by shading each inequality’s half-plane, then keeping only the part they all share. With two inequalities you look for the overlap of two half-planes; with three you look for the overlap of three, and so on. Testing the origin in each inequality is still the fastest way to orient the individual half-planes, whenever the origin is not on that inequality’s own boundary; a boundary running through the origin, such as x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 or y≥0y \ge 0y≥0, needs one of the other off-line points instead.

Worked example 4 Graph the system y>x−1y > x - 1y>x−1 and y≤−x+3y \le -x + 3y≤−x+3

Handle one inequality at a time, then take the overlap.

The first inequality y>x−1y > x - 1y>x−1 has boundary y=x−1y = x - 1y=x−1, a line with slope 111 and yyy-intercept (0,−1)(0, -1)(0,−1). The symbol is strict, so the line is dashed. Testing the origin, 0>0−10 > 0 - 10>0−1 is 0>−10 > -10>−1, which is true, so shade the side containing the origin, above this line.

The second inequality y≤−x+3y \le -x + 3y≤−x+3 has boundary y=−x+3y = -x + 3y=−x+3, slope −1-1−1 and yyy-intercept (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3). The symbol is inclusive, so the line is solid. Testing the origin, 0≤−0+30 \le -0 + 30≤−0+3 is 0≤30 \le 30≤3, which is true, so shade the side containing the origin, below this line.

The two boundaries cross where x−1=−x+3x - 1 = -x + 3x−1=−x+3, that is 2x=42x = 42x=4, so x=2x = 2x=2 and then y=1y = 1y=1: the point (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1). That crossing point sits on the dashed boundary y=x−1y = x - 1y=x−1, so it marks where the two lines meet, not a solution of the system; it is excluded because the first inequality is strict. The feasible region is the wedge that is above the dashed line and below the solid line at the same time.

The origin sits inside the overlap, which agrees with both tests coming out true. A point in only one of the half-planes, such as (3,3)(3, 3)(3,3), fails the system. That point gives 3>23 > 23>2, true for the first inequality, but 3≤03 \le 03≤0, false for the second, so the point is left out of the feasible region.

Worked example 5 Graph the system x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, y≥0y \ge 0y≥0, and x+y≤4x + y \le 4x+y≤4

The first two inequalities are the special vertical and horizontal kind. The region x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 is on or to the right of the yyy-axis, and y≥0y \ge 0y≥0 is on or above the xxx-axis. Together they pin everything to the first quadrant. Both boundaries are solid, since both symbols are inclusive.

The third inequality x+y≤4x + y \le 4x+y≤4 has boundary x+y=4x + y = 4x+y=4, the line through (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0) and (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4), drawn solid. Testing the origin gives 0≤40 \le 40≤4, which is true, so its half-plane is the side toward the origin, below the line.

Overlapping all three leaves a triangle. Its edges are the two axes and the line x+y=4x + y = 4x+y=4, and its three corners, where the boundaries meet in pairs, are (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0), and (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4).

Every point of this triangle satisfies all three inequalities. The interior point (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1), for instance, has 1≥01 \ge 01≥0, then 1≥01 \ge 01≥0, and then 1+1=2≤41 + 1 = 2 \le 41+1=2≤4, all true. A point just outside, such as (3,3)(3, 3)(3,3), has 3+3=6≤43 + 3 = 6 \le 43+3=6≤4 false, so it is not in the feasible region even though it clears the first two conditions. A bounded region like this triangle, described by its edges and its corner points, is exactly the picture the next lesson builds on.

(0,2): 2<3 is true, and 2>0 is true(0, 2)\text{: } 2 < 3 \text{ is true, and } 2 > 0 \text{ is true}(0,2): 2<3 is true, and 2>0 is true

The point (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2) fails y>xy > xy>x because 2>42 > 42>4 is false; (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) fails y>xy > xy>x because 1>11 > 11>1 is false; (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) fails y<3y < 3y<3 because 5<35 < 35<3 is false. Only (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2) satisfies both.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Dashed and solid mixed up. A strict symbol (<<< or >>>) gives a dashed boundary because the line is excluded. An inclusive symbol (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥) gives a solid boundary because the line is included. Match the line style to the symbol, the same way you matched an open or filled circle on the number line.
  • Guessing the side instead of testing. Do not assume “greater than” always means shade upward. After you solve for yyy it often does, but the reliable move is to substitute a test point and shade by the true-or-false result.
  • Using the origin when the line runs through it. If the boundary passes through (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), the origin sits on the line, not on either side of it. Substituting it still gives a definite true or false statement, but that result only confirms whether the boundary is included, which the symbol already told you, and it cannot tell you which half-plane to shade. Pick another off-line point such as (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0) instead.
  • Forgetting to flip when you solve for yyy. Rewriting −2y>4x-2y > 4x−2y>4x as y<−2xy < -2xy<−2x means dividing by the negative −2-2−2, which reverses the symbol and also flips which side to shade. Testing a point sidesteps this trap completely.
  • Shading every half-plane instead of just the overlap. The solution of a system is where the half-planes all overlap, the points that satisfy every inequality at once. The solution is not every point that satisfies at least one of the inequalities.
  • Counting a point on a dashed edge as a solution. A point exactly on the boundary is a solution only when the boundary is solid. On a dashed line the substitution gives an equality, which a strict inequality rejects.

Key takeaways

  • The solution of a two-variable linear inequality is a half-plane, all the points (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) on one side of a boundary line.
  • The boundary is the line from the matching equation. Draw it dashed for a strict inequality (<<<, >>>) and solid for an inclusive one (≤\le≤, ≥\ge≥).
  • Choose the side with a test point: substitute a point that is not on the line. Shade that point’s side if the statement is true, and the other side if it is false. The origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) is easiest, unless the line passes through it.
  • Vertical boundaries come from inequalities in xxx alone (x>cx > cx>c) and horizontal boundaries from inequalities in yyy alone (y≤cy \le cy≤c); the method is unchanged.
  • Check a point by substitution. A point on the boundary is a solution only when the boundary is solid.
  • The solution of a system is the feasible region, the overlap where every inequality holds at once. Graph each half-plane, then keep only the common region.

A bit of history (optional)

One inequality in two variables is easy enough to draw once you know the trick. A list of twenty is another matter, and for a long time nobody thought to ask what such a list looks like.

Joseph Fourier asked, in the eighteen twenties. Fourier is remembered for explaining how heat creeps through a solid. That is work about waves and sums, not about shaded corners of a plane. But he also studied long lists of linear inequalities. He treated a whole list as one object. Each inequality slices the available space in two and throws one part away. What survives every slice is the region. Fourier wanted to know what a region of that kind can look like.

He also found a way to squeeze one variable out of the list at a time. It works much as you eliminate a variable from a system of equations. The procedure is slow, and for a century almost nobody had a use for it. What lasted was the picture underneath. A pile of demands has a shape, and you can point at it.

That picture is the whole of this lesson. Every half-plane you shaded is one slice, and the feasible region is what all the slices left behind.

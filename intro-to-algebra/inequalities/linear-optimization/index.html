Intuition

Suppose a workshop builds chairs and tables. Each chair earns 303030 dollars in profit and each table 505050 dollars, but the shop has only 888 hours of cutting time and 999 hours of assembly time each day. Which mix earns the most while staying within both time limits?

An optimization problem asks for the greatest or least value allowed by a set of restrictions. When the quantity and the restrictions are linear, a graph can turn the search into a short list of points to check.

Learning goals

  • Turn a word problem into an objective and linear constraints, with x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, y≥0y \ge 0y≥0
  • Explain why a linear objective's best value sits at a vertex
  • Find every vertex, evaluate the objective there, and pick the extreme
  • Say why an unbounded region may have no maximum

Constraints, the objective, and the feasible region

Suppose the allowed choices satisfy x+y≤4x+y\le4x+y≤4, x≥0x\ge0x≥0, and y≥0y\ge0y≥0. These constraints form the triangular feasible region from the last lesson. Each constraint keeps one half-plane, and the feasible region is their overlap. Every point in this triangle is an allowed choice, so there are infinitely many choices to compare. A condition such as 2x+y≥62x+y\ge62x+y≥6 would keep the side of its boundary away from the origin. In any model, keep only the overlap of all its constraints.

The constraints here use ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥, so feasible boundary points are included. This matters: if the best boundary point were excluded, there might be no greatest value to reach. Nonnegative amounts need x≥0x\ge0x≥0 and y≥0y\ge0y≥0. If the variables count separate items such as chairs, they must also be whole numbers; nonnegativity alone does not require that.

The objective function is the quantity to maximize or minimize. A function here is simply a rule that gives one value for each allowed pair. For example, P=3x+2yP=3x+2yP=3x+2y gives P=8P=8P=8 at (2,1)(2,1)(2,1). The general linear form is

P=ax+by,P = ax + by,P=ax+by,

where aaa and bbb are fixed numbers from the problem. We seek the largest value (a maximum) or smallest value (a minimum) among the feasible points. The corner-point principle lets us check a finite list instead of trying points at random.

Why the best value sits at a corner

For P=2x+3yP=2x+3yP=2x+3y, the equations 2x+3y=32x+3y=32x+3y=3, 2x+3y=62x+3y=62x+3y=6, and 2x+3y=92x+3y=92x+3y=9 describe points with objective values 333, 666, and 999. These are level lines: the objective is constant along each one.

For any level ccc, solving 2x+3y=c2x+3y=c2x+3y=c for yyy gives

y=−23x+c3.y=-\frac23x+\frac c3.y=−32​x+3c​.

The slope stays −23-\tfrac23−32​, so changing ccc slides the line without turning it. The level lines are parallel. Sliding these lines up and to the right raises PPP; sliding them the other way lowers it.

The corners of this region are (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0), (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1), and (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2). As the three dashed level lines slide toward larger PPP, the last one that still touches the region is P=9P = 9P=9, and it grazes only the corner (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1). So the maximum of P=2x+3yP = 2x + 3yP=2x+3y over this region is 999, at (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1).

For a nonempty, bounded feasible polygon with its boundary included, a nonconstant linear objective has a last level line that touches the region in each direction. That line meets a corner or an entire edge, including its two corners. Checking all the corners therefore finds both extremes.

Here bounded means the region fits inside some large box; nonempty means at least one point satisfies all the constraints. If a=b=0a=b=0a=b=0, then P=0P=0P=0 everywhere. Every feasible point ties, including every corner, and no sliding-line argument is needed.

Why a linear objective is optimized at a corner#

First take the triangle with corners (0,0)(0,0)(0,0), (2,0)(2,0)(2,0), and (0,2)(0,2)(0,2), and let P=x+2yP=x+2yP=x+2y. At the interior point (0.5,0.5)(0.5,0.5)(0.5,0.5), we get P=1.5P=1.5P=1.5. Moving up to (0.5,1)(0.5,1)(0.5,1) stays in the triangle and raises PPP to 2.52.52.5, so that interior point is not best. Moving up helps because the coefficient of yyy is positive. Along the slanted edge x+y=2x+y=2x+y=2, substitute x=2−yx=2-yx=2−y:

P=(2−y)+2y=2+y.P=(2-y)+2y=2+y.P=(2−y)+2y=2+y.

As yyy increases from 000 to 222, this climbs from 222 to 444, so that edge’s maximum is at (0,2)(0,2)(0,2). On either axis edge, increasing its nonzero coordinate also increases PPP. Checking the three corners gives 000, 222, and 444: the maximum is 444 at (0,2)(0,2)(0,2).

The same reasoning works for a nonempty, bounded feasible polygon with its boundary included and P=ax+byP=ax+byP=ax+by nonconstant. An interior point has room for a small move in any direction. If a≠0a\ne0a=0, move a little right when a>0a>0a>0, or left when a<0a<0a<0. Alternatively, when b≠0b\ne0b=0, move up for b>0b>0b>0 or down for b<0b<0b<0, as in the numerical example. At least one coefficient is nonzero, so one of these moves raises PPP while staying inside. Thus an interior point cannot maximize PPP.

Along a straight edge, equal steps change xxx and yyy by fixed amounts, so they change PPP by a fixed amount too. Its value rises, falls, or stays constant along the edge. An endpoint therefore matches or beats every other point of that edge. Any interior point can also be improved until reaching an edge: travel in the chosen direction to the boundary, which is reached because the polygon is bounded and includes that boundary.

Every feasible point is thus matched or beaten by a corner. There are finitely many corners, so the largest corner value is the maximum for the whole polygon. Reverse the improving direction to obtain the same conclusion for the minimum.

The corner-point method

The principle turns into a four-step recipe, for any feasible region that is bounded, has an included boundary, and has at least one point in it:

  1. Graph the feasible region from the constraints, exactly as in the last lesson.
  2. Find every vertex (corner) of the region. Each corner is where two boundary lines cross, so read off which two lines meet there and solve that pair as a 2×22 \times 22×2 system, the way you solved systems in an earlier chapter. Not every such crossing is a vertex of the region: check the result against every other constraint, and discard a crossing that fails one, since it lies outside the feasible region rather than on a corner of it.
  3. Evaluate the objective PPP at each vertex.
  4. Compare the values: the largest is the maximum and the smallest is the minimum, and the vertex where it happens is the point that achieves it.

Worked example 1 Maximize P=3x+2yP = 3x + 2yP=3x+2y subject to 2x+y≤62x + y \le 62x+y≤6, x+2y≤6x + 2y \le 6x+2y≤6, x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, y≥0y \ge 0y≥0

Start with the region. The two axes give the lower and left edges, since x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and y≥0y \ge 0y≥0 pin everything to the first quadrant. The line 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6 runs through (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0) and (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6), and testing the origin gives 0≤60 \le 60≤6, so its half-plane is the side toward the origin. The line x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 runs through (6,0)(6, 0)(6,0) and (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3), and again the origin satisfies it. Shading all four conditions leaves a four-cornered region.

Next, find the vertices. Two come from the axes meeting the slanted lines, and one comes from the two slanted lines meeting each other.

The origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) is where the axes cross. On the xxx-axis, the constraint that actually reaches down to meet it is 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6 with y=0y = 0y=0, giving x=3x = 3x=3, so (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0). On the yyy-axis, the constraint that reaches it is x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 with x=0x = 0x=0, giving y=3y = 3y=3, so (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3). The last corner is where 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6 and x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 cross. Subtracting the second from the first removes the constant:

(2x+y)−(x+2y)=0⟹x−y=0⟹x=y.(2x + y) - (x + 2y) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x - y = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = y.(2x+y)−(x+2y)=0⟹x−y=0⟹x=y.

The other axis crossings are not feasible: (6,0)(6,0)(6,0) gives 2x+y=12>62x+y=12>62x+y=12>6, and (0,6)(0,6)(0,6) gives x+2y=12>6x+2y=12>6x+2y=12>6. Discard them. Each of the four listed corners satisfies every constraint.

Finally, evaluate P=3x+2yP = 3x + 2yP=3x+2y at all four corners.

P(0,0)=0,P(3,0)=9,P(2,2)=6+4=10,P(0,3)=6.\begin{aligned} P(0,0)&=0,\\ P(3,0)&=9,\\ P(2,2)&=6+4=10,\\ P(0,3)&=6. \end{aligned}P(0,0)P(3,0)P(2,2)P(0,3)​=0,=9,=6+4=10,=6.​

The largest value is 101010, reached at (2,2)(2, 2)(2,2). So the maximum of P=3x+2yP = 3x + 2yP=3x+2y over this region is 10\mathbf{10}10, achieved at x=2x = 2x=2, y=2y = 2y=2. Notice the winner is the inside corner where the two slanted limits meet, not one of the axis corners, which is why checking every vertex matters.

When the region is unbounded

A nonempty, bounded feasible polygon with its boundary included has both extremes. An unbounded region stretches forever in some direction. Being unbounded may remove the maximum, the minimum, both, or neither: what matters is how the objective changes in the unbounded direction.

For example, if feasible points extend upward forever and raising yyy keeps raising PPP, there is no maximum. If that move lowers PPP instead, it prevents a minimum. For the nonempty regions here, with x,y≥0x,y\ge0x,y≥0 and inclusive linear constraints, any extreme that exists is still achieved at a corner. We use that extension here; its general proof belongs to later optimization courses. Check the unbounded directions as well as the corners before claiming an extreme exists.

Worked example 2 Minimize C=3x+2yC = 3x + 2yC=3x+2y subject to 2x+y≥42x + y \ge 42x+y≥4, 2x+3y≥82x + 3y \ge 82x+3y≥8, x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, y≥0y \ge 0y≥0

Because both slanted constraints are “greater than or equal to,” each half-plane is the side away from the origin. So the feasible region sits above and to the right of the two lines, and it runs off to infinity in that direction. The region is unbounded, which is fine for a minimum. Push the level line of C=3x+2yC = 3x + 2yC=3x+2y in the decreasing direction, toward the origin, and it last touches the region at a corner.

Find the corners. On the yyy-axis (x=0x = 0x=0), the constraint that reaches it is 2x+y=42x + y = 42x+y=4, giving y=4y = 4y=4, so (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4). On the xxx-axis (y=0y = 0y=0), the constraint that reaches it is 2x+3y=82x + 3y = 82x+3y=8, giving x=4x = 4x=4, so (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0). The middle corner is where 2x+y=42x + y = 42x+y=4 and 2x+3y=82x + 3y = 82x+3y=8 meet. Subtracting the first from the second removes 2x2x2x:

(2x+3y)−(2x+y)=8−4⟹2y=4⟹y=2,(2x + 3y) - (2x + y) = 8 - 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2y = 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = 2,(2x+3y)−(2x+y)=8−4⟹2y=4⟹y=2,

and then 2x+2=42x + 2 = 42x+2=4 gives x=1x = 1x=1: the vertex (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2).

Evaluate C=3x+2yC = 3x + 2yC=3x+2y at the three corners.

C(0,4)=8,C(1,2)=3+4=7,C(4,0)=12.\begin{aligned} C(0,4)&=8,\\ C(1,2)&=3+4=7,\\ C(4,0)&=12. \end{aligned}C(0,4)C(1,2)C(4,0)​=8,=3+4=7,=12.​

The smallest value is 777, at (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2), so the minimum cost is 7\mathbf{7}7. There is no maximum here: starting from any feasible point and moving up and to the right keeps every constraint satisfied while sending C=3x+2yC = 3x + 2yC=3x+2y upward without bound. Reporting “no maximum” is the honest and correct answer when the region is unbounded in the increasing direction.

A feasible region satisfies x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, y≥0y \ge 0y≥0, and x+y≥3x + y \ge 3x+y≥3, and it stretches upward and to the right without end. For the objective P=x+yP = x + yP=x+y, which is true?

Being unbounded does not erase every extreme; it depends on whether the region keeps growing where the objective keeps growing.

Optimization in word problems

Most optimization problems arrive as a story, not as ready-made inequalities. Your job is to name the variables, translate each limited resource into one inequality, add the non-negativity constraints, and write the objective. After that, the corner-point method finishes the work, provided the variables can take any value in the region; if they must be whole numbers because they count separate items, that adds one more thing to check, covered below.

Worked example 3 A workshop's best daily plan

A workshop builds chairs and tables. Each chair needs 222 hours of cutting and 111 hour of assembly. Each table needs 111 hour of cutting and 333 hours of assembly. In a day the shop has at most 888 hours of cutting and 999 hours of assembly available. Each chair brings a profit of 303030 dollars and each table 505050 dollars. How many of each should the shop build to maximize profit?

Every number in the story becomes one coefficient. Laying them out in a table makes the translation easy to check:

Table (yyy)111333505050

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Testing random interior points. The feasible region has infinitely many points, and picking a few from the middle never proves you found the best one. The corner-point principle is what lets you stop: whenever an optimum is attained, it is attained at a vertex, so checking the corners is enough, provided the region has one to check.
  • Missing a vertex. Every corner must go on the list. Skipping the inside corner where two slanted constraints cross is the most common slip, and that corner is often the one that wins, as in the worked examples.
  • Guessing a corner instead of solving for it. A vertex is the exact intersection of two boundary lines. Read off which two lines meet there and solve that 2×22 \times 22×2 system. Do not eyeball the coordinates off the grid, since a near-miss like (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) instead of (2,2)(2, 2)(2,2) changes the objective value.
  • Optimizing the wrong direction. Maximizing takes the largest objective value and minimizing takes the smallest. Compute the value at every corner first, then pick the end you actually want.
  • Expecting a maximum on an unbounded region. If the region runs off to infinity in the direction that increases the objective, there is no maximum. Only report the extreme that exists, and say so when the other one does not.
  • Forgetting the non-negativity limits. When the variables count real things, x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and y≥0y \ge 0y≥0 are real constraints. Leaving them out can invent corners in a quadrant the problem never allows.

Key takeaways

  • An optimization problem pairs an objective function P=ax+byP = ax + byP=ax+by to maximize or minimize with a system of linear constraints whose overlap is the feasible region.
  • Corner-point principle: a linear objective attains both extremes at corners of a nonempty, bounded feasible polygon with its boundary included. For a nonconstant objective, the last level line touches a corner or a whole edge. For a constant objective, every feasible point ties.
  • The method: graph the region, find every vertex by solving the pair of boundary lines that cross there as a 2×22 \times 22×2 system. Then evaluate the objective at each vertex, and take the largest (maximum) or smallest (minimum).
  • On a nonempty, bounded feasible polygon with its boundary included, both extremes exist. On an unbounded region one or both may not exist, because the objective can run off without limit; report only extremes that do exist.
  • For a word problem, name the variables, turn each resource limit into an inequality, add x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and y≥0y \ge 0y≥0, write the objective, and then run the corner-point method. If the variables count separate items, add that they must be whole numbers; the continuous optimum solves that version directly only when its coordinates already come out whole.

A bit of history (optional)

In 194719471947, George Dantzig was working on planning for the American air force. Supply schedules involved many quantities and restrictions, so listing every feasible corner was impractical. He developed the simplex method. On a bounded feasible region, its basic idea is to start at a corner, step to a better neighboring corner, and stop when no neighboring corner improves the value. This searches through corners rather than testing every one in advance.

The route was not obvious to him. In his later account, Dantzig recalled first dismissing movement along edges as likely to be inefficient. Further work led him back to a method based on those moves. The resulting procedure made large planning calculations practical and became a major tool of linear programming.

There was an earlier, independent line of work too. The Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich studied how to organize production and published his approach in 193919391939.

Solving Linear Inequalities

Intuition

You have spent several lessons solving equations by undoing operations until the variable stands alone. Solving an inequality works the exact same way. To solve 3x+4<193x + 4 < 193x+4<19 you peel off the +4+4+4 and then the ×3\times 3×3, one inverse at a time, just as you would for 3x+4=193x + 4 = 193x+4=19. There is a single new rule, and it is the whole reason this lesson exists. Whenever you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, you reverse the inequality symbol. Nothing else about the procedure changes. The payoff is different from an equation, though. An equation usually pins down one value, while an inequality hands you a whole solution set, often a ray or a band of numbers, and sometimes something less simple: two separate rays, every real number, or none at all. You can read that solution set as an inequality, graph it on a number line, and write it as an interval.

Learning goals

  • Isolate the variable with the same inverse moves as an equation
  • Flip the symbol only when scaling by a negative
  • Collect the variable where its coefficient stays positive
  • Operate on all three parts of an and compound at once
  • Union the pieces of an or compound
  • Read a cancelled variable as all reals or no solution

Isolating the variable, one step at a time

Take the one-step inequality x+7>3x + 7 > 3x+7>3. Subtract 777 from both sides. Subtraction never disturbs the symbol, so x>−4x > -4x>−4. The solution set is every number greater than −4-4−4, which is an open circle on −4-4−4 with the shading running right, written in interval notation as (−4,∞)(-4, \infty)(−4,∞): a parenthesis means the number next to it is excluded, a square bracket would mean it is included, and infinity always gets a parenthesis, since no number ever actually reaches it.

That step used a move settled in the previous lesson: adding or subtracting the same quantity from both sides keeps the direction. Multiplying or dividing both sides by a positive number keeps the direction too. So as long as you only add, subtract, or scale by a positive number, solving an inequality is identical to solving the matching equation. Solving the inequality then means applying inverse operations until the variable is alone, undoing the outermost operation first. A two-step inequality simply undoes two operations in turn.

Worked example 1 Solve 3x+4<193x + 4 < 193x+4<19

Undo the operations from the outside in. First subtract 444 from both sides, which does not change the direction:

3x+4−4<19−4⟹3x<15.3x + 4 - 4 < 19 - 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3x < 15.3x+4−4<19−4⟹3x<15.

Now divide both sides by 333. The divisor is positive, so the symbol stays put:

3x3<153⟹x<5.\frac{3x}{3} < \frac{15}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x < 5.33x​<315​⟹x<5.

Read the answer three matching ways. As an inequality it is x<5x < 5x<5. On a number line it is an open circle on 555 (strict, so 555 is excluded) with the shading running left toward the smaller numbers. In interval notation it is (−∞,5)(-\infty, 5)(−∞,5).

Confirm the direction by testing one value inside the solution set and one outside it, always against the original inequality. The value x=0x = 0x=0 should be inside, since 0<50 < 50<5: the original gives 3(0)+4=43(0) + 4 = 43(0)+4=4, and 4<194 < 194<19 is true. The value x=6x = 6x=6 should be outside, since 6<56 < 56<5 is false: the original gives 3(6)+4=223(6) + 4 = 223(6)+4=22, and 22<1922 < 1922<19 is false, exactly as the solution predicts.

The negative flip in action

Watch it happen with plain numbers first, no variable involved. The statement 2<52 < 52<5 is true. Multiply both sides by −1-1−1: 222 becomes −2-2−2, and 555 becomes −5-5−5. Is −2<−5-2 < -5−2<−5 still true? No: −2-2−2 is the larger of the two, so the true statement is now −2>−5-2 > -5−2>−5. On a number line, multiplying by a negative number reflects every point across 000, so left and right trade places; whichever number used to be smaller is now larger. That reflection is the entire reason the symbol has to reverse.

Everything above stays the same until a step forces you to divide or multiply both sides by a negative number. That single step reverses the symbol. Be precise about the trigger. The flip is caused only by multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number. It is not caused by a negative sign sitting somewhere in the problem, and it is not caused by adding or subtracting a negative. Subtracting 777, even though it leaves a smaller number behind, never flips anything. Only the sign of the number you scale both sides by matters.

Worked example 2 Solve 5−3x≥115 - 3x \ge 115−3x≥11

Start by moving the constant. Subtract 555 from both sides, which does not touch the direction:

5−3x−5≥11−5⟹−3x≥6.5 - 3x - 5 \ge 11 - 5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -3x \ge 6.5−3x−5≥11−5⟹−3x≥6.

Now isolate xxx by dividing both sides by −3-3−3. The divisor is negative, so reverse ≥\ge≥ to ≤\le≤:

−3x−3≤6−3⟹x≤−2.\frac{-3x}{-3} \le \frac{6}{-3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x \le -2.−3−3x​≤−36​⟹x≤−2.

The flip happened at the divide-by-negative step, not at the earlier subtraction. As an inequality the answer is x≤−2x \le -2x≤−2. On a number line it is a filled circle on −2-2−2 (inclusive, so −2-2−2 is included) with the shading running left; in interval notation it is (−∞,−2](-\infty, -2](−∞,−2].

Test a value on each side. Inside, x=−5x = -5x=−5 satisfies x≤−2x \le -2x≤−2: the original gives 5−3(−5)=5+15=205 - 3(-5) = 5 + 15 = 205−3(−5)=5+15=20, and 20≥1120 \ge 1120≥11 is true. Outside, x=0x = 0x=0 fails x≤−2x \le -2x≤−2: the original gives 5−3(0)=55 - 3(0) = 55−3(0)=5, and 5≥115 \ge 115≥11 is false. Both checks agree with x≤−2x \le -2x≤−2.

Why is the flip forced, rather than an arbitrary rule you must simply remember? Because you can always avoid dividing by a negative altogether, and when you do, the flip appears on its own.

Why dividing by a negative reverses the symbol#

Watch the idea work on numbers first. Take −5x>15-5x > 15−5x>15. Instead of dividing by −5-5−5, add 5x5x5x to both sides so the variable lands where its coefficient is positive:

0>15+5x.0 > 15 + 5x.0>15+5x.

Read from the other end, that is 15+5x<015 + 5x < 015+5x<0. Subtract 151515 to get 5x<−155x < -155x<−15, then divide by the positive number 555, a move that never flips anything:

x<−3.x < -3.x<−3.

Check it against the shortcut: dividing −5x>15-5x > 15−5x>15 straight through by −5-5−5 and flipping the symbol also gives x<−3x < -3x<−3. Same answer, and the second route never divided by a negative number at all.

The same idea works for any starting numbers. Take any inequality whose variable carries a negative coefficient, written in general as −kx>c-kx > c−kx>c with kkk a positive number. Instead of dividing by −k-k−k, move the variable term to the other side, where its coefficient will be positive. Add kxkxkx to both sides. The left side collapses to 000 and the right becomes c+kxc + kxc+kx, so the statement now reads 0>c+kx0 > c + kx0>c+kx, which is the same as c+kx<0c + kx < 0c+kx<0 read from the other end. Subtract ccc from both sides to get kx<−ckx < -ckx<−c. Because kkk is positive, dividing both sides by kkk keeps the direction, and this gives

x<−ck.x < -\frac{c}{k}.x<−kc​.

Now compare that with the shortcut of dividing the original −kx>c-kx > c−kx>c straight through by −k-k−k and flipping the symbol. That produces x<c−kx < \dfrac{c}{-k}x<−kc​, and since c−k=−ck\dfrac{c}{-k} = -\dfrac{c}{k}−kc​=−kc​, it is the identical statement

x<−ck.x < -\frac{c}{k}.x<−kc​.

Both routes land on exactly the same solution. So the flip is nothing more than a shortcut for moving the variable to the side where its coefficient is positive. That route then divides by the positive number, a move you already trust completely. That is also why the reversal happens at the divide-by-a-negative step, and only there, never for an ordinary addition or subtraction.

Variables on both sides

When the variable appears on both sides, first gather the variable terms on one side and the constants on the other. Use addition and subtraction for that gathering, then finish by dividing. You have a real choice about which side to collect on, and a smart choice can save you the flip entirely. Move the variable to whichever side leaves its coefficient positive, so the final division is by a positive number. Both choices reach the same solution set, as the proof above guarantees. So if you do end up with a negative coefficient, just flip the symbol and you are still correct.

Worked example 3 Solve 4x−3>7x+94x - 3 > 7x + 94x−3>7x+9, two ways

The variable terms are 4x4x4x and 7x7x7x. Collecting on the side with the larger coefficient, the right, keeps that coefficient positive and avoids any flip. Subtract 4x4x4x from both sides:

4x−3−4x>7x+9−4x⟹−3>3x+9.4x - 3 - 4x > 7x + 9 - 4x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -3 > 3x + 9.4x−3−4x>7x+9−4x⟹−3>3x+9.

Subtract 999 from both sides, then divide by the positive number 333:

−12>3x⟹−4>x,-12 > 3x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -4 > x,−12>3x⟹−4>x,

which reads x<−4x < -4x<−4 from the other end. No flip was ever needed.

Collecting on the left instead reaches the same place through a flip. Subtract 7x7x7x from both sides to get −3x−3>9-3x - 3 > 9−3x−3>9, add 333 to get −3x>12-3x > 12−3x>12, then divide by −3-3−3 and reverse the symbol:

−3x−3<12−3⟹x<−4.\frac{-3x}{-3} < \frac{12}{-3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x < -4.−3−3x​<−312​⟹x<−4.

Both routes give x<−4x < -4x<−4, the interval (−∞,−4)(-\infty, -4)(−∞,−4). A quick check confirms it: x=−5x = -5x=−5 is inside, and the original reads 4(−5)−3=−234(-5) - 3 = -234(−5)−3=−23 against 7(−5)+9=−267(-5) + 9 = -267(−5)+9=−26, where −23>−26-23 > -26−23>−26 is true; x=0x = 0x=0 is outside, and −3>9-3 > 9−3>9 is false.

Distributing first

If the inequality contains parentheses, clear them with the distributive property first, then collect and isolate as usual. The negative-flip rule still applies only at a multiply-or-divide-by-a-negative step, so watch for it once the parentheses are gone.

Worked example 4 Solve −2(x−4)<10-2(x - 4) < 10−2(x−4)<10

Distribute the −2-2−2 across the parentheses, being careful with signs: −2⋅x=−2x-2 \cdot x = -2x−2⋅x=−2x and −2⋅(−4)=+8-2 \cdot (-4) = +8−2⋅(−4)=+8, so

−2x+8<10.-2x + 8 < 10.−2x+8<10.

Subtract 888 from both sides to get −2x<2-2x < 2−2x<2. Now divide both sides by −2-2−2; the divisor is negative, so reverse <<< to >>>:

−2x−2>2−2⟹x>−1.\frac{-2x}{-2} > \frac{2}{-2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x > -1.−2−2x​>−22​⟹x>−1.

The answer is x>−1x > -1x>−1, an open circle on −1-1−1 with the shading running right, the interval (−1,∞)(-1, \infty)(−1,∞).

As a check, x=0x = 0x=0 is inside and gives −2(0−4)=8-2(0 - 4) = 8−2(0−4)=8, with 8<108 < 108<10 true, while x=−2x = -2x=−2 is outside and gives −2(−2−4)=12-2(-2 - 4) = 12−2(−2−4)=12, with 12<1012 < 1012<10 false.

Compound inequalities

A compound inequality states two conditions at once, and it comes in two kinds: an “and” compound, where both conditions must hold together, and an “or” compound, where either one is enough.

”And” compounds

An “and” compound written as a<expression<ba < \text{expression} < ba<expression<b requires the expression to obey both bounds. So you solve that compound by doing the same operation to all three parts at the same time, keeping the variable in the middle. Every rule carries over, including the flip: if you multiply or divide all three parts by a negative number, reverse both symbols. Because that negative number also changes the sign of the two bounds, they swap ends, so rewrite the result with the smaller number on the left.

As a warm-up, solve −1≤2x+3<9-1 \le 2x + 3 < 9−1≤2x+3<9. Subtract 333 from all three parts to get −4≤2x<6-4 \le 2x < 6−4≤2x<6, then divide all three parts by the positive number 222 to reach −2≤x<3-2 \le x < 3−2≤x<3, the interval [−2,3)[-2, 3)[−2,3). No flip occurs, because 222 is positive. The next example does need the flip.

Worked example 5 Solve −7<−2x+1≤5-7 < -2x + 1 \le 5−7<−2x+1≤5

Work on all three parts together. First subtract 111 from each part, which does not change either symbol:

−7−1<−2x+1−1≤5−1⟹−8<−2x≤4.-7 - 1 < -2x + 1 - 1 \le 5 - 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -8 < -2x \le 4.−7−1<−2x+1−1≤5−1⟹−8<−2x≤4.

Now divide all three parts by −2-2−2. The divisor is negative, so reverse both symbols. Dividing −8-8−8 by −2-2−2 gives 444, dividing −2x-2x−2x by −2-2−2 gives xxx, and dividing 444 by −2-2−2 gives −2-2−2:

4>x≥−2.4 > x \ge -2.4>x≥−2.

Rewrite it with the smaller number on the left, which is the standard reading:

−2≤x<4.-2 \le x < 4.−2≤x<4.

On a number line this is a filled circle on −2-2−2, an open circle on 444, and a shaded band between them; in interval notation it is [−2,4)[-2, 4)[−2,4).

Test the endpoints. At x=−2x = -2x=−2 the expression is −2(−2)+1=5-2(-2) + 1 = 5−2(−2)+1=5, and −7<5≤5-7 < 5 \le 5−7<5≤5 holds, so −2-2−2 is in, matching the filled circle. At x=4x = 4x=4 the expression is −2(4)+1=−7-2(4) + 1 = -7−2(4)+1=−7, and −7<−7-7 < -7−7<−7 is false, so 444 is out, matching the open circle.

Solve the compound inequality −3≤2x−1<5-3 \le 2x - 1 < 5−3≤2x−1<5 and write the solution as an interval.

Answer choices

Operate on all three parts at once. Add 111 to each part, which keeps both symbols, then divide each part by the positive number 222.

−3≤2x−1<5  ⇒  −2≤2x<6  ⇒  −1≤x<3-3 \le 2x - 1 < 5 \;\Rightarrow\; -2 \le 2x < 6 \;\Rightarrow\; -1 \le x < 3−3≤2x−1<5⇒−2≤2x<6⇒−1≤x<3

The left bound is inclusive, so it takes a bracket, and the right bound is strict, so it takes a parenthesis, giving [−1,3)[-1, 3)[−1,3).

”Or” compounds

An “or” compound is a pair of separate inequalities joined by the word “or”. Solve each one on its own, then combine the two solution sets. A value counts as a solution when it satisfies either piece.

Worked example 6 Solve x+4<3x + 4 < 3x+4<3 or 2x≥42x \ge 42x≥4

Solve each inequality by itself. The first gives

x+4<3⟹x<−1,x + 4 < 3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x < -1,x+4<3⟹x<−1,

and the second gives

2x≥4⟹x≥2.2x \ge 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x \ge 2.2x≥4⟹x≥2.

The solution set is every number that is below −1-1−1 or at least 222. The two pieces do not overlap, so the graph is two separate rays: an open circle on −1-1−1 shaded left, and a filled circle on 222 shaded right. In interval notation the two rays are joined by the union symbol ∪\cup∪, read “union” and meaning every number that belongs to either piece, giving (−∞,−1)∪[2,∞)(-\infty, -1) \cup [2, \infty)(−∞,−1)∪[2,∞).

A spot check confirms the reading: x=−2x = -2x=−2 satisfies the first piece, x=5x = 5x=5 satisfies the second, and x=0x = 0x=0 satisfies neither, so it is correctly left out of the shading.

Answer choices

(−∞,−3)∪[4,∞)(-\infty, -3) \cup [4, \infty)(−∞,−3)∪[4,∞)

The band (−3,4)(-3, 4)(−3,4) is the gap between the two rays: it satisfies neither piece, so it never belongs to an "or" solution. Forgetting to flip the first inequality would instead wrongly stretch the left ray to (−3,∞)(-3, \infty)(−3,∞).

When the variable disappears

Once in a while every copy of the variable cancels while you are solving, and what is left is a statement with no variable in it at all. That leftover is either plainly true or plainly false, and it decides the whole problem. If a false statement remains, no value of xxx can rescue it, so the inequality has no solution and its solution set is empty. If a true statement remains, the inequality held no matter what xxx was, so every real number is a solution.

Worked example 7 Two inequalities where the variable cancels

Solve 2x+5<2x+12x + 5 < 2x + 12x+5<2x+1. Subtract 2x2x2x from both sides, and the variable disappears:

5<1.5 < 1.5<1.

That leftover is false, and there is no xxx left to change it, so the inequality has no solution. This is the shape of x<x−1x < x - 1x<x−1: no number can be less than one below itself.

Now solve 3x−4≤3x+23x - 4 \le 3x + 23x−4≤3x+2. Subtract 3x3x3x from both sides:

−4≤2.-4 \le 2.−4≤2.

That leftover is true and free of xxx, so the original was true for every value of xxx. The solution is all real numbers, the interval (−∞,∞)(-\infty, \infty)(−∞,∞). This is the shape of x≤x+1x \le x + 1x≤x+1: a number is always at most one more than itself.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to flip after a negative. Dividing or multiplying both sides by a negative number reverses the symbol. Turning −3x≥6-3x \ge 6−3x≥6 into x≥−2x \ge -2x≥−2 is wrong; the correct result is x≤−2x \le -2x≤−2. This is one of the most common inequality errors, and the wrong answer is always the right bound with the wrong direction.
  • Flipping when you should not. The reversal happens only for multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative. Adding or subtracting a number, even a negative one, never flips the symbol, and a negative sitting elsewhere in the problem is irrelevant.
  • Flipping because the answer looks negative. The sign of your final answer is not the trigger. Solving 2x>−102x > -102x>−10 gives x>−5x > -5x>−5 with no flip at all, because you divided by the positive number 222.
  • In a compound inequality, skipping a part. Whatever you do, do it to all three parts. When you divide the whole chain by a negative, reverse both symbols, and remember the bounds swap ends so the smaller number ends up on the left.
  • Misreading the special cases. When the variable cancels, a true leftover means all real numbers and a false leftover means no solution. Do not write "x=x =x=" something there, and do not treat an empty solution set as if it were the number zero.
  • Endpoint mismatch on the graph. A strict symbol (<<< or >>>) gets an open circle; an inclusive symbol (≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥) gets a filled circle. Match the circle to the final symbol, not to the one you started with.

Key takeaways

  • Solve an inequality the way you solve an equation: undo operations with inverse moves until the variable stands alone.
  • The one extra rule is the flip: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the symbol, while adding or subtracting never does.
  • With the variable on both sides, collect it where its coefficient stays positive to sidestep the flip; either collection choice gives the same solution set.
  • Clear parentheses by distributing first, then isolate.
  • Solve an and compound a<expression<ba < \text{expression} < ba<expression<b by operating on all three parts at once, flipping both symbols if you scale by a negative. Solve an or compound by solving each piece and taking the union of the results.
  • If the variable cancels, a true leftover means all real numbers and a false leftover means no solution.
  • Present the answer three matching ways: as an inequality, on a number line (open or filled endpoint, shaded toward the solutions), and as an interval.

A bit of history (optional)

Take two numbers on the line, one of them larger. Multiply both by −2-2−2 and the larger one is now the smaller. That exchange is this lesson’s flip, and it was once treated as a scandal.

The complaint belongs to Antoine Arnauld. He was a French scholar of the sixteen hundreds, and he wrote on logic, theology and geometry. In 166716671667 he brought out a book of geometry. He refused one line of the algebra of his day: the claim that 111 is to −1-1−1 as −1-1−1 is to 111.

Read that claim aloud and you can hear what stung him. On the left a larger quantity is compared with a smaller one. On the right a smaller quantity is compared with a larger. A ratio was supposed to record which of the two was which. Here a single ratio described both arrangements at once, so the record looked worthless.

Arnauld was not being slow. He had caught a genuine fact and mistaken it for a contradiction. Comparison does not survive a negative multiplier. His century wanted the order of two numbers to stand untouched by arithmetic. Order is not that durable.

The modern reply is that the reversal is provable rather than embarrassing. Multiplying by a negative reflects the whole line through zero, and a reflection trades left for right.

You proved the same thing here without the mirror. Move the variable to the side where its coefficient stays positive. Divide by that positive number, and the reversed symbol appears on its own. Arnauld’s scandal is your flip, and you now own the reason for it.

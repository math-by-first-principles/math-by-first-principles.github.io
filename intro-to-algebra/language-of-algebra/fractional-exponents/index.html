Fractional Exponents and Radicals

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

For a nonzero base, zero and negative powers were defined so the familiar exponent laws keep holding: a0=1a^0=1a0=1 and a−n=1/ana^{-n}=1/a^na−n=1/an. What could a fractional exponent mean? You cannot multiply a number by itself “half a time.” Asking what the laws require gives fractional powers their meaning and connects powers with roots.

Learning goals

  • Explain why a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 is a square root and the principal-root convention selects a\sqrt{a}a​
  • Read the denominator as the root index and the numerator as the power
  • Switch between a radical and a fractional exponent in either direction
  • Pull perfect-power factors out, so 72\sqrt{72}72​ is 626\sqrt{2}62​
  • Combine like radicals only, matching index and radicand
  • Require a nonnegative radicand for an even index, unlike an odd one

What a one-half power must mean

The product rule adds exponents when you multiply powers of the same base. Keeping that rule for a one-half power would give

91/2⋅91/2=91/2+1/2=9.9^{1/2}\cdot9^{1/2}=9^{1/2+1/2}=9.91/2⋅91/2=91/2+1/2=9.

So 91/29^{1/2}91/2 must be a number that squares to 999: either 333 or −3-3−3. A convention chooses one.

Why a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 must equal a\sqrt{a}a​#

Keeping the product rule gives 161/2⋅161/2=161/2+1/2=1616^{1/2}\cdot16^{1/2}=16^{1/2+1/2}=16161/2⋅161/2=161/2+1/2=16, so 161/216^{1/2}161/2 is 444 or −4-4−4, and we choose 444, as the radical sign does. For any nonnegative aaa, the same rule gives a1/2⋅a1/2=aa^{1/2}\cdot a^{1/2}=aa1/2⋅a1/2=a, so a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 is a square root of aaa. Choosing the principal root, the nonnegative one, gives a1/2=aa^{1/2}=\sqrt{a}a1/2=a​ (which is 000 at a=0a=0a=0). A negative base has no real square root, because squaring a real number never gives a negative result.

The nth root

An nnnth root of aaa is a number whose nnnth power is aaa, where nnn is a positive whole number. The symbol an\sqrt[n]{a}na​ is read “the nnnth root of aaa.” The small number nnn is the index; the number aaa underneath is the radicand. A square root has index 222, usually left unwritten. A cube root has index 333 and asks for three equal factors: 23=82^3=823=8, so 83=2\sqrt[3]{8}=238​=2. A unit-fraction exponent has the form 1/n1/n1/n, with nnn a positive whole number, and a1/na^{1/n}a1/n uses this same root idea.

Why a1/na^{1/n}a1/n is the nnnth root of aaa#

Keeping the power-of-a-power rule gives (271/3)3=27(1/3)⋅3=27(27^{1/3})^3=27^{(1/3)\cdot3}=27(271/3)3=27(1/3)⋅3=27. Since 333 is the only real number whose cube is 272727, 271/3=327^{1/3}=3271/3=3, the cube root of 272727. In general, whenever the root is real,

(a1/n)n=a(1/n)⋅n=a.\left(a^{1/n}\right)^n=a^{(1/n)\cdot n}=a.(a1/n)n=a(1/n)⋅n=a.

We use the real-number fact that every nonnegative radicand has a nonnegative nnnth root; it is unique because increasing a nonnegative number increases its power.

Take an even index, n=6n=6n=6. Both 262^626 and (−2)6(-2)^6(−2)6 equal 646464, because negative factors pair up: (−2)6=(−2)(−2)⋅(−2)(−2)⋅(−2)(−2)=4⋅4⋅4(-2)^6=(-2)(-2)\cdot(-2)(-2)\cdot(-2)(-2)=4\cdot4\cdot4(−2)6=(−2)(−2)⋅(−2)(−2)⋅(−2)(−2)=4⋅4⋅4. So 646464 has two sixth roots, and we choose the nonnegative one, 222; and no real number has sixth power −64-64−64. In general, for an even nnn, negative factors pair up, so a positive aaa has two roots, rrr and −r-r−r (we choose the nonnegative one), zero has root 000, and a negative aaa has none because an even power is never negative. An even index requires a≥0a\ge0a≥0.

Now take an odd index. A cube root of −8-8−8 must be negative, because positive numbers have positive cubes and zero has cube zero. If the root is −d-d−d with d>0d>0d>0, then (−d)3=−(d3)=−8(-d)^3=-(d^3)=-8(−d)3=−(d3)=−8, so d3=8d^3=8d3=8 and d=83=2d=\sqrt[3]{8}=2d=38​=2. Thus −83=−2\sqrt[3]{-8}=-23−8​=−2; indeed (−2)3=−8(-2)^3=-8(−2)3=−8. For any odd nnn, pairing leaves one factor unpaired, so an odd power has the same sign as its base, and the root must have the same sign as aaa. Since (−d)n=−(dn)(-d)^n=-(d^n)(−d)n=−(dn), the root’s distance ddd from zero has dnd^ndn equal to aaa‘s distance from zero, and exactly one nonnegative number does. So there is exactly one real root. With these conventions, a1/n=ana^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}a1/n=na​.

Raising to a fraction like two-thirds

A fractional exponent such as 23\tfrac2332​ combines a root and a power. For 82/38^{2/3}82/3, taking the cube root first gives (83)2=22=4\left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4(38​)2=22=4. Squaring first gives 823=643=4\sqrt[3]{8^2} = \sqrt[3]{64} = 4382​=364​=4: the same answer, by way of the larger number 646464.

For a nonnegative base, the two orders always agree, and the power-of-a-power rule explains why.

Why am/n=(an)m=amna^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m = \sqrt[n]{a^m}am/n=(na​)m=nam​#

Consider 642/364^{2/3}642/3. Taking the cube root first gives 42=164^2=1642=16; squaring first gives 40963=16\sqrt[3]{4096}=1634096​=16. The two routes split the exponent 23\tfrac2332​ as 13⋅2\tfrac13\cdot231​⋅2 or 2⋅132\cdot\tfrac132⋅31​.

For a≥0a\ge0a≥0 and positive whole numbers m,nm,nm,n, apply the power-of-a-power rule in each order:

am/n=(a1/n)m=(an)m,am/n=(am)1/n=amn.\begin{aligned} a^{m/n}&=\left(a^{1/n}\right)^m=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m,\\ a^{m/n}&=\left(a^m\right)^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}. \end{aligned}am/nam/n​=(a1/n)m=(na​)m,=(am)1/n=nam​.​

Both expressions equal am/na^{m/n}am/n. The denominator nnn is the root index and the numerator mmm is the power. Taking the root first usually keeps the numbers smaller.

Converting between radicals and exponents

For a≥0a\ge0a≥0 and positive whole numbers m,nm,nm,n, the identity (an)m=am/n=amn\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m=a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}(na​)m=am/n=nam​ reads in either direction: the root index becomes the exponent’s denominator, and the power becomes its numerator. Radical form often helps you evaluate; exponent form helps you combine powers.

Worked example 3 Rewrite 734\sqrt[4]{7^3}473​ with a fractional exponent, and evaluate 95/29^{5/2}95/2

For 734\sqrt[4]{7^3}473​, the index 444 becomes the denominator and the inside power 333 the numerator:

734=73/4.\sqrt[4]{7^3} = 7^{3/4}.473​=73/4.

For 95/29^{5/2}95/2, read it back as a radical: the denominator 222 calls for a square root and the numerator 555 for a fifth power:

95/2=(9)5=35=243.9^{5/2} = \left(\sqrt{9}\right)^5 = 3^5 = 243.95/2=(9​)5=35=243.

That is far easier than computing 95=590499^5 = 5904995=59049 and then taking its square root.

For a positive base, the exponent laws also work with rational exponents (exponents that are fractions). An exponent of zero still gives a0=1a^0=1a0=1, and a negative rational exponent still means a reciprocal: a−r=1ara^{-r}=\tfrac{1}{a^r}a−r=ar1​ for r>0r>0r>0. Converting roots to exponents lets you combine different indices:

64 643=641/2+1/3=645/6=25=32.\sqrt{64}\,\sqrt[3]{64}=64^{1/2+1/3}=64^{5/6}=2^5=32.64​364​=641/2+1/3=645/6=25=32.

This agrees with 8⋅4=328\cdot4=328⋅4=32. A common denominator explains why: 646=2\sqrt[6]{64}=2664​=2 because 26=642^6=6426=64, so 64=23\sqrt{64}=2^364​=23 and 643=22\sqrt[3]{64}=2^2364​=22, and their product is 23+22^{3+2}23+2. In general, for positive bases a,ba,ba,b and rational exponents r,sr,sr,s,

aras=ar+s,ar/as=ar−s,(ar)s=ars,(ab)r=arbr.\begin{gathered} a^r a^s=a^{r+s},\qquad a^r/a^s=a^{r-s},\\ (a^r)^s=a^{rs},\qquad (ab)^r=a^r b^r. \end{gathered}aras=ar+s,ar/as=ar−s,(ar)s=ars,(ab)r=arbr.​

For the first law, write r=m/nr=m/nr=m/n and s=p/ns=p/ns=p/n with m,pm,pm,p integers and nnn a positive whole number, and set t=ant=\sqrt[n]{a}t=na​, so ak/n=tka^{k/n}=t^kak/n=tk for any integer kkk; then aras=tmtp=tm+p=ar+sa^r a^s=t^m t^p=t^{m+p}=a^{r+s}aras=tmtp=tm+p=ar+s by the integer exponent law. The other laws follow the same way. Positive bases avoid the problems of negative ones; for example, the power-of-a-power rule cannot be used freely with a negative base:

((−8)2)1/2=64=8,(−8)2⋅(1/2)=(−8)1=−8.\begin{gathered}\left((-8)^2\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{64}=8,\\(-8)^{2\cdot(1/2)}=(-8)^1=-8.\end{gathered}((−8)2)1/2=64​=8,(−8)2⋅(1/2)=(−8)1=−8.​

Simplifying radicals

The product rule for roots splits a root across a product, provided every root involved is real. For even indices, both factors must be nonnegative. For odd indices, either factor may be negative. Pre-Algebra’s product rule for square roots is the case n=2n=2n=2 of this more general rule.

Why abn=an bn\sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]{a}\,\sqrt[n]{b}nab​=na​nb​#

For 9⋅49\cdot49⋅4, the product of the square roots is 3⋅2=63\cdot2=63⋅2=6. Squaring it gives 62=36=9⋅46^2=36=9\cdot462=36=9⋅4, and 666 is nonnegative. Therefore 9⋅4=94\sqrt{9\cdot4}=\sqrt9\sqrt49⋅4​=9​4​.

For a general index nnn, put u=anu=\sqrt[n]{a}u=na​ and v=bnv=\sqrt[n]{b}v=nb​. Then un=au^n=aun=a and vn=bv^n=bvn=b, so the integer power rule gives

(uv)n=unvn=ab.(uv)^n=u^n v^n=ab.(uv)n=unvn=ab.

Thus uvuvuv is an nnnth root of ababab. For an even index, take a,b≥0a,b\ge0a,b≥0; then u,vu,vu,v and their product are nonnegative, so uvuvuv is the principal root. For an odd index, the real root is unique, including when a factor is negative. In both cases,

abn=an bn.\sqrt[n]{ab}=\sqrt[n]{a}\,\sqrt[n]{b}.nab​=na​nb​.

∎

For a quotient, 50/2\sqrt{50}/\sqrt250​/2​ is positive and its square is 50/2=2550/2=2550/2=25, so it is the positive square root of 252525, namely 555. The same reasoning gives

abn=anbn,\sqrt[n]{\frac ab}=\frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}},nba​​=nb​na​​,

with bbb nonzero, and at an even index aaa nonnegative and bbb positive. Raising the right side to the nnnth power gives a/ba/ba/b, with the required nonnegative sign when nnn is even.

To simplify a radical, pull out any factor that is a perfect power for the index: a perfect square under a square root, a perfect cube under a cube root. Split the radicand into that factor times whatever is left, then use the product rule to take the root of the factor.

Worked example 4 Simplify 72\sqrt{72}72​ and 803\sqrt[3]{80}380​

The largest perfect-square factor of 727272 is 363636, since 72=36⋅272 = 36 \cdot 272=36⋅2:

72=36⋅2=36 2=62.\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{36}\,\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}.72​=36⋅2​=36​2​=62​.

For 803\sqrt[3]{80}380​, the largest perfect-cube factor is 8=238 = 2^38=23, since 80=8⋅1080 = 8 \cdot 1080=8⋅10:

803=8⋅103=83 103=2103.\sqrt[3]{80} = \sqrt[3]{8 \cdot 10} = \sqrt[3]{8}\,\sqrt[3]{10} = 2\sqrt[3]{10}.380​=38⋅10​=38​310​=2310​.

Neither leftover radicand, 222 or 101010, has a further perfect-power factor for its index, so both are fully simplified.

Adding and subtracting radicals

Two radicals are like radicals when they have the same index and the same radicand, such as 232\sqrt{3}23​ and 535\sqrt{3}53​. Like radicals combine the way like terms do, because the shared radical is a common factor, pulled out by the distributive law:

23+53=(2+5)3=73.2\sqrt{3} + 5\sqrt{3} = (2 + 5)\sqrt{3} = 7\sqrt{3}.23​+53​=(2+5)3​=73​.

No general rule splits a root across a sum: 9+16=3+4=7\sqrt9+\sqrt{16}=3+4=79​+16​=3+4=7, while 9+16=25=5\sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=59+16​=25​=5. You cannot add radicands as a general rule. For example, 2+3\sqrt2+\sqrt32​+3​ stays as two terms in simplest form. Before adding coefficients, rewrite the terms with the same index and radicand where possible. Different displayed indices can sometimes match, as in 94=91/4=31/2=3\sqrt[4]{9}=9^{1/4}=3^{1/2}=\sqrt349​=91/4=31/2=3​, and other radicals become like terms only after you pull out perfect-power factors.

Worked example 5 Simplify 12+27\sqrt{12} + \sqrt{27}12​+27​

The radicands differ, so first simplify each one by pulling out its largest perfect-square factor.

Since 12=4⋅312 = 4 \cdot 312=4⋅3,

12=4 3=23,\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4}\,\sqrt{3} = 2\sqrt{3},12​=4​3​=23​,

and since 27=9⋅327 = 9 \cdot 327=9⋅3,

27=9 3=33.\sqrt{27} = \sqrt{9}\,\sqrt{3} = 3\sqrt{3}.27​=9​3​=33​.

Now both terms carry 3\sqrt{3}3​, so they are like radicals:

12+27=23+33=53.\sqrt{12} + \sqrt{27} = 2\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3} = 5\sqrt{3}.12​+27​=23​+33​=53​.

The common radical appeared only after each root was simplified.

Negative fractional exponents

A negative fractional exponent combines two instructions: take a reciprocal, and evaluate a root and a power. For example, 8−1/3=1/83=1/28^{-1/3}=1/\sqrt[3]{8}=1/28−1/3=1/38​=1/2. In general, with m/nm/nm/n in lowest terms and m,nm,nm,n positive whole numbers,

a−m/n=1am/n=1(an)m.a^{-m/n}=\frac{1}{a^{m/n}}=\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m}.a−m/n=am/n1​=(na​)m1​.

The root must exist and the denominator must be nonzero, so an even nnn requires a>0a>0a>0, while an odd nnn allows any nonzero real aaa.

For a negative base, lowest terms matter: (−8)2/6(-8)^{2/6}(−8)2/6 is read as (−8)1/3=−2(-8)^{1/3}=-2(−8)1/3=−2, not as a sixth root of −8-8−8. With an odd reduced denominator, root-first and power-first still agree: (−83)2=(−2)2=4\left(\sqrt[3]{-8}\right)^2=(-2)^2=4(3−8​)2=(−2)2=4 and (−8)23=643=4\sqrt[3]{(-8)^2}=\sqrt[3]{64}=43(−8)2​=364​=4, because ((−2)2)3=((−2)3)2=64\bigl((-2)^2\bigr)^3=\bigl((-2)^3\bigr)^2=64((−2)2)3=((−2)3)2=64. In general, if t=ant=\sqrt[n]{a}t=na​ with nnn odd, then (tm)n=(tn)m=am(t^m)^n=(t^n)^m=a^m(tm)n=(tn)m=am, so tmt^mtm is the unique real nnnth root of ama^mam.

Taking a reciprocal keeps a number’s sign: (−8)−1/3=1/(−2)=−1/2(-8)^{-1/3}=1/(-2)=-1/2(−8)−1/3=1/(−2)=−1/2, while a positive base always gives a positive result. The minus in the exponent does not mean a negative answer.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading a1/2a^{1/2}a1/2 as half of aaa. 91/2=9=39^{1/2} = \sqrt{9} = 391/2=9​=3, not 9÷2=4.59 \div 2 = 4.59÷2=4.5. The exponent’s denominator is a root index, not a divisor of the base.
  • Confusing a fractional exponent with a negative one. a1/3a^{1/3}a1/3 is the cube root a3\sqrt[3]{a}3a​, while a−3a^{-3}a−3 is the reciprocal 1a3\tfrac{1}{a^3}a31​. A fraction in the exponent means a root; a minus sign in the exponent means a reciprocal. Only a−1/3=1a3a^{-1/3} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{a}}a−1/3=3a​1​ does both.
  • Swapping the numerator and denominator in am/na^{m/n}am/n. For 82/38^{2/3}82/3 take the cube root and then square, giving (83)2=4\left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 4(38​)2=4; do not take a square root and cube.
  • Adding radicands. 9+16=5\sqrt{9 + 16} = 59+16​=5, but 9+16=7\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 79​+16​=7, and 2+3\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}2​+3​ is not 5\sqrt{5}5​. Only like radicals add: 23+53=732\sqrt{3} + 5\sqrt{3} = 7\sqrt{3}23​+53​=73​.
  • Thinking a negative fractional exponent gives a negative value. 8−2/3=148^{-2/3} = \tfrac{1}{4}8−2/3=41​, not −4-4−4. The minus sign takes a reciprocal; it does not change the sign.

Key takeaways

  • A unit-fraction exponent gives a root: a1/n=ana^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}a1/n=na​. An even index needs a≥0a\ge0a≥0 and selects the nonnegative root; an odd index allows negative radicands.
  • A fractional exponent m/nm/nm/n uses denominator nnn as the root index and numerator mmm as the power. For positive whole numbers m,nm,nm,n, root-first and power-first agree when a≥0a\ge0a≥0, and also for a negative base when the reduced denominator is odd. Reduce the exponent first when considering a negative base.
  • The exponent laws work for rational exponents with positive bases. Converting roots to powers lets you combine different indices.
  • The product and quotient rules for roots let you pull out perfect powers. For an even index, radicands must be nonnegative; a quotient also needs a nonzero denominator. Like radicals have the same index and radicand and combine by adding coefficients.
  • A negative exponent takes a reciprocal, requiring the positive power to be defined and nonzero. It does not change that power’s sign.

A bit of history (optional)

A fractional power is something you can hear. In twelve-tone equal temperament, an octave doubles a note’s frequency through twelve equal ratios. If each semitone multiplies the frequency by rrr, then r12=2r^{12}=2r12=2, so r=21/12r=2^{1/12}r=21/12.

By 1584, Zhu Zaiyu, a prince of China’s Ming imperial family, had set out a precise calculation for this tuning system. His later work on roots used abacuses, long before electronic calculators, and reached about twenty-five digits of accuracy, joining mathematics with an old musical problem: dividing an octave into evenly spaced steps.

The twelfth root can be reached through successive roots, two square roots and then a cube root: 2(1/2)(1/2)(1/3)=21/122^{(1/2)(1/2)(1/3)}=2^{1/12}2(1/2)(1/2)(1/3)=21/12. Seven semitones, the equal-tempered fifth, have frequency ratio 27/122^{7/12}27/12: the root sets one step, and the numerator counts the steps.

Sources: Bei Peng on Zhu Zaiyu’s music theory; Origin of Irrational Numbers and Their Approximations (PDF, section 31).

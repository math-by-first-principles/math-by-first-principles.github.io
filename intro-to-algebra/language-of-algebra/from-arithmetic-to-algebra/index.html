From Arithmetic to Algebra

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Arithmetic finds answers for particular numbers. But how can you describe a pattern that works for every pair, without checking pair after pair? Algebra uses letters to express those general relationships. The same notation can also describe a particular unknown you want to find.

Learning goals

  • Read algebra as arithmetic's move from particular numbers to general claims
  • State the familiar laws as claims about every allowed number
  • Recognize an identity as an equation true for every allowed value
  • Test equivalence by substitution, and say what testing cannot prove
  • Disprove a general claim with a single counterexample

Letters can name unknowns or describe patterns

Adding 333 and 555 gives one answer. But why does swapping them leave that answer unchanged, and would the same thing happen with another pair? Algebra lets you ask about the pattern as well as calculate a particular result.

The tool that makes this possible is the variable: a letter that stands for a number whose name we do not know yet. Sometimes the name is unknown because we are trying to find it, as when we solve x+3=7x + 3 = 7x+3=7 for the one value that fits. Other times the name is unknown on purpose, because we want a single statement to hold true no matter which number replaces the letter.

Watch how the second use replaces an endless list. You could check that

2+3=3+2,6+1=1+6,40+9=9+40,\begin{gathered}2 + 3 = 3 + 2,\\6 + 1 = 1 + 6,\\40 + 9 = 9 + 40,\end{gathered}2+3=3+2,6+1=1+6,40+9=9+40,​

and keep going forever without ever finishing. Or you can write the whole pattern once:

a+b=b+a.a + b = b + a.a+b=b+a.

Here aaa and bbb can stand for any two numbers. The equation records a claim about every pair. In x+3=7x + 3 = 7x+3=7, by contrast, we are looking for the particular number that makes the equation true. Both uses of letters will matter throughout algebra.

The laws every number obeys

The familiar laws of arithmetic still apply when you use letters. They hold for real numbers, the numbers on the number line, including negatives, fractions, and numbers such as 2\sqrt22​. Multiplication’s inverse law has one exception: zero has no reciprocal.

One more law links addition and multiplication, the distributive law:

a(b+c)=ab+ac.a(b + c) = ab + ac.a(b+c)=ab+ac.

The commutative law lets you swap the order in a sum or product. The associative law lets you change the grouping. The identity law says that adding 000 or multiplying by 111 leaves a number unchanged: 000 is the additive identity and 111 the multiplicative identity. The inverse law pairs a number with its opposite (its additive inverse), or with its reciprocal (its multiplicative inverse) when the number is nonzero.

These laws describe arithmetic you already use. The next argument shows why addition can be reordered.

Why a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a for every pair of numbers#

Combine 444 stones with 777 stones. You get the same heap whether you begin with the 444 or the 777. The total is 111111 in either order. The same reasoning works for piles of aaa and bbb stones: changing the order does not add or remove a stone, so a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a for whole-number counts.

Adding a number means moving by it on the number line: right for a positive number and left for a negative one. So 3+(−5)3+(-5)3+(−5) and (−5)+3(-5)+3(−5)+3 describe the same two moves in opposite orders. Both land at −2-2−2: in either order, 333 units of rightward movement cancel 333 units of leftward movement, leaving 222 units to the left.

Now let the two moves have any sizes. If both point the same way, placing their lengths end to end gives the same total distance in either order. If either move is zero, it adds no distance.

For opposite directions, if the shorter move comes second, it walks back over part of the first. If it comes first, the longer move walks back over all of it and keeps going. Either way, the moves cancel over the shorter length. Call the rightward distance ppp and the leftward distance nnn. If p≥np \ge np≥n, the moves cancel over nnn units, leaving p−np-np−n units to the right, whichever comes first. If n>pn>pn>p, they cancel over ppp units, leaving n−pn-pn−p units to the left. When p=np=np=n, both orders return to the start.

The cancellation depends on the distances, not their order. This covers positive, negative, and zero moves, including fractional distances, so a+ba+ba+b and b+ab+ab+a reach the same point for every pair.

∎

Identities: equations that are always true

An equation joins two expressions with an equals sign. Compare:

x+3=7,a+0=a,x+1=x.x + 3 = 7, \qquad a + 0 = a, \qquad x + 1 = x.x+3=7,a+0=a,x+1=x.

The first is true only when x=4x=4x=4. Finding the values that make an equation true is called solving. The second is true for every aaa. The third is never true: no number equals one more than itself. Other equations can be true at several values, such as x2=4x^2=4x2=4 at x=2x=2x=2 and x=−2x=-2x=−2.

An identity is an equation true for every allowed value of its variables. The laws above are identities, with zero excluded from the reciprocal rule. Unlike x+3=7x+3=7x+3=7, an identity does not single out a value to find: it states a relationship that holds throughout its allowed inputs.

Check your understanding

Which equation states a general arithmetic law, an identity true for every allowed value rather than an equation to solve for one value?

Answer choices

The multiplicative identity law says that multiplying any real number by 111 leaves it unchanged.

a⋅1=aa \cdot 1 = aa⋅1=a

Here the letter lets one equation describe every number, not a particular unknown to find. In contrast x+5=12x + 5 = 12x+5=12 holds only at x=7x = 7x=7, 2n=82n = 82n=8 only at n=4n = 4n=4, and x−4=0x - 4 = 0x−4=0 only at x=4x = 4x=4.

Equivalent expressions

Two expressions are equivalent when they give the same value for every allowed input. They form the two sides of an identity. For example, 2(x+3)2(x+3)2(x+3) and 2x+62x+62x+6 look different, but the distributive law says they always agree.

Why 2(x+3)2(x + 3)2(x+3) and 2x+62x + 62x+6 are equivalent#

With x=4x=4x=4, two copies of 4+34+34+3 can be regrouped as two 444‘s and two 333‘s:

2(4+3)=(4+3)+(4+3)=(4+4)+(3+3)=2(4)+6.\begin{aligned}&2(4+3)\\&=(4+3)+(4+3)\\&=(4+4)+(3+3)\\&=2(4)+6.\end{aligned}​2(4+3)=(4+3)+(4+3)=(4+4)+(3+3)=2(4)+6.​

Nothing in this regrouping depends on 444. Replacing it with any real number xxx gives

2(x+3)=(x+3)+(x+3)=(x+x)+(3+3)=2x+6.\begin{aligned} 2(x+3)&=(x+3)+(x+3)\\ &=(x+x)+(3+3)\\ &=2x+6. \end{aligned}2(x+3)​=(x+3)+(x+3)=(x+x)+(3+3)=2x+6.​

The associative and commutative laws allow the regrouping, so the equality holds for every xxx.

∎

The same picture appears in a rectangle, where the distributive law becomes a statement about area.

Not every pair that looks close is equivalent. The expressions 2(x+3)2(x + 3)2(x+3) and 2x+32x + 32x+3 differ only by where the parentheses reach, but they are not the same. Finding one value where the two expressions part ways is all it takes to prove they are not equivalent.

Worked example 1 Are these expressions equivalent?

Start with 4(n+1)4(n + 1)4(n+1) and 4n+44n + 44n+4. The distributive law multiplies the 444 across both parts of the sum:

4(n+1)=4n+4.4(n + 1) = 4n + 4.4(n+1)=4n+4.

Because the distributive law holds for every number, this equality holds for every nnn, so the two expressions are equivalent.

Now compare 4(n+1)4(n + 1)4(n+1) with 4n+14n + 14n+1. Here the 444 has reached only the nnn, not the 111. Test a single value, say n=2n = 2n=2:

4(2+1)=12,4(2)+1=9.4(2 + 1) = 12, \qquad 4(2) + 1 = 9.4(2+1)=12,4(2)+1=9.

The two disagree at n=2n = 2n=2, so 4(n+1)4(n + 1)4(n+1) and 4n+14n + 14n+1 are not equivalent. One value where they differ is enough to settle it.

Substitution and the power of a single counterexample

To substitute is to replace a variable with a chosen number and then do the arithmetic. Writing the number in parentheses where the letter used to be keeps the operations and signs intact, so 5x5x5x at x=7x = 7x=7 becomes 5(7)=355(7) = 355(7)=35 rather than the meaningless ”575757.” Substitution is how you test whether a claimed identity is actually true.

Testing can settle a claim that is false, but it cannot prove an identity. One disagreement is enough to disprove an “every number” claim; a list of agreements still leaves other numbers untested.

Why one counterexample settles a claim but examples never prove it#

Suppose someone claims n+1=2nn+1=2nn+1=2n for every number. At n=1n=1n=1, both sides are 222. But at n=2n=2n=2, they are 333 and 444. That failure is a counterexample: it disproves the claim about every number.

The same logic applies to any proposed identity. One disagreement rules it out, because “every” includes the value that failed. A finite list of agreements cannot settle it: there are still numbers outside that list where it might fail.

A general argument covers all allowed inputs. In the previous proof, regrouping worked for any xxx, so it established the identity without testing each number separately.

∎

The next example shows exactly how a couple of lucky agreements can mislead you.

Worked example 2 Is n+n=n⋅nn + n = n \cdot nn+n=n⋅n an identity?

A claim that two expressions are always equal is a claim about every number, so try a few values and stay alert for one that breaks it.

At n=2n = 2n=2 the two sides agree:

2+2=4,2⋅2=4.2 + 2 = 4, \qquad 2 \cdot 2 = 4.2+2=4,2⋅2=4.

At n=0n = 0n=0 they agree again, since 0+0=00 + 0 = 00+0=0 and 0⋅0=00 \cdot 0 = 00⋅0=0. It is tempting to declare the equation an identity, but two successes prove nothing. Try n=3n = 3n=3:

3+3=6,3⋅3=9.3 + 3 = 6, \qquad 3 \cdot 3 = 9.3+3=6,3⋅3=9.

The disagreement at n=3n=3n=3 disproves the claimed identity. The matches at 000 and 222 did not tell us what would happen at other values.

Check your understanding

A student tests n2=4nn^2 = 4nn2=4n at n=0n=0n=0 and n=4n=4n=4, and both tests agree. Which response correctly decides whether the equation is an identity?

Answer choices

The successful tests at 000 and 444 leave other numbers untested. Substituting 222 gives a counterexample:

22=4,4(2)=82^2 = 4, \qquad 4(2) = 822=4,4(2)=8

An identity must hold at every allowed input, so this one disagreement disproves the claim. Being between two successful inputs does not make 222 successful, and no number of successful tests can cancel a counterexample.

The order of operations does not change

Bringing in letters changes nothing about the order of operations. Parentheses still come first, then exponents, then multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right. So 2+3x2 + 3x2+3x means 2+(3x)2 + (3x)2+(3x), with the multiplication done before the addition, and it is a different expression from (2+3)x(2 + 3)x(2+3)x. When you substitute a number, the order of operations decides the result.

Worked example 3 Evaluate 7−2x7 - 2x7−2x when x=3x = 3x=3

Substitute 333 for xxx, in parentheses, then follow the order of operations:

7−2x=7−2(3).7 - 2x = 7 - 2(3).7−2x=7−2(3).

Multiplication comes before subtraction, so multiply first:

7−2(3)=7−6=1.7 - 2(3) = 7 - 6 = 1.7−2(3)=7−6=1.

The value is 111. It is not (7−2)(3)=15(7 - 2)(3) = 15(7−2)(3)=15; the subtraction does not happen first just because it is written first.

Check your understanding

Evaluate 3+4x3 + 4x3+4x when x=2x = 2x=2.

Answer choices

Substitute 222 for xxx, then multiply before adding, just as the order of operations requires.

3+4(2)=3+8=113 + 4(2) = 3 + 8 = 113+4(2)=3+8=11

The answer is 111111, not (3+4)(2)=14(3 + 4)(2) = 14(3+4)(2)=14; the multiplication 4x4x4x happens before the addition.

Turning words into algebra

To translate a problem, name the unknown with a letter and turn the relationships into operations. “More than” and “increased by” signal addition; “times” and “the product of” signal multiplication. “Divided by” signals division, as does “per” in a rate such as miles per hour. Watch the order in “less than”: ”555 less than nnn” means n−5n-5n−5, because nnn is the starting amount.

A phrase such as “twice a number” names a value, giving the expression 2n2n2n. A statement such as “twice a number is ten” says two values are equal, giving the equation 2n=102n=102n=10. Look for that claim of equality before adding an equals sign.

Worked example 4 Translate two statements into algebra

Name the unknown number nnn in each, then read carefully for a claim that two amounts are equal.

“Twice a number, decreased by five” is a phrase. Twice the number is 2n2n2n, and decreased by five subtracts 555:

2n−5.2n - 5.2n−5.

It names a value and asserts nothing, so it is an expression.

“The sum of a number and eight is twenty” is a full statement, and the word is acts as the equals sign. The sum of the number and eight is n+8n + 8n+8, set equal to 202020:

n+8=20.n + 8 = 20.n+8=20.

That equals sign makes it an equation, a sentence you could later solve to find nnn.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing an identity with an equation to solve. a+0=aa + 0 = aa+0=a is an identity, true for every aaa, while x+3=7x + 3 = 7x+3=7 is true for exactly one value, x=4x = 4x=4, and false for every other. Decide which kind you are looking at before you do anything else.
  • Thinking a few checks prove an identity. Passing at two or three values proves nothing, because a claim can hold at some values and fail at others (n+n=n⋅nn + n = n \cdot nn+n=n⋅n agrees at n=0n = 0n=0 and n=2n = 2n=2 but fails at n=3n = 3n=3). One counterexample, on the other hand, disproves a claim for good.
  • Distributing to only part of a sum. 2(x+3)2(x + 3)2(x+3) is 2x+62x + 62x+6, not 2x+32x + 32x+3; the factor must reach every term inside the parentheses.
  • Reading a “less than” phrase in word order. ”555 less than nnn” means n−5n - 5n−5, not 5−n5 - n5−n; find the amount you start from before subtracting.
  • Assuming every operation is commutative. Addition and multiplication can be reordered, but subtraction and division cannot: 5−3≠3−55 - 3 \neq 3 - 55−3=3−5 and 8÷2≠2÷88 \div 2 \neq 2 \div 88÷2=2÷8.
  • Dropping the order of operations once letters appear. 2+3x2 + 3x2+3x means 2+(3x)2 + (3x)2+(3x), not (2+3)x(2 + 3)x(2+3)x. Multiplication still comes before addition.
  • Reading 5x5x5x as two digits. A number next to a variable means multiply, so at x=2x = 2x=2 the value of 5x5x5x is 5(2)=105(2) = 105(2)=10, not 525252.

Key takeaways

  • A variable can stand for an unknown number or for any number in a general claim.
  • The arithmetic laws (commutative, associative, identity, inverse, and distributive) still apply to real numbers. A reciprocal requires a nonzero number.
  • An identity holds for every allowed input; its two sides are equivalent expressions.
  • A counterexample disproves a claimed identity. Substitution can find one, but a finite list of agreements cannot replace a general argument.
  • An expression names a value; an equation states equality. Translate carefully and keep the usual order of operations when evaluating.

A bit of history (optional)

People used the familiar arithmetic laws long before they gave them their modern names. In 1814, French army officer and mathematics teacher Francois-Joseph Servois introduced commutative and distributive while studying mathematical operations. Around 1843, Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton used associative in his work on quaternions, a new kind of number. Quaternion multiplication could change when its factors changed order, even though regrouping still worked. Naming the laws helped mathematicians describe exactly what these new numbers kept and what they changed.

An older convention came from Rene Descartes. In his 1637 Geometry, he used letters near the start of the alphabet, such as aaa, bbb, and ccc, for known quantities, and letters near the end, such as xxx, yyy, and zzz, for unknowns. The habit spread, but it remains a convention. In 2(x+3)=2x+62(x+3)=2x+62(x+3)=2x+6, xxx can be any number; there is no particular unknown to find. The equation, not the choice of letter, tells you what job the variable is doing.

Sources: Servois; Hamilton’s use of associative; Descartes and algebraic notation.

Expanding and Factoring Expressions

Intuition

You already know how to multiply a number across a sum: 2(x+3)=2x+62(x + 3) = 2x + 62(x+3)=2x+6 turns a product into a sum you can read term by term. This lesson uses that same rule, the distributive law, in two directions. Forward, it multiplies a whole expression across another, so (x+2)(x+3)(x + 2)(x + 3)(x+2)(x+3) becomes x2+5x+6x^2 + 5x + 6x2+5x+6. Backward, it pulls a shared factor back out, so 6x+96x + 96x+9 becomes 3(2x+3)3(2x + 3)3(2x+3). Expanding and factoring are these two directions of one idea, both built on the distributive law you already trust from arithmetic.

Learning goals

  • Expand a product so the factor reaches every term
  • Multiply two expressions by pairing every term with every term
  • Treat FOIL as a name for four products, not a general rule
  • Factor by pulling out the greatest common factor
  • Check any factoring by re-expanding it

Expanding a factor across every term

To expand a product is to multiply it out into a sum, using the distributive law. You met the law for a sum of two terms, a(b+c)=ab+aca(b + c) = ab + aca(b+c)=ab+ac, but nothing about it stops at two terms. The factor out front reaches every term inside the parentheses, however many there are.

Why a(b+c+d)=ab+ac+ada(b + c + d) = ab + ac + ada(b+c+d)=ab+ac+ad#

The distributive law is stated for a sum of two terms, a(b+c)=ab+aca(b + c) = ab + aca(b+c)=ab+ac, but a sum can have more terms than that. To see that the factor still reaches each one, group the extra terms and apply the two-term law twice. Treat b+c+db + c + db+c+d as (b+c)+d(b + c) + d(b+c)+d, a sum of the single quantity b+cb + cb+c and the term ddd:

a((b+c)+d)=a(b+c)+ad.a\big((b + c) + d\big) = a(b + c) + ad.a((b+c)+d)=a(b+c)+ad.

That is the two-term law with b+cb + cb+c playing the role of the first term. Now distribute across the remaining pair:

a(b+c)+ad=ab+ac+ad.a(b + c) + ad = ab + ac + ad.a(b+c)+ad=ab+ac+ad.

The factor aaa has multiplied every term inside. The same grouping works for a sum of any length, so a factor outside a parenthesis multiplies each term within it, one at a time. That is the whole content of expanding: no term is ever skipped.

∎

The factor out front need not be a bare number. It can carry a sign or a variable, and the rule does not change: multiply it against each term, following the sign and exponent rules you already know. When two powers of the same base meet, their exponents add, so x⋅x=x2x \cdot x = x^2x⋅x=x2 and x⋅x2=x3x \cdot x^2 = x^3x⋅x2=x3. A negative factor flips the sign of each term it lands on, exactly as a leading minus did in the last lesson. The flip happens because a minus sign in front is the factor −1-1−1 waiting to be distributed.

Worked example 1 Expand three products

In each product, multiply the outside factor against every inside term, keeping each sign.

Distributing a negative number across a difference, the sign of each term flips:

−3(x−4)=(−3)(x)+(−3)(−4)=−3x+12.-3(x - 4) = (-3)(x) + (-3)(-4) = -3x + 12.−3(x−4)=(−3)(x)+(−3)(−4)=−3x+12.

Distributing a single variable, the powers add where the variable meets itself:

x(2x+3)=x⋅2x+x⋅3=2x2+3x.x(2x + 3) = x \cdot 2x + x \cdot 3 = 2x^2 + 3x.x(2x+3)=x⋅2x+x⋅3=2x2+3x.

Distributing a term that carries both a coefficient and a power, handle the numbers and the exponents separately:

2x2(3x−5)=2x2⋅3x−2x2⋅5=6x3−10x2.2x^2(3x - 5) = 2x^2 \cdot 3x - 2x^2 \cdot 5 = 6x^3 - 10x^2.2x2(3x−5)=2x2⋅3x−2x2⋅5=6x3−10x2.

In each case the factor reached both terms. The second term is never left behind.

One case deserves a second look, because the factor is hidden. Subtracting a whole group, as in a−(b+c)a - (b + c)a−(b+c), is the same as multiplying that group by −1-1−1 and adding, so every term inside changes sign:

a−(b+c)=a−b−c,−(a−b)=−a+b.a - (b + c) = a - b - c, \qquad -(a - b) = -a + b.a−(b+c)=a−b−c,−(a−b)=−a+b.

This is the leading-minus rule from the last lesson seen as one more case of expanding, with the hidden factor being −1-1−1.

Check your understanding

Expand −2x(3x−4)-2x(3x - 4)−2x(3x−4).

Answer choices

Multiply −2x-2x−2x against each term inside, watching the signs and adding exponents where xxx meets xxx.

−2x(3x−4)=(−2x)(3x)+(−2x)(−4)=−6x2+8x-2x(3x - 4) = (-2x)(3x) + (-2x)(-4) = -6x^2 + 8x−2x(3x−4)=(−2x)(3x)+(−2x)(−4)=−6x2+8x

The product (−2x)(−4)(-2x)(-4)(−2x)(−4) is +8x+8x+8x, so the second term is positive; missing that sign flip gives the wrong −6x2−8x-6x^2 - 8x−6x2−8x.

Multiplying two expressions

Multiplying two multi-term expressions, such as (a+b)(c+d)(a + b)(c + d)(a+b)(c+d), looks like a new problem, but a picture makes it concrete first. Give a rectangle a width of a+ba + ba+b and a height of c+dc + dc+d. Splitting the width into aaa and bbb, and the height into ccc and ddd, carves the rectangle into four smaller rectangles, and their areas are exactly the four products you get from multiplying every part of one sum by every part of the other.

The same result follows from the distributive law alone, with no picture needed. Treat the first factor as a single quantity for a moment: nothing in the distributive law requires the outside factor to be short, so let the whole of a+ba + ba+b play that role.

Why (a+b)(c+d)=ac+bc+ad+bd(a + b)(c + d) = ac + bc + ad + bd(a+b)(c+d)=ac+bc+ad+bd#

Nothing in the distributive law says the outside factor has to be a single letter. Treat the whole quantity a+ba + ba+b as one factor and distribute it across c+dc + dc+d, the usual way:

(a+b)(c+d)=(a+b)c+(a+b)d.(a + b)(c + d) = (a + b)c + (a + b)d.(a+b)(c+d)=(a+b)c+(a+b)d.

Each of those two pieces is itself a distributive-law step, this time with ccc and ddd spreading back across a+ba + ba+b:

(a+b)c=ac+bc,(a+b)d=ad+bd.(a + b)c = ac + bc, \qquad (a + b)d = ad + bd.(a+b)c=ac+bc,(a+b)d=ad+bd.

Adding the four pieces gives

(a+b)(c+d)=ac+bc+ad+bd.(a + b)(c + d) = ac + bc + ad + bd.(a+b)(c+d)=ac+bc+ad+bd.

Every term of the first factor has multiplied every term of the second, one product for each pairing, matching the four cells of the rectangle above. Because the argument used nothing but the distributive law, it does not stop at two terms each. So to multiply any two expressions, multiply every term of the first by every term of the second, then combine like terms.

∎

For the common case of two two-term factors, the four products come in a memorable order. Reading (a+b)(c+d)(a + b)(c + d)(a+b)(c+d) left to right, they are the product of the First terms acacac and the Outer terms adadad. Then come the Inner terms bcbcbc and the Last terms bdbdbd, so the four initials spell the mnemonic FOIL. It is a handy way to be sure you have all four products, but it is only a name for what the distributive law already told you. FOIL works only when each factor has exactly two terms. The underlying rule, every term times every term, is what you fall back on the moment a factor has three terms or more.

Worked example 2 Multiply two binomials

Multiply every term of the first factor by every term of the second, then gather like terms.

For (x+2)(x+3)(x + 2)(x + 3)(x+2)(x+3), the four products are x⋅xx \cdot xx⋅x, x⋅3x \cdot 3x⋅3, 2⋅x2 \cdot x2⋅x, and 2⋅32 \cdot 32⋅3:

(x+2)(x+3)=x2+3x+2x+6.(x + 2)(x + 3) = x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6.(x+2)(x+3)=x2+3x+2x+6.

The middle terms 3x3x3x and 2x2x2x are like terms, so combine them:

x2+3x+2x+6=x2+5x+6.x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6 = x^2 + 5x + 6.x2+3x+2x+6=x2+5x+6.

The same method handles coefficients and negatives. For (2x−1)(x+4)(2x - 1)(x + 4)(2x−1)(x+4),

(2x−1)(x+4)=2x2+8x−x−4=2x2+7x−4,(2x - 1)(x + 4) = 2x^2 + 8x - x - 4 = 2x^2 + 7x - 4,(2x−1)(x+4)=2x2+8x−x−4=2x2+7x−4,

where 2x⋅x=2x22x \cdot x = 2x^22x⋅x=2x2, the outer and inner products 8x8x8x and −x-x−x combine to 7x7x7x, and (−1)(4)=−4(-1)(4) = -4(−1)(4)=−4.

Check your understanding

Multiply (x+4)(x+2)(x + 4)(x + 2)(x+4)(x+2).

Answer choices

Multiply every term of the first factor by every term of the second, then combine the like middle terms.

(x+4)(x+2)=x2+2x+4x+8=x2+6x+8(x + 4)(x + 2) = x^2 + 2x + 4x + 8 = x^2 + 6x + 8(x+4)(x+2)=x2+2x+4x+8=x2+6x+8

The outer and inner products 2x2x2x and 4x4x4x combine to 6x6x6x; dropping them gives the wrong x2+8x^2 + 8x2+8.

Worked example 3 Multiply a binomial by a trinomial

When a factor has three terms, FOIL no longer applies, but every term times every term still does. Multiply each of the two terms of x+2x + 2x+2 against all three terms of x2+3x−1x^2 + 3x - 1x2+3x−1:

(x+2)(x2+3x−1)=x(x2+3x−1)+2(x2+3x−1).(x + 2)(x^2 + 3x - 1) = x(x^2 + 3x - 1) + 2(x^2 + 3x - 1).(x+2)(x2+3x−1)=x(x2+3x−1)+2(x2+3x−1).

Expand each product:

x(x2+3x−1)=x3+3x2−x,2(x2+3x−1)=2x2+6x−2.x(x^2 + 3x - 1) = x^3 + 3x^2 - x, \qquad 2(x^2 + 3x - 1) = 2x^2 + 6x - 2.x(x2+3x−1)=x3+3x2−x,2(x2+3x−1)=2x2+6x−2.

Add them and combine the like x2x^2x2 terms and the like xxx terms:

x3+3x2−x+2x2+6x−2=x3+5x2+5x−2.x^3 + 3x^2 - x + 2x^2 + 6x - 2 = x^3 + 5x^2 + 5x - 2.x3+3x2−x+2x2+6x−2=x3+5x2+5x−2.

That is six products in all, one for each pairing of a term from the first factor with a term from the second.

Check your understanding

How many products does multiplying (x2+4x−3)(x+5)(x^2 + 4x - 3)(x + 5)(x2+4x−3)(x+5) require, before combining any like terms?

Answer choices

Every term of the first factor (three terms) must multiply every term of the second factor (two terms), which is 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6 products, not the number of terms added together and not the four FOIL names, which only cover two two-term factors.

(x2+4x−3)(x+5)=x2(x+5)+4x(x+5)−3(x+5)(x^2 + 4x - 3)(x + 5) = x^2(x + 5) + 4x(x + 5) - 3(x + 5)(x2+4x−3)(x+5)=x2(x+5)+4x(x+5)−3(x+5)

Expanding those three pieces gives six products before any of them combine.

(x+5)(x−5)(x + 5)(x - 5)(x+5)(x−5) and (x+3)2(x + 3)^2(x+3)2 both expand by the same every-term-times-every-term method, but their middle terms behave in a memorable way: one pair cancels, the other pair doubles.

Factoring by reversing distribution

Expanding turns a product into a sum. Factoring runs the machine backward. In this lesson, that means turning a sum into a product by pulling out a factor shared by every term. The tool is the distributive law read from right to left, ab+ac=a(b+c)ab + ac = a(b + c)ab+ac=a(b+c), which says a factor common to each term can be lifted outside a parenthesis.

Why pulling out a common factor is the distributive law reversed#

The distributive law, read left to right, expands: a(b+c)=ab+aca(b + c) = ab + aca(b+c)=ab+ac. Every equation can be read in either direction, so read this one right to left:

ab+ac=a(b+c).ab + ac = a(b + c).ab+ac=a(b+c).

This says that if a factor aaa appears in every term of a sum, it can be pulled out to the front. The other factors of each term stay behind inside the parenthesis. Nothing new is needed; it is the same identity, used in reverse.

To factor 6x+96x + 96x+9, look for a factor common to both terms. Since 6x=3⋅2x6x = 3 \cdot 2x6x=3⋅2x and 9=3⋅39 = 3 \cdot 39=3⋅3, the number 333 divides each, so lift it out:

6x+9=3⋅2x+3⋅3=3(2x+3).6x + 9 = 3 \cdot 2x + 3 \cdot 3 = 3(2x + 3).6x+9=3⋅2x+3⋅3=3(2x+3).

For x2+3xx^2 + 3xx2+3x the shared factor is the variable xxx, because x2=x⋅xx^2 = x \cdot xx2=x⋅x and 3x=x⋅33x = x \cdot 33x=x⋅3:

x2+3x=x⋅x+x⋅3=x(x+3).x^2 + 3x = x \cdot x + x \cdot 3 = x(x + 3).x2+3x=x⋅x+x⋅3=x(x+3).

In both cases you can check the result at once by expanding it again: 3(2x+3)=6x+93(2x + 3) = 6x + 93(2x+3)=6x+9 and x(x+3)=x2+3xx(x + 3) = x^2 + 3xx(x+3)=x2+3x, right back to where you started.

∎

The factor worth pulling out is the greatest common factor, or GCF: the largest factor every term shares. It has two parts. The numerical part is the greatest common divisor of the coefficients, and the variable part is each common variable raised to the smallest power that appears. For 4x2+6x4x^2 + 6x4x2+6x the coefficients 444 and 666 share the factor 222, and both terms contain at least one xxx, so the GCF is 2x2x2x. Pulling it out,

4x2+6x=2x⋅2x+2x⋅3=2x(2x+3).4x^2 + 6x = 2x \cdot 2x + 2x \cdot 3 = 2x(2x + 3).4x2+6x=2x⋅2x+2x⋅3=2x(2x+3).

Taking out less than the full GCF leaves the job unfinished. The forms 2(2x2+3x)2(2x^2 + 3x)2(2x2+3x) and x(4x+6)x(4x + 6)x(4x+6) are both correct as far as they go, but each still hides a shared factor inside, so neither is fully factored.

The area model reads backward here too. A rectangle whose area is 6x+96x + 96x+9 and whose height is the common factor 333 must have width 2x+32x + 32x+3. So factoring is just naming the side lengths of the rectangle you already drew when expanding.

Worked example 5 Factor out the greatest common factor

Find the GCF, lift it out, then confirm the answer by expanding it again.

For 12x2−8x12x^2 - 8x12x2−8x, the coefficients 121212 and 888 share 444 and both terms contain xxx, so the GCF is 4x4x4x:

12x2−8x=4x(3x−2).12x^2 - 8x = 4x(3x - 2).12x2−8x=4x(3x−2).

Expanding the answer returns the original, which is the check that the factoring is right: 4x(3x−2)=12x2−8x4x(3x - 2) = 12x^2 - 8x4x(3x−2)=12x2−8x, as it should.

Check your understanding

Factor out the greatest common factor of 8x2+12x8x^2 + 12x8x2+12x.

Answer choices

The greatest common factor combines the largest shared number with the lowest shared power of xxx. The coefficients 888 and 121212 share 444, and both terms contain xxx, so the GCF is 4x4x4x.

8x2+12x=4x⋅2x+4x⋅3=4x(2x+3)8x^2 + 12x = 4x \cdot 2x + 4x \cdot 3 = 4x(2x + 3)8x2+12x=4x⋅2x+4x⋅3=4x(2x+3)

The other choices pull out only part of the GCF, so a shared factor still remains inside their parentheses.

Check your understanding

To check a proposed factoring, you expand it back out and compare. Expand 3x(3x+2)3x(3x + 2)3x(3x+2) using the distributive law. What do you get?

Answer choices

Distributing 3x3x3x across 3x+23x + 23x+2 multiplies it into both terms:

3x(3x+2)=3x⋅3x+3x⋅2=9x2+6x3x(3x + 2) = 3x \cdot 3x + 3x \cdot 2 = 9x^2 + 6x3x(3x+2)=3x⋅3x+3x⋅2=9x2+6x

So if the expression you started with was 9x2+6x9x^2 + 6x9x2+6x, this factoring checks out; if it was anything else, it does not. The other options come from multiplying only part of the expression or losing track of an exponent.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Distributing to only the first term. A factor must reach every term inside, so x(x+5)=x2+5xx(x + 5) = x^2 + 5xx(x+5)=x2+5x, not x2+5x^2 + 5x2+5, and 3(2x−4)=6x−123(2x - 4) = 6x - 123(2x−4)=6x−12, not 6x−46x - 46x−4.
  • Dropping the middle terms of a product. Multiplying two binomials makes four products, and the two middle ones combine only when they are like terms, as they are here: (x+2)(x+3)=x2+5x+6(x + 2)(x + 3) = x^2 + 5x + 6(x+2)(x+3)=x2+5x+6, not x2+6x^2 + 6x2+6.
  • Mishandling a sign on a negative factor. A negative factor flips the sign of every term: −2(x−3)=−2x+6-2(x - 3) = -2x + 6−2(x−3)=−2x+6, not −2x−6-2x - 6−2x−6, and −(x−4)=−x+4-(x - 4) = -x + 4−(x−4)=−x+4.
  • Squaring a sum term by term. (x+3)2(x + 3)^2(x+3)2 is (x+3)(x+3)=x2+6x+9(x + 3)(x + 3) = x^2 + 6x + 9(x+3)(x+3)=x2+6x+9, not x2+9x^2 + 9x2+9. The middle term 6x6x6x comes from the two cross products and is the piece most often forgotten.
  • Pulling out only part of the GCF. 8x2+12x=4x(2x+3)8x^2 + 12x = 4x(2x + 3)8x2+12x=4x(2x+3). Writing 2x(4x+6)2x(4x + 6)2x(4x+6) leaves a shared factor of 222 still inside, so the expression is not yet fully factored.
  • Confusing x⋅xx \cdot xx⋅x with x+xx + xx+x. Multiplying gives x⋅x=x2x \cdot x = x^2x⋅x=x2, while adding gives x+x=2xx + x = 2xx+x=2x. So x(x+1)=x2+xx(x + 1) = x^2 + xx(x+1)=x2+x, where the first term is a power and the second is not.

Key takeaways

  • To expand is to multiply out a product with the distributive law, so a factor reaches every term: a(b+c+d)=ab+ac+ada(b + c + d) = ab + ac + ada(b+c+d)=ab+ac+ad, and −3(x−4)=−3x+12-3(x - 4) = -3x + 12−3(x−4)=−3x+12.
  • To multiply two expressions, multiply every term of the first by every term of the second, then combine like terms: (a+b)(c+d)=ac+bc+ad+bd(a + b)(c + d) = ac + bc + ad + bd(a+b)(c+d)=ac+bc+ad+bd, so (x+2)(x+3)=x2+5x+6(x + 2)(x + 3) = x^2 + 5x + 6(x+2)(x+3)=x2+5x+6.
  • FOIL names the four products (First, Outer, Inner, Last) for two binomials only; the rule every term times every term is what handles three terms or more.
  • To factor is to run distribution in reverse, pulling out the greatest common factor. That factor is the greatest common divisor of the coefficients times each common variable at its lowest power. So 6x+9=3(2x+3)6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)6x+9=3(2x+3) and 4x2+6x=2x(2x+3)4x^2 + 6x = 2x(2x + 3)4x2+6x=2x(2x+3).
  • Check any factoring by re-expanding. If the product does not return the original expression, a factor or a sign is off.

Two products with a shortcut shape

Some products have a tidy shape, and expanding them is the same distribution as always. Take a sum times a difference of the same two terms:

(x+5)(x−5)=x2−5x+5x−25=x2−25.(x + 5)(x - 5) = x^2 - 5x + 5x - 25 = x^2 - 25.(x+5)(x−5)=x2−5x+5x−25=x2−25.

The two middle terms −5x-5x−5x and 5x5x5x cancel, leaving only x2−25x^2 - 25x2−25. A squared binomial is a product with itself:

(x+3)2=(x+3)(x+3)=x2+3x+3x+9=x2+6x+9.(x + 3)^2 = (x + 3)(x + 3) = x^2 + 3x + 3x + 9 = x^2 + 6x + 9.(x+3)2=(x+3)(x+3)=x2+3x+3x+9=x2+6x+9.

Here the middle terms add rather than cancel, giving the 6x6x6x. Both results come straight from multiplying every term by every term; there is nothing to memorize beyond the method you already have.

A bit of history (optional)

The rectangle you cut apart in this lesson is about twenty-three centuries old.

Around 300 BCE, Euclid gathered the geometry of his day into a work called the Elements. Book II of it reads oddly to a modern eye. Its propositions carry no algebraic symbols and no variables standing for an unknown number, though Euclid still labeled the points of his diagrams with letters, A, B, C, and so on, purely to refer to them in the proof. They concern lines, rectangles and squares, and nothing else. One of them says this. Take two straight lines, and cut one of them into two parts. Then the rectangle contained by the two lines equals the two rectangles contained by the uncut line and each part of the cut one. Rewrite that sentence in symbols and you have the distributive law, a(b+c)=ab+aca(b + c) = ab + aca(b+c)=ab+ac.

Euclid could not have written it that way. Algebraic notation was still nineteen centuries in his future, so he proved the fact the way his geometry allowed: by drawing the rectangle and dividing it.

The picture never left. Slicing a rectangle into acacac, bcbcbc, adadad and bdbdbd to expand (a+b)(c+d)(a + b)(c + d)(a+b)(c+d) is Book II with modern labels. Reading that same rectangle backward is equally Book II. You were handed a height of 333 and an area of 6x+96x + 96x+9, and you recovered the width 2x+32x + 32x+3. A Greek geometer would have described that as naming a side, and you described it as factoring. Factor is Latin for one who makes, so the factors of an expression are simply its makers.

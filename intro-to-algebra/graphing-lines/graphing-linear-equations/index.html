Graphing Linear Equations

Intuition

You already know that a linear equation in two variables, such as 2x+y=62x + y = 62x+y=6, has infinitely many solutions: for this equation, one ordered pair for every value you feed in for xxx. (A few special equations work the other way around, with one fixed xxx and every yyy imaginable; you will meet those later in this lesson.) Writing them in a table is honest but endless: you can never list them all. Graphing settles the matter in one stroke. Because those solution pairs are points, you can plot them, and when you do they never scatter. They fall exactly on a straight line. That single line shows every solution at once, so a whole endless list becomes one picture. You can draw that picture in seconds, read answers off of it, and later use it to see where two lines meet.

Learning goals

  • Treat the graph as the equation's whole solution set
  • Plot two solutions to fix the line, and a third to check
  • Build a table by solving for yyy and choosing inputs
  • Find both intercepts by setting each variable to zero
  • Draw y=cy = cy=c horizontal and x=cx = cx=c vertical
  • Test a point by substituting its coordinates

What the graph of an equation is

A solution of a linear equation in two variables is an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that makes the equation true. In the last lesson you saw that a single such equation has infinitely many of these pairs. Since every ordered pair is a point on the coordinate plane, the whole collection of solutions is a collection of points, and that collection has a name.

The graph of an equation is the set of all points (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) whose coordinates make the equation true, plotted on the coordinate plane. Nothing more and nothing less: a point is on the graph exactly when it is a solution. Likewise, a point that is not a solution is off the graph. So “the graph” and “the solution set” are two names for the same thing, one described with algebra and the other drawn as a picture.

For a linear equation this graph is always a straight line, which is exactly why the equation is called linear. That fact is what makes graphing fast. A line is fixed by just two different points. So once you have found two solutions and plotted them, you can lay a ruler across them and draw the entire graph. The rest of this lesson is really two questions, then: how to find a few solution points, and how to read the line they determine.

Graphing with a table of values

The most direct way to graph a linear equation is to find several solutions, plot them, and draw the line they fall on. Finding solutions is the job you already did last lesson: solve the equation for yyy, choose values of xxx, and compute the matching yyy. Each pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) is one point of the graph. A good habit is to pick a small spread of xxx-values, including zero and a negative, so the points are not all crowded together.

Worked example 1 Graph y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1 from a table

The equation is already solved for yyy, so choose a handful of xxx-values and compute each yyy.

y=2(−1)−1=−3,y=2(0)−1=−1,y=2(1)−1=1,y=2(2)−1=3.\begin{aligned} y = 2(-1) - 1 &= -3, &\qquad y = 2(0) - 1 &= -1, \\ y = 2(1) - 1 &= 1, &\qquad y = 2(2) - 1 &= 3. \end{aligned}y=2(−1)−1y=2(1)−1​=−3,=1,​y=2(0)−1y=2(2)−1​=−1,=3.​

Collecting the results in a table keeps the pairs straight:

Plot the four points. They line up perfectly, so lay a ruler across them and draw a straight line. Extend it past the outer points and add arrowheads to show it runs on forever in both directions. That line is the graph of y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1.

Why do the points always line up instead of scattering? The table already hints at the reason: each time xxx climbs by 111, the value of yyy climbs by the same 222.

Why the solution points fall on a straight line#

Take the equation y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1 and watch yyy as xxx grows by 111. Going from any value xxx to the next value x+1x + 1x+1, the new yyy is

2(x+1)−1=2x+2−1=(2x−1)+2,2(x + 1) - 1 = 2x + 2 - 1 = (2x - 1) + 2,2(x+1)−1=2x+2−1=(2x−1)+2,

which is exactly 222 more than the old yyy. This increase of 222 does not depend on where you started. From x=0x = 0x=0 to x=1x = 1x=1, from x=5x = 5x=5 to x=6x = 6x=6, from anywhere to the next step, yyy goes up by the same 222. The same holds for any size of step, not just whole ones: moving xxx by 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ moves yyy by 111, and moving xxx by 0.10.10.1 moves yyy by 0.20.20.2. Every step, of any size, produces exactly twice as much vertical change, so there is no gap or bend hiding between the grid points either.

That steadiness is the whole reason the points line up. Each time you move one unit to the right, you move the same fixed amount up. So from any plotted point the next one sits one across and a fixed jump away, over and over. Repeating one identical step can never bend: it marches in a single fixed direction, and a single fixed direction is what a straight line is. The particular equation only sets the size of that vertical jump. Every linear equation, once yyy is alone on one side, has its lone xxx-term multiplied by a constant. So every such equation’s jump is a fixed number, and its graph is always straight. (Just how steep that line looks is the subject of the next lesson.)

∎

The same routine graphs any linear equation that is not vertical. When the equation is not already solved for yyy, solve for it first, then build the table. (A vertical equation such as x=4x = 4x=4 cannot be solved for yyy at all, since it has no yyy in it. It gets its own quick rule later in this lesson.)

Worked example 2 Graph x+y=4x + y = 4x+y=4 from a table

First solve for yyy by subtracting xxx from both sides:

y=4−x.y = 4 - x.y=4−x.

Now choose a few values of xxx, including a negative one so the points spread out, and compute yyy:

Notice the check built into the table: the points really do line up. So a fourth row that landed off the line would warn you of an arithmetic slip.

Graphing with the intercepts

Because two points are enough to fix a line, you do not need a long table. You need only two solutions, and two of them are especially easy to find and to plot: the points where the graph crosses the axes.

The x-intercept is the point where the line crosses the x-axis. Every point on the x-axis has y=0y = 0y=0, so to find it, set y=0y = 0y=0 and solve for xxx. The y-intercept is the point where the line crosses the y-axis. Every point on the y-axis has x=0x = 0x=0, so set x=0x = 0x=0 and solve for yyy. Each choice makes one term drop out, which keeps the arithmetic short.

Take 3x+2y=123x + 2y = 123x+2y=12. For the x-intercept, set y=0y = 0y=0:

3x+2(0)=12,3x=12,x=4,3x + 2(0) = 12, \qquad 3x = 12, \qquad x = 4,3x+2(0)=12,3x=12,x=4,

so the line crosses the x-axis at (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0). For the y-intercept, set x=0x = 0x=0:

3(0)+2y=12,2y=12,y=6,3(0) + 2y = 12, \qquad 2y = 12, \qquad y = 6,3(0)+2y=12,2y=12,y=6,

so it crosses the y-axis at (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6). Plot those two points, draw the line through them, and the graph is done.

Three cautions keep this method reliable. The intercepts are just two convenient solutions. So if one of them turns out awkward (a fraction, say), you are free to pick an easier value of xxx instead and use that point. And a line through the origin, such as y=2xy = 2xy=2x, has both intercepts at the very same point (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), because setting x=0x = 0x=0 and setting y=0y = 0y=0 both return the origin. In that one case you need a second point somewhere else, so choose any other xxx and compute its yyy. A third case: a horizontal or vertical line only crosses one axis at an ordinary point (a horizontal line like y=3y = 3y=3 never touches the x-axis at all). Those lines are graphed straight from their own rule, covered next, rather than from intercepts.

Worked example 3 Graph 3x−4y=123x - 4y = 123x−4y=12 using its intercepts

Find the x-intercept by setting y=0y = 0y=0:

3x−4(0)=12,3x=12,x=4,3x - 4(0) = 12, \qquad 3x = 12, \qquad x = 4,3x−4(0)=12,3x=12,x=4,

so the x-intercept is (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0). Find the y-intercept by setting x=0x = 0x=0:

3(0)−4y=12,−4y=12,y=−3,3(0) - 4y = 12, \qquad -4y = 12, \qquad y = -3,3(0)−4y=12,−4y=12,y=−3,

so the y-intercept is (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3). The second intercept is below the x-axis, which is fine. Plot (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0) on the x-axis and (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3) on the y-axis, then draw the line through the two points to graph 3x−4y=123x - 4y = 123x−4y=12.

5x+2(0)=20,5x=20,x=4.5x + 2(0) = 20, \qquad 5x = 20, \qquad x = 4.5x+2(0)=20,5x=20,x=4.

So the x-intercept is (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0). Setting x=0x = 0x=0 instead would give the y-intercept (0,10)(0, 10)(0,10).

Horizontal and vertical lines

Two kinds of lines look different from the slanted ones above, and they come from equations that mention only one variable.

Consider y=3y = 3y=3. Read literally, it says the y-coordinate is 333 and says nothing at all about xxx. So xxx is free to be any number while yyy must stay 333: the pairs (−2,3)(-2, 3)(−2,3), (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3), (1,3)(1, 3)(1,3), and (100,3)(100, 3)(100,3) are all solutions. Plot a few and they sit at the same height, three units above the x-axis. Connecting them gives a horizontal line. Every equation of the form y=cy = cy=c graphs as a horizontal line at height ccc.

Now consider x=−2x = -2x=−2. It fixes the x-coordinate at −2-2−2 and says nothing about yyy. So this time yyy is free and xxx stays −2-2−2: the pairs (−2,−2)(-2, -2)(−2,−2), (−2,0)(-2, 0)(−2,0), and (−2,2)(-2, 2)(−2,2) are all solutions. They line up in a single column two units left of the y-axis, and connecting them gives a vertical line. Every equation of the form x=cx = cx=c graphs as a vertical line through x=cx = cx=c.

These fit the same pattern as the slanted lines. You can write y=3y = 3y=3 as 0⋅x+y=30 \cdot x + y = 30⋅x+y=3: the coefficient of xxx is zero, so changing xxx changes nothing and yyy stays pinned at 333. In the same way, x=−2x = -2x=−2 is x+0⋅y=−2x + 0 \cdot y = -2x+0⋅y=−2. They are ordinary linear equations, just with one of the two coefficients equal to zero. A quick way to keep them straight is to read the equation as a rule about one coordinate. Read that way, y=3y = 3y=3 says “stay at height 333” (a flat, horizontal line), and x=−2x = -2x=−2 says “stay in the column −2-2−2” (a standing, vertical line).

Worked example 4 Graph x=4x = 4x=4 and y=−1y = -1y=−1 on the same axes

The equation x=4x = 4x=4 fixes the x-coordinate at 444 and leaves yyy free. Therefore its solutions are (4,−3)(4, -3)(4,−3), (4,0)(4, 0)(4,0), (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2), and so on, all in the column four units right of the y-axis. They form a vertical line through x=4x = 4x=4.

The equation y=−1y = -1y=−1 fixes the y-coordinate at −1-1−1 and leaves xxx free. Therefore its solutions are (−3,−1)(-3, -1)(−3,−1), (0,−1)(0, -1)(0,−1), (2,−1)(2, -1)(2,−1), and so on, all at the height one unit below the x-axis. They form a horizontal line through y=−1y = -1y=−1.

Drawn on the same axes, the vertical line x=4x = 4x=4 and the horizontal line y=−1y = -1y=−1 cross at the single point where both rules hold at once, namely (4,−1)(4, -1)(4,−1).

By contrast x=7x = 7x=7 is a vertical line, while y=2xy = 2xy=2x and x+y=4x + y = 4x+y=4 are slanted lines.

Checking whether a point is on a line

Because the graph is exactly the set of solutions, deciding whether a given point lies on a line takes no drawing at all. Substitute the point’s coordinates into the equation. If the two sides come out equal, the point is a solution and sits on the line. If they do not, the point is off the line.

Is (3,5)(3, 5)(3,5) on the graph of y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1? Put in x=3x = 3x=3 and y=5y = 5y=5:

2(3)−1=5,2(3) - 1 = 5,2(3)−1=5,

and the right side equals the point’s yyy-value of 555, so (3,5)(3, 5)(3,5) is on the line. Is (2,4)(2, 4)(2,4) on the graph of 3x+2y=123x + 2y = 123x+2y=12? Substitute:

3(2)+2(4)=6+8=14,3(2) + 2(4) = 6 + 8 = 14,3(2)+2(4)=6+8=14,

which is not 121212, so (2,4)(2, 4)(2,4) is not on the line. The test is the same substitution you used to check solutions last lesson, now read geometrically. On the line means the pair solves the equation, and off the line means the pair does not.

Worked example 5 Find a missing coordinate so a point lies on a line

The point (k,7)(k, 7)(k,7) lies on the line y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1. Because the point is on the line, its coordinates must satisfy the equation, so substitute y=7y = 7y=7 and solve for the unknown xxx-coordinate kkk:

7=2k−1,8=2k,k=4.7 = 2k - 1, \qquad 8 = 2k, \qquad k = 4.7=2k−1,8=2k,k=4.

So the point is (4,7)(4, 7)(4,7).

For a second case, find the point on 3x+2y=123x + 2y = 123x+2y=12 whose x-coordinate is 111. Substitute x=1x = 1x=1 and solve for yyy:

3(1)+2y=12,2y=9,y=92.3(1) + 2y = 12, \qquad 2y = 9, \qquad y = \tfrac{9}{2}.3(1)+2y=12,2y=9,y=29​.

So the point is (1,92)\left(1, \tfrac{9}{2}\right)(1,29​). The fractional yyy is no problem: the line passes through plenty of points that do not land on whole-number grid marks, and this is one of them.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Drawing a line through a single point. One point does not fix a line. Plot at least two solutions, and use a third as a check that they truly line up before you draw.
  • Swapping which variable to zero for the intercepts. The x-intercept comes from setting y=0y = 0y=0 (not x=0x = 0x=0), and the y-intercept from setting x=0x = 0x=0. Zero the coordinate of the axis you are crossing, which is the opposite letter.
  • Mixing up horizontal and vertical. y=cy = cy=c is a horizontal line (the height is locked), and x=cx = cx=c is a vertical line (the column is locked). It is easy to reverse these because y=cy = cy=c looks like it should be “up and down.”
  • Not solving for yyy before making a table. You can plug an xxx-value straight into 2x+y=52x + y = 52x+y=5 and then solve the leftover equation for yyy each time, but that repeats work. Solving once for y=5−2xy = 5 - 2xy=5−2x first is faster and invites fewer sign errors.
  • Assuming every point on the line has whole-number coordinates. Between the tidy grid points the line is packed with fractional solutions like (1,92)\left(1, \tfrac{9}{2}\right)(1,29​). They are on the graph too.
  • Sign slips in the table. For y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1 at x=−1x = -1x=−1, the value is 2(−1)−1=−32(-1) - 1 = -32(−1)−1=−3, not 111. Substitute the negative carefully, including its sign.

Key takeaways

  • The graph of an equation is the set of all points (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) that solve it. For a linear equation the graph is a straight line, so “the graph” and “the solution set” name the same thing.
  • Two points fix a line, so to graph one you only need two solutions (plot a third as a check).
  • Table method: solve for yyy, choose several xxx-values, compute each yyy, plot the pairs, and draw the line through them.
  • Intercept method: the x-intercept comes from setting y=0y = 0y=0 and solving for xxx; the y-intercept comes from setting x=0x = 0x=0 and solving for yyy. These are two convenient points to plot.
  • y=cy = cy=c graphs as a horizontal line at height ccc; x=cx = cx=c graphs as a vertical line through x=cx = cx=c.
  • A point is on a line exactly when its coordinates satisfy the equation. Substitute to check: equal sides mean on the line, unequal sides mean off it.

A bit of history (optional)

For centuries people mostly solved equations without ever drawing one. An equation was something you worked on in words and numbers. Hardly anyone thought to look at it.

One early break came from Nicole Oresme, a French scholar of the 1300s who later became a bishop. He wanted a way to picture how one quantity shifts while a second quantity shifts alongside it. So he drew the second quantity as a row of upright bars, each bar as tall as the value it stood for. When a quantity grew at a steady rate, the tops of those bars ran along a straight edge. Steady change, he saw, has a straight shape. That is the claim this whole lesson rests on, noticed about three hundred years early.

Oresme had no grid to draw it on. That idea took shape gradually over the following centuries, with a major step in the 1630s from Rene Descartes and Pierre de Fermat, two Frenchmen who reached it separately and never worked together. Their move was to let a pair of numbers name a point, so an equation in xxx and yyy could name a whole curve, building the grid Oresme’s picture was still missing and making the question of what an equation looks like far easier to ask.

The name of your subject keeps the answer on record. Linear comes from linea, the Latin word for a line, because the solutions of ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c always land on one. Find two of them and draw.

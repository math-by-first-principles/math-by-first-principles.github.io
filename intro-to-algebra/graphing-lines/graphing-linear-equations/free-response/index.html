    1. Which pairs the line actually contains . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    The graph of a linear equation is not a picture that happens to run near its solutions. It is the solutions, and nothing else. This question uses that in both directions: deciding whether a given point belongs, and completing a point so that it does.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Decide which of the points (3,6)(3, 6)(3,6), (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) and (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) lie on the graph of 4x−y=64x - y = 64x−y=6. Show the substitution behind each verdict.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Two further points are known to lie on the same line. One of them is (k,10)(k, 10)(k,10); the other is the point of the line whose yyy-coordinate is 111. Find the missing coordinate of each.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Part A could also have been settled by first rearranging the equation into y=4x−6y = 4x - 6y=4x−6 and then comparing each point's yyy-coordinate against 4x−64x - 64x−6. Explain why rearranging cannot change which points lie on the graph, and say what job the rearranged form does better.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every part of this turns on one sentence from the lesson: what the word graph actually means. Write that sentence out before you start, and each part below becomes a substitution rather than a drawing.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      You are handed one of the two coordinates each time, so put it into the equation where it belongs. What is left is a single-unknown equation of the kind you have been solving for weeks.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Two equations with the same solutions cannot have different graphs, because a graph has nothing in it except solutions. Say why that settles the first half, then ask which of the two forms makes producing a solution shorter.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    (3,6)(3, 6)(3,6) and (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) are on the line; (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) is not, since it gives −6-6−6 rather than 666.

    Part B

    k=4k = 4k=4, so the first point is (4,10)(4, 10)(4,10); the second point is (74,1)\left(\tfrac{7}{4}, 1\right)(47​,1).

    • (74,1)\left(\tfrac{7}{4}, 1\right)(47​,1) may be written (1.75,1)(1.75, 1)(1.75,1); what is not the same answer is rounding it to (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1), which is not on the line at all

    Part C

    Rearranging produces an equation with exactly the same solutions, and a graph is nothing but its solutions, so the picture is untouched. The rearranged form is worth having because it computes yyy from a chosen xxx in a single step, which is what building a table of values needs.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Substitute each pair into 4x−y=64x - y = 64x−y=6, putting the first coordinate in for xxx and the second for yyy, then compare the two sides.

    For (3,6)(3, 6)(3,6):

    4(3)−6=12−6=6,4(3) - 6 = 12 - 6 = 6,4(3)−6=12−6=6,

    which is exactly the 666 the equation demands, so (3,6)(3, 6)(3,6) is on the line.

    For (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2), the subtraction of a negative yyy-value is where a sign usually slips:

    4(1)−(−2)=4+2=6,4(1) - (-2) = 4 + 2 = 6,4(1)−(−2)=4+2=6,

    so (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) is on the line as well.

    For (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6):

    4(0)−6=0−6=−6,4(0) - 6 = 0 - 6 = -6,4(0)−6=0−6=−6,

    and −6-6−6 is not 666, so (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) is off the line. Compare it with the first point: (3,6)(3, 6)(3,6) and (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) share a yyy-coordinate, and sharing one coordinate with a point of the line settles nothing at all.

    Part B

    A point lies on the line exactly when its coordinates satisfy the equation. Each of these points hands you one coordinate, and the equation then supplies the other.

    For (k,10)(k, 10)(k,10), put y=10y = 10y=10 into 4x−y=64x - y = 64x−y=6 and solve for the xxx-coordinate:

    4k−10=6,4k=16,k=4,4k - 10 = 6, \qquad 4k = 16, \qquad k = 4,4k−10=6,4k=16,k=4,

    so that point is (4,10)(4, 10)(4,10).

    For the point whose yyy-coordinate is 111, put y=1y = 1y=1 in and solve the same way:

    4x−1=6,4x=7,x=74,4x - 1 = 6, \qquad 4x = 7, \qquad x = \tfrac{7}{4},4x−1=6,4x=7,x=47​,

    so that point is (74,1)\left(\tfrac{7}{4}, 1\right)(47​,1). The fraction is not a symptom of a mistake. Between the corners of the grid the line is packed with solutions whose coordinates are not whole numbers, and this is one of them.

    Part C

    Rearranging is not a change of subject. It is a change of clothes.

    Subtract 4x4x4x from both sides of 4x−y=64x - y = 64x−y=6, then multiply through by −1-1−1:

    −y=6−4x,y=4x−6.-y = 6 - 4x, \qquad y = 4x - 6.−y=6−4x,y=4x−6.

    Every step there is reversible, so a pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) satisfies one equation exactly when it satisfies the other: the two equations have precisely the same solutions. The graph of an equation is nothing but its set of solutions, so two equations with the same solutions have the same graph, down to the last point. The picture cannot notice which form you happened to write.

    What the rearrangement does change is the labour. In 4x−y=64x - y = 64x−y=6 a chosen xxx still leaves an equation to be solved for yyy; in y=4x−6y = 4x - 6y=4x−6 the value of yyy is computed outright:

    x=3:  y=4(3)−6=6.x = 3: \; y = 4(3) - 6 = 6.x=3:y=4(3)−6=6.

    That is exactly what a table of values wants, one row at a time, and it is also what part A's three checks become: compute 4x−64x - 64x−6 and see whether it agrees with the yyy you were handed.

    In one line

    Of the three points, (3,6)(3, 6)(3,6) and (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) satisfy 4x−y=64x - y = 64x−y=6 and so lie on its graph, while (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) gives −6-6−6 and does not. The point (k,10)(k, 10)(k,10) has k=4k = 4k=4, and the point of the line with yyy-coordinate 111 is (74,1)\left(\tfrac{7}{4}, 1\right)(47​,1). Rearranging into y=4x−6y = 4x - 6y=4x−6 leaves the graph identical, because the solutions are identical; all it changes is that each yyy is now computed in one step.

    Another way: Test the points with the rearranged form instead

    Once the equation is written as y=4x−6y = 4x - 6y=4x−6, checking a point becomes a comparison rather than a two-sided evaluation: compute 4x−64x - 64x−6 from the point's xxx-coordinate and see whether it agrees with the point's yyy-coordinate.

    x=3:  4(3)−6=6  (matches y=6),x=0:  4(0)−6=−6  (does not match y=6).x = 3: \; 4(3) - 6 = 6 \; \text{(matches } y = 6), \qquad x = 0: \; 4(0) - 6 = -6 \; \text{(does not match } y = 6).x=3:4(3)−6=6(matches y=6),x=0:4(0)−6=−6(does not match y=6).

    The verdicts are the same, because the equations are the same equation.

    When it is worth it When several points are being checked against one line. Rearranging costs one step and then saves a step on every point after that.

    2. Two routes to the same picture . Foundational, 11 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    Two points are enough to fix a line, but they have to be found before they can be plotted. A table produces solutions one row at a time; the intercepts produce two of them almost for free. This question runs both routes and then asks what they have in common.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Rearrange x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 so that it gives yyy directly from xxx, and use it to find the solutions at x=−2x = -2x=−2, x=0x = 0x=0, x=2x = 2x=2 and x=4x = 4x=4. Then say what makes those four values of xxx convenient for this particular equation.

    2. B
      Part B.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Part A produced four solutions and part B produced two. Say what the two routes have in common, where a fifth solution of x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 has to land once it is plotted, and what it would mean if one of your four table points did not sit in line with the rest.

      Carry your own answer forward Answer this from the four points and the two intercepts you actually produced above. What is being credited is the account of where further solutions have to fall, and of what an out-of-line point signals, not the particular values you obtained.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part B

      An intercept is where the line meets one of the axes, and every point of an axis has one coordinate you already know without computing anything. Decide which coordinate that is before you touch the equation.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    y=3−x2y = 3 - \tfrac{x}{2}y=3−2x​, giving the solutions (−2,4)(-2, 4)(−2,4), (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3), (2,2)(2, 2)(2,2) and (4,1)(4, 1)(4,1); even values of xxx are what keep every yyy a whole number.

    • y=6−x2y = \tfrac{6 - x}{2}y=26−x​ is the same rearrangement written differently; it is the four solutions that have to match

    Part B

    Setting y=0y = 0y=0 gives the x-intercept (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0); setting x=0x = 0x=0 gives the y-intercept (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4).

    Part C

    Both routes only ever produce solutions; the intercepts are simply two that are quick to compute and easy to plot. Any further solution must land on the same straight line, since the graph is the whole solution set and a linear equation's is straight. A point sitting apart from the rest means the arithmetic in that row went wrong.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Rearranging first is what makes a table quick: do the algebra once, then only arithmetic.

    Subtract xxx from both sides of x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 and divide by 222:

    2y=6−x,y=6−x2=3−x2.2y = 6 - x, \qquad y = \tfrac{6 - x}{2} = 3 - \tfrac{x}{2}.2y=6−x,y=26−x​=3−2x​.

    Now compute one yyy for each chosen xxx:

    x=−2:  y=3+1=4;x=0:  y=3−0=3.x = -2: \; y = 3 + 1 = 4; \qquad x = 0: \; y = 3 - 0 = 3.x=−2:y=3+1=4;x=0:y=3−0=3.

    x=2:  y=3−1=2;x=4:  y=3−2=1.x = 2: \; y = 3 - 1 = 2; \qquad x = 4: \; y = 3 - 2 = 1.x=2:y=3−1=2;x=4:y=3−2=1.

    So the four solutions are (−2,4)(-2, 4)(−2,4), (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3), (2,2)(2, 2)(2,2) and (4,1)(4, 1)(4,1).

    The choice of xxx-values was not innocent. The rearranged form halves xxx, so an even xxx leaves a whole number behind while an odd one would leave a half. Taking −2-2−2, 000, 222 and 444 therefore lands every point on a corner of the grid, and it also spreads the points either side of zero instead of crowding them into one corner.

    Part B

    Each intercept is found by zeroing the coordinate of the axis being crossed, which is always the opposite letter to the one you are looking for.

    Every point of the x-axis has y=0y = 0y=0, so set y=0y = 0y=0 in 4x−3y=124x - 3y = 124x−3y=12:

    4x−3(0)=12,4x=12,x=3,4x - 3(0) = 12, \qquad 4x = 12, \qquad x = 3,4x−3(0)=12,4x=12,x=3,

    and the x-intercept is (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0).

    Every point of the y-axis has x=0x = 0x=0, so set x=0x = 0x=0:

    4(0)−3y=12,−3y=12,y=−4,4(0) - 3y = 12, \qquad -3y = 12, \qquad y = -4,4(0)−3y=12,−3y=12,y=−4,

    and the y-intercept is (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4). A negative intercept is nothing unusual; it simply puts that crossing below the origin. Plot (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0) and (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4), draw the line through them, and the graph is finished.

    Part C

    Both routes do the same thing, and it is worth saying what.

    A table row is a solution: choose xxx, then compute the yyy that makes the equation true. An intercept is a solution too: choose the value 000 for one of the two letters, then compute the other. The intercept route does not produce a different kind of point, only a different way of choosing which solutions to compute, picked because zero makes one term vanish and because the resulting points sit on the axes, where they are easy to plot accurately.

    That also settles where a fifth solution of x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 has to land. The graph of an equation is its whole set of solutions, and for a linear equation that set is a straight line, so any further solution is a point of the same line as the four already plotted. Take x=6x = 6x=6:

    y=3−62=0,so (6,0),y = 3 - \tfrac{6}{2} = 0, \qquad \text{so } (6, 0),y=3−26​=0,so (6,0),

    which continues the same line rather than starting a new one.

    And if one of the four points had sat apart from the rest, the graph would not be to blame. A linear equation's graph does not bend, so the fault would lie in that row's arithmetic. Recompute the row and the point rejoins the others, which is exactly why plotting a third and fourth solution is worth the trouble.

    In one line

    Rearranged, x+2y=6x + 2y = 6x+2y=6 becomes y=3−x2y = 3 - \tfrac{x}{2}y=3−2x​, whose solutions at x=−2,0,2,4x = -2, 0, 2, 4x=−2,0,2,4 are (−2,4)(-2, 4)(−2,4), (0,3)(0, 3)(0,3), (2,2)(2, 2)(2,2) and (4,1)(4, 1)(4,1); even values of xxx are what keep the halving whole. The graph of 4x−3y=124x - 3y = 124x−3y=12 crosses the axes at (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0) and (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4). Both routes simply produce solutions, so every further solution lies on that equation's own straight line, and a point out of line means a slip in the arithmetic rather than a bend in the graph.

    Another way: Choose the two easiest solutions, which need not be the intercepts

    The intercept route is a habit, not a rule, and it earns its place only when the two points it produces are easy to plot. For 4x−3y=124x - 3y = 124x−3y=12 they are. For 4x−3y=104x - 3y = 104x−3y=10 they are not: y=0y = 0y=0 gives x=104x = \tfrac{10}{4}x=410​ and x=0x = 0x=0 gives y=−103y = -\tfrac{10}{3}y=−310​, and neither lands on a corner of the grid.     x=1:  −3y=6,  y=−2;x=4:  −3y=−6,  y=2,x = 1: \; -3y = 6, \; y = -2; \qquad x = 4: \; -3y = -6, \; y = 2,x=1:−3y=6,y=−2;x=4:−3y=−6,y=2,

    which gives (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) and (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2), two points as good as any for drawing through.

    When it is worth it Whenever an intercept comes out fractional. Two points fix the line whichever two they are, so there is nothing sacred about the pair that happens to sit on the axes.

    (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) is on the line and (7,1)(7, 1)(7,1) is not. Two tested points suffice because the graph is a straight line and only one straight line passes through a given pair of points, so the line drawn through the two intercepts is the graph itself.

    Substitute both candidates into the equation.

    2(5)+5(2)=10+10=20,2(7)+5(1)=14+5=19.2(5) + 5(2) = 10 + 10 = 20, \qquad 2(7) + 5(1) = 14 + 5 = 19.2(5)+5(2)=10+10=20,2(7)+5(1)=14+5=19.

    The first gives 202020, so (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) is on the line. The second gives 191919, not 202020, so (7,1)(7, 1)(7,1) is off it.

    The two intercepts are (10,0)(10, 0)(10,0) and (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4), both solutions, so both are points of the graph. The graph of a linear equation is a straight line, and only one straight line passes through two distinct points, so the graph and the line drawn through those two points are the same set. Every point of the drawing is therefore a solution, and every solution is on the drawing.

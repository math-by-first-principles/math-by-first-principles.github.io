    1. What the two rules ask of a pair of slopes . Foundational, 13 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 13 pts

    Each pair below is settled by the same two numbers, their slopes, and an equation in standard form hides its slope until you solve for yyy. This question is that routine three times over: get both slopes into view, then ask what each rule has to say about them.

    1. A
      Part A.

      The line LLL has equation 4x+10y=254x + 10y = 254x+10y=25. Write the slope of any line parallel to LLL, and the slope of any line perpendicular to LLL. Show the step that gets LLL's own slope into view.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 5 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Classify each pair as parallel, perpendicular, or neither, and show the comparison of slopes that decides it.

      Pair 1: 5x−2y=85x - 2y = 85x−2y=8 and y=−52x+1y = -\tfrac{5}{2}x + 1y=−25​x+1.

      Pair 2: y=52x−4y = \tfrac{5}{2}x - 4y=25​x−4 and 2x+5y=202x + 5y = 202x+5y=20.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Changing the sign of a slope and taking its negative reciprocal are two different operations. Using Pair 1's slopes, explain which of the product test's demands a bare sign change fails to meet, and name the only slopes for which the two operations happen to agree.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing can be decided until both slopes are visible, and an equation in standard form is not showing you its slope. Solve each one for yyy first, and do all of the comparing afterwards.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      There are two rules and two pairs here, so run both rules on each pair instead of stopping at the first thing that looks familiar. Equal slopes settle one rule; a product of exactly −1-1−1 settles the other.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask what the number −1-1−1 is really demanding of a product: it pins down a sign and it pins down a size. Then check which of those two demands each operation on a slope actually meets.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    A parallel line has slope −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​; a perpendicular line has slope 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​.

    Part B

    Pair 1 is neither: its slopes are opposite in sign but not reciprocal, and their product is not −1-1−1. Pair 2 is perpendicular: its slopes are negative reciprocals, and their product is −1-1−1.

    Part C

    A sign change fixes the sign of the product but not its size: Pair 1's slopes multiply to −254-\tfrac{25}{4}−425​, and only the reciprocal brings the size down to 111. The two operations agree only for the slopes 111 and −1-1−1.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The equation of LLL is in standard form, so the coefficient of xxx is not the slope. Solve for yyy first:

    10y=−4x+25,y=−25x+52.10y = -4x + 25, \qquad y = -\tfrac{2}{5}x + \tfrac{5}{2}.10y=−4x+25,y=−52​x+25​.

    So LLL has slope −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​. A parallel line climbs at the same rate, so it has that same slope, −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​.

    A perpendicular line has the negative reciprocal. Flip 25\tfrac{2}{5}52​ to 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​ and change the sign, and since the slope was already negative, changing its sign makes the result positive:

    m⊥=−1−25=52.m_{\perp} = -\frac{1}{-\tfrac{2}{5}} = \tfrac{5}{2}.m⊥​=−−52​1​=25​.

    The product test confirms it:

    −25⋅52=−1010=−1.-\tfrac{2}{5} \cdot \tfrac{5}{2} = -\tfrac{10}{10} = -1.−52​⋅25​=−1010​=−1.

    Part B

    Get every slope into view before comparing anything.

    Pair 1. Solve the first equation for yyy:

    −2y=−5x+8,y=52x−4.-2y = -5x + 8, \qquad y = \tfrac{5}{2}x - 4.−2y=−5x+8,y=25​x−4.

    The two slopes are 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​ and −52-\tfrac{5}{2}−25​. They are not equal, so the lines are not parallel. Multiply them:

    52⋅(−52)=−254,\tfrac{5}{2} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{5}{2}\right) = -\tfrac{25}{4},25​⋅(−25​)=−425​,

    which is not −1-1−1, so they are not perpendicular either. Pair 1 is neither.

    Pair 2. Solve the second equation for yyy:

    5y=−2x+20,y=−25x+4.5y = -2x + 20, \qquad y = -\tfrac{2}{5}x + 4.5y=−2x+20,y=−52​x+4.

    The two slopes are 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​ and −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​, which are not equal, so not parallel. Their product is

    52⋅(−25)=−1010=−1,\tfrac{5}{2} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{2}{5}\right) = -\tfrac{10}{10} = -1,25​⋅(−52​)=−1010​=−1,

    so Pair 2 is perpendicular.

    Part C

    Write both operations out on a slope mmm. Changing the sign gives −m-m−m; the negative reciprocal gives −1m-\tfrac{1}{m}−m1​. The product test asks for a product of exactly −1-1−1, which is two demands at once: the right sign, and the right size.

    A sign change meets the first and ignores the second. On Pair 1's slope 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​ it produces −52-\tfrac{5}{2}−25​, and

    52⋅(−52)=−254.\tfrac{5}{2} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{5}{2}\right) = -\tfrac{25}{4}.25​⋅(−25​)=−425​.

    The sign is right and the size is 254\tfrac{25}{4}425​, not 111. Reciprocating is the step that fixes the size, because a number times its reciprocal is 111:

    52⋅(−25)=−1.\tfrac{5}{2} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{2}{5}\right) = -1.25​⋅(−52​)=−1.

    The two operations give the same answer exactly when −m=−1m-m = -\tfrac{1}{m}−m=−m1​, that is when mmm is its own reciprocal. Reciprocating changes the size of a number unless that size is already 111, so this happens only for m=1m = 1m=1 and m=−1m = -1m=−1. (A slope of 000 is not in the running at all: it has no reciprocal.) So a line of slope 111 really is perpendicular to a line of slope −1-1−1, and there the shortcut is right by accident rather than by rule.

    In one line

    Lines parallel to LLL have slope −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​ and lines perpendicular to LLL have slope 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​. Pair 1 is neither, since 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​ and −52-\tfrac{5}{2}−25​ multiply to −254-\tfrac{25}{4}−425​, and Pair 2 is perpendicular, since 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​ and −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​ multiply to −1-1−1. A bare sign change gets the sign of the product right and its size wrong, and it agrees with the negative reciprocal only for the slopes 111 and −1-1−1.

    2. Perpendicular to the same line . Reasoning, 14 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Reasoning 14 pts

    A single line has infinitely many lines perpendicular to it, standing at right angles all along its length. This question asks what those perpendicular lines have to do with one another: first on one example, then for every slope at once, and finally in the two cases the slope rule was never able to reach.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Line LLL has slope 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​. Line MMM is perpendicular to LLL, and line NNN is perpendicular to MMM. Find the slope of MMM, and then the slope of NNN.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Now prove it for every such line. Let LLL be a line whose slope mmm is neither 000 nor undefined, and let MMM and NNN be any two lines perpendicular to LLL. Prove that MMM and NNN have equal slopes. Begin by saying why MMM and NNN have slopes at all, and finish by stating carefully what equal slopes do and do not settle about the two lines themselves.

      Complete the derivation Each line should follow from the one above it. Say what lets you take each step. 6 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Part B excluded two cases: LLL horizontal, and LLL vertical. Take them one at a time. In each case work out which lines are perpendicular to LLL, decide whether the conclusion of part B still holds for them, and say what settles it. Explain why the product test cannot be what settles either case.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      The whole question is one operation applied twice, so keep asking what the negative reciprocal does to a number when you feed it the result of doing it once. A quantity that comes back to where it started is worth noticing.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Write the perpendicularity of each line as its own equation in the product test, then look at what the two equations have in common. The hypothesis that mmm is not zero is sitting there because exactly one step needs it.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      In each excluded case, work out first what a line perpendicular to LLL actually looks like, and only then ask whether there are two slopes to put into a product. One of the cases has slopes to compare and the other has none.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    MMM has slope −43-\tfrac{4}{3}−34​, and NNN has slope 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​, the slope LLL started with.

    Part B

    Both perpendicular slopes are forced to be −1m-\tfrac{1}{m}−m1​, one and the same number, so MMM and NNN have equal slopes. That makes them parallel if they are two different lines, and it leaves open the case in which they are one line.

    Part C

    The conclusion holds in both. The lines perpendicular to a horizontal line are the vertical lines, alike by inspection; the lines perpendicular to a vertical line are the horizontal lines, which all have slope 000. Neither case can be settled by the product test, because each has a line with no slope to multiply.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Each step is one negative reciprocal.

    From LLL to MMM, flip 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ and change the sign:

    mM=−134=−43.m_M = -\frac{1}{\tfrac{3}{4}} = -\tfrac{4}{3}.mM​=−43​1​=−34​.

    From MMM to NNN, do the same to −43-\tfrac{4}{3}−34​. Flipping gives −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​, and changing the sign gives

    mN=−1−43=34.m_N = -\frac{1}{-\tfrac{4}{3}} = \tfrac{3}{4}.mN​=−−34​1​=43​.

    Each step passes the product test: 34⋅(−43)=−1\tfrac{3}{4} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{4}{3}\right) = -143​⋅(−34​)=−1 and −43⋅34=−1-\tfrac{4}{3} \cdot \tfrac{3}{4} = -1−34​⋅43​=−1. Two flips have carried the slope back to where it began, so NNN has the same slope as LLL.

    Part B

    First, the slopes exist. A vertical line is perpendicular only to horizontal lines. So if MMM were vertical, LLL would have to be horizontal, with slope 000, which the hypothesis rules out. Therefore MMM is not vertical and has a slope, and the same argument covers NNN. Without this step the product test would be applied to a line that has no slope to supply.

    Next, the equality. Call the two slopes mMm_MmM​ and mNm_NmN​. Perpendicularity to LLL is the product test in each case:

    m⋅mM=−1,m⋅mN=−1.m \cdot m_M = -1, \qquad m \cdot m_N = -1.m⋅mM​=−1,m⋅mN​=−1.

    Since m≠0m \neq 0m=0, each equation may be divided by mmm:

    mM=−1m,mN=−1m.m_M = -\frac{1}{m}, \qquad m_N = -\frac{1}{m}.mM​=−m1​,mN​=−m1​.

    The two right-hand sides are the same number, so mM=mNm_M = m_NmM​=mN​. Any two lines perpendicular to LLL therefore have equal slopes. (Subtracting the two product equations gives it just as well: m(mM−mN)=0m\left(m_M - m_N\right) = 0m(mM​−mN​)=0 with m≠0m \neq 0m=0 forces mM=mNm_M = m_NmM​=mN​.)

    Last, what that settles. Equal slopes alone do not make two lines parallel. Writing MMM as y=mMx+bMy = m_M x + b_My=mM​x+bM​ and NNN as y=mNx+bNy = m_N x + b_Ny=mN​x+bN​, the slopes agree, so either the intercepts differ, in which case the lines never meet and are parallel, or the intercepts agree too and the two equations describe a single line. So the proved statement is this: MMM and NNN are parallel whenever they are two distinct lines.

    Part C

    Case 1: LLL is horizontal, with slope 000. The lines perpendicular to a horizontal line are the vertical lines x=cx = cx=c. Any two of those run straight up and down side by side, so they never meet unless ccc is the same for both, in which case they are the same line. The conclusion of part B survives: two lines perpendicular to LLL are parallel whenever they are distinct.

    What settled it was inspection, not arithmetic. A vertical line has no slope, so there is no number to write in the second factor of

    m⋅mM=−1,m \cdot m_M = -1,m⋅mM​=−1,

    and the equation cannot even be set up. The right angle between a horizontal line and a vertical line is read off the picture, exactly as it is for the two axes.

    Case 2: LLL is vertical, so LLL itself has no slope. The lines perpendicular to a vertical line are the horizontal lines y=ky = ky=k, every one of them with slope 000:

    mM=0=mN.m_M = 0 = m_N.mM​=0=mN​.

    Their slopes are equal, so the conclusion holds again, and two distinct horizontal lines are parallel. This time the perpendicular lines do have slopes, but the product test is still unavailable, because now it is LLL that has no slope to contribute.

    So the statement of part B is true more widely than its proof reaches. The proof needs mmm to be a nonzero number; the two excluded cases have to be checked on their own, and both check out.

    In one line

    MMM has slope −43-\tfrac{4}{3}−34​ and NNN has slope 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​, the slope LLL began with. In general, if LLL has slope mmm with m≠0m \neq 0m=0, every line perpendicular to LLL has slope −1m-\tfrac{1}{m}−m1​, so any two of them have equal slopes and are parallel whenever they are distinct lines. Both excluded cases hold too: the perpendiculars to a horizontal line are the vertical lines, the perpendiculars to a vertical line are the horizontal lines, and neither case can be settled by a product of slopes.

    Another way: One flip, applied to one number

    Part B can be run in a sentence once you notice that the perpendicular slope is determined by mmm alone. Perpendicularity to LLL pins each of MMM and NNN to the value

    −1m,-\frac{1}{m},−m1​,

    and a quantity determined by mmm cannot take two different values for one value of mmm. So the two slopes agree, with no manipulation at all.

    When it is worth it When you want the reason rather than the algebra. The written-out version is still worth having, because it is the one that shows exactly where the hypothesis m≠0m \neq 0m=0 is needed.

    Line PPP has slope −52-\tfrac{5}{2}−25​. Line QQQ is perpendicular to PPP, and line RRR is perpendicular to PPP as well, through a point that is not on QQQ.     Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    y=34x−4y = \tfrac{3}{4}x - 4y=43​x−4.

    Part B

    y=−43x+21y = -\tfrac{4}{3}x + 21y=−34​x+21.

    The route has slope −43-\tfrac{4}{3}−34​, the same as the part B street, and a different yyy-intercept, so the two are parallel and distinct. A rider who stays on the route never reaches that street.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    A street that never meets Elm Street runs in the same direction, so it has the same slope. Elm Street is in standard form, so solve for yyy to see that slope:

    −4y=−3x+12,y=34x−3.-4y = -3x + 12, \qquad y = \tfrac{3}{4}x - 3.−4y=−3x+12,y=43​x−3.

    The slope is 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​, and the new street carries that slope through the marker (12,5)(12, 5)(12,5). Point-slope form:

    y−5=34(x−12),y−5=34x−9.y - 5 = \tfrac{3}{4}(x - 12), \qquad y - 5 = \tfrac{3}{4}x - 9.y−5=43​(x−12),y−5=43​x−9.

    Add 555 to both sides:

    y=34x−4.y = \tfrac{3}{4}x - 4.y=43​x−4.

    The two intercepts, −3-3−3 for Elm Street and −4-4−4 for the new street, are different, which is what makes this a second street rather than Elm Street written out again.

    Part B

    This street needs the negative reciprocal of Elm Street's slope 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​: flip it to 43\tfrac{4}{3}34​ and change the sign, giving −43-\tfrac{4}{3}−34​. The product test checks it:

    34⋅(−43)=−1212=−1.\tfrac{3}{4} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{4}{3}\right) = -\tfrac{12}{12} = -1.43​⋅(−34​)=−1212​=−1.

    Now point-slope form through (12,5)(12, 5)(12,5):

    y−5=−43(x−12),y−5=−43x+16.y - 5 = -\tfrac{4}{3}(x - 12), \qquad y - 5 = -\tfrac{4}{3}x + 16.y−5=−34​(x−12),y−5=−34​x+16.

Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Intuition

Two lines can sit in the plane in a few basic ways. They can run in exactly the same direction and never touch, like the two rails of a train track: those are parallel. They can cross, and among all the ways to cross, one is special, meeting square at a right angle like the corner of a page: those are perpendicular. A line’s direction is captured completely by its slope, so both of these relationships must be readable straight from the slopes. This lesson pins down exactly how: two distinct lines are parallel when their slopes are equal, and perpendicular when their slopes are negative reciprocals. One family of lines has no slope to compare at all: pairing a vertical line with another vertical line, with a horizontal line, or with any other slanted line, is decided by sight instead, and each of those cases gets its own short treatment later. Once you can read the slope patterns and handle that one exception, you can classify any pair of lines. You can also build new lines to order, parallel or perpendicular to whatever line you are handed.

Learning goals

  • Match slopes for parallel lines with different intercepts
  • Take the negative reciprocal for a perpendicular slope
  • Treat vertical and horizontal lines as the exception to the product rule
  • Classify two lines by comparing their slopes
  • Write the required line through a point with point-slope form

Parallel lines: equal slopes

Slope measures direction, the tilt of a line as rise over run. Two lines point the same way exactly when they climb at the same rate, so it should feel right that parallel lines have equal slopes. Suppose one line rises two units for every unit across and the other rises three. Then the steeper one is slowly pulling away from the flatter one, and sooner or later they cross. Only when the two rates match does the distance between the lines hold steady forever, and holding a steady distance is what it means to be parallel.

That picture is convincing, but the algebra makes it exact. The algebra also sorts out the one case the picture glosses over: two lines with the same slope that are secretly the same line.

Why equal slopes mean parallel#

Try it first on the two lines drawn above, y=12x+2y = \tfrac{1}{2}x + 2y=21​x+2 and y=12x−1y = \tfrac{1}{2}x - 1y=21​x−1. A meeting point is an xxx where both right-hand sides agree, so set them equal:

12x+2=12x−1.\tfrac{1}{2}x + 2 = \tfrac{1}{2}x - 1.21​x+2=21​x−1.

Subtract 12x\tfrac{1}{2}x21​x from both sides:

2=−1.2 = -1.2=−1.

That is false for every xxx, so no meeting point exists: the lines never cross, exactly as the picture shows. Now compare a pair with different slopes, y=12x+2y = \tfrac{1}{2}x + 2y=21​x+2 and y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1. Setting those equal gives 12x+2=2x−1\tfrac{1}{2}x + 2 = 2x - 121​x+2=2x−1, so 3=32x3 = \tfrac{3}{2}x3=23​x and x=2x = 2x=2: one definite crossing. Last, compare the first line with itself, y=12x+2y = \tfrac{1}{2}x + 2y=21​x+2 against y=12x+2y = \tfrac{1}{2}x + 2y=21​x+2. Setting those equal gives 2=22 = 22=2, true for every xxx, so every point is shared: the two equations describe one line written twice. Three splits, three outcomes: different slopes cross once, equal slopes with different intercepts never cross, and equal slopes with equal intercepts share every point. Nothing about any of that depended on these particular numbers. The general argument below repeats the same moves with letters in place of digits, so it settles every pair of lines at once.

Take two nonvertical lines and write each in slope-intercept form, y=m1x+b1y = m_1 x + b_1y=m1​x+b1​ and y=m2x+b2y = m_2 x + b_2y=m2​x+b2​. The lines meet at any point where their yyy-values agree, so set the right-hand sides equal and see which xxx can satisfy both:

m1x+b1=m2x+b2.m_1 x + b_1 = m_2 x + b_2.m1​x+b1​=m2​x+b2​.

Gather the xxx-terms on one side:

(m1−m2) x=b2−b1.(m_1 - m_2)\,x = b_2 - b_1.(m1​−m2​)x=b2​−b1​.

Everything now hinges on whether the slopes are equal. Suppose first that they differ, so m1≠m2m_1 \neq m_2m1​=m2​. Then m1−m2m_1 - m_2m1​−m2​ is not zero, and you may divide by it:

x=b2−b1m1−m2.x = \frac{b_2 - b_1}{m_1 - m_2}.x=m1​−m2​b2​−b1​​.

That is one definite number, a single xxx where the lines cross. Different slopes force exactly one crossing, so lines with different slopes are never parallel.

Now suppose the slopes are equal, m1=m2m_1 = m_2m1​=m2​. Then m1−m2=0m_1 - m_2 = 0m1​−m2​=0, and the equation collapses to 0=b2−b10 = b_2 - b_10=b2​−b1​. If the intercepts differ, b1≠b2b_1 \neq b_2b1​=b2​, this says 000 equals a nonzero number, which is impossible: no xxx works, the lines share no point, and they are parallel. If instead the intercepts are also equal, b1=b2b_1 = b_2b1​=b2​, then every xxx works, and the two equations describe one and the same line lying on top of itself.

So among nonvertical lines, equal slopes with different intercepts is precisely the parallel case, and it is the only way two such lines can fail to cross.

∎

One family sits outside this argument because it has no slope to compare: the vertical lines. A vertical line has the equation x=cx = cx=c and an undefined slope, so slope-intercept form never applies to it. Even so, any two vertical lines with different ccc run straight up and down side by side and never meet, so distinct vertical lines are always parallel to one another. Two vertical lines with the same ccc, such as x=3x = 3x=3 and x=3x = 3x=3, are not a parallel pair at all: they are the same line written twice, exactly the coincident case you already saw for slanted lines. You handle vertical lines by inspection rather than by matching a slope number.

Worked example 1 Decide whether two lines are parallel

Are the lines y=−3x+5y = -3x + 5y=−3x+5 and 6x+2y=16x + 2y = 16x+2y=1 parallel? Parallelism is about slope, so get both slopes into view. The first line is already in slope-intercept form, and its slope is m1=−3m_1 = -3m1​=−3.

The second line is in standard form, so solve it for yyy to expose its slope. Subtract 6x6x6x from both sides, then divide every term by 222:

2y=−6x+1,y=−3x+12.2y = -6x + 1, \qquad y = -3x + \tfrac{1}{2}.2y=−6x+1,y=−3x+21​.

Its slope is m2=−3m_2 = -3m2​=−3 as well. The two slopes are equal, −3=−3-3 = -3−3=−3, and the intercepts differ, 5≠125 \neq \tfrac{1}{2}5=21​, so the lines are parallel and not the same line.

Perpendicular lines: negative reciprocal slopes

Perpendicular is the right-angle case, and the slope rule for it is less obvious than for parallel lines. Two lines with defined, nonzero slopes are perpendicular exactly when their slopes are negative reciprocals: flip one slope over and change its sign to get the other. Written as an equation, the slopes satisfy

m1⋅m2=−1,equivalentlym2=−1m1.m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad m_2 = -\frac{1}{m_1}.m1​⋅m2​=−1,equivalentlym2​=−m1​1​.

So a line of slope 222 is perpendicular to a line of slope −12-\tfrac{1}{2}−21​. A line of slope −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ is perpendicular to a line of slope 43\tfrac{4}{3}34​, and in every case the product of the two slopes is −1-1−1. This is not a rule to accept on faith. It falls out of a single idea: a perpendicular line is the original line turned a quarter turn. Turning a slope triangle a quarter turn does a predictable thing to its rise and run.

Why perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals#

Try it first on the line drawn above: it has slope 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, built from a rise of 111 and a run of 222. Starting at PPP, step 222 to the right and 111 up, and you reach a second point on the line. That is the slope triangle in the picture, with legs 222 across and 111 up. Rotate that triangle a quarter turn (90 degrees counterclockwise) about PPP. The step 222 right becomes a step 222 up, and the step 111 up becomes a step 111 left, a run of −1-1−1. The turned triangle has rise 222 and run −1-1−1, so the rotated line has slope 2−1=−2\dfrac{2}{-1} = -2−12​=−2. Multiply the two slopes to check: 12⋅(−2)=−1\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot (-2) = -121​⋅(−2)=−1, exactly as the rule predicts. If the line tilts down instead, with rise −1-1−1 and run 222 (slope −12-\tfrac{1}{2}−21​), the same turn sends that downward step to a step right. The turned triangle then has run 111 and rise 222: slope 21=2\dfrac{2}{1} = 212​=2, and again −12⋅2=−1-\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = -1−21​⋅2=−1. None of that depended on the particular numbers 111 and 222, or on the line tilting up rather than down. Redo the same three moves: mark off a rise and a run, then turn the triangle a quarter turn. Use letters instead of digits, and the same pattern holds for every slope.

Start with a line L1L_1L1​ that is neither horizontal nor vertical. Write its slope as a fraction, m1=abm_1 = \dfrac{a}{b}m1​=ba​, where bbb is a horizontal run and aaa is the matching vertical rise: pick bbb positive, and let aaa carry the sign of the slope, so aaa is negative exactly when L1L_1L1​ tilts down. Both aaa and bbb are nonzero, since the line tilts. Pick any point PPP on L1L_1L1​. Starting at PPP, step bbb to the right and then aaa up or down according to its sign, and you land on a second point of L1L_1L1​. The two steps form a slope triangle with a horizontal leg bbb and a vertical leg aaa.

Now rotate the whole figure a quarter turn (ninety degrees counterclockwise) about the point PPP. Rotation is rigid and turns every direction by the same angle. So the line L1L_1L1​ swings to a new line L2L_2L2​ through PPP, and that new line meets L1L_1L1​ at a right angle. That is exactly a line perpendicular to L1L_1L1​, so finding the slope of L2L_2L2​ finds the slope of the perpendicular direction.

Track the slope triangle through the turn. A quarter turn counterclockwise sends a step ”bbb to the right” to a step ”bbb up”. The same turn sends the step ”aaa up or down” to a horizontal step of −a-a−a: an upward step (aaa positive) turns to a step left, and a downward step (aaa negative) turns to a step right. Starting again at PPP, the rotated triangle has run −a-a−a and rise bbb, which makes the slope of L2L_2L2​

m2=riserun=b−a=−ba.m_2 = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{b}{-a} = -\frac{b}{a}.m2​=runrise​=−ab​=−ab​.

Compare that with m1=abm_1 = \dfrac{a}{b}m1​=ba​: the fraction has been flipped and its sign changed, so m2m_2m2​ is the negative reciprocal of m1m_1m1​. Multiplying the two slopes confirms it directly:

m1⋅m2=ab⋅(−ba)=−abab=−1.m_1 \cdot m_2 = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \left(-\frac{b}{a}\right) = -\frac{ab}{ab} = -1.m1​⋅m2​=ba​⋅(−ab​)=−abab​=−1.

The argument runs backward too. Suppose two nonvertical lines have slopes whose product is −1-1−1, which forces one slope to be −ba-\dfrac{b}{a}−ab​ when the other is ab\dfrac{a}{b}ba​. Then the same quarter turn carries the first line onto a line parallel to the second. Parallel lines lean the same way, so the two lines meet at a right angle. Perpendicular and “slopes multiply to −1-1−1” are therefore the same condition, each guaranteeing the other.

∎

With the rule in hand, the two jobs it does are quick: reading off a perpendicular slope, and testing whether two given lines are perpendicular.

Worked example 2 Find a perpendicular slope, then test a pair of lines

First, find the slope of any line perpendicular to 2x−3y=62x - 3y = 62x−3y=6. Solve for yyy to read its slope. Subtract 2x2x2x and divide by −3-3−3:

−3y=−2x+6,y=23x−2,-3y = -2x + 6, \qquad y = \tfrac{2}{3}x - 2,−3y=−2x+6,y=32​x−2,

so this line has slope 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​. A perpendicular line has the negative reciprocal slope: flip 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ to 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​ and change the sign,

m⊥=−32.m_{\perp} = -\frac{3}{2}.m⊥​=−23​.

Second, are y=23x+1y = \tfrac{2}{3}x + 1y=32​x+1 and y=−32x+4y = -\tfrac{3}{2}x + 4y=−23​x+4 perpendicular? Multiply their slopes and check for −1-1−1:

23⋅(−32)=−66=−1.\frac{2}{3} \cdot \left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) = -\frac{6}{6} = -1.32​⋅(−23​)=−66​=−1.

The product is −1-1−1, so yes, the two lines meet at a right angle.

The horizontal and vertical case

The negative-reciprocal rule quietly assumes both lines have real-number slopes. So one pair of perpendicular lines slips through the cracks and has to be handled on its own: a horizontal line and a vertical line.

A horizontal line has slope 000, and a vertical line has an undefined slope. Picture the xxx-axis and the yyy-axis: one runs flat, the other stands straight up, and they cross at a perfect right angle. So a horizontal line is perpendicular to every vertical line, plainly and by sight. But you cannot confirm it with m1⋅m2=−1m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1m1​⋅m2​=−1, because a vertical line has no slope number to multiply, and 000 times “undefined” is not a meaningful product. On the horizontal side the trouble shows up differently: the negative reciprocal of 000 would be −10-\tfrac{1}{0}−01​. Dividing by zero is not allowed, so slope 000 has no negative reciprocal at all. The perpendicular partner of a horizontal line is a vertical line, and you know it because it stands upright, not because a slope product came out to −1-1−1.

Testing whether two lines are parallel, perpendicular, or neither

Check for a vertical line first, since the slope tests below need a real slope on both sides. If either line is vertical: two vertical lines are parallel when their equations differ and the same line when they match, a vertical line and a horizontal line are perpendicular, and a vertical line paired with any other slanted line is neither.

Once both lines have a real slope, the rest of the routine is short. Find the slope of each line, putting each equation into slope-intercept form or applying the slope formula to two points if that is what you are given. Then compare the two slopes: if they are equal, the lines are parallel (or the same line, when the intercepts match too). If their product is −1-1−1, the lines are perpendicular. If neither test holds, the lines are neither, crossing at some angle that is not a right angle.

Worked example 3 Classify three pairs of lines

Classify each pair as parallel, perpendicular, or neither.

Pair A: y=4x−1y = 4x - 1y=4x−1 and y=4x+6y = 4x + 6y=4x+6. Both slopes are 444, and the intercepts differ, so the lines are parallel.

Pair B: y=32x+5y = \tfrac{3}{2}x + 5y=23​x+5 and 2x+3y=92x + 3y = 92x+3y=9. Convert the second: 3y=−2x+93y = -2x + 93y=−2x+9, so y=−23x+3y = -\tfrac{2}{3}x + 3y=−32​x+3. The slopes are 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​ and −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​, and their product is

32⋅(−23)=−1,\frac{3}{2} \cdot \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) = -1,23​⋅(−32​)=−1,

so the lines are perpendicular.

Pair C: y=2x+1y = 2x + 1y=2x+1 and y=−2x+1y = -2x + 1y=−2x+1. The slopes are 222 and −2-2−2. They are not equal, so the lines are not parallel, and their product is 2⋅(−2)=−42 \cdot (-2) = -42⋅(−2)=−4, not −1-1−1, so they are not perpendicular either. This pair is neither. Watch the trap: the slopes look related, but a sign flip alone is not the same as a negative reciprocal, which also flips the fraction.

A sign flip was never enough, and this is the step it was missing, the fraction turning over. The readout sentence changes to name the right angle, and the two lines visibly square up.

Second, make the two lines parallel. Move the second line’s yyy-intercept away from the first line’s, to −2-2−2, then set its rise and run to match the first line’s. Now the two lines have the same slope and different intercepts, and the crossing dot disappears: parallel lines have nowhere to meet, and the readout sentence stops naming a crossing point.

Third, make them coincide. Holding that same slope, walk the second line’s yyy-intercept back onto the first line’s. The picture now shows what looks like a single line, and the dot is still gone, this time because the two lines meet at every point rather than none. This is the one moment where looking is not enough: only the readout sentence tells you the two equations describe the same line.

Writing equations of parallel and perpendicular lines

The slope rules also let you build a line to specification. Given a line and a point not on it, write the line through that point that is parallel, or perpendicular, to the one you were handed. When the given line has a slope, the plan is two steps. Get the slope you need from it (the same slope for parallel, the negative reciprocal for perpendicular). Then feed that slope and the given point into point-slope form and simplify.

When the given line is vertical or horizontal instead, skip point-slope form and use what you already know about writing x=cx = cx=c and y=cy = cy=c equations, together with this lesson’s exception: a vertical line’s parallel partner is vertical, and its perpendicular partner is horizontal. Through the point (5,1)(5, 1)(5,1), the line parallel to x=−2x = -2x=−2 is x=5x = 5x=5 (still vertical, now through x=5x = 5x=5), and the line perpendicular to x=−2x = -2x=−2 is y=1y = 1y=1 (horizontal, at the point’s height).

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Flipping the sign but not the fraction for a perpendicular slope. The perpendicular of slope 333 is −13-\tfrac{1}{3}−31​, not −3-3−3. A negative reciprocal both flips the fraction and changes the sign, so 3⋅(−13)=−13 \cdot (-\tfrac{1}{3}) = -13⋅(−31​)=−1, while 3⋅(−3)=−93 \cdot (-3) = -93⋅(−3)=−9.
  • Flipping the fraction but not the sign. The perpendicular of slope 25\tfrac{2}{5}52​ is −52-\tfrac{5}{2}−25​, not 52\tfrac{5}{2}25​. Without the minus sign the product is +1+1+1, not −1-1−1, so the angle is not a right angle.
  • Reading a slope before solving for yyy. In 6x+2y=16x + 2y = 16x+2y=1 the slope is not 666. Solve for yyy first to get y=−3x+12y = -3x + \tfrac{1}{2}y=−3x+21​; only then is the coefficient of xxx the slope.
  • Calling equal-slope lines “the same line”. Equal slopes with different intercepts are parallel, two separate lines. They coincide only when the intercepts match as well.
  • Forcing the product rule onto a horizontal and a vertical line. A vertical line has no slope number, so you cannot check m1⋅m2=−1m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1m1​⋅m2​=−1 for it. A horizontal line and a vertical line are perpendicular by sight; that pair is the one exception to the slope test.
  • Using the given point as the intercept. In the point-slope step, the point (2,−1)(2, -1)(2,−1) is not the yyy-intercept unless its xxx-coordinate is 000. Substitute the point into point-slope form and simplify to find the real intercept.

Key takeaways

  • Two nonvertical lines are parallel exactly when their slopes are equal, m1=m2m_1 = m_2m1​=m2​, with different yyy-intercepts. Equal slopes and equal intercepts mean the same line.
  • Two nonvertical lines are perpendicular exactly when their slopes are negative reciprocals, m2=−1m1m_2 = -\dfrac{1}{m_1}m2​=−m1​1​, equivalently m1⋅m2=−1m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1m1​⋅m2​=−1. Flip the fraction and change the sign.
  • Distinct vertical lines x=cx = cx=c are parallel to each other, and every horizontal line is perpendicular to every vertical line. That right angle is confirmed by sight, since a vertical line has no slope to put in the product rule, and 000 has no negative reciprocal.
  • To classify two lines, check for a vertical line first and handle it by sight. Otherwise find both slopes (convert to y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b or use the slope formula), then compare: equal for parallel, product −1-1−1 for perpendicular, otherwise neither.
  • To write a parallel or perpendicular line through a point, take the required slope (same slope, or the negative reciprocal), then substitute that slope and the point into point-slope form and simplify. For a vertical or horizontal given line, write x=cx = cx=c or y=cy = cy=c directly instead.

A bit of history (optional)

A stone wall that leans will eventually fall. So how did a builder with no instruments find true vertical?

The answer was a string and a weight. Hang a lump of lead on a cord, let it stop swinging, and gravity does the whole job for you. The taut string points straight down, and it is square to level ground. The Romans called that tool a perpendiculum, from pendere, to hang. Masons had been trusting it for thousands of years before anyone wrote a proof about it.

Our word for a right angle is that tool’s name. Something perpendicular is something that agrees with the plumb line. So the idea of one line standing square to another was named after a cord with a weight tied to the bottom of it. Geometry borrowed the word from the building site.

“Parallel” is Greek and older still. It comes from parallelos, built from para, beside, and allelos, each other. Lines that travel beside one another and never touch.

Both words describe a picture. Neither of them mentions a number, and that is worth noticing, because this lesson has mostly left the picture behind. For any two distinct lines that both have a slope, you can now settle both questions without a drawing: equal slopes for parallel, a product of −1-1−1 for perpendicular. Only a vertical line still falls back on the picture the two words came from, whether it is paired with another vertical line, a horizontal line, or any other slanted line.

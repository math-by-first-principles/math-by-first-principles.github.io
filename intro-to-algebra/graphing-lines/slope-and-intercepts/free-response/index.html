    1. Two numbers, each with its own sign . Foundational, 9 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 9 pts

    Slope-intercept form puts a line's tilt and its crossing of the vertical axis on the face of the equation. Reading them back is a matching exercise, and only two things go wrong: a sign gets dropped, or the equation is read before its terms are in the order the form expects.

    1. A
      Part A.

      State the slope and give the yyy-intercept as a point for each of these three lines: y=6x−7y = 6x - 7y=6x−7, y=9−4xy = 9 - 4xy=9−4x, and y=−34xy = -\tfrac{3}{4}xy=−43​x.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      The line below is drawn on a grid of unit squares. Write its equation in slope-intercept form.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    Part B

    y=2x−3y = 2x - 3y=2x−3.

    Part C

    Every point of the yyy-axis has x=0x = 0x=0, and putting x=0x = 0x=0 into the form leaves y=by = by=b, so the crossing is (0,b)(0, b)(0,b). Writing the two terms in the other order is the same sum added the other way round, which changes nothing about which number is attached to xxx.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Match each equation against y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b, where mmm is whatever multiplies xxx and bbb is the constant.

    In the first, the constant is −7-7−7, not 777, so the crossing sits below the origin:

    y=6x+(−7),m=6,b=−7.y = 6x + (-7), \qquad m = 6, \qquad b = -7.y=6x+(−7),m=6,b=−7.

    The second lists its terms in the other order, so reorder it before reading anything:

    y=9−4x=−4x+9,m=−4,b=9.y = 9 - 4x = -4x + 9, \qquad m = -4, \qquad b = 9.y=9−4x=−4x+9,m=−4,b=9.

    The number written first is the constant here, and the slope is the number attached to xxx, minus sign included. Reading left to right instead would have taken the tilt from a number that is not touching xxx at all.

    The third has no constant term written, which means the constant is 000:

    y=−34x+0,m=−34,b=0,y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 0, \qquad m = -\tfrac{3}{4}, \qquad b = 0,y=−43​x+0,m=−43​,b=0,

    so that line crosses at the origin. In all three cases the yyy-intercept is the point (0,b)(0, b)(0,b), not the bare number bbb.

    Part B

    Take the two numbers the form needs in the order the picture hands them over.

    First the constant, which costs no work at all: the line crosses the vertical axis three units below the origin, so b=−3b = -3b=−3.

    Then the slope. Move along the line from that crossing to the next crossing of two gridlines it passes through, which is one unit right and two units up. A run of 111 with a rise of 222 gives

    m=21=2.m = \frac{2}{1} = 2.m=12​=2.

    Put both numbers into y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b:

    y=2x−3.y = 2x - 3.y=2x−3.

    Check it against a point the drawing agrees on. At x=2x = 2x=2 the equation gives y=2(2)−3=1y = 2(2) - 3 = 1y=2(2)−3=1, and the line does pass through the crossing two units right of the origin and one unit above it.

    Part C

    Two separate questions, and one line of the form settles each.

    Why the constant is the crossing. The yyy-axis is the collection of points whose first coordinate is 000, so the line meets it at whatever yyy comes out when xxx is 000. Put that into the form:

    y=m(0)+b=b.y = m(0) + b = b.y=m(0)+b=b.

    The mxmxmx term is wiped out whatever mmm happens to be, which is why the crossing depends on bbb alone, and why the crossing as a point is (0,b)(0, b)(0,b).

    Why the order does not matter. The expressions 9−4x9 - 4x9−4x and −4x+9-4x + 9−4x+9 are one sum written in its two possible orders, and addition gives the same result either way:

    9+(−4x)=−4x+9.9 + (-4x) = -4x + 9.9+(−4x)=−4x+9.

    So the two equations describe the same line, and the slope is whichever number is attached to xxx in either of them. What the reordering changes is only which number your eye reaches first, and that is exactly why it is worth rewriting an equation into the form's order before reading it.

    Foundational 11 pts

    An equation does not have to arrive in slope-intercept form, and until it is in that form the numbers on the page are not the slope and the intercept, however much they look the part.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Rewrite 3x+4y=243x + 4y = 243x+4y=24 in slope-intercept form, then state its slope and give its yyy-intercept as a point.

    2. B
      Part B.

      Carry your own answer forward Graph the line from whichever slope and intercept your part A produced. What is credited here is starting at the crossing of the vertical axis and stepping by the slope, not the pair of numbers turning out to be the expected one.

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why neither the number multiplying xxx in 3x+4y=243x + 4y = 243x+4y=24 nor the number on the right of it can be trusted as the slope or the intercept, naming the step in your own work that changed each one.

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Slope-intercept form is not a pattern you spot inside an equation; it is a shape you solve towards. Ask what would have to be true of one side before any number in the equation could be called the slope.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

      Once the xxx-term is out of the way, the yyy is still carrying a coefficient. Whatever you divide by has to reach every term on both sides, the constant included, or the equation is only half solved.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Line the two numbers you began with up against the two you ended with, and find the operations standing between each pair. A number that has been operated on twice was never the thing it looked like.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    y=−34x+6y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 6y=−43​x+6, with slope −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ and yyy-intercept (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6).

    Part B

    Plot the crossing (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) first. Then step 444 units right and 333 units down, the run and the rise the slope names, to reach (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3). The straight line through those two points is the graph.

    Part C

    Neither number survives the solving. Clearing the xxx-term carries it across, which reverses its sign, and dividing by the coefficient of yyy then rescales it and the constant alike. Only once yyy stands alone does the equation say y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b, so only then do its numbers mean mmm and bbb.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Slope-intercept form wants yyy alone on one side with nothing multiplying it, so solve the equation for yyy.

    Move the xxx-term across first, by subtracting 3x3x3x from both sides:

    4y=−3x+24.4y = -3x + 24.4y=−3x+24.

    The yyy still carries a coefficient of 444, so divide by 444. Every term means both of them, not just the one that moved:

    y=−34x+6.y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 6.y=−43​x+6.

    Now the reading is immediate: m=−34m = -\tfrac{3}{4}m=−43​ and b=6b = 6b=6, so the yyy-intercept is the point (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6).

    A cheap check goes back to the original equation. At x=0x = 0x=0 it says 4y=244y = 244y=24, so y=6y = 6y=6, which is the crossing just found.

    Part B

    The form hands over exactly what drawing a line needs: one point to start from, and a direction to leave it in.

    The intercept is the free point, because it takes no calculation. Plot (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) on the vertical axis.

    For the direction, read the slope as a rise over a run:

    m=−34=−34.m = -\tfrac{3}{4} = \frac{-3}{4}.m=−43​=4−3​.

    A run of 444 to the right comes with a rise of −3-3−3, which is three units down. Stepping from the crossing:

    (0,6)  ⟶  (0+4, 6−3)=(4,3).(0, 6) \;\longrightarrow\; (0 + 4,\ 6 - 3) = (4, 3).(0,6)⟶(0+4, 6−3)=(4,3).

    Plot (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3), lay a ruler across the two points and draw. The negative slope is what makes the line fall as it travels right. Stepping once more, to (8,0)(8, 0)(8,0), is a cheap check that the first two points were placed correctly.

    Part C

    Slope-intercept form is a claim about the SHAPE of an equation, not about the numbers that appear in it, and an equation not yet in that shape is not making the claim.

    Track the coefficient of xxx through the work. It starts on the left of

    3x+4y=24,3x + 4y = 24,3x+4y=24,

    is carried across, which reverses its sign, and is then divided by 444 along with everything else. Two separate steps acted on it, so the number that finally multiplies xxx is neither the one written at the start nor its opposite.

    The constant travels a shorter road and is changed all the same: the first step leaves it alone, and the second divides it by 444. So the number on the right of the original equation is not the height of the crossing either.

    The general point outlives the two numbers. Reading mmm and bbb is legitimate only once yyy stands alone with a coefficient of 111, because that is the only arrangement the form describes. Stopping one step early, at

    4y=−3x+24,4y = -3x + 24,4y=−3x+24,

    leaves an equation whose numbers are still halfway through the journey.

    In one line

    3x+4y=243x + 4y = 243x+4y=24 becomes y=−34x+6y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 6y=−43​x+6, so the slope is −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ and the yyy-intercept is (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6); the line is graphed by plotting (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) and stepping four right and three down to (4,3)(4, 3)(4,3); and the original equation's numbers are not mmm and bbb because carrying the xxx-term across reverses a sign and dividing by the coefficient of yyy rescales what is left.

    Another way: Divide first, move second

    The two steps can be taken in the other order, and the division is easier to get right when it comes first. Divide every term of 3x+4y=243x + 4y = 243x+4y=24 by 444 straight away:

    34x+y=6.\tfrac{3}{4}x + y = 6.43​x+y=6.

    The yyy now has a coefficient of 111 already, so one subtraction finishes the job:

    y=−34x+6.y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 6.y=−43​x+6.

    When it is worth it When the coefficient of yyy does not divide the other numbers evenly. Doing the division while both terms are still side by side makes it harder to divide one of them and forget the other, which is the usual way this goes wrong.

    3. Two rules that are mirror images, and a claim that is not true . Reasoning, 12 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Reasoning 12 pts

    The two intercepts are found by rules that look so alike they get swapped for each other constantly. This question separates them, and then puts a claim about every line to the test.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find both intercepts of the line y=23x−4y = \tfrac{2}{3}x - 4y=32​x−4. Give each as a point, and show the equation you solved to get each one.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 5 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Here is a claim: for every line, the xxx-intercept and the yyy-intercept are two different points. Refute it with one specific line. Give that line's equation, work out both of its intercepts, and name the feature of the line that makes the claim fail.

      Construct a counterexample Give one specific case, and show it breaks the claim. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why setting x=0x = 0x=0 locates the crossing of the yyy-axis and setting y=0y = 0y=0 locates the crossing of the xxx-axis, and not the other way round. Aim at an explanation someone could rebuild from scratch rather than a rule to be memorised.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every part here turns on one small fact about the coordinate plane: a point sitting on an axis has a zero in one of its two coordinates, and deciding which coordinate that is settles both rules at once.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      A claim about every line is finished off by a single line that breaks it, so you are looking for one equation, not an argument. Ask whether a line can meet both axes in the same place, and if so, which place that would have to be.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Write down three or four points that sit on the vertical axis and look at what they all have in common. Then do the same for the horizontal axis. Each rule is that observation and nothing more.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The yyy-intercept is (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4) and the xxx-intercept is (6,0)(6, 0)(6,0).

    Part B

    The claim is false. Take y=3xy = 3xy=3x. Setting x=0x = 0x=0 gives (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), and setting y=0y = 0y=0 gives (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) as well, so the two intercepts are one and the same point. Any line through the origin does this.

    Part C

    Each rule simply names an axis. Every point of the yyy-axis has first coordinate 000, so demanding x=0x = 0x=0 is demanding to be on that axis, and the equation then supplies the matching height. The xxx-axis is the points whose second coordinate is 000, so the other rule is the same move with the roles swapped.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Each intercept comes from zeroing one coordinate, and which coordinate to zero is decided by which axis the line is crossing.

    The yyy-axis is where x=0x = 0x=0, so put x=0x = 0x=0 into the equation:

    y=23(0)−4=−4,y = \tfrac{2}{3}(0) - 4 = -4,y=32​(0)−4=−4,

    which gives the point (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4). That is just the constant of the equation, exactly as slope-intercept form promises.

    The xxx-axis is where y=0y = 0y=0, so set y=0y = 0y=0 and solve what is left:

    0=23x−4,23x=4,x=4⋅32=6,0 = \tfrac{2}{3}x - 4, \qquad \tfrac{2}{3}x = 4, \qquad x = 4 \cdot \tfrac{3}{2} = 6,0=32​x−4,32​x=4,x=4⋅23​=6,

    which gives the point (6,0)(6, 0)(6,0). Multiplying by the reciprocal is what clears the fraction; dividing by 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ is the same move.

    Neither answer is a bare number. An intercept is a place, so it is reported as a point, and each point carries its zero in the coordinate belonging to the axis being crossed.

    Part B

    A claim about EVERY line is destroyed by a single line that breaks it, so the job is to produce one, not to argue in general.

    Take y=3xy = 3xy=3x. Its yyy-intercept comes from x=0x = 0x=0:

    y=3(0)=0,y = 3(0) = 0,y=3(0)=0,

    so that point is (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0). Its xxx-intercept comes from y=0y = 0y=0:

    0=3x,x=0,0 = 3x, \qquad x = 0,0=3x,x=0,

    so that point is (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) too. One point, where the claim promised two, so the claim is false.

    What makes it fail is the line passing through the origin. The origin is the one point lying on both axes, so a line through it crosses both axes in the very same place. This is a whole family of counterexamples rather than a freak case: y=3xy = 3xy=3x, y=−xy = -xy=−x and y=15xy = \tfrac{1}{5}xy=51​x all do it.

    The repaired claim is worth stating carefully, and it has to be checked in both directions. For a line with a nonzero slope, the two intercepts are different points exactly when the line does not pass through the origin. Forwards: if the line does pass through the origin then b=0b = 0b=0, and both rules land on (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), as above. Backwards: if the two intercepts are the same point, that point lies on both axes at once, and the origin is the only such point, so the line passes through the origin. Both directions hold, so this version is safe. The excluded cases are a slope of 000, and a vertical line, which has no slope to speak of. Take the flat lines first: the line y=0y = 0y=0 meets the xxx-axis at every one of its points rather than at one, and y=by = by=b with b≠0b \neq 0b=0 never meets it at all, so neither has a single xxx-intercept to compare. The vertical lines are excluded by the same wording, since asking for a nonzero slope is already asking the line to have a slope, and x=ax = ax=a has none.

    Part C

    Neither rule is about intercepts to begin with. Each one describes an axis, and the equation is only asked to supply the missing coordinate.

    The yyy-axis is exactly the collection of points whose first coordinate is zero: (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4), (0,7)(0, 7)(0,7) and (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) all sit on it, and (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0) does not. So to land on the yyy-axis you must set the first coordinate to zero, which is the demand x=0x = 0x=0, and the equation then tells you the only height that goes with it:

    y=m(0)+b=b.y = m(0) + b = b.y=m(0)+b=b.

    The xxx-axis is the mirror image, the points whose second coordinate is zero. To land on it you set y=0y = 0y=0, and the equation is left to be solved for xxx.

    So the letter you set to zero is the letter that does NOT name the axis you are crossing, which sounds backwards until you see why: you zero the coordinate that the axis holds fixed.

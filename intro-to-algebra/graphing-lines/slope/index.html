Slope

Intuition

Picture two roads climbing a hill. Both go up, but one is a gentle grade you could bike and the other is nearly a wall. To tell them apart you need a number, not just the word “uphill.” Lines on a graph are the same. Some climb steeply, some drift up gently, some fall, and some stay perfectly flat. In the last lesson you saw that a line has a constant equal step. Move one unit to the right and the height changes by the same fixed amount every time. That fixed amount is exactly the number we want. This lesson names it the slope, and turns “how steep, and which way” into a single value you can compute from any two points on a nonvertical line.

Learning goals

  • Measure slope as rise over run
  • Compute m=y2−y1x2−x1m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​ in a consistent order
  • Explain why every pair of points on a nonvertical line agrees
  • Classify a slope as positive, negative, zero, or undefined, and say why a vertical line has none
  • Read a slope from a graph, and use it to find another point on the line

Rise over run

To measure how a line tilts, follow it from one point to another. Then split the trip into two moves: how far across, and how far up or down.

The run is the horizontal change, how far you move left or right. The rise is the vertical change, how far you move up or down over that same trip. The tilt of the line is captured by comparing the two. A big rise for a small run is steep; a small rise for a big run is gentle. We compare them as a ratio, rise divided by run, and give that ratio a name.

The slope of a line is its rise divided by its run, measured between any two different points on it (except a straight up-and-down line, which we will come back to):

slope=riserun=change in ychange in x.\text{slope} = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}.slope=runrise​=change in xchange in y​.

The slope is written with the letter mmm. The word “rise” stays honest even when a line goes downhill: a downhill move counts as a negative rise, so the slope simply comes out negative. The picture below shows the run and the rise as the two legs of a right triangle drawn under a line.

For the line drawn above, going from the lower point to the upper one you move 333 to the right and 222 up, so its slope is 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​. This is the same equal step from the last lesson, now measured. There the height changed by a fixed amount each time xxx rose by 111. The slope is that fixed step, the amount yyy rises for a run of exactly one unit. A line that is not vertical has just one slope, and once you know it you know the tilt exactly. A vertical line is the one exception, and you will see why later in this lesson.

The slope formula

Reading rise and run off a picture is fine when a graph is in front of you. But usually you are handed two points as numbers and want the slope without drawing anything. The rise and run are just differences of coordinates, so the slope becomes a short formula.

Take two points (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) and (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​) on the line. Moving from the first to the second, the vertical change (the rise) is y2−y1y_2 - y_1y2​−y1​, and the horizontal change (the run) is x2−x1x_2 - x_1x2​−x1​. Dividing gives the slope:

m=y2−y1x2−x1.m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}.m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​.

The subscripts are just labels that keep the two points apart: (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) is “point one” and (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​) is “point two.” The one rule to respect is order. Subtract the coordinates in the same order on the top and the bottom. If y2−y1y_2 - y_1y2​−y1​ sits on top, then x2−x1x_2 - x_1x2​−x1​ must sit on the bottom, never x1−x2x_1 - x_2x1​−x2​.

Worked example 1 Find the slope through (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) and (6,11)(6, 11)(6,11)

Label the points (x1,y1)=(2,3)(x_1, y_1) = (2, 3)(x1​,y1​)=(2,3) and (x2,y2)=(6,11)(x_2, y_2) = (6, 11)(x2​,y2​)=(6,11), then substitute into the formula, keeping the same order on top and bottom.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=11−36−2=84=2.m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{11 - 3}{6 - 2} = \frac{8}{4} = 2.m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​=6−211−3​=48​=2.

The slope is 222. The line rises 222 units for every 111 unit it runs, so it climbs fairly steeply from left to right.

Worked example 2 Find the slope through (−1,5)(-1, 5)(−1,5) and (2,−1)(2, -1)(2,−1)

Take (x1,y1)=(−1,5)(x_1, y_1) = (-1, 5)(x1​,y1​)=(−1,5) and (x2,y2)=(2,−1)(x_2, y_2) = (2, -1)(x2​,y2​)=(2,−1). Substitute carefully, watching the signs of the negative coordinates.

m=−1−52−(−1)=−63=−2.m = \frac{-1 - 5}{2 - (-1)} = \frac{-6}{3} = -2.m=2−(−1)−1−5​=3−6​=−2.

The slope is −2-2−2. It is negative, so the line falls as you move to the right. To see that the order does not matter, swap the labels and put (2,−1)(2, -1)(2,−1) first:

m=5−(−1)−1−2=6−3=−2,m = \frac{5 - (-1)}{-1 - 2} = \frac{6}{-3} = -2,m=−1−25−(−1)​=−36​=−2,

the same answer.

It never matters which point you call first. If you swap them, both the top and the bottom change sign, and a fraction with both signs flipped keeps its value, since −a−b=ab\frac{-a}{-b} = \frac{a}{b}−b−a​=ba​. So pick either point as “point one” and simply stay consistent all the way through.

What is the slope of the line through (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2) and (4,8)(4, 8)(4,8)?

Answer choices

Substitute the points into the slope formula, keeping the same order on top and bottom.

m=8−24−1=63=2.m = \frac{8 - 2}{4 - 1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2.m=4−18−2​=36​=2.

The slope is 222. Dividing run by rise instead would give the wrong value 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, and 666 is only the rise, not the ratio.

Why every pair of points gives the same slope

The formula uses two points, but a line has infinitely many. If you and a friend each pick a different pair of points on the same line, do you get the same slope? You must, or “the slope of the line” would be a meaningless phrase. Here is why the answer is always yes, for any line that is not vertical.

Why the slope does not depend on which two points you choose#

A horizontal line settles the question immediately: every point on it has the same height, so the rise between any two of its points is always 000, and the slope is always 000.

For every other nonvertical line, picture two different pairs of points, each pair forming a right triangle whose horizontal leg is the run and whose vertical leg is the rise. Picking a different pair of points draws a larger or smaller triangle, but every point sits on the same straight line, so all of these triangles are the same shape, scaled copies of one another. A larger triangle has a proportionally larger rise and run, so the ratio of rise to run does not change. The figure below shows exactly this: two triangles on one line, one twice the size of the other, both reporting the same slope.

Here is the same reason in symbols. The line is straight because it has a constant rate: the rise is always the same fixed multiple of the run. Doubling the run doubles the rise, and in general, for one fixed number kkk that belongs to this particular line,

rise=k×run.\text{rise} = k \times \text{run}.rise=k×run.

That single number kkk is the rise you get from a run of 111, and the straightness of the line is the promise that kkk never changes as you move along it.

Feed two different points (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) and (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​) into the slope formula. As long as the line is not vertical, two different points on it always have different xxx-coordinates, so x2−x1x_2 - x_1x2​−x1​ is never 000 and the division below is safe. Their run is x2−x1x_2 - x_1x2​−x1​ and their rise is y2−y1y_2 - y_1y2​−y1​, and by the fact above,

y2−y1=k(x2−x1).y_2 - y_1 = k(x_2 - x_1).y2​−y1​=k(x2​−x1​).

So the slope the formula reports is

m=y2−y1x2−x1=k(x2−x1)x2−x1=k.m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{k(x_2 - x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = k.m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​=x2​−x1​k(x2​−x1​)​=k.

The two points canceled out completely. Whatever pair you chose, the formula reports the same number kkk, so every nonvertical line really does have just one slope, and the formula finds it every time.

∎

The proof says the two triangles must give the same slope, and the picture above shows one case of it. The figure below lets you test the claim yourself, because the rise and the run are separate controls. You set each one, and the line and its staircase are redrawn to match.

Start at a rise of 222 over a run of 333 and read the line. Now set the rise to 444 and the run to 666. The staircase doubles in size, but the line does not move at all: 4/64/64/6 reduces to 2/32/32/3, the same slope. Same line, same slope, a bigger triangle, exactly what the reasoning above predicts.

Then take the rise down through 111, 000 and −1-1−1 with the run held at 333. At a rise of 000 the line is flat and the staircase’s upright leg vanishes. That is what a slope of 000 looks like: run as far as you like and the height never changes. Below that the line falls to the right, and the readout swaps “up” for “down” without the arithmetic changing at all.

Check your understanding

Two points on a line give a slope triangle with rise 555 and run 333. Which rise-and-run pair could come from a different pair of points on that same line?

Answer choices

Every pair of points on the same nonvertical line gives the same slope, so the ratio of rise to run must always reduce to 53\tfrac{5}{3}35​.

Only rise 101010 and run 666 match: 106=53\tfrac{10}{6} = \tfrac{5}{3}610​=35​. Rise 555 and run 555 gives slope 111, rise 333 and run 555 gives 35\tfrac{3}{5}53​ (the reciprocal), and rise 101010 and run 333 gives a different ratio entirely.

Positive, negative, zero, and undefined slope

The sign of the slope tells you the line’s direction, and two special cases push the formula to its edges.

A positive slope means the rise and run have the same sign: as you move to the right, with xxx increasing, yyy increases too. So the line climbs from lower left to upper right. A negative slope means the rise and run have opposite signs: moving right, yyy decreases, so the line falls from upper left to lower right. The larger the slope in size, the steeper the climb or fall. A slope of 333 rises three units per step across, much steeper than a slope of 14\tfrac{1}{4}41​.

A zero slope is the flat case. A horizontal line never changes height, so between any two of its points the rise is 000 while the run is some nonzero number, and

m=0run=0.m = \frac{0}{\text{run}} = 0.m=run0​=0.

This matches the horizontal lines y=cy = cy=c from the last lesson: their height is locked, so they rise by nothing, and their slope is 000.

An undefined slope is the vertical case, and it is the one place the formula refuses to answer. A vertical line never changes its xxx-coordinate, so between any two of its points the run is 000:

m=rise0.m = \frac{\text{rise}}{0}.m=0rise​.

Dividing by zero has no meaning, since no number multiplied by 000 can give a nonzero rise. So a vertical line has no slope value at all, and we say its slope is undefined. It is tempting to call it “infinite,” but infinity is not a number and the division simply has no result. Notice the sharp contrast with a horizontal line: a horizontal line has slope exactly 000, a perfectly good number, while a vertical line has no slope at all. Flat and upright are opposites here, not near-misses.

Worked example 3 Slopes of a horizontal and a vertical line

First find the slope through (−2,4)(-2, 4)(−2,4) and (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4). Both points have the same height, y=4y = 4y=4, so the rise is zero:

m=4−43−(−2)=05=0.m = \frac{4 - 4}{3 - (-2)} = \frac{0}{5} = 0.m=3−(−2)4−4​=50​=0.

The slope is 000: these points sit on the horizontal line y=4y = 4y=4.

Now find the slope through (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1) and (5,7)(5, 7)(5,7). Both points have the same xxx-coordinate, x=5x = 5x=5, so the run is zero:

m=7−(−1)5−5=80.m = \frac{7 - (-1)}{5 - 5} = \frac{8}{0}.m=5−57−(−1)​=08​.

Division by zero has no value, so this slope is undefined. These points sit on the vertical line x=5x = 5x=5.

m=9−24−4=70, which is undefined.m = \frac{9 - 2}{4 - 4} = \frac{7}{0}, \text{ which is undefined.}m=4−49−2​=07​, which is undefined.

The points (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2) and (4,9)(4, 9)(4,9) both have x=4x = 4x=4. The other pairs are all defined: (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1) and (7,1)(7, 1)(7,1) share a yyy-coordinate and give slope 000; (−1,−3)(-1, -3)(−1,−3) and (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) give a positive slope of 222; and (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) and (−2,9)(-2, 9)(−2,9) give a negative slope of −1-1−1.

Reading slope from a graph

When a line is already drawn for you, you can read its slope straight off the grid without any coordinates, by building a slope triangle. Pick two points where the line crosses grid corners, then count.

Count the run first: how many units you move right to get from the left point across to under the right point. Then count the rise: how many units up or down you move to reach the line, counting down as negative. The slope is the rise you counted over the run you counted. Reading from left to right keeps the run positive, so the sign comes entirely from the rise. Count upward and the slope is positive; count downward and it is negative.

Worked example 4 Read the slope of the graphed line

The diagram above already counted a run of 222 and a rise of −1-1−1 from (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) to (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0), giving a slope of −12-\tfrac{1}{2}−21​. As a check, plug the same two points into the slope formula instead of counting:

m=0−12−0=−12.m = \frac{0 - 1}{2 - 0} = \frac{-1}{2}.m=2−00−1​=2−1​.

The two methods agree, so counting a slope triangle and using the formula are really the same calculation seen two different ways.

Answer choices

From (−2,1)(-2, 1)(−2,1) to (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0): the run is 444 units to the right, and the rise is −1-1−1 (down 111, since the height drops from 111 to 000). The slope is the rise over the run: −14=−14\dfrac{-1}{4} = -\tfrac{1}{4}4−1​=−41​.

Flipping rise and run gives −4-4−4. Flipping and also dropping the negative sign gives 41\tfrac{4}{1}14​. Dropping only the negative sign gives 14\tfrac{1}{4}41​, which would describe a line climbing to the right instead of falling.

Stepping along a line from its slope

Slope also runs in reverse. If you know a line’s slope and one point on it, you can walk to as many other points as you like, with no equation at all. Read the slope as rise over run, then step. From the known point, move right by the run and up or down by the rise, and you land on another point of the line.

For a slope of 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ starting at (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2), step 444 to the right and 333 up to reach (5,5)(5, 5)(5,5), which is also on the line. You can step the other way too: 444 left and 333 down reaches (−3,−1)(-3, -1)(−3,−1). A whole number slope like 222 is really 21\tfrac{2}{1}12​, a run of 111 and a rise of 222. So from (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) you step to (1,3)(1, 3)(1,3), then (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5), and onward.

Worked example 5 Step to another point using the slope

A line has slope −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ and passes through (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4). Read the slope as a run of 333 and a rise of −2-2−2 (down 222). Stepping right from (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4):

(3,4)  ⟶  (3+3,  4−2)=(6,2).(3, 4) \;\longrightarrow\; (3 + 3,\; 4 - 2) = (6, 2).(3,4)⟶(3+3,4−2)=(6,2).

So (6,2)(6, 2)(6,2) is on the line. Stepping the opposite way, left 333 and up 222:

(3,4)  ⟶  (3−3,  4+2)=(0,6).(3, 4) \;\longrightarrow\; (3 - 3,\; 4 + 2) = (0, 6).(3,4)⟶(3−3,4+2)=(0,6).

So (0,6)(0, 6)(0,6) is on the line as well. This stays entirely inside this lesson: you are locating points on the line from its slope, not writing down the line’s equation, which is the next lesson’s task.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Putting the run on top. Slope is rise over run, vertical change over horizontal change, not the other way around. Flipping them gives the reciprocal of the correct slope, not the slope itself, and that reciprocal is usually a different number.
  • Subtracting in a different order on top and bottom. Use y2−y1x2−x1\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​, not y2−y1x1−x2\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_1 - x_2}x1​−x2​y2​−y1​​. Mixing the orders flips the sign of your answer.
  • Mixing up which coordinate is which. The rise uses only the yyy-values and the run uses only the xxx-values. Subtracting a yyy from an xxx scrambles the ratio.
  • Calling a vertical line’s slope “infinite” or “zero.” A vertical line’s slope is undefined because the run is 000 and you cannot divide by zero. A horizontal line’s slope is 000. Do not swap these two.
  • Forgetting the sign of the rise when the line falls. A downhill move is a negative rise, so the slope is negative. When you count off a graph, count downward as negative.
  • Reading “steeper” as “bigger number” for negative slopes. Compare steepness with the size of the number, its distance from 000: a slope of −4-4−4 is steeper than a slope of 111, even though −4-4−4 is the smaller number.

Key takeaways

  • The slope mmm of a line measures its steepness and direction as rise over run, the change in yyy divided by the change in xxx.
  • Slope formula: for points (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) and (x2,y2)(x_2, y_2)(x2​,y2​),   m=y2−y1x2−x1\; m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​. Subtract in the same order on top and bottom.
  • Every pair of points on a nonvertical line gives the same slope, because the rise is always the same fixed multiple of the run.
  • Positive slope rises left to right; negative falls. A horizontal line has slope 000 (rise 000); a vertical line has undefined slope (run 000, division by zero), never “infinite.”
  • Read slope from a graph with a slope triangle: count the run to the right, then the rise (down is negative).
  • A slope farther from 000 means a steeper line, no matter its sign.

A bit of history (optional)

Nearly every English-language algebra book writes mmm for slope, but nobody is fully sure why.

The popular explanation is that mmm stands for monter, a French verb meaning to climb. It is a tidy story, and it appears to be false: French mathematicians of the period, including Descartes, did not use mmm this way, and nobody has found a text that makes the connection. The earliest known use of mmm for slope is not English at all. It shows up in 1757, in a work by the Italian mathematician Vincenzo Riccati. English textbooks picked it up much later, in the 1840s, and it slowly became the standard choice, but no one has found a reason the letter itself was chosen. The best available guess is a plain one: mmm was simply free to use, and once a few popular textbooks adopted it, later authors followed along.

The idea underneath the letter is much older and much better travelled. Builders and surveyors were measuring steepness as a ratio of climb to distance long before anyone drew a coordinate grid, a practice that survives today on road signs warning of a one in ten hill. Push the same ratio between two points that slide closer and closer together, and it grows into calculus, which uses that limit to measure the steepness of a curve at a single point.

So the slope triangle you counted off the grid in this lesson is a small piece of a very long road. The symbol is an accident. The ratio is not.

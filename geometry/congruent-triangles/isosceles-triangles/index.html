Isosceles and Equilateral Triangles

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Cut a triangular flag with two matching edges. A fold through their common corner seems to bring one edge onto the other. Can that fold explain why two corners match, and where it reaches the third edge? Splitting the flag into two congruent triangles lets us answer those questions from the given lengths.

Learning goals

  • Identify the base and opposite angles from a triangle's marked equal sides
  • Prove the base-angle theorem and its converse using triangle congruence
  • Calculate missing lengths and angles in isosceles and equilateral triangles
  • Explain why the vertex bisector, median, and altitude coincide in an isosceles triangle

Find the base from the equal sides

An isosceles triangle has at least two congruent sides. Choose a pair of equal sides: their common endpoint is the vertex for this choice, their included angle is the vertex angle, and the opposite side is the base. The two angles at the base’s endpoints are the base angles. These roles follow the marked equal sides, even when the triangle is tilted.

For example, suppose the side lengths in centimeters are

PQ=11.PR=11.QR=14.\begin{aligned} PQ&=11. \\ PR&=11. \\ QR&=14. \end{aligned}PQPRQR​=11.=11.=14.​

The chosen equal sides meet at PPP. The base is QRQRQR, and the base angles are at QQQ and RRR. Each base angle is opposite one of the equal sides.

Throughout this lesson, triangles have three noncollinear vertices. An equilateral triangle qualifies as isosceles too; any pair of its sides can be the chosen equal pair.

Check your understanding

Triangle KLM has the following equal sides:

KL=LM.\begin{aligned} KL&=LM. \end{aligned}KL​=LM.​

Which is its base, and which pair of angles will this lesson connect?

Answer choices

The equal sides meet at L, so L is the vertex opposite base KM. The base angles are at K and M, opposite LM and KL respectively.

Why the base angles match

Equal sides force equal angles opposite them. Take a second flag with equal edges of 999 centimeters and a vertex angle of 74∘74^\circ74∘; its base length is not specified. Draw the internal angle bisector from its vertex to the base. Each part at the vertex is 37∘37^\circ37∘.

The two smaller triangles have a side of length 999, the same included 37∘37^\circ37∘ angle, and the shared bisector segment. Side-angle-side (SAS) therefore makes those triangles congruent. Their base angles match. Since together those angles use what remains of 180∘180^\circ180∘, each is

180∘−74∘2=53∘.\frac{180^\circ-74^\circ}{2}=53^\circ.2180∘−74∘​=53∘.

The length 999 and angle 74∘74^\circ74∘ did not make the congruence work: what mattered was equal sides and a bisected included angle. Here is the argument for any isosceles triangle.

The isosceles base-angle theorem#

In △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC, let

AB=AC.\begin{aligned} AB&=AC. \end{aligned}AB​=AC.​

Draw the internal bisector of ∠BAC\angle BAC∠BAC, meeting BCBCBC at DDD.

Compare △ABD\triangle ABD△ABD with △ACD\triangle ACD△ACD. The given, shared side, and bisector’s definition supply these facts respectively:

AB=AC.AD=AD.∠BAD≅∠DAC.\begin{aligned} AB&=AC. \\ AD&=AD. \\ \angle BAD&\cong\angle DAC. \end{aligned}ABAD∠BAD​=AC.=AD.≅∠DAC.​

The equal angle lies between the two named sides in each triangle, so SAS gives

△ABD≅△ACD.\begin{aligned} \triangle ABD&\cong\triangle ACD. \end{aligned}△ABD​≅△ACD.​

The corresponding angles ∠ABD\angle ABD∠ABD and ∠ACD\angle ACD∠ACD are congruent. Because DDD is inside BCBCBC, these are the original base angles ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC and ∠ACB\angle ACB∠ACB.

∎

The base-angle theorem says that if two sides of a triangle are congruent, the angles opposite those sides are congruent. Triangle congruence is justified by the rigid-motion results from the preceding lessons; the angle equality is a conclusion of that congruence, not a fact inferred from the picture.

In triangle ABC, AD is the internal bisector of angle BAC, with D on BC. The given equal sides are

Which evidence proves triangles ABD and ACD congruent before any base-angle equality is used?

Answer choices

Worked example 1 Locate the repeated angle before calculating

Problem. In △FGH\triangle FGH△FGH, the givens are

FG=GH.m∠F=68∘.\begin{aligned} FG&=GH. \\ m\angle F&=68^\circ. \end{aligned}FGm∠F​=GH.=68∘.​

Find m∠Hm\angle Hm∠H and m∠Gm\angle Gm∠G.

Solution. The equal sides meet at GGG. Their opposite angles, at HHH and FFF, are the matching base angles, so

m∠H=68∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle H&=68^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠H​=68∘.​

Subtract both base angles from the triangle total:

m∠G=180∘−68∘−68∘=44∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle G&=180^\circ-68^\circ-68^\circ \\ &=44^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠G​=180∘−68∘−68∘=44∘.​

A triangle has equal sides UV and UW, and its vertex angle at U measures 38 degrees. What is the measure of each base angle?

Answer choices

(180∘−38∘)/2=71∘.\begin{aligned} (180^\circ-38^\circ)/2&=71^\circ. \end{aligned}(180∘−38∘)/2​=71∘.​

Reverse the reasoning

Equal angles also force equal opposite sides. For example, suppose in △JKL\triangle JKL△JKL that

m∠J=47∘.m∠K=47∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle J&=47^\circ. \\ m\angle K&=47^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠Jm∠K​=47∘.=47∘.​

The opposite sides KLKLKL and JLJLJL must match. This is the converse of the base-angle theorem. The theorem alone does not prove it; a reversed claim needs its own argument.

Imagine two copies of this triangle with JJJ and KKK exchanging roles. The base JKJKJK has the same length in either direction. Its two end angles are 47∘47^\circ47∘ and 47∘47^\circ47∘ in both copies. Angle-side-angle (ASA) fixes the triangle, so the sides leading to LLL must match under that exchange.

The converse of the base-angle theorem#

In △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC, suppose

∠ABC≅∠ACB.\begin{aligned} \angle ABC&\cong\angle ACB. \end{aligned}∠ABC​≅∠ACB.​

Compare the triangle named BCABCABCA with the same triangle named CBACBACBA.

The angle at BBB in the first name matches the equal angle at CCC in the second. The angle at CCC matches the equal angle at BBB. Between those angles, the shared side gives

BC=CB.\begin{aligned} BC&=CB. \end{aligned}BC​=CB.​

By ASA,

△BCA≅△CBA.\begin{aligned} \triangle BCA&\cong\triangle CBA. \end{aligned}△BCA​≅△CBA.​

The correspondence exchanges BBB and CCC and keeps AAA fixed. The corresponding sides BABABA and CACACA therefore have equal lengths.

∎

The proof uses one triangle twice with a different correspondence, not a new assumption about its appearance. We can now say both directions: in a triangle, two sides are equal exactly when their opposite angles are equal.

Worked example 2 Use the converse to form an equation

Problem. In △RST\triangle RST△RST, the givens are

∠R≅∠T.RS=4x+1 centimeters.ST=6x−9 centimeters.\begin{aligned} \angle R&\cong\angle T. \\ RS&=4x+1\text{ centimeters}. \\ ST&=6x-9\text{ centimeters}. \end{aligned}∠RRSST​≅∠T.=4x+1 centimeters.=6x−9 centimeters.​

Find xxx and both lengths.

Solution. The side opposite RRR is STSTST, and the side opposite TTT is RSRSRS. The converse gives

ST=RS.\begin{aligned} ST&=RS. \end{aligned}ST​=RS.​

Therefore,

4x+1=6x−9.4x+1=6x-9.4x+1=6x−9.

Subtract 4x4x4x and add 999:

10=2x.x=5.\begin{aligned} 10&=2x. \\ x&=5. \end{aligned}10x​=2x.=5.​

Substitute into each length expression:

RS=4(5)+1=21 centimeters.ST=6(5)−9=21 centimeters.\begin{aligned} RS&=4(5)+1 \\ &=21\text{ centimeters}. \\ ST&=6(5)-9 \\ &=21\text{ centimeters}. \end{aligned}RSST​=4(5)+1=21 centimeters.=6(5)−9=21 centimeters.​

Both lengths are positive.

Check your understanding

To prove the converse, compare triangle BCA with CBA when angles B and C are congruent. The conclusion to be proved is

AB=AC.\begin{aligned} AB&=AC. \end{aligned}AB​=AC.​

Which fact supplies the included side for ASA without assuming that conclusion?

Answer choices

The known equal angles are at B and C. Their included side is BC, which equals itself when named CB. ASA exchanges B and C while keeping A fixed, so the following equality follows afterward:

BA=CA.\begin{aligned} BA&=CA. \end{aligned}BA​=CA.​

Using it as a given would make the proof circular.

When all three parts match

An equilateral triangle has three congruent sides. An equiangular triangle has three congruent interior angles. In an equilateral triangle with sides of length 888, applying the base-angle theorem to two different pairs of equal sides makes all three angles equal. The triangle sum then gives

180∘÷3=60∘.180^\circ\div3=60^\circ.180∘÷3=60∘.

The length 888 has no special role. Every equilateral triangle is equiangular, with three 60∘60^\circ60∘ angles.

Conversely, three equal angles give equal opposite sides by the converse theorem. Thus every equiangular triangle is equilateral. These angle measures do not specify the side length: equilateral triangles of side lengths 888 and 121212 are not congruent.

Worked example 3 Two different kinds of equality

Problem. Case A. An equilateral triangle has perimeter 575757 centimeters. Find each side length and each angle measure.

Case B. A triangle has two angles of 60∘60^\circ60∘. Must its sides equal those in Case A?

Solution. Case A. Its three equal side lengths total 575757 centimeters, so each side is

57÷3=19 centimeters.\begin{aligned} 57\div3&=19\text{ centimeters}. \end{aligned}57÷3​=19 centimeters.​

Its three equal angles total 180∘180^\circ180∘, so each angle is 60∘60^\circ60∘.

Case B. No. The third angle is also 60∘60^\circ60∘, so the converse makes this triangle equilateral. Its side length is not specified; it need not be 191919 centimeters.

One segment does three jobs

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of its opposite side. An altitude is a perpendicular segment from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side. An internal angle bisector halves the angle at its own vertex. They describe three different jobs; in an isosceles triangle, the segment from the vertex between the equal sides can do all three.

Return to the 11,11,1411,11,1411,11,14 flag and its vertex bisector PMPMPM. The base-angle argument applies here, giving congruent halves by SAS. Their corresponding base pieces match, so their lengths in centimeters are

QM=7.MR=7.\begin{aligned} QM&=7. \\ MR&=7. \end{aligned}QMMR​=7.=7.​

Their angles at MMM match too. Those angles form a straight angle and therefore are each 90∘90^\circ90∘. Thus the bisector is also a median and an altitude.

The vertex bisector is a median and an altitude#

Suppose

AB=AC.\begin{aligned} AB&=AC. \end{aligned}AB​=AC.​

Let ADADAD be the internal vertex bisector as in the first proof. That proof gives, by SAS,

△ABD≅△ACD.\begin{aligned} \triangle ABD&\cong\triangle ACD. \end{aligned}△ABD​≅△ACD.​

Corresponding sides give

BD=DC.\begin{aligned} BD&=DC. \end{aligned}BD​=DC.​

Thus DDD is the midpoint of BCBCBC and ADADAD is a median.

Corresponding angles give

∠ADB≅∠ADC.\begin{aligned} \angle ADB&\cong\angle ADC. \end{aligned}∠ADB​≅∠ADC.​

These two adjacent angles total 180∘180^\circ180∘ because B,D,CB,D,CB,D,C lie on one line. Equal halves of that total are 90∘90^\circ90∘, so

AD⊥BC.\begin{aligned} AD&\perp BC. \end{aligned}AD​⊥BC.​

The segment ADADAD is an altitude.

∎

You can start with the median instead. The equal sides, equal half-base lengths, and shared median give SSS congruence. It follows that the median bisects the vertex angle and is perpendicular to the base. Starting with the altitude gives the same line, because there is only one perpendicular to the base through that vertex, as proved in Proving Lines Parallel or Perpendicular. These conclusions concern the vertex between the equal sides; an arbitrary median elsewhere need not do all three jobs.

The line through this segment is a line of symmetry: reflecting across it keeps the vertex and the midpoint fixed and exchanges the two base endpoints, which lie equal distances on opposite sides of the midpoint along a perpendicular line. The whole triangle then matches itself. An equilateral triangle has such a symmetry line through each vertex, giving three reflection axes.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the wrong base. Find the common endpoint of the equal sides first. The base lies opposite it.
  • Assuming the converse. Equal base angles do imply equal opposite sides, but that direction needs the ASA argument above.
  • Excluding equilateral triangles. Three equal sides include a pair of equal sides, so equilateral triangles are isosceles.
  • Giving every median three roles. The coincidence proved here needs an isosceles triangle and the segment from the vertex between its equal sides.

Key takeaways

  • Base-angle theorem: equal triangle sides have equal opposite angles.
  • Converse: equal triangle angles have equal opposite sides.
  • Equilateral and equiangular: each condition implies the other; every angle is 60∘60^\circ60∘.
  • Vertex segment: in an isosceles triangle, the internal bisector from the vertex between the equal sides is also a median, an altitude, and part of a reflection axis.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid’s proof of the base-angle theorem has an unexpectedly busy drawing. He extends both equal sides of the triangle, chooses equal longer segments, and joins their endpoints across the figure. Two comparisons of triangles eventually establish the equal base angles. His proposition also proves an equality between angles outside the triangle.

The proposition became known as the pons asinorum, Latin for the bridge of asses. The origin of the nickname is uncertain: accounts connect it either with the difficulty readers faced or with the bridge-like drawing. It is a nickname attached to the proposition, not a name Euclid gave his students.

Pappus of Alexandria, who worked centuries later, is credited with a much shorter argument. He compares the original triangle with itself, exchanging the two base vertices. No extra lines appear, but the reader has to accept that two different orderings of one triangle can make a useful comparison. The shorter drawing asks for a less obvious way of looking.

David Joyce’s edition and commentary on Proposition I.5 presents Euclid’s construction, the uncertain nickname, and the proof attributed to Pappus.

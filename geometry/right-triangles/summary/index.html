Right Triangles and Trigonometry: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Hypotenuse and legs
The hypotenuse lies opposite a right triangle's right angle and is its longest side. The other two sides are legs.
Hypotenuse altitude and foot
The perpendicular from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse meets its open segment. Its meeting point is the foot; it need not be the midpoint.
Altitude notation
In ABCABCABC right at CCC, let CH⊥ABCH\perp ABCH⊥AB, with HHH interior. Lengths are a=BCa=BCa=BC, b=CAb=CAb=CA, c=ABc=ABc=AB, p=AHp=AHp=AH, q=HBq=HBq=HB, and h=CHh=CHh=CH.
Geometric mean
For positive numbers u,vu,vu,v, the geometric mean is uv\sqrt{uv}uv​, the positive value between them in the equal ratios u/uv=uv/vu/\sqrt{uv}=\sqrt{uv}/vu/uv​=uv​/v.
Pythagorean triple
Three positive integers whose two smaller squares add to the largest square. A common positive length factor preserves the right-triangle relationship.
Reference angle, opposite, and adjacent
Select an acute angle first. Its opposite leg is across from it; its adjacent leg meets it and the right angle. The hypotenuse is excluded from adjacent leg.
Inverse trigonometric operation
An operation taking a valid side ratio and returning the acute angle that produces it. The notation sin⁡−1\sin^{-1}sin−1 means inverse sine, not a reciprocal.
Elevation and depression
An elevation angle turns upward from the horizontal through the eye; a depression angle turns downward from it. A vertical reference gives the complementary angle.
Rise, run, and sloped length
Rise is vertical change; run is horizontal distance. The sloped length is the hypotenuse of the resulting right triangle. Percent gradient compares rise with run.

Problem types, step by step

Recover lengths at a hypotenuse altitude

  1. Identify the right angle, foot, whole hypotenuse, and its two pieces; establish correspondence by AA before forming ratios.
  2. Recover a missing piece by subtraction when needed. The altitude uses the two pieces; a leg uses the whole and its touching piece.
  3. Solve for the squared length, then take the positive root. When comparing legs, their squared ratio equals the matching piece ratio.

Find an exact missing side

  1. Locate the hypotenuse from the right angle and convert all lengths to common units.
  2. Add leg squares for a hypotenuse, or subtract a leg square from the hypotenuse square. Simplify the positive radical; retain exactness unless rounding is requested.

Distinguish the theorem's proof from its converse

  1. For the theorem, recall AA's two leg-square relations. Adding them uses the partition p+q=cp+q=cp+q=c to give the hypotenuse square.
  2. For the converse, construct a right triangle with the two proposed legs. Use the established theorem to match its third side, then SSS to transfer the right angle.

Classify proposed side lengths

  1. Check positivity and put the longest length last. Reject lengths failing the strict triangle inequality.
  2. Compare the longest-side square with the sum of the other squares. For variable lengths, retain both the existence bounds and the squared comparison; equality boundaries are separate cases.

Work forward or backward with special triangles

  1. Read the angle markings and identify the given side's role; establish a special triangle before using its ratio.
  2. Recover the common positive size parameter, then obtain each requested side. For a supplied perimeter, add the three side expressions before solving for that parameter.

Evaluate and connect trigonometric ratios

  1. Select the acute reference angle and name its opposite leg, adjacent leg, and hypotenuse. Find a missing side by Pythagoras if needed.
  2. Substitute exact lengths or special-angle values. For complements, exchange sine and cosine and reverse tangent.
  3. For related values, use the squared-value identity with a positive root, then the quotient identity. Check positivity, bounds, and the sum of squares.

Solve and check a right triangle

  1. Check sufficient valid data: one acute angle and a specified side, or two specified sides. Angles alone fix only shape; one side alone leaves freedom. An angle and perimeter can fix scale too.
  2. For a side, write a ratio equation using the known pair and isolate the unknown. For an angle, apply the matching acute inverse operation in degree mode; subtract from 90∘90^\circ90∘ for its complement.
  3. Retain original expressions or stored values until final rounding. Check side order, acute-angle sum, the original ratios, and Pythagoras.

Build a height, depression, or ramp model

  1. Draw the actual horizontal reference, vertical differences, and sloping lengths. Establish the ground levels and a common vertical sight plane.
  2. Use the eye's elevation relative to the target base. A depression calculation uses the vertical drop to the target, not automatically the eye's entire height.
  3. Choose a ratio for the actual known lengths. For gradient, use horizontal run; recover it first if only surface length is known. State units and round the final estimate sensibly.

Combine two observations

  1. Draw station order and identify any genuinely shared rise or run. Equal instrument heights on unequal ground do not give equal eye elevations.
  2. For same-side stations, the far run exceeds the near run by the separation; for opposite-side stations, the runs add to the separation. Solve the two compatible ratio equations.
  3. For top and base seen from one eye, add vertical parts on opposite sides of eye level; subtract their rises or drops when both endpoints are on the same side.
  4. Check the original equations and positive distances. Agreement tests consistency without proving every model assumption.

Exam traps

  • Trap Matching vertices by their position in a drawing

    Fix Match right and acute angles in order. A shared vertex need not correspond to itself in the altitude triangles.

  • Trap Assuming every hypotenuse altitude is a median

    Fix Perpendicularity gives right angles, not equal pieces. Keep ppp and qqq distinct unless equality is established.

  • Trap Confusing whole, piece, square, and length

    Fix Use c=p+qc=p+qc=p+q. The altitude uses pqpqpq; the leg at AAA uses cpcpcp. Take a positive square root to recover a length.

  • Trap Applying Pythagoras without identifying a right triangle

    Fix Locate a stated or proved right angle. A label ccc alone proves nothing; for a missing leg, subtract squares rather than adding.

  • Trap Classifying lengths that cannot close

    Fix Check the strict triangle inequality first. A flat equality case has no acute, right, or obtuse classification.

  • Trap Halving every special triangle's hypotenuse

    Fix Halving gives the short leg only in a 30-60-90 triangle. For equal-leg right triangles, divide by 2\sqrt22​.

  • Trap Calling the hypotenuse an adjacent leg

    Fix Adjacent leg excludes the hypotenuse. Switching the acute reference angle exchanges the legs while keeping the hypotenuse.

  • Trap Dropping the squares in the Pythagorean identity

    Fix The squared sine and cosine values add to 111. Their unsquared sum need not. Also, u+v\sqrt{u+v}u+v​ is not generally u+v\sqrt u+\sqrt vu​+v​.

  • Trap Confusing inverse operations, reciprocals, and inputs

    Fix An inverse returns an angle in DEGREE mode. Acute sine/cosine inputs lie strictly between zero and one; positive tangent inputs may exceed one.

  • Trap Rounding early or using approximate data as exact proof

    Fix Keep full intermediate precision and mark rounded outputs with ≈\approx≈. Near agreement of measured side squares does not prove an exactly right angle.

  • Trap Using the wrong distance or height reference

    Fix Tangent uses horizontal run and vertical difference. Include eye elevation relative to the target base; a sloping tape measurement alone supplies neither required quantity.

  • Trap Treating percent gradient or station separation as an angle rule

    Fix A gradient is a rise/run percentage, not degrees. Station-run sums and differences require the stated collinearity and order; doubling a tangent ratio does not double its angle.

Intuition

Two arms of a folding gate have fixed lengths. As you open the gate wider, the free ends move farther apart. The Pythagorean theorem gives their separation when the arms meet at a right angle. The Law of Cosines tells you how to adjust that calculation at any opening.

Learning goals

  • Derive the Law of Cosines from a signed projection and the Pythagorean theorem
  • Explain how the included angle changes the square of the opposite side
  • Use two sides and their included angle to find the third side
  • Recover and check the angles of a triangle from its three sides
  • Choose a triangle law from the given sides and angles

Measure the separation with a projection

In a nondegenerate triangle ABCABCABC, use aaa for the length of BCBCBC, bbb for CACACA, and ccc for ABABAB. Each lowercase letter names the side opposite the corresponding angle. The included angle between two sides is the angle at their shared endpoint. Sides aaa and bbb meet at CCC.

Put CCC at the origin and AAA at (9,0)(9,0)(9,0). Compare two placements of BBB at the same distance from CCC.

In the acute placement, BBB is at (4,3)(4,3)(4,3). The horizontal distance from BBB to AAA is 9−49-49−4 units, and the vertical distance is 333 units. The Pythagorean theorem gives c2c^2c2.

c2=(9−4)2+32=34.\begin{aligned} c^2&=(9-4)^2+3^2\\ &=34. \end{aligned}c2​=(9−4)2+32=34.​

In the obtuse placement, BBB is at (−4,3)(-4,3)(−4,3). Its horizontal coordinate is negative. The same subtraction now gives a larger horizontal distance.

c2=(9−(−4))2+32=178.\begin{aligned} c^2&=(9-(-4))^2+3^2\\ &=178. \end{aligned}c2​=(9−(−4))2+32=178.​

Expanding the square shows what changed. In both placements, the squares of the horizontal and vertical coordinates of BBB add to 252525, the square of BCBCBC. The changing part is the product of −2-2−2, the base length 999, and the signed horizontal coordinate.

34=92+25−2(9)(4),178=92+25−2(9)(−4).\begin{aligned} 34&=9^2+25-2(9)(4),\\ 178&=9^2+25-2(9)(-4). \end{aligned}34178​=92+25−2(9)(4),=92+25−2(9)(−4).​

Find a third side from SAS

Two sides and their included angle form SAS data. The cosine equation uses those three measurements to give the square of the remaining side. Take the positive square root because a side has positive length. Use degree mode on your calculator and round only the final measurements.

  • Acute included angle. Cosine is positive, so the correction reduces the sum of the two side squares.
  • Obtuse included angle. Cosine is negative, so the correction increases that sum.
  • Right included angle. Cosine is zero, so the equation becomes the Pythagorean theorem.

Worked example 1 An acute opening

Problem. Two rods of lengths 777 cm and 101010 cm meet at a hinge opened to 60∘60^\circ60∘. Find the exact distance ddd between the free ends.

Solution. The free ends are opposite the included angle. Since cos⁡60∘=12\cos60^\circ=\tfrac12cos60∘=21​, the correction subtracts 707070 from the sum of the squares.

d2=72+102−70=79,d=79 cm.\begin{aligned} d^2&=7^2+10^2-70\\ &=79,\\ d&=\sqrt{79}\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}d2d​=72+102−70=79,=79​ cm.​

Check your understanding

Two fixed sides meet at an acute angle. The hinge opens to an obtuse angle while the side lengths stay fixed. How does the square of the distance between the free ends change?

Answer choices

Positive cosine makes the correction negative at the acute opening. Negative cosine makes the correction positive at the obtuse opening.

Worked example 2 Two sightings from a dock

Problem. From one dock, the straight distances to two buoys are 230230230 m and 310310310 m. The angle between the sight lines is 124∘124^\circ124∘. Treat all three points as lying in one horizontal plane. Find the straight distance ddd between the buoys to the nearest meter.

Solution. The measured angle is between the two known sides, and ddd lies across from it.

d2=2302+3102−2(230)(310)cos⁡124∘,d≈478 m.\begin{aligned} d^2&=230^2+310^2\\ &\quad-2(230)(310)\cos124^\circ,\\ d&\approx478\text{ m}. \end{aligned}d2d​=2302+3102−2(230)(310)cos124∘,≈478 m.​

The result fits between the difference of the measured lengths, 808080 m, and their sum, 540540540 m.

Problem. Two straight paths of lengths 111111 m and 191919 m start at the same point and meet there at 90∘90^\circ90∘. Use the cosine equation to find the exact distance ddd between their far ends. Explain why the Pythagorean theorem gives the same result.

Find angles from SSS

With SSS data, all three sides are given. First check that the two shorter positive lengths add to more than the longest. This one comparison guarantees the triangle inequalities for positive lengths. Then isolate the cosine of the angle across from the side chosen on the left of the original equation.

cos⁡C=a2+b2−c22ab.\cos C=\frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}.cosC=2aba2+b2−c2​.

As the ray from CCC turns through the upper unit semicircle from 0∘0^\circ0∘ to 180∘180^\circ180∘, the horizontal coordinate decreases from 111 to −1-1−1. Every value strictly between −1-1−1 and 111 occurs at exactly one interior angle. In degree mode the inverse cosine, written arccos⁡\arccosarccos or cos⁡−1\cos^{-1}cos−1, returns that angle. An input of −1-1−1 or 111 would produce a straight or zero angle, so the triangle would be degenerate.

If you need every angle, use the cosine equation for the largest angle and then for one of the others. Subtract both from 180∘180^\circ180∘ to find the third. Starting opposite the longest side lets you see whether any angle is obtuse.

Worked example 4 Recover all three angles

Problem. In triangle ABCABCABC, the side lengths are 888, 131313, and 181818 units for aaa, bbb, and ccc, respectively. Find the measures of AAA, BBB, and CCC to the nearest tenth of a degree, and check the results against the sides.

Solution. The two shorter sides add to 212121 units, more than 181818. The largest angle is CCC, across from the longest side.

cos⁡C=82+132−1822(8)(13)=−91208,C≈115.9∘.\begin{aligned} \cos C&=\frac{8^2+13^2-18^2}{2(8)(13)}\\ &=-\frac{91}{208},\\ C&\approx115.9^\circ. \end{aligned}cosCC​=2(8)(13)82+132−182​=−20891​,≈115.9∘.​

Use the sides touching AAA in the denominator and subtract the square of the side opposite AAA in the numerator.

cos⁡A=132+182−822(13)(18),A≈23.6∘.\begin{aligned} \cos A&=\frac{13^2+18^2-8^2}{2(13)(18)},\\ A&\approx23.6^\circ. \end{aligned}cosAA​=2(13)(18)132+182−82​,≈23.6∘.​

Subtract the unrounded values to obtain BBB.

B=180∘−C−A≈40.5∘.\begin{aligned} B&=180^\circ-C-A\\ &\approx40.5^\circ. \end{aligned}B​=180∘−C−A≈40.5∘.​

The displayed angles add to 180.0∘180.0^\circ180.0∘. Their order from smallest to largest is AAA, BBB, CCC, which matches the order 888, 131313, 181818 of the opposite sides. The negative cosine computed for CCC also agrees with its obtuse measure.

Choose a law from the measurements

Given dataFirst move
Two sides and their included angle (SAS)Use the cosine law to find the third side.
Three sides (SSS)Use the cosine law to find an angle.
Two angles and a side (ASA or AAS)Use the angle sum if needed to complete an opposite pair. Then use the sine law.

Once you have a new measurement, look at the updated data. For example, after finding the third side from SAS, all three sides are available, so the SSS procedure finds the remaining angles.

Worked example 5 Choose a law for two angles and a side

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Opposite and included. In the formula for c2c^2c2, use the angle across from ccc. That same angle lies between the two lengths in the product 2ab2ab2ab.
  • Sign of the projection. Keep the negative sign of the cosine for an obtuse included angle. The minus sign in the formula then makes the correction positive.
  • Recovering angles. Isolate the cosine before using inverse cosine. Check the side lengths form a triangle and keep calculator precision until the end.

Key takeaways

  • The Law of Cosines subtracts twice the base times the signed horizontal projection from the sum of the two adjacent side squares.
  • Use the cosine law first for SAS or SSS. A known opposite pair gives the sine law a starting ratio.
  • The cosine correction is zero at a right angle, giving the Pythagorean theorem.

A bit of history (optional)

In Book II of Euclid’s Elements, the problem took two propositions. In the obtuse case the perpendicular lands outside the triangle. Euclid adds the area of twice a rectangle to the two squares. In the acute case he subtracts twice a rectangle. The diagrams let a reader compare actual areas, with the perpendicular determining which rectangle to use.

Read Book II, Proposition 12 and Book II, Proposition 13 in David E. Joyce’s edition.

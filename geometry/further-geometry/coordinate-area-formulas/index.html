Intuition

A park map gives you the coordinates of every corner. One side bends inward around a pond. If you walk the fence and write down the corners as you reach them, that list gives you enough information to find the area. Walking back the other way gives the same area, even though it changes the sign of the calculation.

Learning goals

  • Explain how signed edge contributions produce the area of a simple polygon
  • Calculate exact areas from ordered vertices with the shoelace formula
  • Predict the effect of changing the starting vertex or reversing the traversal
  • Identify when a crossing makes a vertex order unsuitable for measuring area

Follow the boundary

A simple polygon has a closed boundary made of straight edges, with edges meeting only at the shared endpoints of consecutive edges. It can be concave, with a corner that points inward. A lattice polygon has integer coordinates at every vertex.

Use the usual axes, with positive xxx to the right and positive yyy up. As you follow a simple boundary counterclockwise, the interior is locally on your left. Follow it clockwise and the interior is locally on your right. The signed area will be positive for a counterclockwise walk and negative for a clockwise walk.

Give each directed edge a signed contribution

Draw a horizontal reference line y=ky=ky=k below all the vertices. An edge and the vertical drops from its endpoints enclose a trapezoid down to that line. Give that trapezoid positive area for a leftward edge and negative area for a rightward edge. A vertical edge has zero contribution because its horizontal width is zero.

Worked example 1 Sign a single trapezoid

Problem. The directed edge goes from P(6,4)P(6,4)P(6,4) to Q(2,1)Q(2,1)Q(2,1). Use the reference line y=−1y=-1y=−1 to find its signed trapezoid contribution. Find the contribution of the reverse edge as well.

Check your understanding

An edge moves right by 3 units. Its endpoints are 2 and 6 units above the reference line. What is its signed trapezoid contribution?

Answer choices

A rightward edge contributes negatively. Multiply the width 3 by the average of the two heights, which is 4, and attach the negative sign.

Why the directed trapezoids give signed area#

Cut the plane into vertical strips at the distinct vertex xxx-coordinates. Within the open part of a strip, the straight boundary segments crossing it stay in the same top-to-bottom order because they never meet there. At a vertical line inside the strip, each entry into the polygon is followed by an exit. Thus the region in that strip consists of pieces with paired upper and lower edges. A piece can taper to a point at a strip endpoint. The strips and their interior pieces are finite in number.

For a counterclockwise boundary, an upper edge of a piece has interior below it, so the edge moves left. A lower edge has interior above it, so the edge moves right. The portion of the upper trapezoid in this strip is added and the portion of the lower trapezoid is subtracted. Both reach the same reference line. Their difference is exactly the area between the two edges. If a strip contains two or more pieces, each upper and lower pair contributes its own area.

Every edge trapezoid is accounted for by its portions in these strips. Vertical cut lines have no area. Therefore the signed contributions add to the polygon’s area. In a clockwise walk, all the edge signs switch and the result is the negative of the area.

∎

The shoelace formula

For a simple polygon with successive boundary vertices Pi=(xi,yi)P_i=(x_i,y_i)Pi​=(xi​,yi​) and closing vertex Pn+1=P1P_{n+1}=P_1Pn+1​=P1​, the signed area SSS is

The ordinary area KKK is

K=∣S∣.K=|S|.K=∣S∣.

Write the vertices in two columns and repeat the first row below the last. Each pair of neighboring rows gives two diagonal products. Subtract the product that goes from the upper yyy to the lower xxx from the product that goes from the upper xxx to the lower yyy. The pairs of diagonals look like crossed laces.

Check your understanding

Why does the term for the last edge have to use the first vertex again?

Answer choices

The closing edge brings the walk back to its first vertex. Every vertex then occurs once as a starting endpoint and once as an ending endpoint, so its endpoint products cancel.

Start anywhere or reverse the walk

Changing the first vertex while keeping the same direction moves some terms to the other end of the sum. Every directed edge stays in the list. Reversing the walk swaps the two products for every edge, so each difference changes sign.

Worked example 3 Reverse the lattice walk

Problem. Check that the walk is simple

Connecting the same points in a different sequence can change which edges you draw. Even an absolute value can leave the wrong result when edges cross.

Worked example 5 Find where a crossing loses area

Problem. A rectangle has corners A(0,0)A(0,0)A(0,0), B(8,0)B(8,0)B(8,0), C(8,6)C(8,6)C(8,6), and D(0,6)D(0,6)D(0,6). A walk visits A,C,B,DA,C,B,DA,C,B,D and closes at AAA. Find where its edges cross. Check your understanding

The edges of a four-vertex walk cross, and its shoelace sum is nonzero. What can you conclude about using the ordinary area formula?

Answer choices

Simplicity is a condition on the edges. A nonzero sum cannot establish it, and reversal keeps the same segments.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Leaving the walk open. Repeat the first vertex after the last so the closing edge contributes.
  • Taking absolute values edge by edge. Keep every difference signed, add them, and take the absolute value of the final signed area.
  • Reordering by a coordinate. Read the corners along the boundary. Sorting by xxx can replace boundary edges with segments across the region.

Key takeaways

  • Give each edge its two ordered products, including the edge back to the first vertex.
  • Halve the sum of the differences to obtain signed area. Its absolute value is the ordinary area of a simple polygon.
  • A new starting vertex keeps the signed area. A reverse traversal changes its sign.
  • Check for crossings before treating a vertex list as a simple boundary.

A bit of history (optional)

A surveyor can measure how far each new corner lies north or south and east or west of the previous one. Those readings begin as changes in position. Adding the changes corner by corner turns them into coordinates, which can then be used to find the area of the parcel.

In his 1986 article The Surveyor’s Area Formula, mathematician Bart Braden began with that surveying problem. He showed that when a parcel is divided into triangles, the two triangles on either side of an internal diagonal count it in opposite directions. The entries from those diagonals cancel, leaving the surveyor’s original boundary. Braden’s article, hosted by Appalachian State University

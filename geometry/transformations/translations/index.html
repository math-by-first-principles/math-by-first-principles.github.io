Translations

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Slide a paper cutout across a desk without turning it. One corner arrives at a new place, but that corner alone does not describe the whole move. Every other point must make the same journey. A translation turns that instruction into an exact rule, on a grid or off one.

Learning goals

  • Describe a translation using corresponding points, displacement arrows, and image notation
  • Apply a horizontal and vertical change to every point of a figure
  • Recover a translation from point-image pairs and check the given correspondence
  • Explain which lengths, angles, and parallel relationships a translation preserves

Follow every point

A motion can be described by telling where each point goes. For example, sliding a triangle 444 cm to the right sends each corner, each side point, and each interior point 444 cm to the right. The slide acts on the entire figure.

A transformation is a rule that assigns each point in the plane exactly one image point. The starting figure is the preimage; the resulting figure is the image. Write P↦P′P\mapsto P'P↦P′ to say that PPP maps to P′P'P′, read “P-prime.” The two are corresponding points, paired by the rule. Thus A↦A′A\mapsto A'A↦A′, B↦B′B\mapsto B'B↦B′, and C↦C′C\mapsto C'C↦C′ specify which corners correspond.

A translation moves every point the same distance in the same direction. It is a slide with no turning, folding, or stretching. A displacement arrow runs from a point to its image. Its length gives the travel distance; its arrowhead gives the direction. All displacement arrows of a nonzero translation have equal lengths and the same direction. They need not lie on the same line.

An arrow P→P′P\to P'P→P′ describes a journey, while the segment PQPQPQ joins two points within the figure. Do not confuse how far a point travels with how long a side is. A side can be 777 cm long even when every point travels 444 cm.

There is also a zero translation, in which every point stays where it is. Its travel distance is zero, so it has no travel direction. We include it even though an ordinary spoken “slide” suggests movement.

Worked example 1 Translate a segment without a grid

Problem. An arrow from AAA to A′A'A′ specifies a translation. It points right and downward and has length 444 cm. Describe how to draw the image of an oblique segment UVUVUV on paper. State how far and in which direction each endpoint travels.

Solution. If UUU lies on the supporting line through AAA and A′A'A′, use that same line.

If UUU is off that line, use the compass-and-straightedge parallel-line construction from lesson 2.3 to draw a parallel through the external point UUU.

On the chosen line, take the ray from UUU pointing the same way as the given arrow. Use a ruler to mark U′U'U′ 444 cm along that ray. Thus UUU travels 444 cm in the direction from AAA to A′A'A′.

At VVV, use the same line-choice method: use the supporting line through AAA and A′A'A′ if VVV lies on it; otherwise, construct the parallel through the external point VVV.

On that line, choose the ray from VVV pointing the same way as the given arrow. Use the ruler to mark V′V'V′ 444 cm along the ray. The journey from VVV to V′V'V′ is also 444 cm in that direction.

Join U′U'U′ to V′V'V′. Sliding the original segment carries its points onto this image segment. The two copied arrows specify one common move, even though UVUVUV is slanted.

Check your understanding

Two points each travel 5 cm. The first moves east, and the second moves north. Could these be the journeys of two points under one translation?

Answer choices

A nonzero translation gives every point the same distance and the same direction. Both journeys are 5 cm long, but east and north are different directions, so they cannot belong to one translation.

Describe the slide with coordinates

Coordinates let you give the same instruction without drawing an arrow. In (x,y)(x,y)(x,y), the first coordinate locates a point horizontally and the second vertically. Positive horizontal change moves right; positive vertical change moves up. Negative changes move left or down.

For example, move P(2,−1)P(2,-1)P(2,−1) three units right and four units up. Its new horizontal coordinate is 2+3=52+3=52+3=5, and its new vertical coordinate is −1+4=3-1+4=3−1+4=3. The image is P′(5,3)P'(5,3)P′(5,3).

Every point receives those same two additions. The rule is

(x,y)↦(x+3,y+4).(x,y)\mapsto(x+3,y+4).(x,y)↦(x+3,y+4).

The displacement pair (3,4)(3,4)(3,4) records the horizontal change and then the vertical change. It is an instruction, not the coordinates of a special point. It also does not say the straight travel distance is 3+43+43+4: horizontal and vertical counts describe two different directions.

In general, a translation with displacement pair (a,b)(a,b)(a,b) has rule

(x,y)↦(x+a,y+b).(x,y)\mapsto(x+a,y+b).(x,y)↦(x+a,y+b).

The numbers aaa and bbb are fixed for the whole plane. They may be positive, negative, or zero. Pair (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) gives the zero translation. You already shifted graphs in Algebra I; here the objects are points and plane figures, so the additions act directly on their coordinates.

Worked example 2 Move every vertex of a triangle

Problem. Triangle PQRPQRPQR has P(1,−2)P(1,-2)P(1,−2), Q(4,−1)Q(4,-1)Q(4,−1), and R(2,2)R(2,2)R(2,2). Translate it three units left and two units up. Give the rule and the coordinates of all three image vertices, then draw the image.

Solution. Three left subtracts 333 from each horizontal coordinate. Two up adds 222 to each vertical coordinate:

(x,y)↦(x−3,y+2).(x,y)\mapsto(x-3,y+2).(x,y)↦(x−3,y+2).

For PPP, calculate each coordinate:

x′=1−3=−2,y′=−2+2=0.\begin{aligned} x'&=1-3=-2,\\ y'&=-2+2=0. \end{aligned}x′y′​=1−3=−2,=−2+2=0.​

Thus P′=(−2,0)P'=(-2,0)P′=(−2,0).

For QQQ, the coordinates become 4−3=14-3=14−3=1 and −1+2=1-1+2=1−1+2=1, so Q′=(1,1)Q'=(1,1)Q′=(1,1).

For RRR, the coordinates become 2−3=−12-3=-12−3=−1 and 2+2=42+2=42+2=4, so R′=(−1,4)R'=(-1,4)R′=(−1,4).

Plot the three images and connect corresponding sides in the same order, P′Q′R′P'Q'R'P′Q′R′. Moving only PPP while leaving QQQ and RRR unchanged would change the figure instead of translating it.

Check your understanding

A translation has displacement pair (−2,−5)(-2,-5)(−2,−5). What is the image of S(−3,4)S(-3,4)S(−3,4)?

Answer choices

The pair says two units left and five down. Apply the changes separately.

x′=−3−2=−5x'=-3-2=-5x′=−3−2=−5

y′=4−5=−1y'=4-5=-1y′=4−5=−1

The image is S′(−5,−1)S'(-5,-1)S′(−5,−1).

Recover the rule and check the correspondence

To recover a translation, compare an image coordinate with its starting coordinate. Suppose D(−4,2)D(-4,2)D(−4,2) maps to D′(1,−1)D'(1,-1)D′(1,−1). The horizontal change is 1−(−4)=51-(-4)=51−(−4)=5; the vertical change is −1−2=−3-1-2=-3−1−2=−3. The displacement pair is (5,−3)(5,-3)(5,−3).

This subtraction works because image equals start plus change. Subtracting the start leaves the change. For any specified pair P(x,y)↦P′(x′,y′)P(x,y)\mapsto P'(x',y')P(x,y)↦P′(x′,y′), the changes are x′−xx'-xx′−x and y′−yy'-yy′−y.

One point-image pair determines a translation. If other pairs are supplied, each must have those same changes. Agreement checks the listed points; a translation rule then tells where every other point goes. A few matching corners do not establish that an arbitrary unknown transformation acts that way everywhere.

Worked example 3 Check all the named pairs

Problem. A proposed translation sends A(−2,3)A(-2,3)A(−2,3) to A′(3,1)A'(3,1)A′(3,1) and B(1,−1)B(1,-1)B(1,−1) to B′(6,−3)B'(6,-3)B′(6,−3). It is also supposed to send C(4,2)C(4,2)C(4,2) to C′(8,0)C'(8,0)C′(8,0). Decide whether one translation can make all three specified matches. If not, give the correct image of CCC under the translation determined by the first pair.

Solution. The pair for AAA gives horizontal change 3−(−2)=53-(-2)=53−(−2)=5 and vertical change 1−3=−21-3=-21−3=−2.

The pair for BBB gives 6−1=56-1=56−1=5 and −3−(−1)=−2-3-(-1)=-2−3−(−1)=−2, so it agrees.

The claimed pair for CCC gives 8−4=48-4=48−4=4 and 0−2=−20-2=-20−2=−2. Its horizontal change is different. One translation cannot make all three matches.

The first two pairs use (x,y)↦(x+5,y−2)(x,y)\mapsto(x+5,y-2)(x,y)↦(x+5,y−2). Applying that rule to CCC gives

x′=4+5=9,y′=2−2=0.\begin{aligned} x'&=4+5=9,\\ y'&=2-2=0. \end{aligned}x′y′​=4+5=9,=2−2=0.​

The correct image is C′(9,0)C'(9,0)C′(9,0).

Sometimes the image is given and the preimage is missing. For the rule (x,y)↦(x+2,y−6)(x,y)\mapsto(x+2,y-6)(x,y)↦(x+2,y−6), an image (5,−4)(5,-4)(5,−4) must have started at (3,2)(3,2)(3,2): its horizontal coordinate needs 222 added to reach 555, and its vertical coordinate needs 666 subtracted to reach −4-4−4. Substitution checks both coordinates.

Check your understanding

A translation sends A(2,−3)A(2,-3)A(2,−3) to A′(−1,1)A'(-1,1)A′(−1,1). Which proposed second pair fits that same translation?

Answer choices

The first pair gives horizontal change −1−2=−3-1-2=-3−1−2=−3 and vertical change 1−(−3)=41-(-3)=41−(−3)=4.

Apply those same changes to B.

5−3=25-3=25−3=2

2+4=62+4=62+4=6

Thus B′(2,6)B'(2,6)B′(2,6) fits.

What stays unchanged?

A slide moves a figure without changing the distances within it or the openings between its rays. Trace an angle measuring 58∘58^\circ58∘ and a 777 cm segment on a stiff transparent sheet. Slide the sheet without turning or bending it. The segment has not been pulled longer, and the rays have not opened or closed. The new segment is still 777 cm long and the new angle is still 58∘58^\circ58∘.

We accept this geometric behavior of a slide as a foundation: a translation preserves every distance and every angle measure. Such a motion is called a rigid motion. The tracing gives a picture of that foundation; a few measurements alone would not prove it for every point.

A straight line also slides onto a straight line with the same direction. Two distinct parallel lines therefore remain distinct parallel lines: their common direction and their separation do not change. The image lies on the original line whenever the slide runs along it, since each point then moves along that line. Do not say every image line is distinct from its preimage.

For corresponding points A↦A′A\mapsto A'A↦A′ and B↦B′B\mapsto B'B↦B′, distance preservation says A′B′=ABA'B'=ABA′B′=AB, equality of lengths. The segments themselves are congruent, A′B′‾≅AB‾\overline{A'B'}\cong\overline{AB}A′B′≅AB. Likewise, corresponding angles are congruent and their measures are equal. A translation preserves these quantities; their values need not equal the point’s travel distance.

Worked example 4 Use preservation instead of measuring the picture

Problem. A translation maps D,E,F,GD,E,F,GD,E,F,G to D′,E′,F′,G′D',E',F',G'D′,E′,F′,G′ respectively. Solution. The corresponding segment lengths are equal:

2t+1=13,2t=12,t=6.\begin{aligned} 2t+1&=13,\\ 2t&=12,\\ t&=6. \end{aligned}2t+12tt​=13,=12,=6.​

The angle at EEE corresponds to the angle at E′E'E′, so m∠D′E′F′=72∘m\angle D'E'F'=72^\circm∠D′E′F′=72∘. This uses angle preservation directly.

The two parallel segments keep their directions under the same slide, so D′E′∥F′G′D'E'\parallel F'G'D′E′∥F′G′.

Check your understanding

A translation sends triangle ABC to triangle A′B′C′ with the named correspondence. Given AB=8AB=8AB=8 cm and m∠BAC=46∘m\angle BAC=46^\circm∠BAC=46∘, which conclusion is guaranteed?

Answer choices

The side AB maps to A′B′, so its length stays 8 cm. The angle at A maps to the angle at A′, so its measure stays 46 degrees.

A′B′=8 cmA'B'=8\text{ cm}A′B′=8 cm

m∠B′A′C′=46∘m\angle B'A'C'=46^\circm∠B′A′C′=46∘

These are preserved measurements inside the figure; they do not specify its travel distance.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Changing only one vertex. The same instruction must act on every point of the figure.
  • Checking distance alone. Equal arrow lengths are insufficient if the directions differ.
  • Reversing the subtraction. Image minus preimage gives the displacement from start to finish.
  • Confusing travel with size. A point’s journey and a side’s length measure different things.
  • Reading the displacement as a destination. Pair (a,b)(a,b)(a,b) gives changes; add them to each starting point.

Key takeaways

  • A translation gives every point the same displacement; the zero translation leaves every point in place.
  • Write P↦P′P\mapsto P'P↦P′ to show a point and its corresponding image.
  • Displacement (a,b)(a,b)(a,b) means (x,y)↦(x+a,y+b)(x,y)\mapsto(x+a,y+b)(x,y)↦(x+a,y+b).
  • Recover each change as image coordinate minus starting coordinate, then check every supplied pair.
  • A translation preserves lengths, angle measures, and parallel relationships.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1862, British designer William Morris looked at the rose trellis in the garden of his home, Red House, and turned it into his first wallpaper design. A garden full of irregular growing plants had to become a pattern that could repeat across a wall.

Morris’s wallpapers were printed with hand-cut wooden blocks. His early designs were slow to catch on. Many customers preferred fashionable wallpapers packed with elaborate flowers and effects that made a flat wall seem three-dimensional. Morris drew instead on plants familiar from British gardens and fields.

The scale of the work grew. In 1880, he was asked to decorate rooms at St. James’s Palace. The special wallpaper design needed 68 separate printing blocks to produce a repeat spanning two paper widths. Its vertical repeat was 127 cm. The design that began as one arrangement had to line up across many repeated impressions.

The Victoria and Albert Museum tells the story of Morris’s wallpaper designs, including their printing and changing reception.

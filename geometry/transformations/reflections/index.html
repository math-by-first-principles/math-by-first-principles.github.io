Reflections

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Put a dot on a sheet of thin paper, fold the sheet, and trace the dot onto the other side. Unfold it. The two dots match when you fold again, but what decides their positions? The crease gives a mirror line. A dot on that crease needs no partner elsewhere: it already lies where its image belongs.

Learning goals

  • Locate a reflected point using the mirror's perpendicular midpoint relationship
  • Identify fixed points and distinguish them from a figure occupying its old position
  • Apply reflection rules for axes and other horizontal or vertical mirrors
  • Use both diagonal mirror rules to locate images and check fixed points
  • Explain distance and angle preservation alongside reversal of orientation

A fold defines the image

A reflection sends a point across a chosen line to the place that matches it when the plane is folded along that line. For example, a dot 333 cm from a straight crease matches a dot 333 cm away on the opposite side, measured along the same perpendicular.

The line is the mirror line, also called the line of reflection. As in Translations, write P↦P′P\mapsto P'P↦P′ for a point and its image. A reflection uses one mirror for the whole plane. The segment joining a point to its image is its connector.

Here is the exact reflection definition. If PPP is off mirror mmm, its image P′P'P′ is on the opposite side, the segment PP′PP'PP′ is perpendicular to mmm, and their intersection HHH satisfies PH=HP′PH=HP'PH=HP′. Thus mmm is the perpendicular bisector of PP′PP'PP′: it passes through its midpoint at a right angle. This describes the reflection pair; a general theorem about all points equidistant from two endpoints comes in lesson 9.1.

If PPP lies on mmm, define P′=PP'=PP′=P. Its connector has zero length, so there is no nonzero segment to mark perpendicular. Do not add a right-angle mark to this case.

Equal distances to a line alone do not locate an image. The two points must also line up along one perpendicular. Sliding a proposed image along the far side could keep its distance to the mirror unchanged while losing that alignment.

Worked example 1 Use a perpendicular and an equal distance

Problem. Point PPP is off mirror mmm. Its perpendicular foot HHH lies on mmm, and PH=2.6PH=2.6PH=2.6 cm. Point QQQ lies on mmm. Describe the locations of P′P'P′ and Q′Q'Q′ and find PP′PP'PP′.

Solution. Extend PHPHPH through HHH to the other side of the mirror. Place P′P'P′ on that extension with HP′=2.6HP'=2.6HP′=2.6 cm. Both the right angle and the equal halves are required.

Segment addition gives the full connector length:

PP′=2.6+2.6=5.2 cm.PP'=2.6+2.6=5.2\text{ cm}.PP′=2.6+2.6=5.2 cm.

Point QQQ is already on the fold, so Q′=QQ'=QQ′=Q.

Check your understanding

Point P is off mirror m. Which conditions locate its reflected image P-prime?

Answer choices

The fold pairs opposite-side points along one perpendicular. The mirror crosses their connector at its midpoint, so the two perpendicular distances are equal. Equal distances without that connector condition are insufficient.

Which points stay fixed?

A fixed point is a point whose image is itself. Reflect a point on a crease and it stays in place; reflect one 222 cm off the crease and it goes to the other side. The definition therefore tells us that the fixed points of a reflection are exactly the points on its mirror.

This is a point-by-point claim. For example, reflect the entire horizontal axis y=0y=0y=0 across the vertical axis x=0x=0x=0. The horizontal axis occupies the same set of positions afterward, but its point (3,0)(3,0)(3,0) moves to (−3,0)(-3,0)(−3,0). Only its crossing with the mirror is fixed. A set can occupy its old position while its points exchange places. Testing when a whole figure matches itself will be the focus of Symmetry.

Check your understanding

Reflection across a vertical mirror exchanges A and B on a horizontal segment. Its midpoint H lies on the mirror. The segment occupies its old position. Which named points are fixed?

Answer choices

A goes to B and B goes to A, so neither endpoint is fixed. H lies on the mirror and maps to itself. The segment staying in the same position does not make every point of it fixed.

Horizontal and vertical mirrors

Across a vertical mirror, a point keeps its height. Suppose the mirror is x=2x=2x=2 and a point is (6,1)(6,1)(6,1). The point is 444 units to the right of the mirror. Its image must be 444 units left of it, at (−2,1)(-2,1)(−2,1).

The image’s horizontal coordinate and the starting horizontal coordinate are equally spaced around 222. Their sum is 444, twice the mirror coordinate. For a vertical mirror x=hx=hx=h, this becomes x+x′=2hx+x'=2hx+x′=2h, so

x′=2h−x.x'=2h-x.x′=2h−x.

Across a horizontal mirror, a point keeps its horizontal position. A point (2,4)(2,4)(2,4) is 555 units above y=−1y=-1y=−1, so its image is 555 units below, at (2,−6)(2,-6)(2,−6). In general, for y=ky=ky=k, the heights satisfy y+y′=2ky+y'=2ky+y′=2k.

MirrorRuleCoordinate that stays unchanged
Vertical line x=hx=hx=h(x,y)↦(2h−x,y)(x,y)\mapsto(2h-x,y)(x,y)↦(2h−x,y)Height yyy
Horizontal line y=ky=ky=k(x,y)↦(x,2k−y)(x,y)\mapsto(x,2k-y)(x,y)↦(x,2k−y)Horizontal position xxx
yyy-axis, x=0x=0x=0(x,y)↦(−x,y)(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)(x,y)↦(−x,y)Height yyy
xxx-axis, y=0y=0y=0(x,y)↦(x,−y)(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)(x,y)↦(x,−y)Horizontal position xxx

The axes are the zero-coordinate cases of the first two rules. Reflecting across the xxx-axis changes the yyy-coordinate, because the distance across that mirror is vertical. A point on any of these mirrors satisfies the rule with no change.

Worked example 2 Compare two separate mirrors

Problem. Start with R(3,−1)R(3,-1)R(3,−1) in each case. Find its image and check the perpendicular distances to the mirror.

Case A. Reflect across x=−2x=-2x=−2.

Case B. Reflect across y=3y=3y=3.

Solution. Case A changes only the horizontal coordinate:

x′=2(−2)−3=−7.x'=2(-2)-3=-7.x′=2(−2)−3=−7.

Thus R′(−7,−1)R'(-7,-1)R′(−7,−1). The original is 555 units right of x=−2x=-2x=−2; the image is 555 units left. Their connector is horizontal and therefore perpendicular to the vertical mirror.

Case B changes only the height:

y′=2(3)−(−1)=7.y'=2(3)-(-1)=7.y′=2(3)−(−1)=7.

Thus R′(3,7)R'(3,7)R′(3,7). The original is 444 units below y=3y=3y=3; the image is 444 units above. Their connector is vertical and perpendicular to the horizontal mirror. The cases each start at RRR; they are not successive moves.

Check your understanding

Reflect S(-6,5) across y=2. What is its image?

Answer choices

The mirror is horizontal, so the horizontal coordinate stays -6. The point is three units above y=2, so its image is three units below it.

y′=2(2)−5=−1y'=2(2)-5=-1y′=2(2)−5=−1

Thus S′(−6,−1)S'(-6,-1)S′(−6,−1).

The two diagonal mirrors

A diagonal fold can exchange horizontal and vertical positions. Across y=xy=xy=x, the point (3,1)(3,1)(3,1) matches (1,3)(1,3)(1,3). Folding the square grid along that diagonal exchanges its horizontal and vertical directions with their positive directions matched. The horizontal coordinate becomes the vertical coordinate and vice versa, including their signs:

Across y=x:(x,y)↦(y,x).\text{Across }y=x:\quad(x,y)\mapsto(y,x).Across y=x:(x,y)↦(y,x).

You met this exchange when graphing inverse functions in Algebra I. Here it applies to every point of the plane, including points of figures that are not function graphs.

For the other diagonal, y=−xy=-xy=−x, the fold matches the positive horizontal direction with the negative vertical direction. For example, (3,1)(3,1)(3,1) matches (−1,−3)(-1,-3)(−1,−3). The roles exchange and both signs reverse:

Across y=−x:(x,y)↦(−y,−x).\text{Across }y=-x:\quad(x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x).Across y=−x:(x,y)↦(−y,−x).

These are different mirrors. A point (a,a)(a,a)(a,a) lies on y=xy=xy=x and stays fixed there. A point (a,−a)(a,-a)(a,−a) lies on y=−xy=-xy=−x and stays fixed there. Both rules fix the origin.

Worked example 3 Keep the two diagonals separate

Problem. Starting from T(4,−2)T(4,-2)T(4,−2) in each case, find its reflected image. Then identify whether TTT is fixed by that reflection.

Case A. Use mirror y=xy=xy=x.

Case B. Use mirror y=−xy=-xy=−x.

Solution. Case A exchanges the coordinates without changing their signs, giving T′(−2,4)T'(-2,4)T′(−2,4). The starting coordinates are unequal, so TTT is not on y=xy=xy=x and is not fixed.

Case B exchanges the coordinates and reverses both signs. The new first coordinate is −(−2)=2-(-2)=2−(−2)=2; the new second coordinate is −4-4−4. Thus T′(2,−4)T'(2,-4)T′(2,−4). Since −2-2−2 is not the negative of 444, TTT is not on y=−xy=-xy=−x and is not fixed.

For a segment or polygon, apply the chosen rule to every endpoint or vertex and preserve the original connections. The fold takes straight segments to straight segments, so joining the corresponding image vertices gives the whole boundary image.

Check your understanding

Which point is fixed by reflection across y=-x?

Answer choices

A point is fixed exactly when it lies on the mirror. For (5,-5), the second coordinate is the negative of the first.

(5,−5)↦(5,−5)(5,-5)\mapsto(5,-5)(5,−5)↦(5,−5)

The other three points do not satisfy y=-x.

What the fold preserves and reverses

A reflection preserves the distances within a figure and its angle openings. Imagine tracing a 666 cm segment and a 72∘72^\circ72∘ angle onto one side of thin paper, then folding and tracing them onto the other side. The tracing matches the segment without stretching it and the rays without opening or closing their angle.

We accept reflection preserves every distance and every angle measure as a geometric foundation directly from this fold picture. Reflection is therefore a rigid motion. Measuring a few traced examples supplies evidence; it is not a proof about all points. The later equidistance proof belongs to Perpendicular Bisectors and the Circumcenter, lesson 9.1.

What changes is orientation, the clockwise or counterclockwise order of labeled points around a noncollinear triangle. A slide does not turn the marked face over. From that slide picture, we accept that a translation preserves this clockwise or counterclockwise order. A fold turns the marked face over, reversing this order. We accept orientation reversal directly from that fold picture. A single point or a straight segment has no clockwise boundary order to reverse.

Worked example 4 Match measurements while the order reverses

Problem. Reflection across mmm maps triangle ABCABCABC to A′B′C′A'B'C'A′B′C′ with the stated correspondence. Given AB=9AB=9AB=9 cm and m∠ABC=43∘m\angle ABC=43^\circm∠ABC=43∘, find A′B′A'B'A′B′ and m∠A′B′C′m\angle A'B'C'm∠A′B′C′. Compare the two labeled boundary orders in the picture.

Solution. Distance preservation gives A′B′=9A'B'=9A′B′=9 cm. The endpoints corresponding to A,BA,BA,B are A′,B′A',B'A′,B′, so this is the matching image side.

Angle preservation gives m∠A′B′C′=43∘m\angle A'B'C'=43^\circm∠A′B′C′=43∘. The middle letter remains the vertex: BBB maps to B′B'B′.

The order A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C is counterclockwise, while A′,B′,C′A',B',C'A′,B′,C′ is clockwise. Reversing orientation does not change either measurement.

Check your understanding

A reflection maps noncollinear A, B, C to A-prime, B-prime, C-prime. The order A, B, C is clockwise, AB is 14 cm, and angle ABC is 61 degrees. Which description of the image is correct?

Answer choices

A reflection reverses the orientation of a noncollinear triangle, so the corresponding order is counterclockwise. Its accepted distance and angle preservation give the same side length, 14 cm, and the same angle measure, 61 degrees.

You can draw a reflection across an oblique line on paper without a coordinate rule. With a ruler and protractor, draw a perpendicular through each off-mirror vertex and locate its foot. Use a compass to copy that vertex-to-foot distance to the opposite side along the same perpendicular. Keep any vertex on the mirror unchanged, then join the image vertices in corresponding order. This uses the angle-drawing foundation and the length-copy method already taught; pencil measurements are approximate. It does not assume a later compass-and-straightedge perpendicular construction.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Equal distance without alignment. Check that the connector is also perpendicular to the mirror.
  • Moving a mirror point. A point on the mirror is fixed; its zero connector takes no right-angle mark.
  • Negating the wrong coordinate. Across the horizontal axis, change the height. Across the vertical axis, change the horizontal coordinate.
  • Mixing diagonals. Across y=xy=xy=x, exchange coordinates. Across y=−xy=-xy=−x, exchange them and reverse both signs.
  • Confusing orientation with angle measure. Clockwise order reverses, while each corresponding angle keeps its measure.

Key takeaways

  • A reflection uses one mirror for the whole plane. Every off-mirror point-image connector meets that mirror at a right angle and has its midpoint on it.
  • The fixed points are exactly the points on the mirror.
  • Use (2h−x,y)(2h-x,y)(2h−x,y) across x=hx=hx=h and (x,2k−y)(x,2k-y)(x,2k−y) across y=ky=ky=k.
  • Use (y,x)(y,x)(y,x) across y=xy=xy=x and (−y,−x)(-y,-x)(−y,−x) across y=−xy=-xy=−x.
  • A reflection preserves distances and angle measures while reversing orientation.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1816, Scottish scientist David Brewster was studying light when he became interested in the multiplied images produced by glass plates tilted toward one another. That investigation led to the kaleidoscope.

London instrument maker Robert Brettell Bate built versions with two mirrors inside a brass tube. Turning a collar changed the angle between the mirrors. Light passed through a colored glass slide at one end, and the viewer at the other end saw changing patterns formed by the reflections.

Two examples survive in Cambridge’s Whipple Museum. They bear the words “DR BREWSTER’S PATENT” and the numbers 30 and 40. They were scientific instruments before becoming the sort of optical toy many people recognize today.

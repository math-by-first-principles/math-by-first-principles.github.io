Compositions and Congruence

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Slide a paper cutout, then flip it across a marked line. Would flipping first and sliding second put it in the same place? Each instruction can be clear while their order still matters. A valid sequence of rigid motions establishes an exact match when it carries every point of one cutout onto the other.

Learning goals

  • Track ordered rigid motions and compare the images produced by reversing their order
  • Reverse a sequence by applying its inverse motions in reverse order
  • Replace two mirror reflections with the correct directed translation or rotation
  • Describe glide reflections and explain why their parallel translation and reflection commute
  • Establish congruence by a rigid sequence and distinguish rigid maps from stretches

Follow the order

A sequence gives each motion the image left by the preceding motion. For example, start at (1,2)(1,2)(1,2), move three units right, then reflect across the horizontal axis. The slide produces (4,2)(4,2)(4,2); the reflection acts on that new point and produces (4,−2)(4,-2)(4,−2).

A composition is the single mapping obtained by applying motions in a stated order. We write the order in words: first the slide, then the reflection. Use P′P'P′ after the first motion and P′′P''P′′ after the second. The second motion acts on P′P'P′, not on the original PPP.

Each translation, reflection, and rotation preserves every distance and every angle measure. Those are the accepted slide, fold, and turn foundations. Therefore any sequence of them preserves both: each step passes the same length and angle measure to the next step. This does not derive angle preservation from lengths.

Worked example 1 Compare two orders

Problem. Start at P(2,3)P(2,3)P(2,3) separately in each case. Find both intermediate and final images.

Case A. First translate four units right, then reflect across the yyy-axis.

Case B. First reflect across the yyy-axis, then translate four units right.

Solution. Case A first adds 444 to the horizontal coordinate, giving P′(6,3)P'(6,3)P′(6,3). Reflection then negates that coordinate, giving P′′(−6,3)P''(-6,3)P′′(−6,3).

Case B first gives P′(−2,3)P'(-2,3)P′(−2,3). Adding 444 now gives P′′(2,3)P''(2,3)P′′(2,3).

The final points differ. The reflection reverses a rightward displacement if it happens after the slide. Reversing a composition’s order can change its result; it does not always do so.

Check your understanding

First reflect A(3,-2) across the x-axis, then translate two units left. What is A-double-prime?

Answer choices

Reflecting first changes the height, giving A-prime (3,2). Apply the slide to that image.

3−2=13-2=13−2=1

The final image is A-double-prime (1,2).

Undo the last step first

To undo a journey, retrace its last part before its first part. A slide right 555 followed by a slide up 222 is undone by going down 222, then left 555. The inverse of a mapping returns every image point to its original point.

MotionInverse motion
Translation with changes (a,b)(a,b)(a,b)Translation with changes (−a,−b)(-a,-b)(−a,−b)
Reflection across mmmReflection across the same mmm
Rotation about OOO through a directed angleThe opposite directed angle about the same OOO

A reflection undoes itself because folding back pairs the same two points. An opposite turn retraces the same circle. A zero translation or identity rotation already fixes every point and is its own inverse. A half-turn also undoes itself.

Undo a composition by reversing the order and inverting each motion. The first undoing step must act on the final image to restore the intermediate image.

Worked example 2 Recover the starting point

Problem. A point QQQ is translated five units right and three units down, then rotated 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise about the origin. Its final image is Q′′(2,3)Q''(2,3)Q′′(2,3). Recover QQQ and check the forward journey.

Solution. Undo the rotation first, with a clockwise quarter-turn. The rule (x,y)↦(y,−x)(x,y)\mapsto(y,-x)(x,y)↦(y,−x) gives Q′(3,−2)Q'(3,-2)Q′(3,−2).

Undo the translation with five left and three up:

x=3−5=−2.x=3-5=-2.x=3−5=−2.

y=−2+3=1.y=-2+3=1.y=−2+3=1.

Thus Q(−2,1)Q(-2,1)Q(−2,1).

Check forward: the slide gives (3,−2)(3,-2)(3,−2). A counterclockwise quarter-turn gives (−(−2),3)=(2,3)(-(-2),3)=(2,3)(−(−2),3)=(2,3), the required final image.

Check your understanding

A sequence first reflects across m, then rotates 40 degrees clockwise about O. Which sequence undoes it?

Answer choices

Undo the last motion first: turn 40 degrees counterclockwise about the same center O. This returns the intermediate image. Then reflect across the same mirror m, since that reflection undoes itself.

Two parallel mirrors make a slide

Two reflections in distinct parallel mirrors give a translation across twice the gap, directed from the first mirror toward the second. To see the doubling, first use x=0x=0x=0, then x=3x=3x=3. Point (1,2)(1,2)(1,2) reflects to (−1,2)(-1,2)(−1,2), then to (7,2)(7,2)(7,2). Its horizontal change is 7−1=67-1=67−1=6, twice the 333-unit gap, toward the second mirror.

First justify using one gap everywhere. In the 333-unit example, slide a perpendicular joining segment up or down along the mirrors until its first endpoint reaches any chosen point of the first mirror. A translation along a line leaves that line in place, as in Translations. Both mirrors have that same direction, so the slide leaves both mirror lines in place. Its second endpoint therefore remains on the second mirror.

The slide preserves the segment’s length and right angles and takes it to a straight segment. Its image is thus a 333-unit segment perpendicular to the second mirror. The unique perpendicular foot proved in lesson 2.3 makes it exactly the joining perpendicular from the new first endpoint to that mirror.

Nothing depended on 333 or on the mirrors being vertical. Start with any perpendicular joining segment of length ddd and translate it along the mirrors to any other first foot. Both mirrors stay in place, and the unique-foot result identifies its image as that foot’s perpendicular joining segment. Distance preservation gives length ddd again. Thus the positive gap ddd is the same on every perpendicular.

Now follow both reflections along the perpendicular through any starting point. Choose signed positions with the first mirror at 000 and the second at ddd, positive toward the second mirror. If the point starts at position sss, the first fold gives position −s-s−s. The second fold places it equally far on the other side of ddd:

s′′=2d−(−s).s''=2d-(-s).s′′=2d−(−s).

s′′=s+2d.s''=s+2d.s′′=s+2d.

Nothing depended on the chosen starting position. All these perpendiculars have the same direction because the mirrors are parallel, and the position along the mirrors is unchanged. Every point therefore moves the same distance 2d2d2d in the same direction. No quadrilateral theorem is needed.

Reversing the mirrors reverses the translation direction. If the mirrors coincide, reflecting twice gives the identity, a zero translation.

Two intersecting mirrors make a turn

Intersecting mirrors keep their intersection fixed and together turn the plane through twice their directed angle. For example, take a first mirror directed horizontally right and a second mirror directed 35∘35^\circ35∘ counterclockwise from it. A point whose ray from the intersection has direction 20∘20^\circ20∘ first reflects to direction −20∘-20^\circ−20∘. Its second reflected direction is 90∘90^\circ90∘, equally far from the second mirror’s 35∘35^\circ35∘ direction. The total change is 90−20=7090-20=7090−20=70 degrees counterclockwise.

To generalize that calculation, choose a ray along the first mirror and measure signed directions from it, counterclockwise positive. Let the second mirror’s chosen ray have direction α\alphaα, and a point’s ray have direction θ\thetaθ. The first fold negates the direction. The second fold puts that ray equally far across α\alphaα:

θ′=−θ.\theta'=-\theta.θ′=−θ.

θ′′=2α+θ.\theta''=2\alpha+\theta.θ′′=2α+θ.

Thus every off-center ray turns through 2α2\alpha2α. Both reflections preserve its distance from the intersection, which lies on both mirrors and stays fixed. This meets the rotation definition for every point. Choosing the opposite ray along a mirror changes the doubled turn by a full turn, so the final image is unchanged.

Reversing the mirror order reverses the directed turn. When the mirrors are perpendicular, both orders give a half-turn: 180∘180^\circ180∘ clockwise and counterclockwise have the same final image. These reflections commute, meaning either order gives the same mapping. Reversing order need not change a composition.

Worked example 3 Replace two reflections with one motion

Problem. Identify the single motion and find the final image in each case.

Case A. Start at S(3,−4)S(3,-4)S(3,−4). First reflect across x=−2x=-2x=−2, then across x=1x=1x=1.

Case B. Start at T(4,−1)T(4,-1)T(4,−1). First reflect across the xxx-axis, then across y=xy=xy=x.

Solution. Case A has a 333-unit gap directed right, so the composition translates six units right. For a direct check, the first image is S′(−7,−4)S'(-7,-4)S′(−7,−4) and the second is S′′(9,−4)S''(9,-4)S′′(9,−4).

Case B has a 45∘45^\circ45∘ counterclockwise angle from the rightward ray of the first mirror to the rising ray of the second. Its composition is a 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise rotation about the origin.

Check the two steps: T′(4,1)T'(4,1)T′(4,1), then T′′(1,4)T''(1,4)T′′(1,4). This is also the origin quarter-turn image of T(4,−1)T(4,-1)T(4,−1).

Check your understanding

Two distinct mirrors intersect at O. From the chosen ray on the first mirror to the chosen ray on the second is 28 degrees clockwise. What does reflecting first in the first mirror, then in the second do?

Answer choices

Double the directed angle from the first mirror to the second.

2(28∘)=56∘2(28^\circ)=56^\circ2(28∘)=56∘

The order gives clockwise direction and O stays fixed, so the composition is the stated clockwise rotation.

A glide follows its mirror

A reflection followed by a nonzero slide along its mirror produces a glide reflection. For a horizontal mirror, the reflection changes height while the parallel slide changes horizontal position. These independent changes explain why the two motions commute.

Worked example 4 Check a glide in both orders

Problem. Use mirror y=0y=0y=0 and a translation three units left. Start with R(5,2)R(5,2)R(5,2) separately for each order. Find the intermediate and final images. Decide whether replacing the translation with zero displacement would still give a glide reflection.

Solution. Reflect first: R′(5,−2)R'(5,-2)R′(5,−2). Slide left next: R′′(2,−2)R''(2,-2)R′′(2,−2).

Slide first: R′(2,2)R'(2,2)R′(2,2). Reflect next: R′′(2,−2)R''(2,-2)R′′(2,−2) again.

For any point (x,y)(x,y)(x,y), both orders give (x−3,−y)(x-3,-y)(x−3,−y). The change across the mirror and the change along it do not interfere. The same explanation works with any mirror and a parallel slide.

With zero displacement, the composition is simply the reflection. Our definition requires a nonzero glide.

A nonzero glide has no fixed point: every point changes its position along the mirror. A slide across the mirror does not meet the parallel condition, so the commuting claim above does not apply to it. Example 1 used such a perpendicular slide and obtained different orders.

Check your understanding

Which pair defines a glide reflection, with the same result in either order?

Answer choices

The mirror is horizontal. A four-unit rightward translation is nonzero and parallel to it, so it forms a glide reflection and commutes with the reflection. Zero displacement leaves only a reflection, while an upward slide is not parallel to this mirror.

Congruence means an exact rigid match

Two figures are congruent when a sequence of translations, reflections, and rotations carries one whole figure exactly onto the other. For example, a triangle reflected and then slid still matches its starting cutout exactly. The sequence changes placement but preserves every distance and every angle measure at each step.

The correspondence records which points match. If A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C go to D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F respectively, write △ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF. This order pairs ABABAB with DEDEDE and the angle at BBB with the angle at EEE. Choosing a different letter order can make a different, incorrect claim.

Worked example 5 Specify and verify a whole-figure match

Problem. Triangle ABCABCABC has A(0,1)A(0,1)A(0,1), B(2,1)B(2,1)B(2,1), and C(1,3)C(1,3)C(1,3). Triangle DEFDEFDEF has D(4,1)D(4,1)D(4,1), E(6,1)E(6,1)E(6,1), and F(5,−1)F(5,-1)F(5,−1). Give an ordered rigid sequence taking AAA to DDD, BBB to EEE, and CCC to FFF. Show the intermediate vertices and explain why the whole triangle matches.

Solution. First reflect across the xxx-axis. The images are A′(0,−1)A'(0,-1)A′(0,−1), B′(2,−1)B'(2,-1)B′(2,−1), and C′(1,−3)C'(1,-3)C′(1,−3).

Then translate four units right and two up. The final images are A′′(4,1)=DA''(4,1)=DA′′(4,1)=D, B′′(6,1)=EB''(6,1)=EB′′(6,1)=E, and C′′(5,−1)=FC''(5,-1)=FC′′(5,−1)=F.

Each motion takes straight segments to straight segments, so ABABAB goes to DEDEDE, BCBCBC to EFEFEF, and CACACA to FDFDFD, including every point along each side. The triangle’s interior is carried with its boundary by the same fold and slide. This specified sequence establishes △ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF without a triangle test from a later lesson.

The reverse match exists too: first translate four left and two down, then reflect across the xxx-axis.

A nonrigid mapping fails to preserve some distance or angle. Consider the horizontal stretch (x,y)↦(2x,y)(x,y)\mapsto(2x,y)(x,y)↦(2x,y). It sends (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) and (2,0)(2,0)(2,0), distance 222 apart, to (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) and (4,0)(4,0)(4,0), distance 444 apart. Therefore it is globally nonrigid.

A vertical segment from (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) to (0,3)(0,3)(0,3) does keep its length under this stretch. One preserved segment does not make the plane mapping rigid; rigidity requires preservation for every pair of points and every angle. A stretch therefore cannot be included as a rigid step in the congruence definition.

Check your understanding

A rigid sequence sends A to X, B to Z, and C to Y and carries the whole triangle onto the target. Which congruence statement records that correspondence?

Answer choices

Read each correspondence in the starting letter order. A matches X, B matches Z, and C matches Y. The image order is therefore XZY. The rigid sequence establishes congruence; the ordered names record its matches.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Restarting the second step. Apply it to the intermediate image, not the starting point.
  • Undoing in the original order. Undo the last motion first, using the same mirror or center as needed.
  • Doubling without direction. The first and second mirrors determine the translation or turning direction.
  • Assuming order always changes the result. Perpendicular reflections and the two parts of a glide commute.
  • Calling one preserved length enough. A stretch may preserve selected segments while failing to be rigid on the plane.

Key takeaways

  • A composition follows a stated order; reverse and invert its steps to undo it.
  • Two parallel reflections translate by twice the gap, from the first mirror toward the second.
  • Two intersecting reflections rotate by twice the directed mirror angle about their intersection.
  • A glide uses a nonzero slide parallel to its mirror; either order gives the same map.
  • Congruence means a whole-figure match by a rigid sequence, with corresponding points recorded in order.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1872, Felix Klein took up a professorship at Erlangen in Germany. Geometry had grown into several branches, and he proposed a way to organize them by asking which transformations were allowed and which properties those transformations left unchanged.

The proposal became known as the Erlangen Program. Its setting led to a persistent historical shortcut: it is often called Klein’s inaugural speech. He prepared the famous text for the occasion but did not actually deliver it as that speech. The written program traveled much farther than a single university address could have done. An English translation appeared in the Bulletin of the New York Mathematical Society in July 1893, as dated on the original article’s first page.

The Erlangen mathematics department describes Klein’s appointment and program. John Baez provides the English translation and notes the undelivered speech.

Symmetry

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Trace a rectangular card, turn it halfway around, and lay it back on the tracing. The corners have moved, yet the whole boundary matches. A quarter-turn usually fails. Symmetry asks which motions leave the entire figure occupying exactly its original set of positions.

Learning goals

  • Test candidate reflections and rotations by matching every part of a figure
  • Identify line symmetry and point symmetry in precisely described or drawn figures
  • Calculate finite rotational order and least positive angle while counting identity once
  • Explain why a circle has infinitely many symmetries and no least positive rotation

Match the whole figure

A motion can move points while leaving a figure in the same place. Consider an unmarked rectangle 6 units wide and 2 units tall. A rectangle here is a four-sided figure with four right angles. Put its center at the origin and its corners at (−3,−1)(-3,-1)(−3,−1), (3,−1)(3,-1)(3,−1), (3,1)(3,1)(3,1), and (−3,1)(-3,1)(−3,1). A half-turn negates both coordinates, exchanging opposite corners. It takes each joining side onto the opposite side, so the whole boundary matches. A 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise turn instead sends (3,1)(3,1)(3,1) to (−1,3)(-1,3)(−1,3), absent from the boundary. This turn fails.

A symmetry of a figure is a rigid motion that maps the whole figure exactly onto itself. Every image point must belong to the original figure, and every original position must be covered. Test edges, curves, interiors if included, and any marks that belong to the figure. A point label used only to name a corner is not a decoration unless the task says it is.

For the triangle, rectangle, and square boundaries here, a whole-boundary match must send corners to corners. A rigid motion preserves the angle where two straight sides meet. At a corner this angle is less than 180∘180^\circ180∘; at an interior point of a straight side, the two boundary directions make 180∘180^\circ180∘. A corner therefore cannot land in the middle of a straight side.

Reflections gives the flip rules, and Rotations gives the turn rules. Here we use those rules to test whether the image is the same figure. One failed point disproves a candidate; one successful point does not establish it.

Line symmetry is a fold-and-match test

Fold the same 6-by-2 rectangle along its vertical center line. The left half covers the right half, including the top and bottom edges. Folding along the horizontal center line also works. Folding along a diagonal fails: a long edge would have to cover a short edge, although reflection preserves length.

A line of symmetry is a mirror line whose reflection maps the whole figure onto itself. Points on the line stay fixed; other points can exchange places. A line that merely cuts an area into two equal amounts need not be a line of symmetry.

A square is a rectangle with four equal side lengths. Consider the unmarked square with corners A(2,2)A(2,2)A(2,2), B(−2,2)B(-2,2)B(−2,2), C(−2,−2)C(-2,-2)C(−2,−2), and D(2,−2)D(2,-2)D(2,−2). It has four lines of symmetry: the two center lines x=0x=0x=0 and y=0y=0y=0, and the two diagonals y=xy=xy=x and y=−xy=-xy=−x.

The axis and diagonal reflection rules check the whole boundary:

MirrorCorner images
Vertical center line x=0x=0x=0AAA and BBB exchange; CCC and DDD exchange
Horizontal center line y=0y=0y=0AAA and DDD exchange; BBB and CCC exchange
Diagonal y=xy=xy=xA,CA,CA,C stay fixed; BBB and DDD exchange
Diagonal y=−xy=-xy=−xB,DB,DB,D stay fixed; AAA and CCC exchange

Each rule maps every side onto the side joining its image endpoints, so every boundary point matches. This checks the folds using coordinates and straight-segment mapping, rather than assuming a fold works merely because adjacent sides have equal lengths.

There are no other mirrors. A corner AAA must land on AAA, BBB, CCC, or DDD. Sending it to BBB, CCC, or DDD forces, respectively, x=0x=0x=0, y=−xy=-xy=−x, or y=0y=0y=0 by the perpendicular-midpoint reflection rule. If AAA stays fixed, the two sides at AAA must map onto the two sides at AAA. Their other endpoints, the adjacent corners B,DB,DB,D, must therefore stay or exchange. Keeping both fixed would fix three points not on one line, whereas a reflection fixes only its mirror. Thus B,DB,DB,D exchange, forcing y=xy=xy=x.

Worked example 1 Test two candidate mirrors

Problem. A triangular outline joins A(−3,0)A(-3,0)A(−3,0), B(3,0)B(3,0)B(3,0), and C(0,4)C(0,4)C(0,4) with straight segments. A triangle has three noncollinear vertices joined by three sides. Test each candidate mirror for this whole outline. The coordinate labels are names only.

Case A. The yyy-axis.

Case B. The xxx-axis.

Solution. Case A exchanges AAA and BBB and fixes CCC. The side ABABAB covers itself, and ACACAC and BCBCBC exchange. Reflection takes each full straight segment onto the segment between its image endpoints, so every part of the outline matches.

Case B fixes AAA and BBB but sends CCC to (0,−4)(0,-4)(0,−4). That point is absent from the outline. Thus only the yyy-axis is a line of symmetry among these candidates.

Check your understanding

An unmarked rectangle is 8 units wide and 3 units tall. Which candidate is a line of symmetry?

Answer choices

The horizontal center fold pairs the top and bottom edges and pairs the two halves of each vertical edge. The entire rectangle matches. A diagonal would have to pair edges of lengths 8 and 3, which a reflection cannot do.

Rotational symmetry, order, and identity

For the coordinate square above, the counterclockwise quarter-turn rule (−y,x)(-y,x)(−y,x) sends AAA to BBB, BBB to CCC, CCC to DDD, and DDD to AAA. Each joining side therefore maps onto the next side, matching the whole boundary. Its interior matches too, if included. The matching angles in one full turn are 0∘0^\circ0∘, 90∘90^\circ90∘, 180∘180^\circ180∘, and 270∘270^\circ270∘.

Rotational symmetry usually means that some turn strictly between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 360∘360^\circ360∘ maps the whole figure onto itself. Specify its center. The identity rotation leaves every point fixed; 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 360∘360^\circ360∘ describe the same mapping.

For counting, this lesson includes identity exactly once. The rotational order about a stated center is the number of distinct matching rotations in 0∘≤θ<360∘0^\circ\leq\theta<360^\circ0∘≤θ<360∘, when that number is finite. Thus a square has order 444. Order 111 means only identity matches: the figure has no nontrivial rotational symmetry about that center. We do not count 360∘360^\circ360∘ again.

Worked example 2 Find all matching turns of a square

Problem. An unmarked square has corners A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D in counterclockwise order and center OOO. List its matching counterclockwise angles from 0∘0^\circ0∘ up to but excluding 360∘360^\circ360∘. Give its order, its least positive matching angle, and the image of AAA under that least turn.

Solution. The matching angles are 0∘0^\circ0∘, 90∘90^\circ90∘, 180∘180^\circ180∘, and 270∘270^\circ270∘. Each maps corners to corners in order and carries their joined sides onto the original sides. A smaller positive turn than 90∘90^\circ90∘ puts AAA between corner directions, missing every corner; it cannot match the square. The same argument excludes any angle between successive quarter-turns.

The order is 444, including identity once. The least positive matching angle is 90∘90^\circ90∘, and AAA moves to BBB, the next counterclockwise corner.

Four equal quarter-turns complete the circle:

4×90∘=360∘.4\times90^\circ=360^\circ.4×90∘=360∘.

A proposed least turn must fit a full turn exactly. Suppose a finite-order figure were said to have least positive matching angle 50∘50^\circ50∘. Seven repeats would also match:

7×50∘=350∘.7\times50^\circ=350^\circ.7×50∘=350∘.

The inverse of that 350∘350^\circ350∘ counterclockwise turn is equivalent to the remaining 10∘10^\circ10∘ counterclockwise turn:

360∘−350∘=10∘.360^\circ-350^\circ=10^\circ.360∘−350∘=10∘.

An inverse of a matching turn also matches. This 10∘10^\circ10∘ match contradicts the claim that 50∘50^\circ50∘ was least.

For any figure with finite rotational order nnn about a fixed center, its least positive matching angle α\alphaα satisfies

nα=360∘.n\alpha=360^\circ.nα=360∘.

α=360∘n.\alpha=\frac{360^\circ}{n}.α=n360∘​.

Why are the matching turns evenly spaced? Repeating the least turn gives matching positions because each step matches the whole figure. Take the last repeat before a full turn. If a positive gap smaller than the least turn remained, that remaining turn would also match: it is the inverse of the last matching repeat, up to a full turn. This contradicts the choice of the least positive angle. Likewise, any extra match between consecutive repeats would give a smaller matching turn by undoing the earlier repeat. Thus the repeats account for all matches and divide a full turn exactly. This uses inverses and compositions already taught, not a rule about numbers of polygon sides.

For order 111, the least positive matching angle is 360∘360^\circ360∘, an identity turn. It does not supply a nontrivial symmetry.

A figure has finite rotational order 8 about O, including identity once. What is its least positive matching angle?

Answer choices

Eight equally spaced matching turns fill one full turn.

360∘÷8=45∘360^\circ\div8=45^\circ360∘÷8=45∘

Point symmetry and exact outlines

The 6-by-2 rectangle matches after a half-turn, even though most points move. A figure has point symmetry about OOO when a 180∘180^\circ180∘ turn about OOO maps it onto itself. Thus point symmetry requires a half-turn, rather than a fold across a line. A quarter-turn alone is not the definition.

Letters are useful test figures only when their exact outlines are supplied. A curved font, unequal stroke widths, or a decoration can change the answer. Judge the drawn boundary below, not another printed version of the letter.

Worked example 3 Separate a half-turn from a fold

Problem. The figure is the exact block Z outline shown. Its vertices, in order, are (−3,2)(-3,2)(−3,2), (3,2)(3,2)(3,2), (3,1)(3,1)(3,1), (−1,−1)(-1,-1)(−1,−1), (3,−1)(3,-1)(3,−1), (3,−2)(3,-2)(3,−2), (−3,−2)(-3,-2)(−3,−2), (−3,−1)(-3,-1)(−3,−1), (1,1)(1,1)(1,1), (−3,1)(-3,1)(−3,1). Close the outline with a straight segment. Test a half-turn about O(0,0)O(0,0)O(0,0) and reflection across each coordinate axis.

Solution. The half-turn negates both coordinates. It exchanges each listed vertex with the vertex five positions later: for instance, (−3,2)(-3,2)(−3,2) goes to (3,−2)(3,-2)(3,−2), and (−1,−1)(-1,-1)(−1,−1) goes to (1,1)(1,1)(1,1). The connecting segments pair in the same way. The entire outline therefore matches, giving point symmetry about OOO.

Reflection across the yyy-axis sends the diagonal boundary point (2,12)(2,\tfrac12)(2,21​) to (−2,12)(-2,\tfrac12)(−2,21​). That image is absent from the outline, so the candidate fails.

Reflection across the xxx-axis sends (2,12)(2,\tfrac12)(2,21​) to (2,−12)(2,-\tfrac12)(2,−21​), which is absent from the outline. This candidate fails too.

These tests concern only the named mirrors. Do not infer other mirror lines from the letter’s name.

Check your understanding

What must a point-symmetry test about O check?

Answer choices

Point symmetry means a 180-degree turn about O maps the entire figure onto itself. Points may exchange places. A successful corner match alone leaves the remaining boundary untested.

Triangles and circles need different tests

We can establish a particular triangle’s symmetries from how it is constructed, without a future triangle theorem. Join three points equally spaced around a circle: the three successive central turns are each 120∘120^\circ120∘. The circle and radii are guides, not part of the triangular figure.

A 120∘120^\circ120∘ turn about OOO cycles the three vertices and their connecting sides. Any smaller positive turn misses all three vertices. Thus the triangle has order 333 and least angle 120∘120^\circ120∘, with no point symmetry. Each line through OOO and one vertex also reflects the other two vertices onto one another: their rays are equally far to either side and have equal radii. It fixes that vertex and swaps the joined sides, so there are three symmetry lines. These conclusions are for this constructed triangle. The triangle in Example 1 has a different symmetry pattern.

A circle is the set of points at one fixed positive distance from its center. Any line through the center reflects that distance condition onto itself. Any rotation about the center does the same, because each point retains its radius. Therefore a circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry and infinitely many rotational symmetries, including point symmetry. A line not through the center does not work: it sends the center to a different location and the circle with it.

Worked example 4 Look for a smallest circle turn

Problem. An unmarked circle has center OOO and radius 5 cm. Is a 7∘7^\circ7∘ counterclockwise turn about OOO a symmetry? Can 7∘7^\circ7∘, or any positive angle, be its least positive matching angle?

Solution. Yes. Every point remains 5 cm from OOO, and the inverse turn covers every original circle position.

But half of 7∘7^\circ7∘ also matches:

7∘÷2=3.5∘.7^\circ\div2=3.5^\circ.7∘÷2=3.5∘.

For any proposed positive least angle, halving it gives a smaller positive matching angle. So the circle has no least positive matching angle. The finite-order formula does not apply; its order is not 111 or 360360360.

Check your understanding

Which statement about an unmarked circle of positive radius is correct?

Answer choices

Rotation about the center keeps every point at the same radius, including fractional-degree turns. Any positive candidate for a least angle can be halved. The symmetry lines pass through the center; arbitrary offset lines fail.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Testing one part. A matching corner does not establish a whole-figure symmetry; one failed part rejects it.
  • Counting a full turn twice. Identity at 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 360∘360^\circ360∘ is the same mapping.
  • Confusing order one with a nontrivial turn. Order one has only identity.
  • Replacing point symmetry with a fold. Point symmetry requires a half-turn about the stated point.
  • Ignoring the exact figure. Letter fonts and included decorations can change a symmetry; a circle needs the infinite case.

Key takeaways

  • A symmetry maps every part of the figure exactly onto itself.
  • A line of symmetry gives a matching reflection; point symmetry gives a matching half-turn.
  • Count identity once when reporting finite rotational order.
  • For finite order nnn, the least positive matching angle is 360∘/n360^\circ/n360∘/n; order one gives only identity.
  • A circle has infinitely many center rotations and no least positive matching angle.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1922, the Dutch artist M. C. Escher made a woodcut called Eight Heads. Its human heads fitted together so that the pattern could continue beyond the page. He made it before his first visit to the Alhambra, a palace in Granada, Spain, whose walls carried intricate repeating tile patterns.

Escher copied patterns there, but he did not immediately fill his work with them. For years he concentrated on Italian landscapes. When he and his wife Jetta visited the Alhambra in 1936, they again copied designs that caught their attention. Repeating patterns soon became central to several of his prints, including Metamorphosis I and Development I in 1937.

The museum Escher in The Palace traces this long return to a youthful interest.

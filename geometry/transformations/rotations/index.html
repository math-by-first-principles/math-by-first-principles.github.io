Rotations

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Pin a transparent cutout to a sheet of paper and turn it around the pin. A corner near the pin follows a small circle; a corner farther away follows a larger one. Yet both turn through the same angle. To reproduce the movement, someone needs more than the word “turn.” They need the pin’s position, the amount of turning, and its direction.

Learning goals

  • Describe rotations using centers, directed angles, circular paths, and fixed points
  • Draw rotated images with angle tools and origin coordinate rules
  • Explain distance and angle preservation and the retention of orientation
  • Assess candidate centers and check a common directed angle for paired data

One center and one directed angle

A turn keeps each point the same distance from its center. For example, turn a point 444 cm from a pin through 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise. Its image is still 444 cm from the pin, one quarter of the way around its circle. A point 777 cm from that pin follows a different circle but turns through the same angle.

A rotation about a center OOO sends every point other than OOO around a circle centered at OOO, through one specified angle in one specified direction. Write P↦P′P\mapsto P'P↦P′ as in Translations. The radius condition is OP′=OPOP'=OPOP′=OP, and the directed turn from ray OPOPOP to ray OP′OP'OP′ is the given turn.

Counterclockwise turns run opposite to clock hands; clockwise turns run with them. When we use signed angles, counterclockwise is positive and clockwise is negative. Thus +90∘+90^\circ+90∘ means 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise, while −90∘-90^\circ−90∘ means 90∘90^\circ90∘ clockwise. An ordinary angle measure alone does not choose a turning direction.

The center itself has radius zero. Define O′=OO'=OO′=O; there is no ray OOOOOO to which we could assign a turning angle. For a turn strictly between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 360∘360^\circ360∘, every other point moves. A 0∘0^\circ0∘ turn or a full 360∘360^\circ360∘ turn gives the identity rotation, leaving every point fixed. Full extra turns do not change the final image, although they describe a longer motion along the path.

A quarter-turn is 90∘90^\circ90∘, a half-turn is 180∘180^\circ180∘, and a three-quarter-turn is 270∘270^\circ270∘. A half-turn has the same final image in either direction. A three-quarter-turn counterclockwise has the same final image as a quarter-turn clockwise because the two routes reach the same ray.

Worked example 1 Draw a turn without a grid

Problem. Rotate PPP through 35∘35^\circ35∘ clockwise about OOO, where OP=3.8OP=3.8OP=3.8 cm. Describe how to locate P′P'P′ with a ruler, protractor, and compass. Give OP′OP'OP′ and the image of OOO.

Solution. Place the protractor at OOO, with its baseline on ray OPOPOP. Draw a new ray 35∘35^\circ35∘ clockwise from OPOPOP. The direction matters: drawing the ray on the other side would give a different image.

Set the compass to length OPOPOP. Mark P′P'P′ on the new ray at that same distance from OOO:

OP′=3.8 cm.OP'=3.8\text{ cm}.OP′=3.8 cm.

The center stays fixed, so O′=OO'=OO′=O. A pencil drawing approximates the specified angle and radius; the rotation definition gives their exact intended values.

Turn the figure while the axes stay fixed

A coordinate rule records a turn relative to the original axes. Keep the paper still and turn the cutout on it. Rotating the paper and its axes together would change your viewpoint rather than show the figure’s new coordinates on those fixed axes.

Start with (3,2)(3,2)(3,2) and turn it 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise about the origin. Its position is three units right and two up. Under the turn, “right” becomes “up,” and “up” becomes “left.” The image is two left and three up, or (−2,3)(-2,3)(−2,3).

This reasoning works for signed coordinates too: left becomes down, and down becomes right. Horizontal and vertical roles exchange, with the old vertical contribution becoming a negative horizontal contribution. Thus

90∘ counterclockwise:(x,y)↦(−y,x).90^\circ\text{ counterclockwise}:\quad(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x).90∘ counterclockwise:(x,y)↦(−y,x).

For a half-turn, right becomes left and up becomes down, so both signs reverse. For 90∘90^\circ90∘ clockwise, right becomes down and up becomes right. These direction changes give the following origin rules.

Turn about the originImage of (x,y)(x,y)(x,y)
90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise, or 270∘270^\circ270∘ clockwise(−y,x)(-y,x)(−y,x)
180∘180^\circ180∘ in either direction(−x,−y)(-x,-y)(−x,−y)
90∘90^\circ90∘ clockwise, or 270∘270^\circ270∘ counterclockwise(y,−x)(y,-x)(y,−x)
0∘0^\circ0∘ or 360∘360^\circ360∘(x,y)(x,y)(x,y)

These formulas use the origin as center. For another center, reason from that center’s position. Relative to center (1,2)(1,2)(1,2), point (4,3)(4,3)(4,3) is three right and one up. A quarter-turn counterclockwise puts it one left and three up from the center, at (0,5)(0,5)(0,5). Applying the origin formula to (4,3)(4,3)(4,3) would turn about the wrong point.

Worked example 2 Rotate every vertex on fixed axes

Problem. Triangle ABCABCABC has A(2,1)A(2,1)A(2,1), B(4,1)B(4,1)B(4,1), and C(3,3)C(3,3)C(3,3). Rotate it 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise about the origin. Find all three image vertices and draw the image with the axes unchanged.

Solution. Use (x,y)↦(−y,x)(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)(x,y)↦(−y,x) for each vertex.

For AAA, the old height 111 becomes horizontal coordinate −1-1−1, and the old horizontal coordinate 222 becomes height 222. Thus A′(−1,2)A'(-1,2)A′(−1,2).

For BBB, the image is B′(−1,4)B'(-1,4)B′(−1,4).

For CCC, the image is C′(−3,3)C'(-3,3)C′(−3,3).

Plot those points on the original axes and join A′B′C′A'B'C'A′B′C′ in corresponding order. A rotation takes straight segments to straight segments, so these joined sides give the image boundary.

What a turn preserves

Turn a stiff transparent sheet with a traced 111111 cm segment and a 64∘64^\circ64∘ angle. The segment is not stretched, and the two rays do not open or close. We accept a rotation preserves every distance and every angle measure directly from this turning picture. It is a rigid motion. We also use that it takes straight segments to straight segments. Measurements of a few drawings provide approximate evidence, rather than a proof for the whole plane.

A turn also keeps the sheet’s marked face upward. The clockwise or counterclockwise order of corresponding vertices around a noncollinear triangle stays the same. This is orientation preservation. In the paired triangles of Example 2, following A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C and then following A′,B′,C′A',B',C'A′,B′,C′ goes counterclockwise in both figures. In contrast, Reflections reverse that order by turning the marked face over. Neither motion changes corresponding angle measures.

Worked example 3 Separate turning angle from preserved measurements

Problem. A 120∘120^\circ120∘ rotation maps noncollinear D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F to D′,E′,F′D',E',F'D′,E′,F′ respectively. Given DE=4u−3DE=4u-3DE=4u−3 cm, D′E′=25D'E'=25D′E′=25 cm, and m∠DEF=112∘m\angle DEF=112^\circm∠DEF=112∘, find uuu and the image angle at E′E'E′. The order D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F is clockwise. State the corresponding image order.

Solution. Corresponding side lengths are equal, so

4u−3=25.4u-3=25.4u−3=25.

Adding 333 and dividing by 444 gives u=7u=7u=7.

Angle preservation gives m∠D′E′F′=112∘m\angle D'E'F'=112^\circm∠D′E′F′=112∘. The 120∘120^\circ120∘ turning angle describes how the whole figure moves; it is not added to the angle inside it.

The order D′,E′,F′D',E',F'D′,E′,F′ is clockwise because a rotation preserves orientation.

Points at different radii can travel different distances along their circles. Preserved distance compares two points before and after the turn, such as DEDEDE and D′E′D'E'D′E′. It does not say that every point travels an equal distance.

Check your understanding

A 40-degree rotation sends A, B, C to A-prime, B-prime, C-prime. The noncollinear order A, B, C is counterclockwise and angle ABC measures 57 degrees. What is guaranteed?

Answer choices

A rotation keeps the clockwise or counterclockwise order of corresponding triangle vertices. It also preserves each angle measure directly, so the image angle remains 57 degrees. The turning angle is not added to the angle within the figure.

Investigate a missing center

An image can leave clues about its center. When a nonidentity rotation is known to exist, try this center-finding investigation: draw two nonzero point-image connectors, then draw each connector’s perpendicular bisector. These are lines through the connectors’ midpoints at right angles. Use a ruler and protractor for this exploration.

Two distinct bisectors have at most one intersection. If they intersect, their intersection is a candidate center; if they are parallel, the search provides no candidate. If the two chosen bisectors coincide, choose another point-image pair when one is available. A fixed point has a zero connector, so it supplies no perpendicular bisector. If a rotation is known to be nonidentity, a known fixed point already identifies its center directly. For an identity rotation, every point is fixed and these data do not single out a center.

This search is exploratory here; the general perpendicular-bisector justification returns in lesson 9.1. An intersection alone does not establish a rotation for arbitrary data. At a candidate center, check equal start and finish radii for each pair and check that the same directed angle maps both pairs. If the angles disagree, one rotation cannot make both matches.

Worked example 4 Locate a candidate and verify the turn

Problem. Investigate whether one rotation can send A(−1,−1)A(-1,-1)A(−1,−1) to A′(5,−1)A'(5,-1)A′(5,−1) and B(2,2)B(2,2)B(2,2) to B′(2,−4)B'(2,-4)B′(2,−4). Use the two-bisector search to find a candidate center, then verify both pairs from the rotation definition.

Solution. The horizontal connector AA′AA'AA′ has midpoint (2,−1)(2,-1)(2,−1). Its perpendicular bisector is the vertical line x=2x=2x=2.

The vertical connector BB′BB'BB′ also has midpoint (2,−1)(2,-1)(2,−1). Its perpendicular bisector is the horizontal line y=−1y=-1y=−1. The two distinct lines meet at candidate O(2,−1)O(2,-1)O(2,−1).

Relative to OOO, AAA is three left and A′A'A′ is three right. Both radii have length 333, and a 180∘180^\circ180∘ turn matches them.

Point BBB is three above OOO and B′B'B′ is three below. Again both radii have length 333, and the same 180∘180^\circ180∘ turn matches them. Thus the half-turn about O(2,−1)O(2,-1)O(2,−1) makes both specified matches. Either direction gives this half-turn’s final image.

One nonzero pair without a specified turn generally leaves many possible centers. Two pairs with distinct bisectors give at most one candidate: parallel bisectors have no intersection. When an intersection exists, the common directed-angle check is still necessary. Agreement for the supplied pairs verifies those matches; the chosen rotation then specifies the image of every other point.

Check your understanding

Two nonzero point-image connectors have distinct perpendicular bisectors meeting at O. What additional check is needed for arbitrary proposed data?

Answer choices

The bisector search supplies a candidate. Check the rotation definition at that candidate: each image must keep its point's radius, and both pairs must use the same angle in the same direction. Different directed angles would describe different rotations.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Omitting the center or direction. “Turn 90 degrees” leaves the movement ambiguous.
  • Turning the axes with the figure. Plot the image against the same coordinate axes.
  • Using origin rules at another center. Compare each point’s position with the stated center first.
  • Adding the turning angle to a corner angle. A rotation preserves the corner’s measure.
  • Accepting a bisector intersection without a check. The same directed angle must match every supplied pair.

Key takeaways

  • A rotation uses one center, angle, and direction for every point, each staying on its own circle.
  • The center is fixed; identity turns leave every point fixed.
  • About the origin, use (−y,x)(-y,x)(−y,x) for a counterclockwise quarter-turn, (−x,−y)(-x,-y)(−x,−y) for a half-turn, and (y,−x)(y,-x)(y,−x) for a clockwise quarter-turn.
  • A rotation preserves distances, angle measures, and triangle orientation.
  • Investigate a center with two nonzero connectors and distinct perpendicular bisectors, then check radii and a common directed angle.

A bit of history (optional)

In early 1851, Léon Foucault tested a pendulum in his basement in Paris. He wanted to make Earth’s rotation visible without asking people to watch the stars. The apparatus needed care: even a small sideways push at release could spoil the demonstration.

After the basement experiment worked, Foucault invited scientists to the Paris Observatory. His February 2 invitation asked them to “see the Earth turn.” They watched the demonstration the following day.

In March he repeated it for the public under the high dome of the Panthéon. A brass-covered lead bob hung from a wire 67 meters long. A pointer beneath it traced through a thin layer of sand on a wooden platform, making the change in the swing’s direction relative to the floor visible. Crowds came, and similar pendulums soon appeared in other cities.

The American Physical Society recounts Foucault’s experiments and public demonstrations. The Musée des Arts et Métiers object record identifies the lead enclosed in a brass shell.

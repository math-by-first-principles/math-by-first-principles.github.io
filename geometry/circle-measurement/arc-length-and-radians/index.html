Arc Length and Radians

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

The same turn cuts a short arc on a small circle and a longer arc on a large one. Arc length alone cannot describe the turn. Comparing that length with the circle’s radius removes the effect of size and gives us another way to measure angles.

Learning goals

  • Calculate arc lengths from central angles measured in degrees
  • Justify why the arc-length to radius ratio depends only on the angle
  • Interpret radians and convert between degree and radian measures
  • Find lengths of selected minor and major arcs using radians
  • Recover radii or angles after expressing lengths in matching units

A fraction of a turn gives a fraction of the circumference

A central turn is the rotation from a starting radius to an ending radius along the selected circular route.

An arc length measures distance along a selected curved part of a circle. It is different from the arc’s degree measure and from the straight chord joining its endpoints.

A 90∘90^\circ90∘ turn uses one quarter of a circle. On a circle of radius 666 cm, the full circumference is 12π12\pi12π cm, so that arc is 3π3\pi3π cm long. Equal central turns cut equal arcs in the same circle because a rotation about its center matches one arc to the other. Splitting a turn into equal parts therefore splits the circumference into the same number of equal lengths.

The previous lesson established the circumference rule and reasoning with arbitrarily close bounds. For a turn not exactly given by those equal subdivisions, trap its measure between finer fractional turns: their arc lengths trap the selected length, and both gaps can be made as small as desired.

If a circle has positive radius rrr and the selected arc has central turn α\alphaα degrees, its length is

s=α360⋅2πr.s=\frac{\alpha}{360}\cdot2\pi r.s=360α​⋅2πr.

Here α\alphaα is the numerical degree measure. For a single traversal, it lies from 000 to 360360360; zero gives no travel, and 360360360 gives the whole circumference.

Worked example 1 Measure part of the circumference

Problem. A circle has radius 777 cm. Find the length of the selected 72∘72^\circ72∘ minor arc, first exactly and then to the nearest hundredth of a centimeter.

Solution. The central turn occupies 72/36072/36072/360 of a full turn. Apply that fraction to the circumference.

s=72360⋅2π⋅7,s=14π5 cm,s≈8.80 cm.\begin{aligned} s&=\frac{72}{360}\cdot2\pi\cdot7,\\ s&=\frac{14\pi}{5}\text{ cm},\\ s&\approx8.80\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}sss​=36072​⋅2π⋅7,=514π​ cm,≈8.80 cm.​

Keep the exact expression until the final rounding.

The ratio that removes the circle’s size

If you enlarge a circle without changing the central turn, the selected arc and the radius grow by the same factor.

A fixed turn keeps arc length over radius unchanged#

Start with a radius of 666 cm and a selected arc of 444 cm. Enlarge the circle by a factor of 5/25/25/2. The new radius is 151515 cm and the matching arc is 101010 cm.

Why does enlargement scale a curved arc? Every chord in a polygonal approximation scales by 5/25/25/2, so the sum of those chord lengths does too. Making the approximation arbitrarily close to the arc, as in the preceding lesson, preserves that same factor for the curved length. The ratios are

46=23,1015=23.\begin{aligned} \frac{4}{6}&=\frac23,\\ \frac{10}{15}&=\frac23. \end{aligned}64​1510​​=32​,=32​.​

Now let a positive dilation factor kkk map a circle of radius rrr and selected arc length sss to a larger or smaller circle. Matching the same central turn gives radius krkrkr and arc length ksksks. Since rrr and kkk are positive,

kskr=sr.\begin{aligned} \frac{ks}{kr}&=\frac{s}{r}. \end{aligned}krks​​=rs​.​

The chosen numerical lengths played no special role. Rotations and translations can first align the centers and starting rays, so the argument applies to matching central turns in any two circles.

Worked example 2 Transfer an arc between circles

Problem. The same central angle cuts an arc of length 141414 cm in a circle of radius 888 cm. Find the matching arc length in a circle of radius 202020 cm. Then find the arc-length to radius ratio in each circle.

Solution. The radius grows by the factor 20/820/820/8. The matching arc grows by that factor too.

s=14⋅208,s=35 cm.\begin{aligned} s&=14\cdot\frac{20}{8},\\ s&=35\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ss​=14⋅820​,=35 cm.​

Compare lengths in the same unit before dividing.

14 cm8 cm=74,35 cm20 cm=74.\begin{aligned} \frac{14\text{ cm}}{8\text{ cm}}&=\frac74,\\ \frac{35\text{ cm}}{20\text{ cm}}&=\frac74. \end{aligned}8 cm14 cm​20 cm35 cm​​=47​,=47​.​

The lengths differ, but the common ratio records the same turn.

A radian measures how many radii fit along the arc

Take an arc 333 cm long in a circle of radius 333 cm. The arc is one radius long. The central turn it selects is one radian.

More generally, the radian measure of a central turn is its selected arc length divided by the positive radius, using matching length units.

θ=sr.\theta=\frac{s}{r}.θ=rs​.

The ratio is independent of circle size by the proof above. It counts radii along the curve, not straight chords. An angle of 222 radians selects an arc two radii long; it need not involve the number π\piπ.

The length units cancel in the quotient. The word radian, or the abbreviation rad\mathrm{rad}rad, still identifies the result as an angle measure. A radius of 555 cm is a length; an angle of 555 radians is a turn. They are different kinds of quantities.

Choose an angle, hold it fixed, and vary the positive radius. Predict how the selected arc length changes and whether its ratio to the radius changes. Then select exactly one radian and compare the two lengths.

A full turn uses the entire circumference. Dividing its length by its radius gives

θfull=2π.\theta_{\text{full}}=2\pi.θfull​=2π.

So one full turn is 2π2\pi2π radians and one half-turn is π\piπ radians.

Convert the unit without changing the turn

The equivalence of a half-turn in the two units gives the conversion factors.

180∘=π radians,θradians=αdegrees⋅π180,αdegrees=θradians⋅180π.\begin{gathered} 180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians},\\ \theta_{\text{radians}}=\alpha_{\text{degrees}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180},\\ \alpha_{\text{degrees}}=\theta_{\text{radians}}\cdot\frac{180}{\pi}. \end{gathered}180∘=π radians,θradians​=αdegrees​⋅180π​,αdegrees​=θradians​⋅π180​.​

Use radians to find a selected arc

A radian measure tells you the arc length in units of the radius. Use that meaning before multiplying.

Worked example 4 Follow the major route

Problem. A circle has radius 999 cm. Its selected major arc from AAA through CCC to BBB has central turn 7π/67\pi/67π/6 radians. Find that arc’s exact length.

Solution. The selected arc is 7π/67\pi/67π/6 radii long. Equivalently, its length divided by 999 cm is 7π/67\pi/67π/6. Multiply the ratio by the radius.

s=9⋅7π6,s=21π2 cm.\begin{aligned} s&=9\cdot\frac{7\pi}{6},\\ s&=\frac{21\pi}{2}\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ss​=9⋅67π​,=221π​ cm.​

The turn is between π\piπ and 2π2\pi2π, so its length is between half the circumference and the full circumference, as a major arc should be.

For any positive radius and selected central turn measured in radians, the definition rearranges to

s=rθ.s=r\theta.s=rθ.

The degree formula and this radian formula agree because converting a degree measure multiplies it by π/180\pi/180π/180. The input to s=rθs=r\thetas=rθ must be a radian measure.

Between distinct endpoints, the minor arc uses a turn below π\piπ radians and the major arc uses the remaining turn above π\piπ. Their turns total 2π2\pi2π radians and their lengths total the circumference. Opposite endpoints give two semicircles, each with turn π\piπ radians.

Recover a radius or an angle, with units intact

An unknown radius is the arc length divided by the radian measure. An unknown radian measure is the arc length divided by the radius. Both conclusions come from the same defining ratio.

Worked example 5 Recover the circle from an arc

Problem. A selected minor arc is 222222 cm long and its central angle is 4/54/54/5 radian. Find the circle’s radius and diameter.

Solution. Divide the arc length by the radian measure to recover the radius.

r=22÷45,r=552 cm.\begin{aligned} r&=22\div\frac45,\\ r&=\frac{55}{2}\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}rr​=22÷54​,=255​ cm.​

The diameter is twice the radius.

d=55 cm.d=55\text{ cm}.d=55 cm.

Multiplying the radius by 4/54/54/5 returns the given arc length, so the result fits the circle.

The ratio needs one length unit throughout. For an arc measured in meters and a radius in centimeters, convert one before dividing. Otherwise the bare numerical quotient changes with your choice of units.

A selected arc traversed once cannot have a turn greater than 2π2\pi2π radians. A zero turn gives zero distance; it is the boundary case, not a positive-length arc between distinct endpoints. A journey with a complete lap followed by an extra turn repeats part of the circle, so add the full-lap distance and the extra arc rather than calling it one simple arc.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating a radian measure as a length. It is a ratio of matching length units.
  • Substituting a degree number into s=rθs=r\thetas=rθ. Convert it to radians or use the degree formula.
  • Using the diameter where the radius is needed.
  • Measuring the chord, or taking the shorter arc when the problem selects the major route.
  • Dividing lengths stated in different units without converting them first.

Key takeaways

  • Degree measure gives the selected fraction of a full turn, and therefore of the circumference.
  • Radian measure is selected arc length divided by positive radius in matching units.
  • Arc length is rθr\thetarθ when θ\thetaθ is measured in radians.
  • Specify the circular route before calculating, and keep exact values until rounding is requested.

A bit of history (optional)

For 353535 years, the radian belonged to an unusual category of measurement units. When the international system of units, SI, was established in 196019601960, its organizers placed the two angle units in a separate group called supplementary units. Their relationship to the system’s other unit groups became a subject of debate.

In 198019801980, the international weights-and-measures committee interpreted these angle units as dimensionless derived units. The international conference on weights and measures formally accepted that interpretation in 199519951995 and removed the separate supplementary category. It kept the names and symbols available for use. The conference’s original decision records the disagreement and its administrative resolution.

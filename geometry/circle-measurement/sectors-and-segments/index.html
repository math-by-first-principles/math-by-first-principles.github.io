Sectors, Segments, and Circular Regions

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Two pieces can have the same curved edge and still enclose different amounts of space. Join the arc’s endpoints to the center and you get a slice. Join them to each other and you get a cap. Before choosing a formula, decide which region you have and trace its entire boundary.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish sectors, circular segments, and annuli by their regions and complete boundaries
  • Derive and use sector area formulas with degree or radian measures
  • Calculate circular segment areas using sectors and familiar right triangles
  • Determine annulus areas and include every circle in their total boundary length
  • Combine circular and straight pieces to find areas and complete perimeters

The same arc can bound different regions

A sector is the region bounded by two radii and a chosen arc. Its complete boundary includes the two straight radii as well as the curve.

A circular segment is the region bounded by a chord and a chosen arc with the same endpoints. A chord that is not a diameter determines a smaller, minor segment and a larger, major segment. State which one you want.

An annulus is the ring between two concentric circles, circles with the same center. The outer radius is larger than the positive inner radius. The ring has an outside edge and an edge around its hole.

We use positive radii throughout. Unless a question asks for the outer boundary alone, the complete perimeter counts every boundary component, including the boundary of a hole. Guides drawn inside a region are not extra pieces of its perimeter.

Check your understanding

For the same minor arc, shorter than a semicircle, which has greater area: its sector or its circular segment?

Answer choices

The sector contains the segment and also the triangle between the chord and the two radii. That triangle has positive area for this minor angle, so the sector is larger.

Sector area from a fraction of a turn

Equal central angles in one circle cut out congruent sectors, so they cut out equal areas. A disk of radius 666 cm has area 36π36\pi36π square centimeters. Six equal 60∘60^\circ60∘ sectors fill it without overlapping interiors, so each has area 6π6\pi6π square centimeters.

The circle-area argument was established in Circumference and Circle Area. Here the angle chooses a fraction of that area.

Why sector area follows its central angle#

Take a disk of radius 555 cm and split a full turn into five equal 72∘72^\circ72∘ angles. Rotating any one sector through these steps covers the disk with five congruent pieces. Each sector therefore receives one fifth of the disk’s area, which is 5π5\pi5π square centimeters. Two adjacent pieces receive twice that share.

More generally, divide a full turn into any whole number of equal angles, then take some of those sectors. Area addition gives the same fraction of the disk’s area as of the full turn. Nothing depends on using five pieces or on the radius chosen.

An angle need not be a rational fraction of a turn. You can trap its sector between sectors whose rational fractions are arbitrarily close. Their areas trap its area too. The exhaustion reasoning from the previous lesson extends the same rule to that angle.

∎

If α\alphaα is the numerical degree measure of the selected angle, its fraction of a full turn is α/360\alpha/360α/360. Thus

Asector=α360πr2.A_{\text{sector}}=\frac{\alpha}{360}\pi r^2.Asector​=360α​πr2.

For a proper sector, the angle is strictly between zero and a full turn. The area rule also extends to zero area at zero degrees and the entire disk at 360∘360^\circ360∘.

Worked example 1 Measure the slice and its outline

Problem. A sector has radius 999 cm and central angle 80∘80^\circ80∘. Find its exact area and complete perimeter.

Solution. The sector occupies the same fraction of the disk as its angle occupies of a full turn.

A=80360π(9)2,A=18π cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac{80}{360}\pi(9)^2,\\ A&=18\pi\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=36080​π(9)2,=18π cm2.​

s=80360(2π⋅9),s=4π cm.\begin{aligned} s&=\frac{80}{360}(2\pi\cdot9),\\ s&=4\pi\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ss​=36080​(2π⋅9),=4π cm.​

P=9+9+4π,P=(18+4π) cm.\begin{aligned} P&=9+9+4\pi,\\ P&=(18+4\pi)\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PP​=9+9+4π,=(18+4π) cm.​

Radians simplify the same area rule

A sector of angle 222 radians in a radius-555 circle occupies the fraction 2/(2π)2/(2\pi)2/(2π) of a turn. Multiplying that fraction by the disk area gives 252525 square units. The π\piπ cancels; there is no approximation.

For a radius rrr and a selected angle θ\thetaθ measured in radians, the identical fraction argument gives

Asector=θ2ππr2,Asector=12r2θ.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{sector}}&=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\pi r^2,\\ A_{\text{sector}}&=\frac12r^2\theta. \end{aligned}Asector​Asector​​=2πθ​πr2,=21​r2θ.​

This form requires radians. An angle given in degrees must be converted or used in the degree formula instead. Arc Length and Radians explains the angle convention and the corresponding arc-length rule.

For a proper sector, tracing its two radii and its selected arc gives

P=2r+s.P=2r+s.P=2r+s.

The full disk is an exception to that perimeter expression: its radii are internal guides, so its boundary is just the circle. Similarly, a zero-angle figure is not a proper sector with two distinct straight boundary edges.

Since the radian arc length is rθr\thetarθ, the area can also be written in terms of the arc length:

Asector=12r(rθ),Asector=12rs.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{sector}}&=\frac12r(r\theta),\\ A_{\text{sector}}&=\frac12rs. \end{aligned}Asector​Asector​​=21​r(rθ),=21​rs.​

Worked example 2 Keep the angle convention visible

Problem. A sector has radius 888 cm and central angle 5/65/65/6 radian. Find its exact area and complete perimeter.

Solution. Use the radian area form directly.

A=12(8)2(56),A=803 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12(8)^2\left(\frac56\right),\\ A&=\frac{80}{3}\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=21​(8)2(65​),=380​ cm2.​

A segment is a sector with a triangle removed

For a minor central angle strictly between zero and 180∘180^\circ180∘, the two radii and chord form a triangle inside the minor sector. Subtracting this triangle leaves the minor circular segment.

For instance, a 90∘90^\circ90∘ sector of radius 444 has area 4π4\pi4π square units. Its two radii are perpendicular legs of length 444, so the triangle has area 888 square units. The segment has area 4π−84\pi-84π−8 square units.

For that configuration in general,

Asegment=Asector−A△OAB.A_{\text{segment}}=A_{\text{sector}}-A_{\triangle OAB}.Asegment​=Asector​−A△OAB​.

Use triangle methods you already know. At 60∘60^\circ60∘, the triangle is equilateral: its two equal radii force the other angles to be 60∘60^\circ60∘ as well. A side-666 instance has height 333\sqrt333​ and area 939\sqrt393​. In general its height is r3/2r\sqrt3/2r3​/2, from the special right triangles formed by its altitude.

At 120∘120^\circ120∘, that altitude splits the triangle into two 30∘30^\circ30∘-60∘60^\circ60∘-90∘90^\circ90∘ triangles. At 90∘90^\circ90∘, the two radii already are perpendicular legs. These cases need no general triangle-area formula involving sine.

Worked example 3 A cap has a chord instead of two radii

Problem. A circle has radius 101010 cm. Two radii meet at a right angle. Find the area and complete perimeter of the minor circular segment cut off by their endpoints’ chord.

Solution. Subtract the right triangle from the quarter disk.

Asector=14π(10)2,Asector=25π,A△=12(10)(10),A△=50.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{sector}}&=\frac14\pi(10)^2,\\ A_{\text{sector}}&=25\pi,\\ A_{\triangle}&=\frac12(10)(10),\\ A_{\triangle}&=50. \end{aligned}Asector​Asector​A△​A△​​=41​π(10)2,=25π,=21​(10)(10),=50.​

The segment area is (25π−50)(25\pi-50)(25π−50) square centimeters.

The segment’s boundary uses the chord instead of the two radii. By Pythagoras, its chord length is 10210\sqrt2102​ cm. Its arc is one quarter of the circumference, or 5π5\pi5π cm. Thus

P=(5π+102) cm.P=(5\pi+10\sqrt2)\text{ cm}.P=(5π+102​) cm.

The major segment is the rest of the disk after the minor segment is removed. Subtract the minor segment area from the whole disk area; do not subtract its triangle from the major sector. The major segment contains that triangle. Its perimeter uses the same chord but the longer, major arc.

When the chord is a diameter, each segment is a semicircle. The triangle has zero area, and the boundary is the semicircular arc plus the diameter.

Rings have two circular boundaries

An annulus with outer radius 999 and inner radius 444 contains the outer disk except for the inner disk. Their areas differ by 65π65\pi65π square units. Its two circular boundaries have lengths 18π18\pi18π and 8π8\pi8π, for a total of 26π26\pi26π units.

Write RRR for the outer radius and rrr for the inner radius, with 0<r<R0<r<R0<r<R. Subtraction of areas and addition of boundary lengths give

Aannulus=π(R2−r2),Pall boundaries=2π(R+r).\begin{aligned} A_{\text{annulus}}&=\pi(R^2-r^2),\\ P_{\text{all boundaries}}&=2\pi(R+r). \end{aligned}Aannulus​Pall boundaries​​=π(R2−r2),=2π(R+r).​

The difference of squares can also be factored:

Aannulus=π(R−r)(R+r).A_{\text{annulus}}=\pi(R-r)(R+r).Aannulus​=π(R−r)(R+r).

The width of the ring is R−rR-rR−r. Squaring that width would find a disk’s area, not the area of this ring.

Worked example 4 Measure a ring's material and edging

Problem. An annulus has outer radius 121212 cm and inner radius 777 cm. Find its exact area and the total length of all its boundary circles.

Solution. Subtract the inner disk from the outer disk.

A=π(122−72),A=95π cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\pi(12^2-7^2),\\ A&=95\pi\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=π(122−72),=95π cm2.​

Both circles touch the material and count toward its complete boundary.

P=2π(12)+2π(7),P=38π cm.\begin{aligned} P&=2\pi(12)+2\pi(7),\\ P&=38\pi\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PP​=2π(12)+2π(7),=38π cm.​

Composite regions need a fresh boundary trace

Add areas of pieces with non-overlapping interiors, or subtract an explicitly removed region. Then start the perimeter calculation afresh. A shared internal join contributes no perimeter; a hole contributes its own boundary. Composite and Shaded Figures uses the same area and boundary distinction for earlier shapes.

Worked example 5 A quarter-disk cut removes straight edges

Problem. A square has side length 141414 cm. A quarter disk, centered at one corner with radius 141414 cm, is removed from the square. Find the area and complete perimeter of the remaining region.

Solution. Subtract the quarter disk area from the square area.

A=142−14π(14)2,A=(196−49π) cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=14^2-\frac14\pi(14)^2,\\ A&=(196-49\pi)\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=142−41​π(14)2,=(196−49π) cm2.​

The two square sides from the cut’s center are removed. The other two sides remain. The new curved edge is a quarter circumference of radius 141414 cm.

s=14(2π⋅14),s=7π cm,P=14+14+7π,P=(28+7π) cm.\begin{aligned} s&=\frac14(2\pi\cdot14),\\ s&=7\pi\text{ cm},\\ P&=14+14+7\pi,\\ P&=(28+7\pi)\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ssPP​=41​(2π⋅14),=7π cm,=14+14+7π,=(28+7π) cm.​

A partial ring is another useful composite region. If you keep one quarter of an annulus with radii 101010 and 666, subtract the two quarter-disk areas. The result is 16π16\pi16π square units.

Its two arcs have combined length 8π8\pi8π units. The two straight connectors each have length 444, so the complete perimeter is (8+8π)(8+8\pi)(8+8π) units. Taking a fraction of the annulus’s circular boundaries alone would miss those new straight edges. For any selected angle strictly between zero and a full turn, the same reasoning uses that angle’s fraction of both disk areas, then counts both arcs and both connectors.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong angle convention. The factor of one half with radius squared requires a radian measure. Degrees use the fraction of 360360360 instead.
  • Confusing sector and segment. A minor segment removes the triangle from the minor sector. A major segment is the complement of the minor segment in the disk.
  • Counting only an arc. A sector needs its radii, a segment needs its chord, and a partial ring needs its straight connectors.
  • Treating a ring as a small disk. Its area subtracts squared radii. Its complete perimeter adds the two circumferences.

Key takeaways

  • Sector area: take the angle’s fraction of the disk area. In radians this becomes half the radius squared times the angle.
  • Segment area: use the selected arc and a suitable sector, then account for the triangle. Name minor or major explicitly.
  • Annulus area: subtract the inner disk; its complete boundary includes both circles.
  • Trace the actual boundary after every cut or join. Area addition does not tell you which edges remain exposed.
  • Keep exact values until the end, and distinguish square units from length units.

A bit of history (optional)

Hippocrates of Chios, a Greek geometer of the fifth century BCE, investigated whether curved regions could be matched exactly by squares. Some special crescents, called lunes, gave him a success: he showed that their areas matched areas of triangles. A square with the same area as a triangle was already within the reach of Greek geometry.

These were particular crescents bounded by arcs from different circles. The result did not supply a square equal in area to an entire circle. It was a separate achievement within that larger investigation.

His own account has not survived. Much later, a writer named Simplicius preserved an earlier historian’s description of the lune arguments, adding explanations drawn from Euclid. That chain of quotations is how this part of Hippocrates’ work reached later readers.

Circle Measurement and Constructions: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Circumference and disk
Circumference CCC is the length of a circle's boundary. The enclosed disk has area AAA; a positive radius rrr is half the diameter ddd.
Exact and approximate measures
An expression retaining π\piπ or radicals can be exact. A rounded decimal, or a result from rounded inputs, is approximate and uses ≈\approx≈.
Inscribed and circumscribed polygons
An inscribed polygon has vertices on the circle. A circumscribed tangent polygon surrounds it with sides tangent to the circle.
Method of exhaustion
An informal limiting argument using valid bounds whose gap can be made smaller than every prescribed positive tolerance. A fixed finite table supplies only finite precision.
Radian
The central turn selecting an arc one radius long. Radian measure counts radii along the curve; matching length units cancel, while radians identify the angle.
Sector
The region bounded by two radii and a selected arc. A proper sector has a positive angle smaller than a full turn.
Circular segment
The region bounded by a chord and a selected arc with the same endpoints. A nondiameter chord determines minor and major segments; a diameter gives semicircles.
Annulus
The ring between concentric circles with outer radius RRR and inner radius rrr, where 0<r<R0<r<R0<r<R. Its width is R−rR-rR−r.
Regular polygon and apothem
A regular polygon has equal sides and equal interior angles. Its apothem is the perpendicular center-to-side distance, not the radius to a vertex.

Problem types, step by step

Recover circle measurements and check constraints

  1. When needed, recover the radius or diameter using a chord's perpendicular bisector, Pythagoras, or the angle on a diameter.
  2. Translate the supplied measurement equations or inequalities into conditions on the radius.
  3. Solve and keep only positive lengths satisfying every original condition; intersect allowed ranges when necessary.
  4. Check units and round only the requested final result.

Use bounds or certify rounding

  1. Compute valid inner and outer perimeters or areas; divide by a positive diameter or squared radius to bound π\piπ.
  2. For inverse bounds with a fixed positive numerator, a larger positive denominator gives the smaller quotient.
  3. For an estimate, use the enclosure width to control error. Round lower bounds down and upper bounds up.
  4. Check the whole interval against the requested rounding boundaries; tighten it if they are crossed.

Recognize an exact exhaustion argument

  1. Identify valid bounds containing both the actual quantity and the proposed exact value at every refinement.
  2. Check that the gap can be made smaller than every chosen positive tolerance, not just a displayed finite list.
  3. For a supplied halving rate, apply it to the quantity actually bounded; take a positive square root if it bounds squared lengths.
  4. Rule out any fixed positive discrepancy. For disk area, the polygon perimeters approach CCC and their apothems approach rrr, giving Cr/2Cr/2Cr/2.

Solve selected arc data

  1. Identify the route, convert lengths to one unit and choose degrees or radians consistently.
  2. Use the selected turn in the arc formula; reverse the product to find a missing radius or angle.
  3. Add adjacent nonoverlapping arc lengths, or subtract the other arc from the circumference. Count complete laps separately and check the turn range.

Measure or reconstruct a sector

  1. Use the selected angle's fraction of the disk, or the radian area formula.
  2. For inverse data, solve for the positive radius or allowed angle before calculating other measurements.
  3. For a proper sector, add both radii to its arc; check whether the region instead is a full disk.

Find a circular segment

  1. Name the minor or major segment and identify its chord endpoints.
  2. Find the minor sector and central triangle areas, using Pythagoras or special right triangles for the triangle.
  3. Subtract for the minor segment; take its disk complement for the major segment. Add the selected arc and chord for perimeter.

Measure rings and composite regions

  1. Decompose into pieces with nonoverlapping interiors, or subtract the specified holes and cuts.
  2. For inverse ring data, factor R2−r2R^2-r^2R2−r2 and use any known radius sum or width. Check 0<r<R0<r<R0<r<R.
  3. Trace every exposed boundary afresh, including holes and new connectors; omit internal joins and removed edges.

Construct the regular hexagon

  1. In the given circle with known center OOO and radius r>0r>0r>0, choose AAA on the circle. Set the compass to OAOAOA.
  2. From AAA, mark the two circle crossings B,FB,FB,F. Keep the opening fixed and proceed from BBB in the chosen direction.
  3. At each next vertex select the circle intersection other than the preceding vertex, reaching C,D,E,FC,D,E,FC,D,E,F, then AAA. Join consecutive vertices.
  4. Verify radius-length chords and equilateral center triangles: six forward 60∘60^\circ60∘ turns close, and adjacent center triangles give 120∘120^\circ120∘ interior angles.

Construct the equilateral triangle

  1. Construct the regular hexagon and keep its compass arcs.
  2. Join alternate vertices A,C,EA,C,EA,C,E (or B,D,FB,D,FB,D,F).
  3. Check three equal 120∘120^\circ120∘ center angles and equal radii; SAS gives equal chords.

Construct the square

  1. Draw diameter ACACAC through the known center OOO of the positive-radius circle.
  2. With centers A,CA,CA,C and the same opening ACACAC, draw arcs meeting in two distinct points U,VU,VU,V. Retain the arcs.
  3. Draw line UVUVUV; mark its original-circle intersections B,DB,DB,D and join A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D in circle order.
  4. Check UA=UCUA=UCUA=UC and VA=VCVA=VCVA=VC for the perpendicular bisector. Four 90∘90^\circ90∘ center angles give equal chords by SAS; angles subtending diameters are right angles.

Transfer between constructed polygons

  1. Use the first polygon's measurement to recover the common circle radius or its square.
  2. Use that circle's radius in the second polygon's own side, apothem, perimeter or area relationship.
  3. Keep different supporting circles separate in nested constructions; compare areas by division or subtraction as requested.

e.g. An inscribed square has area 383838 square cm; find the same circle's regular-hexagon perimeter: P=619P=6\sqrt{19}P=619​ cm.

Exam traps

  • Trap Declaring one finite picture exact

    Fix A finite polygon or wedge rearrangement retains a discrepancy. Exhaustion requires valid bounds with arbitrarily small gaps; repeated decimal agreement alone is insufficient.

  • Trap Using a diameter as the radius

    Fix Halve it before squaring for area. Circumference is a length; area has square units and the square of a length scale factor.

  • Trap Treating a radian as a length or requiring pi in it

    Fix Radian measure is an angle ratio and may be rational or decimal. Match the arc and radius units, and use only radians in s=rθs=r\thetas=rθ or A=r2θ/2A=r^2\theta/2A=r2θ/2.

  • Trap Choosing the shorter route automatically

    Fix Follow the stated minor or major arc. Between distinct nonopposite endpoints, the two turns total one full turn; repeated laps need their own distance.

  • Trap Subtracting a triangle from a major sector

    Fix Subtract the triangle from the minor sector, then take the disk complement for the major segment. A diameter gives a semicircle with zero central-triangle area.

  • Trap Letting area arithmetic choose the perimeter

    Fix Trace the actual boundary. Count two radii for a proper sector, a chord for a segment, both circles for an annulus and both connectors for a partial ring.

  • Trap Squaring the ring width

    Fix Subtract squared radii, or use π(R−r)(R+r)\pi(R-r)(R+r)π(R−r)(R+r). Boundary circles add their lengths even though disk areas subtract.

  • Trap Estimating construction marks by eye

    Fix Keep the hexagon opening fixed, exclude the previous intersection and use alternate vertices for its equilateral triangle. Construct the square perpendicular with equal-distance helper crossings.

  • Trap Using a degenerate helper opening

    Fix For diameter AC=2rAC=2rAC=2r, equal helper-circle openings must exceed rrr to give two distinct crossings. Any common opening greater than rrr gives the same perpendicular-bisector line; at rrr they merely touch at OOO.

  • Trap Replacing an apothem or chord by the wrong length

    Fix Use a perpendicular center-to-side apothem in A=Pa/2A=Pa/2A=Pa/2 and straight chords for polygon sides. Sharing a circle does not make different polygons' sides or areas equal.

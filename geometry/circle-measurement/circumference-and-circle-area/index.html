Circumference and Circle Area

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A straight ruler cannot follow a circular edge, but it can measure polygon sides. Put one polygon inside a circle and another around it. Their perimeters give two answers the circumference must lie between. Making that gap as small as needed will turn an approximation into an exact argument.

Learning goals

  • Justify the common circumference-to-diameter ratio using similarity
  • Compute exact or rounded circle measures, including inverse radius and diameter problems
  • Bound circumference and area using inscribed and circumscribed regular polygons
  • Explain and apply exhaustion through arbitrarily narrow valid bounds
  • Derive the circle-area formula and distinguish finite rearrangements from limiting conclusions

Straight sides give bounds

The circumference, CCC, is the length of a circle’s boundary. Its enclosed region is a disk; the disk’s area, AAA, counts square units. A circle has positive radius rrr and diameter d=2rd=2rd=2r.

An inscribed polygon has its vertices on the circle. Its straight sides cut inside the curved boundary, so its perimeter PPP is smaller than CCC. A circumscribed polygon surrounds the circle with tangent sides. Its perimeter QQQ is larger than CCC.

The upper comparison uses convexity: cutting corners from an enclosing convex polygon cannot increase its perimeter, because the joining segment is shorter than the broken path it replaces. Finer inscribed paths remain below the enclosing perimeter as they approach the circular boundary.

P<C,C<Q.\begin{aligned} P&<C,\\ C&<Q. \end{aligned}PC​<C,<Q.​

For regular polygons, joining the center to each vertex divides the polygon into equal triangles. The apothem, hhh, is the perpendicular distance from the center to a side. An outer tangent polygon with parallel sides is a dilation of the inner polygon by r/hr/hr/h.

Exhaustion closes the gap

The method of exhaustion traps an unknown quantity between valid bounds whose gap can be made smaller than any chosen positive amount. It is an informal limiting argument: there is no final polygon that becomes a circle. Every requested accuracy is achieved at some finite refinement.

Worked example 1 What a narrow enclosure certifies

Problem. Polygon measurements establish that a circumference, in centimeters, satisfies the following bounds. Find its value rounded to the nearest hundredth, and give a guaranteed error bound for that rounded estimate.

43.276<C,C<43.284.\begin{aligned} 43.276&<C,\\ C&<43.284. \end{aligned}43.276C​<C,<43.284.​

Solution. Every number in this interval rounds to 43.2843.2843.28: the interval lies strictly between the rounding boundaries 43.27543.27543.275 and 43.28543.28543.285.

The estimate is the midpoint of the given bounds. Its distance to either end is

43.284−43.28=0.004.43.284-43.28=0.004.43.284−43.28=0.004.

Therefore the error is less than 0.0040.0040.004 cm. This certifies an approximation, not the exact equality C=43.28C=43.28C=43.28 cm. Further bounds would be needed for more digits.

Check your understanding

A circumference estimate has a guaranteed error bound that halves at every refinement. Why can this process reach an error smaller than any chosen positive tolerance?

Answer choices

Repeated halving makes the bound as small as requested, although each finite bound may remain positive. Exhaustion needs this ability to keep refining and valid bounds at every stage. A finite table or a smooth-looking picture alone supplies neither an exact value nor a zero error.

For circle polygons, arc bisection supplies that refinement. The next argument explains why, using the earlier chord-bisection theorem and Pythagoras, rather than an unintroduced trigonometric formula.

Why the polygon bounds can be arbitrarily close#

For a radius-222 circle, an inscribed regular hexagon has side 222 and apothem 3\sqrt33​. Half a side and the apothem form a right triangle. Bisecting the arc creates a new chord with squared length

b2=1+(2−3)2,b2=8−43.\begin{aligned} b^2&=1+(2-\sqrt3)^2,\\ b^2&=8-4\sqrt3. \end{aligned}b2b2​=1+(2−3​)2,=8−43​.​

This is about 1.0721.0721.072, less than half the old squared side length, which is 444.

Now take any nondegenerate regular inscribed polygon. Its radius rrr and side sss are positive, and its apothem satisfies 0<h<r0<h<r0<h<r. The old half-side and the radial gap give the new chord bbb by Pythagoras:

h2+(s/2)2=r2,b2=(s/2)2+(r−h)2,b2=2r(r−h).\begin{aligned} h^2+(s/2)^2&=r^2,\\ b^2&=(s/2)^2+(r-h)^2,\\ b^2&=2r(r-h). \end{aligned}h2+(s/2)2b2b2​=r2,=(s/2)2+(r−h)2,=2r(r−h).​

Also s2=4(r−h)(r+h)s^2=4(r-h)(r+h)s2=4(r−h)(r+h). Dividing shows

b2s2=r2(r+h),b2s2<12.\begin{aligned}\frac{b^2}{s^2}&=\frac{r}{2(r+h)},\\\frac{b^2}{s^2}&<\frac12.\end{aligned}s2b2​s2b2​​=2(r+h)r​,<21​.​

Each bisection therefore more than halves the squared side length. Repeating it makes sss as small as needed. The same right triangle gives

r−h=s24(r+h).r-h=\frac{s^2}{4(r+h)}.r−h=4(r+h)s2​.

Its denominator is at least 4r4r4r, so the radial gap also becomes as small as needed. The apothem approaches rrr.

The outer polygon is the r/hr/hr/h dilation of the inner one. Thus

Q=rhP,Q−P=Pr−hh.\begin{aligned} Q&=\frac{r}{h}P,\\ Q-P&=P\frac{r-h}{h}. \end{aligned}QQ−P​=hr​P,=Phr−h​.​

The inner perimeters stay below the first outer perimeter, and the apothems stay above the first positive apothem. These fixed bounds prevent P/hP/hP/h from growing without limit. As r−hr-hr−h shrinks, the perimeter gap can therefore be made smaller than any chosen positive amount. Since CCC stays between them, both perimeters approach CCC. Nothing depended on choosing radius 222.

Why every circle uses the same pi

Earlier, circle similarity showed that a translation and positive dilation map any circle onto another. A dilation multiplies every polygon side by its scale factor. It therefore multiplies every inner and outer perimeter by that factor. Their arbitrarily tight enclosure shows that the curved circumference scales by the same factor too.

The circumference-to-diameter ratio is constant#

A diameter-666 circle dilated by 5/25/25/2 has diameter 151515. If its circumference was CCC, the new circumference is 5C/25C/25C/2. Dividing the new circumference by the new diameter gives

5C/215=C6.\frac{5C/2}{15}=\frac{C}{6}.155C/2​=6C​.

For any diameter d>0d>0d>0 and positive scale factor kkk, the same cancellation gives

kCkd=Cd.\frac{kC}{kd}=\frac{C}{d}.kdkC​=dC​.

Any positive-radius circle can be scaled to any other, so this ratio is shared by all circles. The particular diameter 666 and factor 5/25/25/2 were not essential.

∎

That common, dimensionless number is pi, written π\piπ. Use the same length unit in both measurements before dividing.

π=Cd.\pi=\frac{C}{d}.π=dC​.

Multiplying by the diameter gives the circumference formulas:

C=πd,C=2πr.\begin{aligned} C&=\pi d,\\ C&=2\pi r. \end{aligned}CC​=πd,=2πr.​

The familiar decimal starts 3.14159265…3.14159265\ldots3.14159265…. Keeping π\piπ as a symbol is exact; replacing it by a finite decimal is an approximation.

Worked example 2 A finite hexagon enclosure

Problem. A circle has radius 666 cm. A regular hexagon is inscribed in it. A second regular hexagon has parallel sides tangent to the circle. Find the two polygon perimeters and use them to bound the circumference and π\piπ.

Solution. Each of the six central triangles of the inner hexagon is equilateral, so its side length is 666 cm. The inner perimeter is

P=6(6)P=36 cm.\begin{aligned}P&=6(6)\\P&=36\text{ cm}.\end{aligned}PP​=6(6)=36 cm.​

Pythagoras gives its apothem, h=33h=3\sqrt3h=33​ cm. The outer hexagon is enlarged by r/hr/hr/h, so its perimeter is

Q=36633,Q=243 cm.\begin{aligned} Q&=36\frac{6}{3\sqrt3},\\ Q&=24\sqrt3\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}QQ​=3633​6​,=243​ cm.​

Therefore 36<C<24336<C<24\sqrt336<C<243​, with lengths in centimeters. The diameter is 121212 cm. Dividing both bounds by 121212 gives

3<π<23.3<\pi<2\sqrt3.3<π<23​.

This interval is valid but wide. It does not identify an exact decimal for π\piπ.

From polygon areas to disk area

Adding the center-triangle areas of a regular polygon gives half its perimeter times its apothem, as in regular-polygon area. For the outer tangent polygon, the apothem is the circle radius itself.

For example, a radius-444 circle lies between a hexagon of area 24324\sqrt3243​ and a tangent hexagon of area 32332\sqrt3323​, in square units. These come from perimeters 242424 and 16316\sqrt3163​, and apothems 232\sqrt323​ and 444, respectively. The two polygon areas are about 41.5741.5741.57 and 55.4355.4355.43 square units, respectively. Refined polygons tighten the exact enclosure.

Why the area of a disk is half its circumference times its radius#

Keep radius 444. The circumference is 8π8\pi8π. On refinement, the inner perimeter approaches 8π8\pi8π and its apothem approaches 444. Its area approaches

12(8π)(4)=16π.\frac12(8\pi)(4)=16\pi.21​(8π)(4)=16π.

The outer perimeter approaches the same circumference, and its apothem is already 444, so its area approaches the same number. Both the disk area and 16π16\pi16π lie between the two polygon areas at every refinement. Their difference must be smaller than every chosen positive tolerance, so it is zero.

For a general positive radius rrr, let PPP and QQQ be the inner and outer perimeters, and hhh the inner apothem. The two polygon areas are

I=12Ph,U=12Qr.\begin{aligned} I&=\frac12 Ph,\\ U&=\frac12 Qr. \end{aligned}IU​=21​Ph,=21​Qr.​

Both approach Cr/2Cr/2Cr/2, because the perimeters approach CCC and hhh approaches rrr. More explicitly, their gap is

Ep=r(Q−P)2,Eh=P(r−h)2,U−I=Ep+Eh.\begin{aligned}E_p&=\frac{r(Q-P)}2,\\E_h&=\frac{P(r-h)}2,\\U-I&=E_p+E_h.\end{aligned}Ep​Eh​U−I​=2r(Q−P)​,=2P(r−h)​,=Ep​+Eh​.​

The first contribution uses the shrinking perimeter gap; the second uses the shrinking radial gap while PPP stays bounded. Both contributions can therefore be made small enough for any chosen total tolerance. The disk and Cr/2Cr/2Cr/2 remain inside the same shrinking enclosure. If their difference were any fixed positive amount, a smaller enclosure would rule it out. Therefore

A=12Cr.A=\frac12 Cr.A=21​Cr.

Nothing depended on radius 444. Substituting the circumference formula gives

A=12(2πr)r,A=πr2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12(2\pi r)r,\\ A&=\pi r^2. \end{aligned}AA​=21​(2πr)r,=πr2.​

∎

You may also recognize the thin-wedge picture from Pre-Algebra. A sector is a wedge bounded by two radii and the joining arc. Cutting a disk into equal sectors and alternating them in a row preserves area exactly. A finite row still has curved, scalloped edges; it is not a perfect parallelogram.

Half the original curved boundary runs along the lower side of the row. As the pieces become arbitrarily thin, the corresponding straight base approaches C/2C/2C/2 and the perpendicular height approaches rrr. The limiting area is therefore (C/2)r(C/2)r(C/2)r, consistent with the polygon enclosure proof. The exact conclusion comes from controlling the remaining discrepancy, not from declaring one finite picture rectangular.

Problem. A circle has diameter 14/514/514/5 cm. Find its exact area and its area to the nearest hundredth of a square centimeter, using a calculator’s π\piπ key.

Recover a missing length

A positive radius can be recovered from either measurement. Undo the multiplication in the circumference formula, or undo the multiplication and then the square in the area formula.

Worked example 4 Recover lengths from an exact area

Problem. A disk has area 72π72\pi72π square centimeters. Find its radius, diameter, and circumference exactly.

Solution. Divide the area by π\piπ:

r2=72 cm2.r^2=72\text{ cm}^2.r2=72 cm2.

A radius is positive, so take the positive square root:

r=62 cm.r=6\sqrt2\text{ cm}.r=62​ cm.

Then

d=122 cm,C=12π2 cm.\begin{aligned} d&=12\sqrt2\text{ cm},\\ C&=12\pi\sqrt2\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}dC​=122​ cm,=12π2​ cm.​

The negative square root does not describe a radius. No decimal approximation was needed.

In general, for exact positive circumference or area,

r=C2π,d=Cπ,r=Aπ.\begin{aligned} r&=\frac{C}{2\pi},\\ d&=\frac{C}{\pi},\\ r&=\sqrt{\frac{A}{\pi}}. \end{aligned}rdr​=2πC​,=πC​,=πA​​.​

An approximate input gives an approximate recovered length; extra calculator digits cannot restore information that was never measured.

Compare changes in size

A dilation multiplies circumference by its length factor and disk area by its square. Compare lengths with lengths and areas with areas.

Worked example 5 Use a circumference comparison to find an area

Problem. A larger circle has circumference 5/35/35/3 times that of a smaller circle. The smaller disk has area 54π54\pi54π square centimeters. Find the larger disk’s area exactly.

Solution. Circumference has the same scale factor as radius, so the radius factor is 5/35/35/3. Area has the square of that factor:

Alarge=54π(53)2,Alarge=150π cm2.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{large}}&=54\pi\left(\frac53\right)^2,\\ A_{\text{large}}&=150\pi\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}Alarge​Alarge​​=54π(35​)2,=150π cm2.​

Multiplying area by 5/35/35/3 would treat square units as lengths.

Check your understanding

Which reasoning justifies an exact disk-area formula from finite polygon approximations?

Answer choices

The shrinking enclosure rules out every possible positive difference between the disk area and the proposed value. A picture or a finite list can suggest a result, but it does not establish that arbitrary-accuracy enclosure. Every finite rearrangement still retains curved edges.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Finite pictures and exactness. A polygon with many sides is still a polygon. State what bounds its error and why refinement can reduce that error as far as needed.
  • Radius and diameter. Halve a supplied diameter before using the area formula. Squaring the diameter gives four times the disk area.
  • Lengths and areas. Circumference scales as a length and uses linear units. Area scales by the square of the factor and uses square units.
  • Rounding. Keep exact π\piπ forms until an approximation is requested. Rounded inputs or an approximate π\piπ require an approximate result.

Key takeaways

  • Pi is the common dimensionless ratio of circumference to diameter, justified by circle similarity and length scaling.
  • Circumference is C=2πrC=2\pi rC=2πr, or C=πdC=\pi dC=πd.
  • Exhaustion uses valid enclosures whose gap can be made smaller than any prescribed positive amount.
  • Disk area is A=πr2A=\pi r^2A=πr2, derived by squeezing between polygon areas approaching Cr/2Cr/2Cr/2.
  • Recover a positive radius by undoing the relevant operations, and distinguish exact values from estimates.

A bit of history (optional)

In Measurement of a Circle, Archimedes gave a circle a surprising partner: a right triangle. One leg matched the circle’s radius; the other matched the entire curved boundary. The claim was that the two figures had equal areas, despite looking so different.

The surviving argument is short enough to leave modern readers asking which geometric steps it takes for granted. A modern seminar discussion preserved in Harvard’s online archive compares Greek, Latin, English, and French versions, then unpacks the polygon comparisons and exhaustion hidden in the compact statement. An ancient proof could be brief without being easy to reconstruct.

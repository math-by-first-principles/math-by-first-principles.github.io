Proving a Quadrilateral Is a Parallelogram

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A few marks can force a conclusion about every possible drawing. The challenge is to tell which marks do enough. Two equal sides fit many quadrilaterals, but carefully placed equalities can force both opposite-side pairs to be parallel.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish necessary properties from sufficient evidence that a quadrilateral is a parallelogram
  • Prove four parallelogram tests using triangle congruence and parallel-line converses
  • Choose a complete test and solve for values that satisfy its hypotheses
  • Construct counterexamples showing why incomplete side or diagonal evidence is insufficient

Evidence has a direction

A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel. The preceding lesson proved that its opposite sides and angles are congruent and its diagonals bisect each other. Those statements start with a known parallelogram; this lesson starts with less information and proves the shape is one.

A necessary condition is something every parallelogram must have. A sufficient condition is enough to guarantee that a quadrilateral is a parallelogram. A condition can be both, but reversing a proven statement requires another proof.

For example, having one congruent opposite-side pair is necessary. A parallelogram must have it, yet a quadrilateral with only that evidence might have unequal sides in the other pair. We need a complete test.

Throughout this lesson, ABCDABCDABCD is a convex quadrilateral with vertices in boundary order. Its sides do not cross, its vertices are distinct, and no three consecutive vertices lie on one line. We will use equal alternate interior angles, or supplementary same-side interior angles, to prove lines parallel. These are the converses established in Proving Lines Parallel or Perpendicular: identify the transversal and the angle positions before using them.

Check your understanding

Every parallelogram has congruent opposite sides. What must be established before using the reverse statement as a test?

Answer choices

The direct statement begins with a parallelogram. Its converse begins with congruent opposite sides and needs a route to the definition, both pairs of opposite sides parallel.

Both opposite-side pairs are congruent

Two pairs of equal opposite sides force the parallel directions. Imagine side lengths 8,5,8,58,5,8,58,5,8,5 around a convex quadrilateral. Draw one diagonal: the two triangles have the same three side lengths. Their matching angles will prove the needed parallelism.

Both opposite-side pairs congruent imply a parallelogram#

Suppose the first opposite-side pair has equal lengths, and so does the second:

AB=CD,BC=AD.\begin{gathered} AB=CD,\\ BC=AD. \end{gathered}AB=CD,BC=AD.​

Draw ACACAC. The triangles ABCABCABC and CDACDACDA have the two given side matches and the shared side ACACAC. By SSS,

△ABC≅△CDA.\triangle ABC\cong\triangle CDA.△ABC≅△CDA.

Corresponding angles give the first pair of equal alternate interior angles:

∠BAC≅∠DCA.\angle BAC\cong\angle DCA.∠BAC≅∠DCA.

With transversal ACACAC, this proves AB∥CDAB\parallel CDAB∥CD. The other matching pair is

∠BCA≅∠DAC.\angle BCA\cong\angle DAC.∠BCA≅∠DAC.

Again using ACACAC as transversal proves BC∥ADBC\parallel ADBC∥AD. Both parallel pairs required by the definition have now been proved.

∎

The side lengths 888 and 555 were only an illustration. The SSS argument used the equalities, not their particular values. Thus both opposite-side pairs congruent is a sufficient test.

Worked example 1 Make both side pairs match

Problem. Convex ABCDABCDABCD has the following side lengths in centimeters.

AB=2x+1,CD=11,BC=x+2,AD=7.\begin{gathered} AB=2x+1,\\ CD=11,\\ BC=x+2,\\ AD=7. \end{gathered}AB=2x+1,CD=11,BC=x+2,AD=7.​

Find every value of xxx for which the opposite-side congruence test applies, and decide whether it proves a parallelogram.

Solution. The first pair requires

2x+1=11,2x=10,x=5.\begin{aligned} 2x+1&=11,\\ 2x&=10,\\ x&=5. \end{aligned}2x+12xx​=11,=10,=5.​

The second pair requires

x+2=7,x=5.\begin{aligned} x+2&=7,\\ x&=5. \end{aligned}x+2x​=7,=5.​

Both conditions hold at x=5x=5x=5. The side lengths are positive, and both opposite-side pairs are congruent, so ABCDABCDABCD is a parallelogram. Solving only the first equation would leave half the test unchecked.

Both opposite-angle pairs are congruent

Two equal opposite-angle pairs also force parallel sides. For instance, if one pair measures 68∘68^\circ68∘ each, the other pair must total 224∘224^\circ224∘, so each of those two equal angles is 112∘112^\circ112∘. Every consecutive pair then totals 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Both opposite-angle pairs congruent imply a parallelogram#

First try equal opposite angles of 74∘74^\circ74∘. The other pair has total

360∘−2(74∘)=212∘.360^\circ-2(74^\circ)=212^\circ.360∘−2(74∘)=212∘.

Because its two angles are equal, each is 106∘106^\circ106∘. A 74∘74^\circ74∘ angle and a consecutive 106∘106^\circ106∘ angle are supplementary. The same-side interior-angle converse then gives each parallel pair.

For the general argument, write ppp for the common measure at A,CA,CA,C and qqq for the common measure at B,DB,DB,D. The quadrilateral angle sum gives

2p+2q=360∘,p+q=180∘.\begin{aligned} 2p+2q&=360^\circ,\\ p+q&=180^\circ. \end{aligned}2p+2qp+q​=360∘,=180∘.​

Along transversal ABABAB, angles DABDABDAB and ABCABCABC are same-side interior angles for lines ADADAD and BCBCBC. Their supplementary measures prove AD∥BCAD\parallel BCAD∥BC.

Along transversal BCBCBC, angles ABCABCABC and BCDBCDBCD are same-side interior angles for lines ABABAB and CDCDCD. Their supplementary measures prove AB∥CDAB\parallel CDAB∥CD. The particular angle measures did not matter; equality in each opposite pair made every consecutive sum 180∘180^\circ180∘.

∎

Worked example 2 Use angle evidence

Problem. A convex quadrilateral has angles AAA and CCC of 83∘83^\circ83∘ each. Angles BBB and DDD are congruent. Find the other two measures and prove that the quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

Solution. Remove the known pair from the quadrilateral total:

360∘−2(83∘)=194∘.360^\circ-2(83^\circ)=194^\circ.360∘−2(83∘)=194∘.

The remaining angles are equal, so

m∠B=97∘,m∠D=97∘.\begin{gathered} m\angle B=97^\circ,\\ m\angle D=97^\circ. \end{gathered}m∠B=97∘,m∠D=97∘.​

Both opposite-angle pairs are congruent. The test just proved guarantees that the quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

Check your understanding

Convex ABCD has congruent angles A and C. Which extra fact completes the opposite-angle test?

Answer choices

The test needs both opposite pairs. B and D form the pair that has not yet been checked. An adjacent equality alone leaves the other opposite pair unresolved.

The diagonals bisect each other

If the crossing is the midpoint of each diagonal, the quadrilateral is forced to be a parallelogram. For example, diagonal parts 4,44,44,4 on one line and 7,77,77,7 on the other give two side matches around each vertical-angle pair. SAS supplies the matching triangles.

Diagonals that bisect each other imply a parallelogram#

Let EEE be the intersection of ACACAC and BDBDBD. Suppose

AE=EC,BE=ED.\begin{gathered} AE=EC,\\ BE=ED. \end{gathered}AE=EC,BE=ED.​

Angles AEBAEBAEB and CEDCEDCED are vertical angles, so they are congruent. The two given side equalities surround those angles. SAS gives

△AEB≅△CED.\triangle AEB\cong\triangle CED.△AEB≅△CED.

Corresponding angles BAEBAEBAE and DCEDCEDCE are congruent. They are alternate interior angles along ACACAC, proving AB∥CDAB\parallel CDAB∥CD.

For the other side pair, angles BECBECBEC and DEADEADEA are vertical angles. The same half-diagonal equalities give

△BEC≅△DEA\triangle BEC\cong\triangle DEA△BEC≅△DEA

by SAS. Corresponding angles BCEBCEBCE and DAEDAEDAE are alternate interior angles along ACACAC, proving BC∥ADBC\parallel ADBC∥AD.

∎

The word bisect means divide into two equal parts. Both equalities are needed: saying that ACACAC bisects BDBDBD gives only the equality on BDBDBD.

Worked example 3 Check the common crossing

Problem. In convex ABCDABCDABCD, diagonals ACACAC and BDBDBD meet at EEE. Their parts have the following lengths in centimeters.

AE=3t−2,EC=t+6,BE=9,ED=2t+1.\begin{gathered} AE=3t-2,\\ EC=t+6,\\ BE=9,\\ ED=2t+1. \end{gathered}AE=3t−2,EC=t+6,BE=9,ED=2t+1.​

Find every value of ttt that makes both diagonals bisect each other. Explain the classification.

Solution. On diagonal ACACAC,

3t−2=t+6,2t=8,t=4.\begin{aligned} 3t-2&=t+6,\\ 2t&=8,\\ t&=4. \end{aligned}3t−22tt​=t+6,=8,=4.​

On diagonal BDBDBD,

9=2t+1,8=2t,t=4.\begin{aligned} 9&=2t+1,\\ 8&=2t,\\ t&=4. \end{aligned}98t​=2t+1,=2t,=4.​

At t=4t=4t=4, the halves of ACACAC are each 101010 cm and the halves of BDBDBD are each 999 cm. Both diagonal equalities hold at the same intersection, so ABCDABCDABCD is a parallelogram. The two whole diagonals need not have equal lengths.

Check your understanding

In the midpoint proof, why are angles AEB and CED congruent before any parallel sides have been proved?

Answer choices

Vertical angles are congruent for any two intersecting lines. Together with the two midpoint equalities, they supply SAS without assuming that the quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

One opposite-side pair is parallel and congruent

A single opposite-side pair can be enough when that same pair has both properties. If ABABAB and CDCDCD are parallel and each is 999 units long, a diagonal gives an included angle from the parallel lines and two side matches for SAS.

One opposite-side pair parallel and congruent implies a parallelogram#

Suppose one opposite-side pair has both properties:

AB∥CD,AB=CD.\begin{gathered} AB\parallel CD,\\ AB=CD. \end{gathered}AB∥CD,AB=CD.​

Draw diagonal ACACAC. Its alternate interior angles satisfy

∠BAC≅∠DCA.\angle BAC\cong\angle DCA.∠BAC≅∠DCA.

The given side equality and shared side ACACAC surround these equal angles. SAS gives

△BAC≅△DCA.\triangle BAC\cong\triangle DCA.△BAC≅△DCA.

Thus corresponding angles BCABCABCA and DACDACDAC are congruent. They are alternate interior angles for BCBCBC and ADADAD, with transversal ACACAC, so BC∥ADBC\parallel ADBC∥AD. Together with the given parallel pair, this proves a parallelogram.

∎

Worked example 4 Keep the evidence on one pair

Problem. Convex PQRSPQRSPQRS has one known parallel pair and these side lengths in inches.

PQ∥RS,PQ=4u−3,RS=17.\begin{gathered} PQ\parallel RS,\\ PQ=4u-3,\\ RS=17. \end{gathered}PQ∥RS,PQ=4u−3,RS=17.​

Find a value of uuu that guarantees a parallelogram, and justify it without assuming the other pair parallel.

Solution. Make the already parallel sides congruent:

4u−3=17,4u=20,u=5.\begin{aligned} 4u-3&=17,\\ 4u&=20,\\ u&=5. \end{aligned}4u−34uu​=17,=20,=5.​

At u=5u=5u=5, PQPQPQ and RSRSRS are each 171717 inches. That same opposite-side pair is parallel and congruent, so the proved test supplies the other parallel pair.

What incomplete evidence leaves open

To refute an incomplete test, construct one quadrilateral that satisfies all its givens and fails its conclusion. A sketch alone is not verification: use exact lengths, a construction, or an established theorem.

Here ABABAB and CDCDCD are parallel, but their lengths are 121212 and 666. Each sloping side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 333 and 444, so each has length 555. This quadrilateral has one parallel pair and a different congruent pair. It is not a parallelogram, because parallelograms have congruent opposite sides and the bases here are unequal.

Here AEAEAE and ECECEC are each 666, but BEBEBE is 555 and EDEDED is 333. Such a convex quadrilateral exists: draw two perpendicular lines through EEE and put the four vertices at those distances. It is not a parallelogram, since every parallelogram has both diagonals bisected. One midpoint is not enough.

Worked example 5 Choose the missing fact

Problem. Treat these as separate cases about convex ABCDABCDABCD. Decide which additional fact completes the indicated test in each case, and explain why.

Case A: AB∥CDAB\parallel CDAB∥CD is given. Choose between the following two possible additions to complete the one-pair test.

AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD

or

AD=BC.AD=BC.AD=BC.

Case B: AE=ECAE=ECAE=EC is given, where diagonals ACACAC and BDBDBD meet at EEE. Choose between the following two possible additions to complete the bisection test.

BE=EDBE=EDBE=ED

or

AC=BD.AC=BD.AC=BD.

Solution. Case A: choosing congruence of the already parallel pair completes the one-pair test. Congruence of the other opposite pair alone can hold in the unequal-base counterexample above.

Case B: BE=EDBE=EDBE=ED completes diagonal bisection. Congruent whole diagonals do not say that EEE is the midpoint of BDBDBD; they do not complete this test.

Check your understanding

A convex quadrilateral has one pair of opposite sides parallel, and its other pair congruent. What follows from only these facts?

Answer choices

A parallelogram satisfies the givens, but a quadrilateral with unequal parallel bases and congruent sloping sides also satisfies them. The mixed test requires the same pair to be parallel and congruent.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reversing a property without proof. Start from the actual givens and use a proved converse.
  • Checking one pair only. The opposite-side and opposite-angle tests each require both pairs.
  • Matching the wrong diagonal parts. Diagonal bisection compares the two halves of each diagonal separately.
  • Combining different side pairs. The one-pair test needs one opposite-side pair that is both parallel and congruent.
  • Assuming the conclusion. Do not use parallelogram properties until a test or the definition establishes a parallelogram.

Key takeaways

  • Opposite sides: both opposite-side pairs congruent guarantee a parallelogram.
  • Opposite angles: both opposite-angle pairs congruent guarantee a parallelogram.
  • Diagonals: each diagonal bisecting the other guarantees a parallelogram.
  • One side pair: one opposite-side pair that is both parallel and congruent guarantees a parallelogram.
  • A counterexample must satisfy every given condition while failing the proposed conclusion.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid’s Elements presents a close relative of the one-pair test in Book I, Proposition 33. Euclid starts with two equal, parallel segments and joins their ends in matching directions. He then proves that the joining segments are parallel too.

The small phrase about matching directions matters. Connect the ends the other way and the joining segments cross. The wording keeps the intended shape out of that crossed case. In the proof, Euclid adds a diagonal, obtains an equal angle from the parallels, and compares two triangles, a route still used in geometry classrooms.

Read Proposition 33 in Thomas Heath’s 1908 edition.

Trapezoids and Kites

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

A pair of matching sides can suggest a fold, but which fold does it actually justify? In a trapezoid, parallel bases connect angle and midpoint arguments. In a kite, adjacent equal sides create congruent triangles. Following those reasons tells you which apparent symmetries survive when the drawing changes.

Learning goals

  • Identify bases and justify isosceles tests using the inclusive definitions
  • Prove and apply trapezoid midsegment relationships
  • Compare reflection and rotational symmetries across trapezoid cases
  • Derive kite angle and diagonal properties from congruent triangles
  • Distinguish the guaranteed properties of a kite from the rhombus special case

Choose the bases before using a rule

A trapezoid is a quadrilateral with at least one pair of opposite parallel sides. Choose a parallel pair as its bases; the other two sides are its legs. A parallelogram is therefore a trapezoid, and either opposite pair may be chosen as its bases. In this lesson every named quadrilateral is convex, with vertices in boundary order.

For example, if the chosen bases of ABCDABCDABCD are ABABAB and CDCDCD, the legs are ADADAD and BCBCBC. Calling them legs does not assert that they are nonparallel.

Relative to chosen parallel bases, an isosceles trapezoid has congruent angles at one base. A rectangle qualifies because both angles at any chosen base are right angles. In a parallelogram, consecutive angles are supplementary; they can be congruent only when both are right angles.

Worked example 1 Check the hypothesis on equal legs

Problem. An oblique parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD has chosen bases ABABAB and CDCDCD. Its angle at AAA is 60∘60^\circ60∘, and its angle at BBB is 120∘120^\circ120∘. Explain why its legs are congruent, and decide whether it is an isosceles trapezoid for these bases.

Solution. Opposite sides of a parallelogram are congruent, so its two legs ADADAD and BCBCBC are congruent. It is a trapezoid under our inclusive definition.

Its angles at base ABABAB are different. It is not an isosceles trapezoid. Equal legs alone are not a sufficient test when two opposite-side pairs may be parallel.

Check your understanding

Which extra hypothesis makes congruent legs a sufficient isosceles-trapezoid test?

Answer choices

With exactly one parallel pair, the equal-legs proof below applies. An oblique parallelogram shows why allowing a second parallel pair breaks the test.

Isosceles trapezoids come with a reflection

Equal angles at one base also force equal angles at the other. For instance, base angles of 68∘68^\circ68∘ leave 112∘112^\circ112∘ at each end of the other base: the angles along each leg are supplementary by parallel lines.

Why equal base angles give equal legs and diagonals#

Let ABABAB and CDCDCD be the chosen bases, with congruent angles at AAA and BBB. Reflect in the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB. This swaps AAA and BBB. A line parallel to ABABAB maps onto itself under this reflection, so the line CDCDCD stays in place.

The ray ADADAD maps to the ray from BBB that makes the same base angle, on the same side of ABABAB. That ray is BCBCBC. Their intersections with the line CDCDCD must therefore correspond: DDD maps to CCC.

The reflection pairs the legs ADADAD and BCBCBC, and also pairs the diagonals ACACAC and BDBDBD. A reflection preserves lengths, so each pair is congruent. It also proves that this perpendicular bisector is a reflection axis of the whole trapezoid.

∎

The equal-legs test has a restricted converse. Suppose a convex trapezoid has exactly one opposite parallel pair and congruent legs. Its bases cannot have equal length, because one opposite pair both parallel and congruent would make it a parallelogram. Name the longer base ABABAB.

Why equal legs work with exactly one parallel pair#

Through CCC, draw a parallel to ADADAD meeting ABABAB at EEE. The quadrilateral AECDAECDAECD is a parallelogram. Its opposite sides show that AEAEAE has length CDCDCD, so EEE is strictly between AAA and BBB because CDCDCD is shorter than ABABAB.

Its other opposite-side equality is

EC=AD.EC=AD.EC=AD.

The given congruent legs now make triangle EBCEBCEBC isosceles, with equal sides ECECEC and BCBCBC. Its base angles at EEE and BBB are congruent. Because ECECEC is parallel to ADADAD, its angle at EEE equals the trapezoid’s angle at AAA. Thus the two angles at base ABABAB are congruent.

∎

Worked example 2 Use angle and diagonal information

Problem. A convex isosceles trapezoid ABCDABCDABCD has chosen bases ABABAB and CDCDCD. The angle at AAA is 74∘74^\circ74∘. Its diagonals have lengths, in centimeters,

AC=4x+3BD=6x−11.\begin{gathered} AC=4x+3\\ BD=6x-11. \end{gathered}AC=4x+3BD=6x−11.​

Find the angles at B,C,DB,C,DB,C,D, the value of xxx, and each diagonal length.

Solution. The base angles at AAA and BBB are congruent. Each angle at the other base supplements one of them.

m∠B=74∘m∠C=106∘m∠D=106∘.\begin{gathered} m\angle B=74^\circ\\ m\angle C=106^\circ\\ m\angle D=106^\circ. \end{gathered}m∠B=74∘m∠C=106∘m∠D=106∘.​

The diagonals are congruent, so

4x+3=6x−1114=2xx=7.\begin{aligned} 4x+3&=6x-11\\ 14&=2x\\ x&=7. \end{aligned}4x+314x​=6x−11=2x=7.​

Substitution gives both requested lengths.

AC=31 cmBD=31 cm.\begin{gathered} AC=31\text{ cm}\\ BD=31\text{ cm}. \end{gathered}AC=31 cmBD=31 cm.​

Join the leg midpoints

The segment joining the midpoints of a trapezoid’s legs is its midsegment. It is parallel to the bases, and its length is the average of their lengths. The average comes from two triangle midsegments, one contributing half of each base.

In a triangle, a segment joining two side midpoints is parallel to the third side and half as long. That result follows from similarity; revisit Triangle Proportionality and Midsegments if either conclusion needs a refresher.

Why the trapezoid midsegment averages the bases#

First take bases of 121212 cm and 262626 cm. Draw diagonal ACACAC and let PPP be its midpoint. In triangle ADCADCADC, the segment from leg midpoint MMM to PPP is parallel to CDCDCD and has length 131313 cm. In triangle ABCABCABC, the segment from PPP to leg midpoint NNN is parallel to ABABAB and has length 666 cm. These are parts of the same straight segment, so the whole length is 191919 cm.

For any positive base lengths aaa and bbb, use the same construction. In a convex quadrilateral, MMM and NNN lie on opposite sides of diagonal ACACAC. The triangle theorem makes both MPMPMP and PNPNPN parallel to the bases. The unique parallel through PPP puts them on one straight line, with PPP between MMM and NNN.

MP=b2PN=a2MN=a+b2.\begin{gathered} MP=\frac b2\\ PN=\frac a2\\ MN=\frac{a+b}{2}. \end{gathered}MP=2b​PN=2a​MN=2a+b​.​

Nothing depended on the particular numbers. This proof also works for a parallelogram with chosen bases; it never required exactly one parallel pair.

∎

Worked example 3 Recover a base

Problem. A convex trapezoid has bases of 999 cm and bbb cm. The segment joining its leg midpoints is 161616 cm long. Find bbb, and state the segment’s direction relative to the bases.

Solution. A midsegment has the average base length.

16=9+b232=9+bb=23.\begin{aligned} 16&=\frac{9+b}{2}\\ 32&=9+b\\ b&=23. \end{aligned}1632b​=29+b​=9+b=23.​

The other base is 232323 cm long. The midsegment is parallel to both bases. Its length lies between 999 cm and 232323 cm, as an average should.

Check your understanding

A trapezoid has bases of 18 cm and 30 cm. What is its midsegment length?

Answer choices

The average is half of 48 cm, so the midsegment is 24 cm long. This is the average of the bases, not half their difference.

Compare the symmetry cases

A symmetry maps the whole figure onto itself. The identity, which leaves every point in place, counts as a rotational symmetry. Reflection axes are counted separately.

For an unequal-legged trapezoid with exactly one parallel pair, there is no reflection axis and the identity is its only rotational symmetry. A reflection would have to preserve the unique parallel pair. Because the bases have unequal lengths, it must fix each base and swap its endpoints, forcing the perpendicular bisector of both bases to be one axis. That would match the legs, contrary to their unequal lengths.

A matching rotation must also preserve the unique parallel pair. The unequal bases must each map to themselves. A nonidentity rotation fixing a segment is a half-turn about its midpoint, but the two bases have different midpoints. Thus only the identity can match the whole figure.

For a non-parallelogram isosceles trapezoid, that perpendicular axis exists by the reflection proof, and it is the only one. Its only rotational symmetry is the identity. A nonidentity rotation preserving a quadrilateral moves its vertices in a cycle; it can only be a half-turn or a quarter-turn. A half-turn would swap the unequal bases, and a quarter-turn would require two parallel pairs, so neither works.

For a nonsquare rectangle, there are two reflection axes through opposite side midpoints, and two rotational symmetries: the identity and a half-turn. A square has four reflection axes and four rotations, including the identity.

The inclusive definition also includes other parallelograms. A general oblique parallelogram with unequal adjacent sides has no reflection axis and has the identity and a half-turn. A nonsquare rhombus has two diagonal reflection axes and those same two rotations. These cases follow from Parallelograms and Rectangles, Rhombi, and Squares. An inclusive family need not have one symmetry count.

Check your understanding

Count rotational symmetries including the identity. Which statement is correct?

Answer choices

A nonsquare rectangle has its two midpoint axes and a half-turn as well as the identity. Rectangles and squares are isosceles trapezoids here, so the count of one axis needs the non-parallelogram condition.

A kite has a distinguished diagonal

A kite is a convex quadrilateral with two disjoint pairs of adjacent congruent sides, using each side once. For example, sides of 5,5,8,85,5,8,85,5,8,8 in boundary order can form a kite: pair the two 555s and pair the two 888s. Four equal sides qualify too, so rhombi and squares are kites.

Name the equal-side meeting vertices AAA and CCC:

AB=ADBC=CD.\begin{gathered} AB=AD\\ BC=CD. \end{gathered}AB=ADBC=CD.​

The diagonal ACACAC joins those meeting vertices. It bisects the angles at AAA and CCC, and it is the perpendicular bisector of BDBDBD. It also gives a reflection axis. The opposite angles at BBB and DDD are congruent.

The useful earlier fact is that SSS congruence matches corresponding triangle angles after three side pairs have been matched. The proof in Triangle Congruence explains why side evidence determines the matching.

Why the kite diagonal bisects and reflects#

Draw ACACAC. Triangles ABCABCABC and ADCADCADC have the two given matching side pairs and the common side ACACAC, so they are congruent by SSS. Their corresponding angles show that ACACAC bisects the vertex angles at AAA and CCC, and that the whole angles at BBB and DDD are congruent.

Let OOO be the diagonal intersection. Convexity puts it inside both diagonals. Triangles ABOABOABO and ADOADOADO have equal sides ABABAB and ADADAD, the common side AOAOAO, and congruent included angles because ACACAC bisects the angle at AAA. SAS makes these triangles congruent.

It follows that

BO=DO.BO=DO.BO=DO.

Their angles at OOO are congruent and form a linear pair, so each is a right angle. Thus ACACAC is the perpendicular bisector of BDBDBD. Reflection in ACACAC swaps BBB and DDD and fixes AAA and CCC, so it maps the kite onto itself.

∎

Worked example 4 Read the correct halves

Problem. A convex kite ABCDABCDABCD has equal adjacent pairs AB,ADAB,ADAB,AD and BC,CDBC,CDBC,CD. Its diagonals meet at OOO. The lengths AO,OC,BOAO,OC,BOAO,OC,BO are 999 cm, 444 cm, and 666 cm, respectively. Find DODODO and BDBDBD. Decide whether BDBDBD bisects ACACAC, and justify the decision.

Solution. The diagonal through the equal-side meeting vertices is ACACAC, so it bisects BDBDBD.

DO=6 cmBD=12 cm.\begin{gathered} DO=6\text{ cm}\\ BD=12\text{ cm}. \end{gathered}DO=6 cmBD=12 cm.​

The other diagonal BDBDBD does not bisect ACACAC: the given parts of ACACAC are 999 cm and 444 cm, which are unequal. Perpendicular diagonals do not imply mutual bisection.

Check your understanding

In a convex kite ABCD, the equal pairs meet at A and C. Which conclusion is guaranteed?

Answer choices

SSS across AC bisects the two vertex angles. SAS at the diagonal intersection then proves that AC bisects BD at right angles. The other direction of bisection needs extra information.

The rhombus special case

An ordinary kite need not have a half-turn: in Example 4, a half-turn would swap AAA and CCC, but their distances from BDBDBD are different. A kite that is not a rhombus has just the one diagonal reflection axis. Write its side lengths in boundary order as p,p,q,qp,p,q,qp,p,q,q, with ppp different from qqq. A half-turn or quarter-turn would match a side of length ppp to a side of length qqq, so neither is a symmetry. The vertex between the two sides of length ppp is unique, and so is the vertex between the two sides of length qqq. Any reflection must fix these two vertices individually, so its axis must be their joining line ACACAC.

Worked example 5 Add the missing midpoint

Problem. A convex kite ABCDABCDABCD has equal pairs meeting at AAA and CCC. Its diagonals meet at OOO. In addition, OOO is the midpoint of ACACAC. Prove that ABCDABCDABCD is a rhombus.

Solution. The kite proof already gives that OOO is the midpoint of BDBDBD. Both diagonals now bisect each other, so the test from Proving a Quadrilateral Is a Parallelogram makes ABCDABCDABCD a parallelogram. Its opposite sides are congruent, and its kite side pairs are adjacent and congruent. Together these make all four sides congruent, which is the rhombus condition.

In a rhombus, either diagonal can be chosen through the equal-pair meeting vertices, so both bisection directions hold. A nonsquare rhombus has two diagonal reflection axes and a half-turn; a square has four axes and quarter-turns as well. Those extra symmetries belong to the special cases, not to every kite.

Check your understanding

Which extra statement makes a convex kite a rhombus, when AC joins its equal-side meeting vertices?

Answer choices

The kite already supplies bisection of BD by AC. Bisection in the other direction gives the parallelogram test, and the adjacent equal sides then make a rhombus.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Definitions: A trapezoid needs at least one parallel pair here; rectangles remain included.
  • Equal legs: The sufficient test needs exactly one opposite parallel pair. An oblique parallelogram defeats the unrestricted version.
  • Midsegments: Average the base lengths, rather than the legs or the difference of the bases.
  • Kite diagonals: Name the diagonal through the equal-side meeting vertices before claiming bisection.
  • Special cases: Perpendicular diagonals do not by themselves give mutual bisection or a rhombus.

Key takeaways

  • Isosceles trapezoids: Congruent base angles give congruent legs, congruent diagonals, and a reflection across the common perpendicular bisector of the bases.
  • Trapezoid midsegments: Two triangle midsegments prove parallelism and the average-base length, including parallelograms.
  • Symmetry: Count the identity among rotations, and state the shape case before giving a count.
  • Kites: The diagonal through the equal-side meeting vertices bisects the other diagonal perpendicularly and bisects those two vertex angles.
  • Rhombi: A kite with mutual diagonal bisection is a rhombus; every kite need not have that extra property.

A bit of history (optional)

The names of quadrilaterals have not always divided them into the same families. In Book I of the Elements, Euclid describes squares, oblongs, rhombi, and rhomboids, then groups the remaining quadrilaterals as trapezia. That leaves a different collection from the trapezoids in this lesson. David Joyce’s edition points out another wrinkle: Euclid uses parallelograms throughout his work without defining the name in that list. The definitions and the later arguments have to be read together to see how the figures are being used. Euclid’s Definition 22 and Joyce’s commentary preserve this glimpse of a classification system still different from ours.

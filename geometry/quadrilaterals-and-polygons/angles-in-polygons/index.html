Polygon Angles and Tessellations

▾

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

Four square corners meet neatly at a point. Three regular pentagon corners leave a gap, and a fourth reaches too far. The corner angles decide whether pieces can meet, while a repeating construction decides whether the pieces can cover a whole plane.

Learning goals

  • Derive the interior angle sum of a convex polygon by triangulation
  • Explain why one exterior turn per vertex totals a full turn
  • Calculate regular polygon angles and check whether a proposed side count is possible
  • Compare local angle fits with repeating tilings that cover the plane

Triangles inside a polygon

A polygon’s interior angle sum can be found by counting triangles. For a convex hexagon, draw diagonals from one vertex to the three vertices that are not its neighbors. Four triangles fill the hexagon, so their angle sums give 720∘720^\circ720∘.

In a convex polygon, every interior angle is less than a straight angle, and a segment joining two points in or on it stays in or on it. A regular polygon has equal sides and equal interior angles; return to Classifying Polygons for those definitions and their examples.

Each triangle has angle sum 180∘180^\circ180∘, as proved in Angles in a Triangle. At each hexagon vertex, the triangle angle or angle pieces fill exactly the polygon’s interior angle, so adding the four triangle sums counts every interior angle once.

Interior angle sum of a convex polygon#

For a convex pentagon, diagonals from one vertex make three triangles. Their angles fill the five interior angles without adding an angle at a new interior point.

S=3×180∘S=3\times180^\circS=3×180∘S=540∘S=540^\circS=540∘

Now take any convex polygon with nnn sides, where nnn is a whole number at least 333. Choose a vertex AAA. Join AAA to each nonadjacent vertex. Convexity keeps each diagonal inside the polygon. The diagonals meet only at AAA, so they do not cross each other and they divide the polygon without gaps or overlaps.

Every side except the two sides ending at AAA is the opposite side of one triangle. There are n−2n-2n−2 such sides, so there are n−2n-2n−2 triangles. Their angles fill the polygon’s interior angles exactly, as in the pentagon. Nothing in this count depended on having five sides.

S=(n−2)180∘S=(n-2)180^\circS=(n−2)180∘

∎

This proof uses convexity. In a concave polygon, a diagonal from an arbitrarily chosen vertex can pass outside the polygon, so that fan construction cannot be assumed to work. All angle-sum calculations below concern convex polygons.

Worked example 1 Count triangles before angles

Problem. A convex octagon has eight sides. How many triangles are formed by drawing every diagonal from one vertex? Find its interior angle sum.

Solution. The two sides touching the chosen vertex are excluded from the opposite-side count. The remaining six sides give six triangles.

S=6×180∘S=6\times180^\circS=6×180∘S=1080∘S=1080^\circS=1080∘

The total does not require the octagon to be regular.

Exterior angles are turns

Walking around a square requires four quarter-turns, totaling 360∘360^\circ360∘. At each corner, continue the incoming side straight ahead before turning toward the next side. The angle between that forward extension and the outgoing side is the exterior turn we use.

The exterior turn and the interior angle form a linear pair. If their measures are eee and iii, then

i+e=180∘.i+e=180^\circ.i+e=180∘.

For a convex polygon, all these turns go in the same direction. Choose one traversal direction and one exterior turn at every vertex, including the starting vertex when you return. Do not add both exterior angles that could be drawn beside an interior angle.

Exterior turns of a convex polygon#

A convex hexagon has six linear pairs. Together they total 1080∘1080^\circ1080∘. Its interior angles use 720∘720^\circ720∘ of that amount, leaving

E=1080∘−720∘E=1080^\circ-720^\circE=1080∘−720∘E=360∘.E=360^\circ.E=360∘.

For a convex nnn-gon, all nnn linear pairs total 180n180n180n degrees. Subtract the interior sum just proved:

E=180n∘−180(n−2)∘E=180n∘−180n∘+360∘E=360∘.\begin{aligned} E&=180n^\circ-180(n-2)^\circ\\ E&=180n^\circ-180n^\circ+360^\circ\\ E&=360^\circ. \end{aligned}EEE​=180n∘−180(n−2)∘=180n∘−180n∘+360∘=360∘.​

This is one complete turn, independent of the number of sides. The square’s quarter-turns were one instance; the subtraction works for every convex polygon.

∎

Regular polygons and possible side counts

In a convex regular decagon, ten equal turns share a full turn. Each exterior turn is 36∘36^\circ36∘, and each interior angle is 144∘144^\circ144∘. Equal interior angles have equal supplements, so the exterior turns are equal too.

For a convex regular nnn-gon, let eee be one exterior turn and iii one interior angle. Sharing the full turn and then taking a supplement gives

e=360∘n.e=\frac{360^\circ}{n}.e=n360∘​. i=180∘−360∘n.i=180^\circ-\frac{360^\circ}{n}.i=180∘−n360∘​.

Dividing the total interior sum equally gives the same interior angle:

i=(n−2)180∘n.i=\frac{(n-2)180^\circ}{n}.i=n(n−2)180∘​.

Problem. Find one exterior turn and one interior angle of a convex regular 151515-gon.

Solution. Divide the full turn among the fifteen vertices.

e=360∘15e=\frac{360^\circ}{15}e=15360∘​e=24∘e=24^\circe=24∘

Take the supplement to find the interior angle.

i=180∘−24∘i=180^\circ-24^\circi=180∘−24∘i=156∘i=156^\circi=156∘

A proposed regular polygon angle must give a whole number of sides at least three. For instance, an exterior turn of 40∘40^\circ40∘ gives nine sides, but 50∘50^\circ50∘ gives 7.27.27.2 sides, which cannot describe a polygon. Rounding a side count would change the angle.

From corners to a whole tiling

A tessellation, or tiling, covers the whole plane with no gaps and no overlapping interiors. Here a regular tessellation uses congruent copies of one convex regular polygon, meeting whole side to whole side. A small patch illustrates the repeating rule; the rule must allow continuation beyond the patch.

Four squares fit at a vertex because four right angles total 360∘360^\circ360∘. Rows and columns of squares also demonstrate a way to continue that fit throughout the plane.

If kkk congruent regular polygon corners meet at a tiling vertex, their angles must fill a full turn:

ki=360∘.ki=360^\circ.ki=360∘.

Here kkk must be a whole number at least three. A total below 360∘360^\circ360∘ leaves a gap, while a total above it forces overlap. This angle condition is necessary at a vertex; a vertex fit alone does not demonstrate a whole tiling.

Worked example 5 Test the corners

Problem. Each interior angle of a regular pentagon is 108∘108^\circ108∘. Compare three and four congruent regular pentagons meeting at one vertex. Find the gap or excess angle in each case. Can regular pentagons form a regular tessellation as defined here?

Solution. Three corners leave a gap.

3×108∘=324∘3\times108^\circ=324^\circ3×108∘=324∘360∘−324∘=36∘360^\circ-324^\circ=36^\circ360∘−324∘=36∘

Four corners require overlap.

4×108∘=432∘4\times108^\circ=432^\circ4×108∘=432∘432∘−360∘=72∘432^\circ-360^\circ=72^\circ432∘−360∘=72∘

No integer number of pentagon corners totals 360∘360^\circ360∘: three are too few and four are already too many. A regular tessellation by these pentagons is impossible.

The angle test leaves only three candidates. An equilateral triangle has a 60∘60^\circ60∘ interior angle, a square has 90∘90^\circ90∘, and a regular hexagon has 120∘120^\circ120∘. They require six, four, and three corners respectively.

To see that there are no other regular candidates, the interior-angle formula gives at least 60∘60^\circ60∘ per corner. Thus the integer kkk lies from three through six. Substitute each possibility into the angle condition and solve for the side count:

Corners at a vertexRequired interior angleRequired sides

These remaining candidates really do tile. Squares use the grid above. Equilateral triangles fit in alternating orientations along a straight row; a matching row joins along all its exposed sides, and the same arrangement repeats.

Regular hexagons fit in staggered columns. Along each column, horizontal sides match; neighboring columns match along the slanted sides. Repeating the same column placement fills each adjacent strip, with three 120∘120^\circ120∘ corners at every vertex.

The argument has two jobs: angle sums rule out impossible candidates, and these repeating constructions establish the three possible regular tessellations. Mixed tiles need their own angle and continuation checks.

This passes the local angle test. A repeating placement must still match edges and cover the plane to demonstrate a whole tiling.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Counting sides as triangles. A fan in a convex polygon has two fewer triangles than sides.
  • Mixing the two sums. Interior sums depend on the side count; one exterior turn per vertex of a convex polygon always totals a full turn.
  • Dividing unequal angles equally. An angle sum alone does not make a polygon equiangular.
  • Rounding a side count. A noninteger result means the proposed regular polygon angle is impossible.
  • Stopping at one vertex. A local fit is necessary, but a whole tiling also needs matching edges and continuation.

Key takeaways

  • Convex polygon interior sum: count n−2n-2n−2 triangles, each contributing 180∘180^\circ180∘.
  • Exterior turns: choose exactly one per vertex in a consistent traversal; their total is 360∘360^\circ360∘.
  • Regular polygon angles: share the full turn equally, then take the supplement for an interior angle.
  • Check that a proposed side count is a whole number at least three.
  • A regular tiling needs an exact 360∘360^\circ360∘ vertex fit and a construction that continues across the plane.

A bit of history (optional)

Johannes Kepler is remembered for studying planetary motion, but his search for order also led him to patterns of polygons. His 1619 book, Harmonices Mundi, brought geometry, music, and astronomy into one ambitious work.

The book did not keep flat patterns separate from questions about the heavens. Kepler investigated arrangements of regular polygons as part of the same search for harmony. The Mathematical Association of America’s account describes his systematic study of tessellations alongside his work on polyhedra and planetary motion. A digitized copy at the Library of Congress preserves the diagrams and the original pages.

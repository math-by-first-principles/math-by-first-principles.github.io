Parallelograms

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A frame made from two pairs of parallel bars can lean without becoming a rectangle. Its changing outline raises a useful question: which relationships follow from the parallel bars themselves? A diagonal lets us answer with triangles whose exact match we can prove.

Learning goals

  • Identify the parallel-side hypotheses in a parallelogram
  • Prove opposite-side and angle relationships using parallel lines and congruence
  • Calculate missing lengths and angles from parallelogram properties
  • Justify diagonal bisection without assuming a symmetry
  • Compare half-turn and reflection symmetry in general and special parallelograms

Start with the definition

A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Throughout this lesson, vertices are named in boundary order and the quadrilaterals are convex, with four distinct corners.

For parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD, the hypotheses are

AB∥CD,BC∥AD.\begin{gathered} AB\parallel CD,\\ BC\parallel AD. \end{gathered}AB∥CD,BC∥AD.​

A tilted drawing supplies neither of these facts on its own. A rectangle, rhombus, and square are special parallelograms, so the properties proved here apply to them too. Under our inclusive convention, every parallelogram is also a trapezoid because a trapezoid needs at least one parallel opposite-side pair.

Alternate interior angles made by a transversal across parallel lines are congruent. ASA proves two triangles congruent from two corresponding angles and their included side; use Parallel Lines and Transversals or SAS and ASA Congruence for a refresher.

Check your understanding

Which information is the definition's starting point for a parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD?

Answer choices

The definition requires both parallel opposite-side pairs. Equal diagonals, four right angles, and four equal sides are extra conditions that a general parallelogram need not have.

A diagonal reveals opposite sides and angles

Opposite sides of a parallelogram have matching lengths. For example, if two neighboring sides are 777 cm and 444 cm, the sides opposite them are 777 cm and 444 cm too. The reason is the matching triangles formed by a diagonal, rather than the apparent balance of the sketch.

Opposite sides and angles of a parallelogram#

Let ABCDABCDABCD be any parallelogram and draw ACACAC. Using ACACAC as a transversal of the two given parallel pairs gives

∠BAC≅∠DCA,∠BCA≅∠DAC.\begin{gathered} \angle BAC\cong\angle DCA,\\ \angle BCA\cong\angle DAC. \end{gathered}∠BAC≅∠DCA,∠BCA≅∠DAC.​

The included side ACACAC is common to both triangles. ASA therefore gives the ordered correspondence

△ABC≅△CDA.\triangle ABC\cong\triangle CDA.△ABC≅△CDA.

Corresponding sides and angles of these congruent triangles give

AB=CD,BC=DA,∠ABC≅∠CDA.\begin{gathered} AB=CD,\\ BC=DA,\\ \angle ABC\cong\angle CDA. \end{gathered}AB=CD,BC=DA,∠ABC≅∠CDA.​

The other two whole angles consist of the two matched angle pieces at each end of ACACAC. Adding their measures gives

m∠DAB=m∠DAC+m∠CAB,m∠BCD=m∠BCA+m∠ACD,m∠DAB=m∠BCD.\begin{aligned} m\angle DAB&=m\angle DAC+m\angle CAB,\\ m\angle BCD&=m\angle BCA+m\angle ACD,\\ m\angle DAB&=m\angle BCD. \end{aligned}m∠DABm∠BCDm∠DAB​=m∠DAC+m∠CAB,=m∠BCA+m∠ACD,=m∠BCD.​

No side length or particular angle size was needed: both opposite-side pairs and both opposite-angle pairs match in every parallelogram.

∎

Worked example 1 Use the correct opposite side

Problem. ABCDABCDABCD is a parallelogram. The lengths in centimeters satisfy

AB=3x+2,CD=23,BC=9.\begin{gathered} AB=3x+2,\\ CD=23,\\ BC=9. \end{gathered}AB=3x+2,CD=23,BC=9.​

Find xxx and the length of ADADAD.

Solution. Opposite sides ABABAB and CDCDCD are congruent, so

3x+2=23,3x=21,x=7.\begin{aligned} 3x+2&=23,\\ 3x&=21,\\ x&=7. \end{aligned}3x+23xx​=23,=21,=7.​

The side opposite BCBCBC is ADADAD, so ADADAD is 999 cm. The two neighboring sides need not match each other.

Check your understanding

In parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD, diagonal ACACAC is drawn. Which reason proves that ∠BAC\angle BAC∠BAC and ∠DCA\angle DCA∠DCA are congruent before triangle congruence is known?

Answer choices

The given parallel sides are ABABAB and CDCDCD, and ACACAC crosses both. The angle pair is alternate interior, so its congruence comes from the parallel lines, not from the property being proved.

Consecutive angles share a straight-angle total

Consecutive angles are angles at neighboring vertices. In a parallelogram they are supplementary, meaning their measures add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. For instance, a 72∘72^\circ72∘ corner has 108∘108^\circ108∘ neighbors: those are same-side interior angles across parallel lines.

In the diagram above, ABABAB is a transversal of the parallel lines ADADAD and BCBCBC. The same-side interior-angle theorem gives

m∠DAB+m∠ABC=180∘.m\angle DAB+m\angle ABC=180^\circ.m∠DAB+m∠ABC=180∘.

Taking each side in turn as the transversal proves this for every consecutive pair. Together with opposite-angle congruence, one corner therefore determines the other three.

Worked example 2 Choose the angle relationship first

Problem. ABCDABCDABCD is a parallelogram with

m∠A=(2x+14)∘,m∠B=(5x+40)∘.\begin{gathered} m\angle A=(2x+14)^\circ,\\ m\angle B=(5x+40)^\circ. \end{gathered}m∠A=(2x+14)∘,m∠B=(5x+40)∘.​

Find xxx and the measures of angles AAA and CCC.

Solution. Angles AAA and BBB are consecutive, so add their measures rather than equating them.

(2x+14)+(5x+40)=180,7x+54=180,7x=126,x=18.\begin{aligned} (2x+14)+(5x+40)&=180,\\ 7x+54&=180,\\ 7x&=126,\\ x&=18. \end{aligned}(2x+14)+(5x+40)7x+547xx​=180,=180,=126,=18.​

Substitution gives

m∠A=2(18)+14,m∠A=50∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle A&=2(18)+14,\\ m\angle A&=50^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠Am∠A​=2(18)+14,=50∘.​

Angle CCC is opposite AAA, so its measure is also 50∘50^\circ50∘. Angle BBB is 130∘130^\circ130∘, which checks the straight-angle total.

Worked example 3 A diagonal splits one corner

Problem. In parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD, diagonal ACACAC lies inside angle DABDABDAB. Its two parts measure

m∠BAC=29∘,m∠CAD=38∘.\begin{gathered} m\angle BAC=29^\circ,\\ m\angle CAD=38^\circ. \end{gathered}m∠BAC=29∘,m∠CAD=38∘.​

Find the whole angles DABDABDAB and BCDBCDBCD, and then angle ABCABCABC.

Solution. The diagonal divides the whole angle into two parts, which need not be equal.

m∠DAB=29∘+38∘,m∠DAB=67∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle DAB&=29^\circ+38^\circ,\\ m\angle DAB&=67^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠DABm∠DAB​=29∘+38∘,=67∘.​

The opposite whole angle matches it.

m∠BCD=67∘.m\angle BCD=67^\circ.m∠BCD=67∘.

The consecutive angle is supplementary to it.

m∠ABC=180∘−67∘,m∠ABC=113∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle ABC&=180^\circ-67^\circ,\\ m\angle ABC&=113^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠ABCm∠ABC​=180∘−67∘,=113∘.​

One angle of a parallelogram is 118∘118^\circ118∘. What is the measure of either neighboring angle?

A neighboring angle is supplementary to the given angle.

180∘−118∘=62∘.180^\circ-118^\circ=62^\circ.180∘−118∘=62∘.

The opposite angle, rather than a neighboring one, measures 118∘118^\circ118∘.

Each diagonal cuts the other in half

To bisect a segment means to divide it into two equal lengths. If diagonals of a parallelogram meet at EEE, a 141414 cm diagonal has two 777 cm parts, while a 101010 cm diagonal has two 555 cm parts. Bisection compares parts of the same diagonal; it does not make the two diagonals equal.

Diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other#

Let the diagonals of parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD intersect at EEE. The intersection is inside the convex quadrilateral. We already proved that opposite sides have equal lengths, so

AB=CD.AB=CD.AB=CD.

Since ABABAB is parallel to CDCDCD, the two diagonals give alternate interior-angle pairs:

∠BAE≅∠DCE,∠ABE≅∠CDE.\begin{gathered} \angle BAE\cong\angle DCE,\\ \angle ABE\cong\angle CDE. \end{gathered}∠BAE≅∠DCE,∠ABE≅∠CDE.​

The equal sides lie between these equal angles, so ASA gives

△ABE≅△CDE.\triangle ABE\cong\triangle CDE.△ABE≅△CDE.

Corresponding parts now give

AE=CE,BE=DE.\begin{gathered} AE=CE,\\ BE=DE. \end{gathered}AE=CE,BE=DE.​

Thus EEE is the midpoint of each diagonal. This proof used parallel sides and the proved opposite-side property, without assuming a symmetry or assuming that the crossing was a midpoint.

∎

Worked example 4 Keep the two diagonals separate

Problem. The diagonals of parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD meet at EEE. Lengths in centimeters satisfy

AE=2x+1,EC=5x−11,BE=6.\begin{gathered} AE=2x+1,\\ EC=5x-11,\\ BE=6. \end{gathered}AE=2x+1,EC=5x−11,BE=6.​

Find xxx and the full lengths ACACAC and BDBDBD.

Solution. The two parts of ACACAC have equal lengths.

2x+1=5x−11,12=3x,x=4.\begin{aligned} 2x+1&=5x-11,\\ 12&=3x,\\ x&=4. \end{aligned}2x+112x​=5x−11,=3x,=4.​

Each part of ACACAC is 999 cm. Each part of BDBDBD is 666 cm.

AC=18 cm,BD=12 cm.\begin{gathered} AC=18\text{ cm},\\ BD=12\text{ cm}. \end{gathered}AC=18 cm,BD=12 cm.​

The unequal full lengths are consistent with both diagonals being bisected.

Check your understanding

In parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD, diagonals meet at EEE. Which pair of conclusions follows from diagonal bisection?

Answer choices

The congruence proof matches AEAEAE with CECECE, and BEBEBE with DEDEDE. It does not match a piece of one diagonal with a piece of the other, and it establishes no right angle.

Choose sides, angles, or diagonals. Predict which measurements will agree, then move D. The construction keeps opposite sides parallel; the written proofs explain the observations.

Turning works, while a fold can fail

A half-turn is a rotation through 180∘180^\circ180∘. It sends a point to the opposite side of its center, along the same line and at the same distance. See Rotations for the point-by-point definition.

Let OOO be the intersection of the diagonals of a parallelogram. For a concrete instance, if OAOAOA and OCOCOC are both 888 cm on opposite rays, a half-turn sends AAA to CCC; it similarly exchanges the endpoints of a diagonal with 555 cm halves. The bisection proof supplies exactly those equal distances for every parallelogram.

The half-turn symmetry of a parallelogram#

Diagonal bisection says that OOO is the midpoint of ACACAC and of BDBDBD. A half-turn about OOO therefore exchanges AAA with CCC and BBB with DDD. A rotation sends a segment to the segment joining its endpoint images, so it exchanges each side with its opposite side. The entire boundary and its interior match the original parallelogram.

∎

A half-turn does not imply a reflection. An oblique parallelogram has no right angles; a general oblique member with unequal adjacent sides has no reflection axes.

Here is why no alternative axis rescues that general member. A reflection of a convex quadrilateral must preserve its four corners and their boundary adjacency. If it fixes a corner, its axis passes through that corner. It cannot fix both noncollinear incident sides individually, so it must swap them, forcing adjacent side lengths to be equal. That is excluded here.

If no corner is fixed and some corner is exchanged with a neighbor, those consecutive angles must be equal. Since they are supplementary, each would be 90∘90^\circ90∘, also excluded. The only remaining vertex exchange pairs both opposite vertices; its axis would have to be perpendicular to both crossing diagonals, which is impossible. These cases rule out every reflection for an oblique parallelogram with unequal adjacent sides.

Rectangles and rhombi can have additional reflection symmetries; a square has four reflection axes. Their full properties and proofs belong to the later lesson on special parallelograms. The word “general” matters: the no-reflection conclusion does not apply to all special members.

Worked example 5 Compare two proposed motions

Problem. A parallelogram has a 65∘65^\circ65∘ corner and unequal adjacent sides of 666 cm and 101010 cm. Its diagonals meet at OOO.

Case A: Does the half-turn about OOO map it onto itself? Explain, and count its rotational symmetries, including the identity.

Case B: Can reflection in a diagonal map it onto itself? Explain.

Solution. Case A: Yes. Diagonal bisection makes OOO the midpoint of both diagonals, so the half-turn exchanges opposite vertices. Every symmetry preserves the diagonal intersection, so a nonidentity rotational symmetry has center OOO. It must send a 65∘65^\circ65∘ corner to the opposite 65∘65^\circ65∘ corner: the neighboring corners are 115∘115^\circ115∘, and a nonidentity rotation fixes no corner. The opposite corners lie on opposite rays from OOO, which forces a half-turn. With the identity included, there are 222 rotational symmetries.

Case B: No. Reflection in either diagonal would fix its endpoints and exchange the two sides incident at each endpoint. Those incident lengths are 666 cm and 101010 cm, so they cannot match.

Check your understanding

Which symmetry statement is true for every parallelogram, including special members?

Answer choices

Diagonal bisection proves the half-turn for every parallelogram. Reflection axes and quarter-turns require extra conditions that a general oblique parallelogram lacks; special members can have more symmetry.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Starting with a conclusion. To prove opposite sides congruent, begin with the given parallel pairs and prove triangle congruence first.
  • Confusing neighbors with opposites. Opposite angles are congruent; consecutive angles have a 180∘180^\circ180∘ sum.
  • Mixing the diagonals. Bisection matches the two pieces of each diagonal, not every segment at the crossing.
  • Assuming extra symmetry. A half-turn does not supply a reflection axis, equal diagonals, or perpendicular diagonals.

Key takeaways

  • A parallelogram starts with both pairs of opposite sides parallel.
  • Parallel-line angle facts and triangle congruence prove opposite-side and opposite-angle congruence.
  • Consecutive angles are supplementary.
  • The diagonals bisect each other, which proves the half-turn about their intersection.
  • A general oblique parallelogram with unequal adjacent sides has no reflection axes; special members may have more symmetry.

A bit of history (optional)

Opening an older geometry book can make a familiar diagram look strange for an unexpected reason: its letters may run in a different order. In the 1855 edition of Euclid edited by Dionysius Lardner, Book I, Proposition 34 labels the same parallel-sided shape differently from the boundary-order convention used here. A reader who follows the letters without tracing the sides can mistake a diagonal for an edge.

The proof is easier to recognize than the lettering. It draws a diagonal, uses alternate angles from parallel lines, and matches the two triangles by their angles and shared side. Later corollaries in that edition gather further facts about the diagonals. Comparing the actual steps reveals the connection between an old page and a modern proof even when the labels initially hide it. The digitized 1855 proposition preserves both the argument and its unfamiliar names.

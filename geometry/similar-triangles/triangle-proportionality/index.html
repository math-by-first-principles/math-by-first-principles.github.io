Triangle Proportionality and Midsegments

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Slide a segment across a triangle while keeping it parallel to the base. The two side points move together: halfway down one side means halfway down the other. The same idea lets a compass divide a segment into equal parts even when an unmarked straightedge cannot measure those parts.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios in a parallel triangle cut
  • Prove parallelism from proportional interior divisions using the side-splitter converse
  • Apply both conclusions of the midsegment theorem and match the central triangle correctly
  • Compare corresponding intervals where parallel lines cut two transversals
  • Construct equal parts and supplied ratios with compass and unmarked straightedge

A parallel cut gives two useful ratios

A parallel cut puts the side points at the same fraction of their whole sides. Suppose

AD=8AB=28AE=6AC=21.\begin{aligned}AD&=8\\AB&=28\\AE&=6\\AC&=21.\end{aligned}ADABAEAC​=8=28=6=21.​

The whole-side fractions both equal 2/72/72/7. The leftover lengths are

DB=20EC=15.\begin{aligned}DB&=20\\EC&=15.\end{aligned}DBEC​=20=15.​

Therefore, the part-to-part ratios both equal 2/52/52/5. These are different ratios describing the same division.

The side-splitter theorem, also called the triangle proportionality theorem, states the following. In a nondegenerate triangle ABCABCABC, let DDD lie between A,BA,BA,B and EEE between A,CA,CA,C. If DE∥BCDE\parallel BCDE∥BC, then

ADDB=AEEC.\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}.DBAD​=ECAE​.

The corresponding part-to-whole equality is AD/AB=AE/ACAD/AB=AE/ACAD/AB=AE/AC. Name the denominator before choosing which version to use.

Why a parallel cut divides the sides proportionally#

In the numerical example, the shared whole-side fraction 2/72/72/7 leaves 5/75/75/7 on each side. Dividing the used fraction by the leftover fraction gives 2/52/52/5, whether the whole side has length 282828 or 212121.

In general, DE∥BCDE\parallel BCDE∥BC gives ∠ADE≅∠ABC\angle ADE\cong\angle ABC∠ADE≅∠ABC by corresponding angles. The triangles also share the angle at AAA, so AA similarity gives △ADE∼△ABC\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC△ADE∼△ABC.

Let kkk be their common small-to-whole fraction. Interior points ensure 0<k<10<k<10<k<1.

AD=k AB,AE=k AC,DB=(1−k)AB,EC=(1−k)AC.\begin{aligned}AD&=k\,AB,\\AE&=k\,AC,\\DB&=(1-k)AB,\\EC&=(1-k)AC.\end{aligned}ADAEDBEC​=kAB,=kAC,=(1−k)AB,=(1−k)AC.​

Dividing each first part by its leftover part gives the same ratio.

ADDB=k1−k,AEEC=k1−k.\begin{aligned}\frac{AD}{DB}&=\frac{k}{1-k},\\\frac{AE}{EC}&=\frac{k}{1-k}.\end{aligned}DBAD​ECAE​​=1−kk​,=1−kk​.​

∎

Reverse the statement to prove parallelism

Equal interior division ratios can prove that a segment is parallel to the base. The side-splitter converse uses the same nondegenerate triangle and interior point conditions: if AD/DB=AE/ECAD/DB=AE/ECAD/DB=AE/EC, then DE∥BCDE\parallel BCDE∥BC.

Why proportional interior divisions force a parallel#

First take

AD=5DB=8AC=26.\begin{aligned}AD&=5\\DB&=8\\AC&=26.\end{aligned}ADDBAC​=5=8=26.​

Dilate from AAA by

k=AD/AB,=5/13.\begin{aligned}k&=AD/AB,\\&=5/13.\end{aligned}k​=AD/AB,=5/13.​

This sends BBB to DDD and CCC to a point E0E_0E0​ strictly inside ACACAC, because 0<k<10<k<10<k<1. The accepted line-image property sends BCBCBC to the unique parallel through DDD, so DE0∥BCDE_0\parallel BCDE0​∥BC. Now the direct theorem gives AE0:E0C=5:8AE_0:E_0C=5:8AE0​:E0​C=5:8, so

AE0=10E0C=16.\begin{aligned}AE_0&=10\\E_0C&=16.\end{aligned}AE0​E0​C​=10=16.​

A given interior point EEE dividing that same side into 101010 and 161616 must be this point E0E_0E0​.

For the general argument, dilate from AAA by k=AD/ABk=AD/ABk=AD/AB, which lies strictly between 0 and 1. The image of BBB is DDD, and the image E0E_0E0​ of CCC lies strictly inside segment ACACAC. Since AAA is not on BCBCBC, the accepted line-image property makes its image the unique parallel to BCBCBC through DDD. Thus DE0∥BCDE_0\parallel BCDE0​∥BC, and the direct theorem gives E0E_0E0​ the same division ratio as the given EEE. An interior division of one fixed segment with one given positive ratio is unique. To see this, let its whole length be L>0L>0L>0 and suppose first-part lengths x,yx,yx,y lie strictly between 000 and LLL.

xL−x=yL−y,x(L−y)=y(L−x),xL=yL,x=y.\begin{aligned}\frac{x}{L-x}&=\frac{y}{L-y},\\x(L-y)&=y(L-x),\\xL&=yL,\\x&=y.\end{aligned}L−xx​x(L−y)xLx​=L−yy​,=y(L−x),=yL,=y.​

Equal distances from AAA on the same ray identify the same point. Therefore E0=EE_0=EE0​=E, and the given segment DEDEDE is the constructed parallel.

Worked example 2 Test the converse in two cases

Problem. In each case, ABCABCABC is nondegenerate, DDD lies between A,BA,BA,B, and EEE between A,CA,CA,C. Decide whether DEDEDE is parallel to BCBCBC.

Case (a):

AD=7DB=9AE=10.5EC=13.5.\begin{aligned}AD&=7\\DB&=9\\AE&=10.5\\EC&=13.5.\end{aligned}ADDBAEEC​=7=9=10.5=13.5.​

Case (b):

AD=7DB=9AE=10.5EC=12.\begin{aligned}AD&=7\\DB&=9\\AE&=10.5\\EC&=12.\end{aligned}ADDBAEEC​=7=9=10.5=12.​

Solution. In case (a), the part-to-part ratios agree.

10.513.5=79.\frac{10.5}{13.5}=\frac79.13.510.5​=97​.

The converse proves parallelism.

In case (b), the right-side ratio is 7/87/87/8, while the left-side ratio is 7/97/97/9. They differ. The direct theorem says a parallel would force equal ratios, so DEDEDE is not parallel to BCBCBC.

Check your understanding

Why does the converse proof construct an actual parallel through D?

Answer choices

The direct theorem gives the constructed intersection the required ratio. Uniqueness on the same side then identifies that intersection with the given point E. This establishes the desired parallelism without assuming it.

Midpoints produce a midsegment triangle

A midsegment joins the midpoints of two sides of a triangle. It has two conclusions: it is parallel to the third side and has half that side’s length.

Why a midsegment is parallel and half as long#

Take a triangle with sides

AB=16AC=22BC=26.\begin{aligned}AB&=16\\AC&=22\\BC&=26.\end{aligned}ABACBC​=16=22=26.​

Its midpoint divisions give

AD=DB,=8AE=EC,=11.\begin{aligned}AD&=DB,\\&=8\\AE&=EC,\\&=11.\end{aligned}ADAE​=DB,=8=EC,=11.​

Both part-to-part ratios are 111, so the converse proves DE∥BCDE\parallel BCDE∥BC. AA similarity then gives the half-size fraction, so DE=13DE=13DE=13.

For any nondegenerate triangle, midpoints D,ED,ED,E make both part-to-part ratios equal to 111. The converse makes DEDEDE parallel to BCBCBC. The resulting similarity has AD/AB=1/2AD/AB=1/2AD/AB=1/2, so

DE=12 BC.DE=\frac12\,BC.DE=21​BC.

Also, dilation from AAA by 1/21/21/2 sends BBB to its midpoint and CCC to an interior point of ACACAC. Its line image is the parallel through that midpoint. The direct theorem then gives a 1:11:11:1 division on ACACAC, so the intersection is its midpoint.

∎

Worked example 3 Match the central triangle in the correct order

Problem. A triangle has

AB=14AC=18BC=24.\begin{aligned}AB&=14\\AC&=18\\BC&=24.\end{aligned}ABACBC​=14=18=24.​

Let D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F be the midpoints of AB,AC,BCAB,AC,BCAB,AC,BC, respectively. Find the three sides of DEFDEFDEF, write its correspondence with the outer triangle, and give the factor from the outer triangle to the central one.

Solution. Apply the midsegment theorem to each pair of midpoints.

DE=12BC,=12,EF=12AB,=7,FD=12AC,=9.\begin{aligned}DE&=\frac12 BC,\\&=12,\\EF&=\frac12 AB,\\&=7,\\FD&=\frac12 AC,\\&=9.\end{aligned}DEEFFD​=21​BC,=12,=21​AB,=7,=21​AC,=9.​

Thus DEDEDE matches CBCBCB, EFEFEF matches BABABA, and FDFDFD matches ACACAC. Their endpoints give DDD to CCC, EEE to BBB, and FFF to AAA. The ordered statement is

△DEF∼△CBA.\triangle DEF\sim\triangle CBA.△DEF∼△CBA.

The factor from the outer triangle CBACBACBA to DEFDEFDEF is 1/21/21/2; the reverse factor is 222. Writing DEFDEFDEF to ABCABCABC would mismatch these unequal sides.

Parallel cross-lines and two transversals

Three distinct parallel lines meet two distinct transversals at A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C and P,Q,RP,Q,RP,Q,R, in the same cross-line order. The corresponding intercepted intervals satisfy

ABBC=PQQR.\frac{AB}{BC}=\frac{PQ}{QR}.BCAB​=QRPQ​.

Why parallel cross-lines give proportional intervals#

For intersecting transversals, first imagine their intersection OOO outside the three cross-lines. Distances OA,OB,OCOA,OB,OCOA,OB,OC of 3,11,173, 11, 173,11,17 and matching distances OP,OQ,OROP,OQ,OROP,OQ,OR of 4.5,16.5,25.54.5, 16.5, 25.54.5,16.5,25.5 all have factor 1.51.51.5. Subtracting gives intervals 8,68, 68,6 on the first transversal and 12,912, 912,9 on the second, still with that factor.

In general, extend the transversals to their intersection OOO. Cross-section triangles have matching angles from the parallel lines and a shared or vertical angle at OOO. AA gives one common factor for the distances from OOO on the two transversals. Subtract endpoint distances for intervals on one side of OOO; add them when an interval crosses OOO. Both operations preserve the common factor, so corresponding intercepted intervals are proportional. A cross-line through OOO has zero endpoint distances and need not be used to form a triangle.

For parallel transversals, suppose the first strip intercepts 666 units and the next 999 on the left. A diagonal in each strip creates two triangles with two equal angle pairs from the parallels and a shared included side. ASA congruence forces right-hand intercepts of 666 and 999 as well.

If the transversals are parallel, there is no intersection OOO. Consider one strip between two cross-lines, labeled A,PA,PA,P on the first and B,QB,QB,Q on the second. Draw AQAQAQ. The parallels are

AB∥PQAP∥BQ.\begin{aligned}AB\parallel PQ\\AP\parallel BQ.\end{aligned}AB∥PQAP∥BQ.​

Therefore, alternate interior angles give

∠BAQ≅∠PQA,∠BQA≅∠PAQ.\begin{aligned}\angle BAQ&\cong\angle PQA,\\\angle BQA&\cong\angle PAQ.\end{aligned}∠BAQ∠BQA​≅∠PQA,≅∠PAQ.​

The included side AQAQAQ is shared. Thus △ABQ≅△QPA\triangle ABQ\cong\triangle QPA△ABQ≅△QPA by ASA, giving AB=PQAB=PQAB=PQ.

Apply the same argument in the next strip to obtain BC=QRBC=QRBC=QR. Both corresponding interval factors are 111, so the ratio equality holds for parallel transversals too.

∎

Divide a segment with compass and straightedge

Equal compass steps on an auxiliary ray can transfer equal fractions to any given segment. The compass fixes the step length; it does not measure a quotient with a ruler. Use the earlier segment and angle-copy constructions and the parallel-line construction to draw the required parallels.

For a 2:32:32:3 division, mark five equal compass steps on an auxiliary ray, join the last point to BBB, and construct the parallel through the second step. The first two of the five auxiliary steps become 2/52/52/5 of ABABAB, leaving 3/53/53/5 on the other side.

The figure opens at its completed stage so the construction remains meaningful on paper. Step back to see the auxiliary ray, equal compass openings, last-point join, angle copy, and intersection added in order. To obtain a positive integer ratio p:qp:qp:q, use p+qp+qp+q equal steps and draw the parallel through step ppp.

To divide ABABAB into nnn equal parts, draw a ray from AAA not on line ABABAB. Mark nnn consecutive equal compass steps P1,…,PnP_1,\ldots,P_nP1​,…,Pn​, join PnBP_nBPn​B, and construct parallels through the earlier step points. In triangle APnBAP_nBAPn​B, the point at step iii is i/ni/ni/n of the auxiliary whole, so its intersection with ABABAB is i/ni/ni/n of that whole too. Consecutive intersections therefore give equal parts.

Worked example 5 Construct four equal parts without a marked ruler

Problem. Using compass and unmarked straightedge, divide a given segment ABABAB into four equal parts. Describe the construction and explain the ratio made by the third division point.

Solution. Draw an auxiliary ray from AAA away from line ABABAB. Keep one compass opening to mark four equal steps ending at P4P_4P4​. Join P4P_4P4​ to BBB. Copy the appropriate angle to construct parallels through P1,P2,P3P_1,P_2,P_3P1​,P2​,P3​, meeting ABABAB at X1,X2,X3X_1,X_2,X_3X1​,X2​,X3​.

The side-splitter theorem in triangle AP4BAP_4BAP4​B gives the whole-side fractions 1/41/41/4, 2/42/42/4, and 3/43/43/4. Their differences are all 1/41/41/4, so the four consecutive parts of ABABAB are equal. The third point has AX3:X3B=3:1AX_3:X_3B=3:1AX3​:X3​B=3:1, not 3:43:43:4.

A supplied ratio can also come from two given positive segments, rather than an integer step count. Copy the first given length aaa as AUAUAU and the second bbb as UVUVUV, consecutively on an auxiliary ray. Join VBVBVB and construct the parallel through UUU, meeting ABABAB at XXX.

The side-splitter theorem gives

AX/XB=AU/UV,=a/b.\begin{aligned}AX/XB&=AU/UV,\\&=a/b.\end{aligned}AX/XB​=AU/UV,=a/b.​

This constructs the ratio of the supplied segments; it does not assume that every numerical real length can be constructed.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Mixing parts and wholes. AD/DBAD/DBAD/DB and AD/ABAD/ABAD/AB have different denominators and different values.
  • Forgetting interior points. The ordinary side-splitter statements here require the points to lie between the named endpoints.
  • Keeping only half the midsegment theorem. A midsegment is both parallel to the third side and half its length.
  • Reordering corresponding intervals. Compare the same cross-line gaps in the same order on both transversals.
  • Measuring instead of constructing. Equal compass openings and constructed parallels establish exact divisions with an unmarked straightedge.

Key takeaways

  • A parallel triangle cut gives equal part-to-part ratios and equal part-to-whole fractions.
  • Equal interior division ratios force a parallel through the side-splitter converse.
  • A midsegment is parallel to the third side and half as long; the central correspondence is DEFDEFDEF to CBACBACBA.
  • Parallel cross-lines cut proportional corresponding intervals, including when the transversals are parallel.
  • Auxiliary rays, copied lengths, and constructed parallels transfer exact divisions to a given segment.

A bit of history (optional)

One construction problem can have more than one good arrangement. Euclid’s Book VI, Proposition 9 divides a segment using equal steps on an auxiliary ray. A later commentator, al-Nayrizi, working around the year 900, described a different arrangement: he placed equal steps on perpendiculars at opposite ends of the segment and joined selected points across the diagram.

The guide to the translated proposition preserves that alternative alongside Euclid’s. A commentator could transmit an old text and still offer a new way to carry out its task. The finished division was the same; the organization of the compass-and-straightedge work changed.

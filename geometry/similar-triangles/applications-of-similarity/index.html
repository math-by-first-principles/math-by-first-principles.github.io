Applications of Similarity

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

You can reach the end of a shadow or place a tape on your own riverbank even when you cannot reach a treetop or the opposite shore. A smaller triangle can carry the missing measurement, provided you can explain why its angles match the larger triangle’s angles.

Learning goals

  • Justify shadow and mirror triangle correspondence from explicit geometric and physical assumptions
  • Determine inaccessible distances using established sight lines and angle relationships
  • Convert units before applying map and drawing scale factors in either direction
  • Derive perimeter scaling by adding corresponding scaled side lengths
  • Evaluate model assumptions and report measurements with appropriate precision

Begin with the model, then the proportion

A physical situation does not become a pair of similar triangles just because a sketch looks convincing. Identify the straight segments, establish two corresponding angle pairs, and name the matched lengths. Then AA similarity supplies the proportion. Finally check its direction, units, and whether the reported precision fits the measurements.

If a required assumption is not established, the proposed answer is not guaranteed. That does not mean every possible realization is impossible or that the numerical answer must always be wrong. It means the reasoning has not yet justified it.

Shadows compare vertical heights with their own shadows

Use vertical objects on level ground, measured at the same time, with sunrays treated as parallel. Each object makes a right triangle: its vertical height, its own shadow from base to tip, and the ray from its top to that tip. The base angles at the shadow tips agree because the ground is level and the sunrays are parallel. Together with the right angles at the bases, this establishes AA.

Worked example 1 Measure a tree using a calibration pole

Problem. A vertical calibration pole is 1.501.501.50 m tall and casts its own 1.201.201.20 m shadow. At the same time, a vertical tree casts a 16.816.816.8 m shadow on the same level ground. Treat the sunrays as parallel. Estimate the tree’s height to the nearest tenth of a meter.

Solution. The base right angles match and the parallel rays give matching tip angles. Thus △PQR∼△TUV\triangle PQR\sim\triangle TUV△PQR∼△TUV, with PPP to TTT, QQQ to UUU, and RRR to VVV. Height corresponds to height, and shadow to shadow.

H16.8=1.501.20,H=16.8⋅1.25,=21.0 m.\begin{aligned}\frac{H}{16.8}&=\frac{1.50}{1.20},\\H&=16.8\cdot1.25,\\&=21.0\text{ m}.\end{aligned}16.8H​H​=1.201.50​,=16.8⋅1.25,=21.0 m.​

The estimated tree height is approximately 21.021.021.0 m. The tree’s shadow starts at the tree base, not at the calibration pole. A sloping surface or a pole that leans would require a different justification.

Check your understanding

Which facts justify AA for the usual simultaneous shadow model?

Answer choices

The two base angles are right angles. Parallel sunrays meeting level ground make equal corresponding acute angles at the shadow tips, providing the second pair.

A mirror compares ground legs and eye height

Place a small horizontal mirror MMM on level ground between the observer’s foot FFF and the object’s base BBB. Let EEE be the observer’s eye, vertically above FFF, and TTT the object’s top, vertically above BBB. Align the sight path TTT to MMM to EEE in one vertical plane.

Use the physical reflection assumption: incoming and reflected rays make equal angles with the mirror’s perpendicular. Their acute angles with the horizontal mirror are therefore equal as well. Geometry does not establish this physical law; the model supplies it. Along with the right angles at F,BF,BF,B, it gives △EFM∼△TBM\triangle EFM\sim\triangle TBM△EFM∼△TBM by AA.

Worked example 2 Use eye height rather than total person height

Problem. Under that horizontal-mirror and equal-angle reflection model on level ground, an observer sees a vertical tower’s top in mirror MMM. The eye height and the two ground legs are

EF=1.62 mFM=2.7 mMB=17.6 m.\begin{aligned}EF&=1.62\text{ m}\\FM&=2.7\text{ m}\\MB&=17.6\text{ m}.\end{aligned}EFFMMB​=1.62 m=2.7 m=17.6 m.​

The mirror lies between F,BF,BF,B. Estimate the tower height TBTBTB to the nearest tenth of a meter.

Solution. The reflection assumption gives the equal acute angles at MMM; vertical heights on level ground give right angles at F,BF,BF,B. AA matches EEE with TTT, FFF with BBB, and MMM with itself. Therefore eye height matches tower height, and the two ground legs match.

TB1.62=17.62.7,TB=10.56 m,TB≈10.6 m.\begin{aligned}\frac{TB}{1.62}&=\frac{17.6}{2.7},\\TB&=10.56\text{ m},\\TB&\approx10.6\text{ m}.\end{aligned}1.62TB​TBTB​=2.717.6​,=10.56 m,≈10.6 m.​

The estimated tower height is approximately 10.610.610.6 m. The hundredths digit rounds the calculated 10.5610.5610.56 to 10.610.610.6 at the requested tenth. The approximation sign reflects a measurement-model estimate rather than an exact physical height. Use the eye’s height above the ground, not the observer’s total height, and do not replace FMFMFM by slanted EMEMEM or whole FBFBFB.

Check your understanding

In the mirror model, which proportion correctly pairs the ground and vertical legs?

Answer choices

Object height TB matches eye height EF, and object-to-mirror ground distance MB matches observer-to-mirror ground distance FM. Both ratios compare the object triangle with the observer triangle.

Check your understanding

The mirror calculation gives 10.56 m, and the task requests the nearest tenth. Which report is appropriate?

Answer choices

The hundredths digit 6 rounds the tenths digit upward, giving approximately 10.6 m. Keeping the approximation language does not turn measured model inputs into an exact physical height.

Survey an inaccessible perpendicular distance

In the top view below, A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C lie on one straight accessible bank, with BBB between A,CA,CA,C. Surveying has established AT⊥ACAT\perp ACAT⊥AC. Here, TTT is the target across the river. Through accessible CCC, construct CD⊥ACCD\perp ACCD⊥AC on dry land. Choose DDD where that line meets the sight line through T,BT,BT,B, extended past BBB.

The order on that sight line is T,B,DT,B,DT,B,D. The right angles at A,CA,CA,C match, and the angles at BBB are vertical angles, so

△TAB∼△DCB.\triangle TAB\sim\triangle DCB.△TAB∼△DCB.

The correspondence is TTT to DDD, AAA to CCC, and BBB to BBB. Thus inaccessible ATATAT matches accessible DCDCDC, while bank interval ABABAB matches bank interval CBCBCB.

Worked example 3 Keep every measured segment on dry land

Problem. In a top-view river survey, A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C are collinear with BBB between A,CA,CA,C, and T,B,DT,B,DT,B,D are collinear with BBB between T,DT,DT,D. Surveying establishes

AT⊥ACCD⊥AC.\begin{aligned}AT\perp AC\\CD\perp AC.\end{aligned}AT⊥ACCD⊥AC.​

Segment CDCDCD is on dry land. The measured lengths are

AB=42.0 mBC=15.0 mCD=11.0 m.\begin{aligned}AB&=42.0\text{ m}\\BC&=15.0\text{ m}\\CD&=11.0\text{ m}.\end{aligned}ABBCCD​=42.0 m=15.0 m=11.0 m.​

Estimate the perpendicular river distance ATATAT to the nearest tenth of a meter. Identify why TBTBTB is a different distance.

Solution. AA was established before setting up a ratio. Match the perpendicular segments and the bank segments in the same order.

AT11.0=42.015.0,AT=30.8 m.\begin{aligned}\frac{AT}{11.0}&=\frac{42.0}{15.0},\\AT&=30.8\text{ m}.\end{aligned}11.0AT​AT​=15.042.0​,=30.8 m.​

The estimate is approximately 30.830.830.8 m across the river along ATATAT. Segment TBTBTB is a slanted sight line, not the perpendicular crossing. The construction never requires measuring ATATAT or walking to TTT.

Maps require common units and a named direction

A scale 1:32001:32001:3200 means drawing length divided by real length is 1/32001/32001/3200 when both use the same unit. The reciprocal ratio, real length divided by drawing length, is 320032003200. Writing centimeters over meters without conversion does not give that dimensionless factor.

Worked example 4 Convert a map length into a ground distance

Problem. A map uses one uniform scale 1:32001:32001:3200. A straight path measures 7.47.47.4 cm on the map. Find its real length in meters, and state the drawing-to-real and real-to-drawing factors.

Solution. The real length is 320032003200 times the drawing length when both are in centimeters.

Lreal=7.4⋅3200,=23680 cm,Lreal=236.8 m.\begin{aligned}L_{\rm real}&=7.4\cdot3200,\\&=23680\text{ cm},\\L_{\rm real}&=236.8\text{ m}.\end{aligned}Lreal​Lreal​​=7.4⋅3200,=23680 cm,=236.8 m.​

The drawing-to-real factor is 320032003200; the reverse real-to-drawing factor is 1/32001/32001/3200. Name the starting figure and target figure when choosing which reciprocal to use.

Check your understanding

A 1:5000 map shows a segment of 3 cm. Which equation uses common units correctly?

Answer choices

The dimensionless scale multiplies centimeters to give centimeters. Convert the resulting 15000 cm into meters afterward, giving 150 m.

If a printer stretches the horizontal and vertical directions by different factors, the print has no single similarity scale. One correct width comparison cannot establish a scale for every route and boundary edge.

Perimeter scales by adding the scaled sides

Why one length factor also scales the perimeter#

For a triangular boundary with sides 5,7,95, 7, 95,7,9 cm, the perimeter is 212121 cm. An image with length factor 2.42.42.4 has sides 12,16.8,21.612, 16.8, 21.612,16.8,21.6 cm. Their sum is 50.450.450.4 cm, which is 2.4⋅212.4\cdot 212.4⋅21.

In general, for original side lengths a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c and a positive scale factor kkk from the original to the image,

Pimage=ka+kb+kc,=k(a+b+c),=kPoriginal.\begin{aligned}P_{\rm image}&=ka+kb+kc,\\&=k(a+b+c),\\&=kP_{\rm original}.\end{aligned}Pimage​​=ka+kb+kc,=k(a+b+c),=kPoriginal​.​

The same addition works for any matching polygon boundary: add all corresponding sides once. This argument concerns lengths around the boundary.

Worked example 5 Use the boundary length on a scale plan

Problem. A triangular garden plan has boundary side lengths 5.05.05.0, 6.56.56.5, and 8.08.08.0 cm. The uniform scale is 1:5001:5001:500. Find the planned real perimeter in meters.

Solution. Add the drawing sides, then apply the common length factor to their sum.

Pdrawing=5.0+6.5+8.0,=19.5 cm,Preal=500⋅19.5,=9750 cm,Preal=97.5 m.\begin{aligned}P_{\rm drawing}&=5.0+6.5+8.0,\\&=19.5\text{ cm},\\P_{\rm real}&=500\cdot19.5,\\&=9750\text{ cm},\\P_{\rm real}&=97.5\text{ m}.\end{aligned}Pdrawing​Preal​Preal​​=5.0+6.5+8.0,=19.5 cm,=500⋅19.5,=9750 cm,=97.5 m.​

The three real sides would be 25.025.025.0, 32.532.532.5, and 40.040.040.0 m; their sum confirms 97.597.597.5 m. This is the complete boundary length, not a single direct distance across the garden.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Assuming similarity from appearance. Establish angle pairs from the stated model first.
  • Measuring the wrong shadow. Each shadow runs from its object’s own base to its own tip.
  • Mixing mirror lengths. Eye height matches object height; ground legs match ground legs.
  • Mixing units or directions. Convert units before forming a dimensionless scale ratio, then state its direction.
  • Treating a missing assumption as an impossibility. An unsupported model result is not guaranteed; further evidence or a revised setup is needed.

Key takeaways

  • Shadow AA depends on vertical objects, level ground, simultaneous measurements and parallel sunrays.
  • Mirror AA also needs a horizontal mirror and the physical equal-angle reflection assumption.
  • A dry-land survey can infer an inaccessible distance after collinearities and angle pairs are established.
  • A uniform map scale applies to every corresponding length after unit conversion.
  • Perimeter uses the same length factor because it is the sum of corresponding boundary sides.

A bit of history (optional)

Later writers credited Thales with measuring pyramids from shadows. Diogenes Laertius, writing centuries after Thales, reports an account in which the measurement is taken when an object’s shadow equals its height. Plutarch describes a comparison using a stick and its shadow.

The MacTutor biography by J. J. O’Connor and E. F. Robertson presents these differing accounts and explains the uncertainty of evidence about Thales. No writing of his survives. The shadow reasoning can be checked mathematically without treating a late story as a contemporary eyewitness record.

The Angle-Bisector Ratio Theorem

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

An angle bisector makes two equal angles. Does that force it to cut the opposite side in half? Move the top vertex of a triangle and watch the answer change. The angle halves remain equal, while the two side parts follow the lengths of the sides next to the angle.

Learning goals

  • Match opposite-side parts with the adjacent sides in a consistent endpoint order
  • Prove the internal angle-bisector ratio theorem using an auxiliary parallel
  • Calculate missing lengths while distinguishing a whole side from its parts
  • Apply the converse using positive interior side divisions
  • Distinguish equal angle halves from equal side parts and perpendicular distances

Match each part with its neighboring side

In a nondegenerate triangle ABCABCABC, the internal angle bisector from AAA meets BCBCBC at a point DDD strictly between BBB and CCC. It satisfies ∠BAD≅∠DAC\angle BAD\cong\angle DAC∠BAD≅∠DAC.

For a numerical picture, take adjacent sides 10 and 15 and an opposite side 20. A drawing of the actual internal angle bisector gives opposite-side parts of about 8 and 12. Their ratio 8:12 matches 10:15, even though the equal angle halves have not cut the opposite side into equal parts.

The angle-bisector ratio theorem states that

BDDC=ABAC.\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{AB}{AC}.DCBD​=ACAB​.

The part next to BBB pairs with side ABABAB, and the part next to CCC pairs with side ACACAC. Reversing both ratios is valid; reversing only one changes the claim.

Try a symmetric triangle, then move the apex sideways. The displayed decimals are approximate measurements of the drawing. They suggest an exact relationship that the parallel construction below proves.

Worked example 1 Use the part beside B with side AB

Problem. In triangle ABCABCABC, ADADAD is the internal bisector of ∠BAC\angle BAC∠BAC. Suppose

Find DCDCDC.

Solution. The two side ratios have the same endpoint order.

4.8DC=915,=35,DC=4.8⋅53,=8 cm.\begin{aligned}\frac{4.8}{DC}&=\frac9{15},\\&=\frac35,\\DC&=4.8\cdot\frac53,\\&=8\text{ cm}.\end{aligned}DC4.8​DC​=159​,=53​,=4.8⋅35​,=8 cm.​

The longer adjacent side ACACAC goes with the longer opposite-side part DCDCDC. The two angle halves are equal even though 4.84.84.8 and 888 are unequal.

A parallel turns equal angles into equal lengths

The two small triangles ABDABDABD and ACDACDACD are not automatically similar. Equal angles at AAA give just one corresponding angle pair. Add a parallel to create the relationship we can justify.

Why an internal bisector gives the side ratio#

First consider an actual internal bisector in a triangle with

AB=11AC=17BC=21.\begin{aligned}AB&=11\\AC&=17\\BC&=21.\end{aligned}ABACBC​=11=17=21.​

Through CCC, construct a parallel to ADADAD that meets BABABA extended beyond AAA at EEE. The parallel angle pairs make ∠AEC\angle AEC∠AEC and ∠ACE\angle ACE∠ACE equal to the two angle halves at AAA. Hence triangle AECAECAEC has equal angles at E,CE,CE,C, so

AE=AC,=17\begin{aligned}AE&=AC,\\&=17\end{aligned}AE​=AC,=17​

by the earlier isosceles converse.

In triangle BECBECBEC, the parallel cut ADADAD now gives

BD:DC=BA:AE,=11:17.\begin{aligned}BD:DC&=BA:AE,\\&=11:17.\end{aligned}BD:DC​=BA:AE,=11:17.​

The whole base of length 212121 consequently divides into 8.258.258.25 and 12.7512.7512.75. We started with the true bisector; we did not choose a point with those lengths and assume it bisected the angle.

Now use general letters. Draw the parallel through CCC to line ADADAD, meeting line BABABA at EEE. The order is B,A,EB,A,EB,A,E. To justify it, BBB and CCC lie on opposite sides of line ADADAD, since DDD is interior to BCBCBC. The parallel through CCC cannot cross ADADAD, so its intersection with line BABABA lies beyond AAA on the opposite side from BBB. This reasoning works for acute and obtuse triangles.

The parallel lines give the following two angle equalities.

∠AEC≅∠BAD,∠ACE≅∠CAD.\begin{aligned}\angle AEC&\cong\angle BAD,\\\angle ACE&\cong\angle CAD.\end{aligned}∠AEC∠ACE​≅∠BAD,≅∠CAD.​

The first uses corresponding angles along transversal BAEBAEBAE; the second uses alternate interior angles along ACACAC. The bisector makes ∠BAD≅∠CAD\angle BAD\cong\angle CAD∠BAD≅∠CAD, so the angles at EEE and CCC in triangle AECAECAEC are equal. Their opposite sides are equal: AE=ACAE=ACAE=AC.

In triangle BECBECBEC, point AAA is interior to BEBEBE, point DDD is interior to BCBCBC, and AD∥ECAD\parallel ECAD∥EC. Apply the side-splitter theorem, already proved using AA similarity.

BAAE=BDDC.\frac{BA}{AE}=\frac{BD}{DC}.AEBA​=DCBD​.

Substitute

AE=ACBA=AB\begin{aligned}AE&=AC\\BA&=AB\end{aligned}AEBA​=AC=AB​

to obtain BD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/AC, as required.

∎

Use the whole side when the parts are unknown

If AB:AC=m:nAB:AC=m:nAB:AC=m:n, then BD:DC=m:nBD:DC=m:nBD:DC=m:n. The whole side BCBCBC contains m+nm+nm+n ratio parts. In particular,

BDBC=ABAB+AC.\frac{BD}{BC}=\frac{AB}{AB+AC}.BCBD​=AB+ACAB​.

This is a part-to-whole fraction. It is different from BD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/AC.

The converse identifies an angle bisector

The converse starts with a nondegenerate triangle and a point DDD strictly between B,CB,CB,C. If BD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/AC, then ADADAD internally bisects ∠BAC\angle BAC∠BAC.

Why the matching interior ratio identifies the bisector#

For a numerical example, let

AB=18AC=30BC=28.\begin{aligned}AB&=18\\AC&=30\\BC&=28.\end{aligned}ABACBC​=18=30=28.​

Suppose the given interior point has

BD=10.5DC=17.5.\begin{aligned}BD&=10.5\\DC&=17.5.\end{aligned}BDDC​=10.5=17.5.​

Construct the actual internal bisector, meeting BCBCBC at D0D_0D0​. The direct theorem makes its parts have ratio 18:30=3:518:30=3:518:30=3:5, so they too must be 10.510.510.5 and 17.517.517.5. Thus DDD is the true bisector intersection D0D_0D0​.

In general, the actual bisector meets BCBCBC at an interior point D0D_0D0​ and the direct theorem gives BD0/D0C=AB/ACBD_0/D_0C=AB/ACBD0​/D0​C=AB/AC. Both DDD and D0D_0D0​ divide the same side with the same positive ratio.

Keep the theorem’s meaning precise

Halving the angle does not usually halve the side. If AB=ACAB=ACAB=AC, the theorem gives BD/DC=1BD/DC=1BD/DC=1, so DDD is the midpoint. Conversely, if an internal bisector meets the midpoint, then BD/DC=1BD/DC=1BD/DC=1 gives AB=ACAB=ACAB=AC. Equal opposite-side parts occur precisely in this isosceles case.

The ratio AB/ACAB/ACAB/AC compares two sides of one triangle. It is not an image-to-preimage dilation scale factor, and it does not prove ABDABDABD similar to ACDACDACD. For example, if AB≠ACAB\ne ACAB=AC, the proposed matching AAA to AAA, BBB to CCC, DDD to DDD would compare unequal AB,ACAB,ACAB,AC but compare shared ADADAD with itself. Those ratios cannot share one scale factor.

This theorem compares lengths along the opposite side with the adjacent side lengths. It does not compare perpendicular distances from a point to the two lines forming the angle. No perpendicular-distance property is needed for this proof or its converse.

Check your understanding

Which lengths does the angle-bisector ratio theorem compare?

Answer choices

It compares lengths along the opposite side with lengths of the two adjacent sides. Perpendicular distances belong to a different theorem and are not premises here.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reversing only one ratio. Pair BDBDBD with ABABAB, and DCDCDC with ACACAC.
  • Replacing a part by the whole. DCDCDC and BCBCBC are different lengths.
  • Halving the side automatically. Equal angles force equal side parts only when AB=ACAB=ACAB=AC.
  • Assuming the small triangles are similar. A single equal-angle pair is insufficient.
  • Forgetting the interior hypothesis. The converse here requires DDD strictly between BBB and CCC.

Key takeaways

  • An internal bisector gives BD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/ACBD/DC=AB/AC, with the neighboring endpoints matched.
  • An auxiliary parallel creates AE=ACAE=ACAE=AC, allowing the side-splitter theorem to supply the ratio.
  • Whole-side calculations use BD/BC=AB/(AB+AC)BD/BC=AB/(AB+AC)BD/BC=AB/(AB+AC).
  • The same positive interior division ratio identifies the actual bisector by the converse.
  • Equal angle halves, equal side parts and perpendicular distances are different statements.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid’s Book VI, Proposition 3 places the ratio statement and its converse together. The proposition first starts with a bisected angle and proves proportional side parts, then starts with proportional parts and proves a bisected angle.

The translated proposition also uses an auxiliary parallel. Its two directions need separate arguments: reversing the words of a true statement does not automatically produce another true statement. The pairing in the old text reflects two proof obligations, not a shortcut around the second one.

The Triangle Inequality

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Three rods do not always make a triangular frame. If one rod is too long, the other two cannot reach across it, even when stretched straight. If they only just reach, the frame lies flat. The triangle inequality tells you how much room the lengths leave for a corner, and why a detour cannot shorten a direct route.

Learning goals

  • Prove why two sides of a triangle together exceed its third side
  • Classify positive side triples, distinguishing triangles from flat boundary arrangements
  • Determine strict third-side ranges, including restrictions on variable lengths
  • Compare direct and broken paths using triangle and polygon inequalities

When three sides can close

Two sides of a triangle together are longer than the third side. For lengths 555, 666, and 888, the two shorter rods have a combined length of 111111, leaving room to bend while their free ends still reach the endpoints of the 888-unit base.

Now keep the rods of lengths 555 and 666, but use a base of length 111111. The rods meet only when laid along the base. This is a degenerate triangle, a flat boundary arrangement with collinear vertices. An ordinary, nondegenerate triangle has three noncollinear vertices and positive side lengths.

With a base of length 121212, even the fully stretched rods leave a gap of 111. Bending them cannot make them reach farther.

The triangle inequality says that in every nondegenerate triangle, the sum of any two side lengths is strictly greater than the third. Here ABABAB names the length of segment ABABAB. For triangle ABCABCABC:

AB+AC>BC.AB+AC>BC.AB+AC>BC.

Also,

AB+BC>AC.AB+BC>AC.AB+BC>AC.

And,

AC+BC>AB.AC+BC>AB.AC+BC>AB.

Why the inequality is strict

In the previous lesson, a larger angle in one triangle faced a longer side. Equal sides also gave equal opposite angles in an isosceles triangle.

We can turn a sum of two sides into one straight segment by extending a side. That gives a comparison inside one larger triangle.

Two sides together exceed the third side#

First follow a numerical instance. Extend BABABA beyond AAA to DDD, copying ACACAC onto ADADAD, and join DDD to CCC. In an instance where angle ACDACDACD is 35∘35^\circ35∘ and angle BCABCABCA is 50∘50^\circ50∘, the equal sides ADADAD and ACACAC make angle ADCADCADC measure 35∘35^\circ35∘ too.

The whole angle at CCC is

m∠BCD=50∘+35∘.m\angle BCD=50^\circ+35^\circ.m∠BCD=50∘+35∘.

Thus it measures 85∘85^\circ85∘, larger than the 35∘35^\circ35∘ angle at DDD. In triangle BCDBCDBCD, side BDBDBD is therefore longer than BCBCBC. But BDBDBD consists of BABABA followed by a copy of ACACAC, giving the desired sum.

Now take any nondegenerate triangle ABCABCABC. On ray BABABA beyond AAA, choose DDD so that

AD=AC.AD=AC.AD=AC.

The order is B,A,DB,A,DB,A,D, and CCC is off their line. Join DCDCDC.

Triangle ACDACDACD is isosceles, so

m∠ACD=m∠ADC.m\angle ACD=m\angle ADC.m∠ACD=m∠ADC.

Ray DADADA coincides with ray DBDBDB, so angle ADCADCADC is angle BDCBDCBDC. Also, ray CACACA lies inside angle BCDBCDBCD: the segment from vertex CCC to the interior point AAA of side BDBDBD splits that angle. The other part, angle BCABCABCA, is positive because ABCABCABC is nondegenerate, so

m∠BCD>m∠ACD.m\angle BCD>m\angle ACD.m∠BCD>m∠ACD.

Therefore angle BCDBCDBCD is greater than angle BDCBDCBDC. Apply the larger-angle result in triangle BCDBCDBCD:

BD>BC.BD>BC.BD>BC.

Segment addition and the copied length give

BD=BA+AD.BD=BA+AD.BD=BA+AD.

Hence,

BA+AC>BC.BA+AC>BC.BA+AC>BC.

Nothing depended on the numbers 353535 and 505050. The strict step used a positive angle at CCC, which every nondegenerate triangle has. Relabeling the vertices proves the other two side-sum inequalities.

∎

Check your understanding

In the extension proof, B, A, and D lie in that order, and AD is a copy of AC. Which fact makes angle BCD strictly larger than angle BDC?

Answer choices

The equal sides AC and AD give equal base angles ACD and ADC. Ray DA is ray DB, so ADC is also BDC.

The whole angle BCD contains ACD plus the positive angle BCA. This proves the angle comparison before we compare BD and BC.

Test a proposed set of lengths

Positive lengths make a triangle exactly when each pair adds to more than the remaining length. To see why lengths passing the test can close, join the two shorter rods at a movable joint. We accept the geometric continuity fact that as the joint turns, the distance between the free ends takes every value from their difference when folded back to their sum when straight. Every distance strictly between those endpoints occurs with a bend. Because both rods are positive, their difference is strictly less than the longer rod. The longest requested length is at least as long as that rod, and passing the longest-side test puts it below their sum. It therefore lies strictly between the endpoints and can be fitted between the free ends.

For a numerical test, first identify a longest length. You need check only whether the other two add to more than that length. The other sums automatically exceed a smaller length because every length is positive.

Worked example 1 Distinguish a triangle, a flat boundary, and a gap

Problem. Classify each proposed side triple as a nondegenerate triangle, a flat boundary arrangement, or an impossible assembly. All lengths are in the same unit.

Case A: 555, 999, 121212.

Case B: 444, 666, 101010.

Case C: 333, 777, 121212.

Solution. Case A: Compare the sum of the two shorter lengths with the longest:

5+9>12.5+9>12.5+9>12.

The lengths make a nondegenerate triangle.

Case B: The comparison is equality:

4+6=10.4+6=10.4+6=10.

The pieces can only meet flat, so they do not make an ordinary triangle.

Case C: The pieces are too short even when stretched straight:

3+7<12.3+7<12.3+7<12.

They cannot close.

The positivity condition matters. A zero or negative proposed side fails before any triangle test. Also, checking an arbitrary pair can miss the problem: lengths 222, 555, and 999 satisfy the comparison of 5+95+95+9 with 222, but the two shorter lengths cannot span 999.

Find the whole third-side range

The third-side range lists every possible length for a third side when the other two are fixed. With sides 777 and 121212, the third side must be less than their sum, 191919. It must also be greater than their difference, 555, so the 777-unit side and the proposed third side together exceed the 121212-unit side.

Worked example 2 Keep both strict bounds

Problem. Two sides of a triangle measure 777 centimeters and 121212 centimeters. Find the range of the third side xxx, and list the possible integer values.

Solution. The sum comparison gives the upper bound:

x<7+12.x<7+12.x<7+12.

The inequality involving the longest fixed side gives the lower bound:

7+x>12.7+x>12.7+x>12.

The remaining comparison, x+12>7x+12>7x+12>7, holds automatically for every positive xxx.

Together these give

5<x<19.5<x<19.5<x<19.

The integer lengths are 666, 777, 888, 999, 101010, 111111, 121212, 131313, 141414, 151515, 161616, 171717, 181818 centimeters. Neither endpoint belongs: 555 or 191919 would make a flat arrangement.

For any positive fixed lengths aaa and bbb, the same three comparisons reduce to

∣a−b∣<x<a+b.|a-b|<x<a+b.∣a−b∣<x<a+b.

The absolute value means the larger fixed length minus the smaller. If the fixed lengths match, the lower bound is zero, still excluded because a side must be positive.

Worked example 3 Let all three lengths depend on a variable

Problem. Proposed side lengths are 2t+12t+12t+1, t+4t+4t+4, and 888 units. Find every real value of ttt for which they make a nondegenerate triangle.

Solution. Since the longest expression may change, check all three inequalities.

First comparison:

(2t+1)+(t+4)>8.(2t+1)+(t+4)>8.(2t+1)+(t+4)>8.

This simplifies to

t>1.t>1.t>1.

Second comparison:

(2t+1)+8>t+4.(2t+1)+8>t+4.(2t+1)+8>t+4.

This gives

t>−5.t>-5.t>−5.

Third comparison:

(t+4)+8>2t+1.(t+4)+8>2t+1.(t+4)+8>2t+1.

This gives

t<11.t<11.t<11.

All three hold exactly when

1<t<11.1<t<11.1<t<11.

Throughout this interval, all side expressions are positive.

At the lower endpoint, 111, the lengths are 333, 555, and 888, which are flat.

At the upper endpoint, 111111, they are 232323, 151515, and 888, also flat.

Direct segments and broken paths

A broken path is a route made from consecutive straight segments. Between distinct points AAA and BBB, a two-segment route through a point PPP off line ABABAB is strictly longer than the direct segment:

AP+PB>AB.AP+PB>AB.AP+PB>AB.

This is the triangle inequality in triangle APBAPBAPB.

If PPP is between AAA and BBB, segment addition gives equality.

If PPP is on their line beyond either endpoint, the path doubles back and is longer. Collinearity by itself does not guarantee equality.

The same comparison works for any finite number of legs. For instance, if the three legs from AAA through BBB and CCC to DDD measure 444, 666, and 888, first replace ABABAB and BCBCBC by ACACAC. That replacement cannot increase the length. Then replace ACACAC and CDCDCD by ADADAD, giving a direct distance at most 181818.

In general, repeated applications of the two-leg result give

AD≤AB+BC+CD.AD\le AB+BC+CD.AD≤AB+BC+CD.

Equality holds exactly when the intermediate points lie on the direct segment in travel order, without backtracking. A turn off the segment or a reversal makes the path longer. We allow the non-strict symbol here because a path may include straight, forward steps.

A polygon inequality compares one side of a closed polygon with all the other sides together. Traveling around those other sides gives a broken path between the chosen side’s endpoints. A nondegenerate polygon cannot have all its vertices on one line, so that route cannot be the same forward straight segment, and its length strictly exceeds the chosen side.

Worked example 4 Bound a polygon side without measuring it

Problem. A nondegenerate quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD has three consecutive sides of lengths 444, 777, and 666 units, in that order. Give a strict upper bound for side DADADA. Explain whether a claimed length of 171717 units is possible.

Solution. The route around the other three sides has length

4+7+6=17.4+7+6=17.4+7+6=17.

The polygon inequality gives

DA<17.DA<17.DA<17.

A claimed length of 171717 would require the route from AAA through BBB and CCC to DDD to lie straight in travel order. That would be flat, so it cannot describe the stated quadrilateral.

Check your understanding

A route goes from A to P to Q to B with positive leg lengths. Which condition makes its total length equal to AB?

Answer choices

A forward route along the direct segment adds to AB by segment addition.

A reversal adds extra distance, and leaving the line creates a strict triangle inequality. Equality therefore needs both collinearity and the stated travel order.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Allowing equality. A sum equal to the longest side gives a flat boundary, not an ordinary triangle.
  • Testing the wrong pair. For positive numerical lengths, compare the two shorter lengths with the longest.
  • Missing positivity. A proposed side must have positive length, including when it is an expression.
  • Keeping only the upper bound. A third side must exceed the difference as well as stay below the sum.
  • Equating collinear with shortest. A collinear path can backtrack; equality needs forward travel along the direct segment.

Key takeaways

  • Triangle inequality. Any two sides of a nondegenerate triangle together exceed the third.
  • Feasibility. Positive lengths close into a triangle when the two shorter lengths sum to more than the longest.
  • Third-side range. The third side lies strictly between the difference and the sum of the fixed sides.
  • Paths. A broken path cannot beat the direct segment; it ties only along that segment in travel order.
  • Polygon inequality. A side of a nondegenerate polygon is shorter than all its other sides together.

A bit of history (optional)

In Book I of Euclid’s Elements, a warning about lengths appears before a construction problem. Proposition 20 proves the triangle inequality. Two propositions later, Euclid asks the reader to construct a triangle from three supplied lengths, but his statement requires each pair to add to more than the third.

The construction draws two circles and uses a meeting point as the new vertex. There is a subtle gap in that old account: why must the circles meet? In his commentary, David Joyce points out that Euclid states the necessary length condition but does not justify that it is sufficient for the intersection. Even an argument that carefully proves its length comparisons can leave a hidden assumption inside its drawing. You can read Proposition 22 and Joyce’s commentary.

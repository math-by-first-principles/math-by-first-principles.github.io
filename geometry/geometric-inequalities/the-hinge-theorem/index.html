The Hinge Theorem

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Open a folding ruler while keeping both arms the same length. Its free ends spread apart. Two triangles built from the same pair of arm lengths behave this way too: the wider opening faces the longer third side. The challenge is to justify that comparison without measuring the gap or trusting the scale of a sketch.

Learning goals

  • Identify the included angles after matching two equal corresponding side pairs
  • Compare third sides or included angles in two triangles
  • Explain why the hinge theorem holds, including a collinear auxiliary case
  • Solve algebraic restrictions from two-triangle comparisons and angle conditions

Fix the arms, compare the opening

Two triangles can have matching pairs of sides without being congruent. For example, give each triangle arms of lengths 666 and 444 units. One opening measures 40∘40^\circ40∘; the other measures 80∘80^\circ80∘. The wider opening leaves a larger gap between the free ends. Connecting those ends gives each triangle its third side.

The included angle is the angle between the two sides being matched. For sides ABABAB and ACACAC, it is angle BACBACBAC, with vertex AAA. It is not an angle chosen merely because its measure is known.

Here ABABAB names the length of segment ABABAB. All triangles in this lesson are nondegenerate, with three noncollinear vertices, so their included angles are strictly between zero and 180∘180^\circ180∘.

If the openings match too, SAS makes the triangles congruent, so their third sides match. If one opening is larger, a copy-and-bisect construction will turn the comparison into a direct route versus a broken route. The triangle inequality, or segment addition when the auxiliary points line up, then gives the strict comparison.

The hinge theorem, also called the SAS inequality, states: if two triangles have two pairs of equal corresponding sides, the triangle with the larger included angle has the longer third side.

For triangles ABCABCABC and DEFDEFDEF, first check

AB=DE.AB=DE.AB=DE.

Also check

AC=DF.AC=DF.AC=DF.

Under those conditions, the angle comparison

m∠BAC>m∠EDFm\angle BAC>m\angle EDFm∠BAC>m∠EDF

gives the side comparison

BC>EF.BC>EF.BC>EF.

PQ and PR meet at P. UV and UW meet at U.

The included angles are QPR and VUW; the remaining sides to compare are QR and VW.

Worked example 1 Compare sides without finding either length

Problem. Triangles ABCABCABC and DEFDEFDEF have corresponding arms of lengths 666 and 444 units. Their included angles at AAA and DDD measure 40∘40^\circ40∘ and 80∘80^\circ80∘, respectively. Compare BCBCBC and EFEFEF. Can you calculate either length using the hinge theorem alone?

Solution. The first figure shows these exact givens. Both side pairs match, and angle DDD is larger. The side opposite DDD is therefore longer:

EF>BC.EF>BC.EF>BC.

The theorem gives an order, not an exact length. The numbers 666, 444, 404040, and 808080 are not inserted into a hinge formula to calculate the third sides.

Why the wider opening gives a longer side

The hinge theorem#

First follow an instance with fixed numbers. Let ABABAB have length 666 and ACACAC length 444, with a right angle at AAA. Inside that right angle, draw AXAXAX of length 444 at 40∘40^\circ40∘ to ABABAB. Triangle ABXABXABX copies any triangle with arms 666 and 444 and an included angle of 40∘40^\circ40∘, by SAS.

We accept this basic fact of plane incidence: a ray from a triangle’s vertex strictly inside its angle meets the opposite side between its endpoints.

The angle from AXAXAX to ACACAC measures 50∘50^\circ50∘. Bisect it with ray APAPAP, making two 25∘25^\circ25∘ parts. This ray meets the opposite side BCBCBC at an interior point PPP.

The copied angle is 40∘40^\circ40∘, while each bisected half is 25∘25^\circ25∘. The equality used by SAS is between those two halves.

Triangles ACPACPACP and AXPAXPAXP have equal 444-unit arms, the common side APAPAP, and equal included angles of 25∘25^\circ25∘. SAS gives

PC=PX.PC=PX.PC=PX.

This fixed-number drawing illustrates the noncollinear Case A of the general proof below. The route from BBB through PPP to XXX is longer than BXBXBX. Replacing PXPXPX by its equal length PCPCPC makes that route as long as BCBCBC. Thus the 90∘90^\circ90∘ triangle has a longer third side than the 40∘40^\circ40∘ triangle. Within this noncollinear case, the reasoning does not depend on the particular angle measures or arm lengths. The general argument below also handles collinear auxiliary points.

Now take any two triangles with the matching pairs stated above, and suppose angle BACBACBAC is larger than angle EDFEDFEDF. Copy angle EDFEDFEDF inside angle BACBACBAC, starting from ray ABABAB. On the new ray choose XXX so that

AX=AC.AX=AC.AX=AC.

SAS identifies the copied triangle:

△ABX≅△DEF.\triangle ABX\cong\triangle DEF.△ABX≅△DEF.

Consequently,

BX=EF.BX=EF.BX=EF.

Bisect the positive angle XACXACXAC. Its bisector ray lies strictly inside angle BACBACBAC, so it meets segment BCBCBC at a point PPP strictly between BBB and CCC. In particular, BPBPBP and PCPCPC are positive. Since APAPAP lies between AXAXAX and ACACAC, ray AXAXAX lies strictly inside angle BAPBAPBAP.

The two equal arms ACACAC and AXAXAX, the common side APAPAP, and the bisected angle give SAS congruence of triangles ACPACPACP and AXPAXPAXP. Hence

PC=PX.PC=PX.PC=PX.

There are two possible cases for the remaining comparison.

Case A: B,P,XB,P,XB,P,X are noncollinear. The triangle inequality in triangle BPXBPXBPX gives

BX<BP+PX.BX<BP+PX.BX<BP+PX.

Substitute the equal length PCPCPC for PXPXPX. Segment addition along BCBCBC then gives

BX<BC.BX<BC.BX<BC.

Case B: B,P,XB,P,XB,P,X are collinear. Apply the same accepted incidence fact in triangle BAPBAPBAP: ray AXAXAX, strictly inside angle BAPBAPBAP, meets segment BPBPBP between BBB and PPP. This meeting point is XXX: XXX lies on both lines AXAXAX and BCBCBC, and these distinct lines meet only once because AAA is off BCBCBC. The order along the line is therefore B,X,P,CB,X,P,CB,X,P,C. These three auxiliary points do not form a triangle, so the strict triangle inequality does not apply.

Instead, segment addition gives

BC=BX+XP+PC.BC=BX+XP+PC.BC=BX+XP+PC.

The last two lengths are positive, so again

BC>BX.BC>BX.BC>BX.

Both cases give the same strict order. Finally substitute the copied third side EFEFEF for BXBXBX:

BC>EF.BC>EF.BC>EF.

∎

Check your understanding

In the collinear proof case, the order is B, X, P, C. Which argument establishes that BC is longer than BX?

Answer choices

The stated order gives a sum of consecutive lengths.

BC=BX+XP+PC.BC=BX+XP+PC.BC=BX+XP+PC.

Both added lengths are positive. Therefore,

BC>BX.BC>BX.BC>BX.

B, P, and X are collinear, so they do not form a triangle to which the strict triangle inequality applies.

Reverse the comparison

With the same two equal side pairs, a longer third side also tells you which opening is larger. For example, suppose both triangles have arms of lengths 777 and 999, while their third sides measure 888 and 121212. The 121212-unit side faces the larger included angle. Both triples pass the triangle inequality, so the example describes actual triangles.

This is the converse hinge theorem: if two triangles have two pairs of equal corresponding sides, the longer third side faces the larger included angle.

The converse hinge theorem#

In the 888-versus-121212 example, equal openings would force equal third sides by SAS. A smaller opening opposite the 121212-unit side would force it to be shorter by the hinge theorem. Both alternatives conflict with the given lengths, leaving a larger opening.

In general, retain the equal pairs ABABAB with DEDEDE, and ACACAC with DFDFDF, and suppose

BC>EF.BC>EF.BC>EF.

If the included angles were equal, SAS would give equal third sides, contradicting the given strict order.

If angle BACBACBAC were smaller than angle EDFEDFEDF, the hinge theorem, applied in the opposite order, would give

BC<EF.BC<EF.BC<EF.

That also contradicts the given. The remaining possibility is

m∠BAC>m∠EDF.m\angle BAC>m\angle EDF.m∠BAC>m∠EDF.

The numerical lengths were not needed for either contradiction, so the argument holds for every matching pair of arms.

∎

Equality completes the picture. Equal included angles give equal third sides by SAS. Equal third sides give equal included angles by SSS, because all three corresponding side pairs then match. Reversing which triangle has the larger angle reverses which has the longer third side.

Worked example 2 Recognize a shared side

Problem. Triangles ABCABCABC and ADCADCADC share side ACACAC. The sides ABABAB and ADADAD are equal. Angle BACBACBAC measures 70∘70^\circ70∘, and angle DACDACDAC measures 40∘40^\circ40∘. Compare BCBCBC and DCDCDC.

Solution. The marked pair ABABAB and ADADAD matches. The second pair is the shared side ACACAC, which has the same length in both triangles.

The stated angles are between those matched sides. The larger included angle is at AAA in triangle ABCABCABC, so

BC>DC.BC>DC.BC>DC.

Check your understanding

Two triangles have equal corresponding arm lengths of 5 and 12 units. Their third sides measure 14 and 9 units. Which conclusion follows?

Answer choices

The two arm pairs match. The longer third side is the 14-unit side.

The converse hinge theorem makes its opposite included angle larger. This conclusion concerns the included angle, not every angle in that triangle.

Turn the comparison into algebra

Worked example 3 Restrict an unknown side

Problem. Existing triangles KLMKLMKLM and PQRPQRPQR satisfy the two equal pairs

KL=PQ,KL=PQ,KL=PQ,

and

KM=PR.KM=PR.KM=PR.

Their included angles at KKK and PPP measure 85∘85^\circ85∘ and 50∘50^\circ50∘. Their third sides are given by

LM=4x+1,LM=4x+1,LM=4x+1,

and

QR=2x+9.QR=2x+9.QR=2x+9.

Find the necessary restriction on xxx.

Solution. The two matched pairs surround the stated angles. Since angle KKK is larger, the hinge theorem gives

4x+1>2x+9.4x+1>2x+9.4x+1>2x+9.

Subtract 2x2x2x, then subtract 111:

2x>8.2x>8.2x>8.

Divide by positive 222:

x>4.x>4.x>4.

At the excluded endpoint, the third sides would match, forcing equal included angles by SSS. The result is a necessary restriction on the stated existing triangles; it does not supply their missing arm lengths.

Worked example 4 Keep the angles possible

Problem. Two triangles have the same two corresponding arm lengths. Their included angles, in degrees, are 3t+103t+103t+10 and t+30t+30t+30. Find every real ttt for which both openings form nondegenerate triangles and the first third side is longer.

Solution. The converse hinge theorem requires the first angle to be larger:

3t+10>t+30.3t+10>t+30.3t+10>t+30.

This gives

t>10.t>10.t>10.

Each included angle must also lie strictly between zero and 180∘180^\circ180∘. For the first angle, these conditions give

t>−103.t> -\frac{10}{3}.t>−310​.

And,

t<1703.t<\frac{170}{3}.t<3170​.

For the second angle, they give

t>−30.t>-30.t>−30.

And,

t<150.t<150.t<150.

All conditions hold exactly when

10<t<1703.10<t<\frac{170}{3}.10<t<3170​.

Conversely, every ttt in this interval keeps both openings strictly between zero and 180∘180^\circ180∘, with the first larger. Joining the positive matched arms at those openings forms two nondegenerate triangles. The forward hinge theorem makes the first third side longer.

At the lower boundary the openings match, so the third sides match. At the upper boundary the first opening is 180∘180^\circ180∘, which is flat and excluded.

Check your understanding

Matching side pairs surround included angles of (2u+15)∘(2u+15)^\circ(2u+15)∘ and 65∘65^\circ65∘. The first third side is longer. Which necessary restriction follows from their order?

Answer choices

The converse hinge theorem makes the first included angle larger.

2u+15>65.2u+15>65.2u+15>65.

Subtract 15 and divide by 2.

u>25.u>25.u>25.

Any full feasibility range must also keep the angle below 180 degrees.

Checking the hypotheses comes before the algebra. One equal pair is insufficient, and an angle outside the matched pair is not the included angle. A sketch can help locate the right parts, but it cannot supply an unmarked equality.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Missing a side pair. Identify two equal corresponding side pairs before comparing the openings.
  • Choosing another angle. Use the angle between the matched sides, even when another angle is labeled.
  • Reversing the order. Larger opening goes with longer third side; the converse keeps that direction.
  • Forgetting equality. Equal openings lead to SAS congruence; equal third sides complete SSS congruence.
  • Ignoring the angle range. Zero and 180∘180^\circ180∘ are flat boundaries, not included angles of ordinary triangles.

Key takeaways

  • Hinge theorem. With two equal corresponding side pairs, a larger included angle gives a longer third side.
  • Converse hinge theorem. Under the same side conditions, a longer third side gives a larger included angle.
  • Equality case. Equal openings and equal third sides go together when the two arm pairs match.
  • Check the matched pairs and included angles before writing an inequality or solving for a variable.

A bit of history (optional)

In Euclid’s Elements, Proposition I.24 compares two triangles whose sides match in pairs. Euclid calls the remaining sides their bases, even though a base need not sit at the bottom of a drawing. His construction makes an extra triangle so that an inequality within one triangle can settle a comparison between two.

The very next proposition asks the question backward. In I.25, the bases have already been compared, and the openings must be ordered. Its short argument calls on the preceding result and on SAS congruence. The sequence preserves a small piece of the book’s architecture: a result proved on one page becomes a tool on the next.

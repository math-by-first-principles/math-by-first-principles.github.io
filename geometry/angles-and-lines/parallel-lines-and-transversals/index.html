Parallel Lines and Transversals

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A line crossing two other lines makes angles at different places. The vertical-angle theorem connects angles at one crossing, but it says nothing by itself about the other crossing. A given parallel relationship supplies that missing connection. We will explain why it works before using it to calculate.

Learning goals

  • Identify corresponding, alternate, and same-side angle pairs from their positions
  • Derive same-side interior sums from the accepted Euclidean parallel postulate
  • Explain corresponding and alternate angle congruence using previously proved angle relationships
  • Calculate missing measures and solve equations justified by given parallel lines
  • Track separate transversals and distinguish conclusions that require parallelism

Name the positions first

A transversal is a line that crosses two coplanar lines at distinct points. In this lesson all the lines lie in one plane, the two crossed lines are distinct, and each crossing is a genuine intersection of two distinct lines. The transversal is not either crossed line.

In the diagram, line ttt crosses ℓ\ellℓ and mmm. At the upper crossing, label the northwest, northeast, southeast, and southwest sectors 1,2,3,41,2,3,41,2,3,4, clockwise. At the lower crossing use 5,6,7,85,6,7,85,6,7,8 in that same order. The labels identify angles, so m∠1m\angle1m∠1 means the measure of angle 111.

Angles 3,4,5,63,4,5,63,4,5,6 are interior angles, between the two crossed lines. Angles 1,2,7,81,2,7,81,2,7,8 are exterior angles, outside that strip. For example, angles 333 and 555 are both interior but lie on opposite sides of ttt.

Two accepted Euclidean foundations

Parallel lines are coplanar lines that do not meet, however far they continue. We accept two parallel-postulate forms as foundations of Euclidean geometry. We are not proving them from a sketch.

The parallel-through-a-point foundation says that, for a line and a point outside it in the same plane, exactly one line through that point is parallel to the given line. Both existence and uniqueness are part of the accepted statement.

The angle form of Euclid’s parallel postulate says that if a transversal makes the two interior angles on one side sum to less than 180∘180^\circ180∘, the crossed coplanar lines meet on that side when extended. The two intersections with the transversal must be distinct.

For instance, same-side interior measures of 70∘70^\circ70∘ and 80∘80^\circ80∘ total 150∘150^\circ150∘. The angle postulate says the crossed lines meet on that side. They cannot be parallel.

The next proof uses the angle form. The existence and uniqueness form will also support the converse tests in the next lesson. We do not accept corresponding-angle congruence as an extra postulate.

Why both interior sums are straight-angle sums

For parallel lines, the two interior angles on either side of a transversal are supplementary. Here is why the total must be exactly 180∘180^\circ180∘, rather than merely at least that much.

In the numbered layout, the left-side sum uses angles 4,54,54,5 and the right-side sum uses 3,63,63,6. Together they use the lower half of the upper crossing and the upper half of the lower crossing. Each half is a straight angle, so their combined total is 360∘360^\circ360∘.

Parallel lines give supplementary same-side interior angles#

Try a left-side sum of 184∘184^\circ184∘. The right-side sum would have to be 360∘−184∘=176∘360^\circ-184^\circ=176^\circ360∘−184∘=176∘. That is below 180∘180^\circ180∘, so the postulate would force the lines to meet on the right. Parallel lines exclude that possibility. A left-side sum of 176∘176^\circ176∘ is also excluded, because it would force a meeting on the left. The balanced possibility is 180∘180^\circ180∘ on each side.

Let L=m∠4+m∠5L=m\angle4+m\angle5L=m∠4+m∠5 and R=m∠3+m∠6R=m\angle3+m\angle6R=m∠3+m∠6. Angle addition and the two straight angles give

m∠3+m∠4=180∘,m∠5+m∠6=180∘,L+R=360∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle3+m\angle4&=180^\circ,\\ m\angle5+m\angle6&=180^\circ,\\ L+R&=360^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠3+m∠4m∠5+m∠6L+R​=180∘,=180∘,=360∘.​

Neither LLL nor RRR can be below 180∘180^\circ180∘: the angle postulate would force the given parallel lines to meet on that side. If LLL were above 180∘180^\circ180∘, then R=360∘−LR=360^\circ-LR=360∘−L would be below 180∘180^\circ180∘, which is impossible. Thus L=180∘L=180^\circL=180∘, and substitution into the total gives R=180∘R=180^\circR=180∘. The trial values above were not needed; the argument applies to every transversal of two distinct parallel lines.

∎

Check your understanding

For parallel lines, why is it insufficient to say only that each same-side interior sum is at least 180 degrees?

Answer choices

The two sums together contain two straight angles, totaling 360 degrees.

If either sum exceeded 180 degrees, the other would fall below 180 degrees and force an intersection by the angle postulate. Thus both are exactly 180 degrees.

Derive the equal-angle families

Corresponding angles of parallel lines are congruent because they supplement the same angle measure.

Corresponding and alternate angle congruence#

For a numerical pass, suppose m∠4=116∘m\angle4=116^\circm∠4=116∘. The linear pair 1,41,41,4 gives m∠1=64∘m\angle1=64^\circm∠1=64∘. The same-side interior pair 4,54,54,5 also gives m∠5=64∘m\angle5=64^\circm∠5=64∘. Thus angles 111 and 555 have equal measures.

For any two distinct parallel lines cut by a transversal, m∠1+m∠4=180∘m\angle1+m\angle4=180^\circm∠1+m∠4=180∘ by the linear-pair result. Also, m∠4+m∠5=180∘m\angle4+m\angle5=180^\circm∠4+m∠5=180∘ by the result just proved. Subtracting the common m∠4m\angle4m∠4 gives m∠1=m∠5m\angle1=m\angle5m∠1=m∠5, so ∠1≅∠5\angle1\cong\angle5∠1≅∠5. This is the congruent-supplements reasoning from Angle Pairs; it does not depend on 116∘116^\circ116∘.

Using the linear pair 2,32,32,3 and same-side interior pair 3,63,63,6 similarly gives ∠2≅∠6\angle2\cong\angle6∠2≅∠6. Vertical angles give ∠3≅∠1\angle3\cong\angle1∠3≅∠1 and ∠7≅∠5\angle7\cong\angle5∠7≅∠5, so transitivity gives ∠3≅∠7\angle3\cong\angle7∠3≅∠7. For the last pair, vertical angles give ∠4≅∠2\angle4\cong\angle2∠4≅∠2 and ∠6≅∠8\angle6\cong\angle8∠6≅∠8. Combining them with ∠2≅∠6\angle2\cong\angle6∠2≅∠6 gives ∠4≅∠8\angle4\cong\angle8∠4≅∠8 by transitivity. These are all four corresponding pairs.

The remaining family, same-side exterior, is supplementary.

Parallel lines give supplementary same-side exterior angles#

Numerically, if m∠1=64∘m\angle1=64^\circm∠1=64∘, then corresponding ∠5\angle5∠5 also measures 64∘64^\circ64∘, and its linear-pair partner ∠8\angle8∠8 measures 116∘116^\circ116∘. The exterior sum is 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Because ∠1≅∠5\angle1\cong\angle5∠1≅∠5 and 5,85,85,8 is a linear pair, substitute m∠1m\angle1m∠1 for m∠5m\angle5m∠5 to get m∠1+m∠8=180∘m\angle1+m\angle8=180^\circm∠1+m∠8=180∘. Similarly, ∠2≅∠6\angle2\cong\angle6∠2≅∠6 and the linear pair 6,76,76,7 give m∠2+m∠7=180∘m\angle2+m\angle7=180^\circm∠2+m∠7=180∘. The numerical measures were not needed for either substitution.

∎

For two distinct parallel lines crossed by a transversal, corresponding and both alternate families are congruent; both same-side families are supplementary. These are forward results: given parallel lines, conclude angle relationships. Reversing a result requires its own proof in the next lesson.

Keep each transversal attached to its own angles

Two transversals can carry different angle measures across the same parallel lines. Follow one crossing line at a time; parallelism does not make every angle in the picture congruent.

Keep the hypothesis with the conclusion

If the parallel condition is removed, vertical-angle congruence and linear-pair sums remain true at each individual crossing. Crossings at different points no longer have a guaranteed equal-angle or supplementary relationship. A tilted sketch cannot supply the missing hypothesis.

Check your understanding

A transversal crosses two distinct coplanar lines, but no parallelism or cross-intersection angle condition is given. Which relationship is still guaranteed?

Answer choices

Two distinct intersecting lines form vertical angles at each crossing. The vertical-angle theorem does not require a second line to be parallel.

The other listed conclusions connect different crossings and require an additional hypothesis. Parallelism would supply one, but it is absent here.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • A family name: Corresponding describes positions. To conclude congruence here, retain the parallel-line hypothesis.
  • Equal or supplementary: Corresponding and alternate pairs are congruent; same-side interior and exterior pairs have measures totaling 180∘180^\circ180∘.
  • The two vertices: Angles at different crossings cannot be vertical angles or a linear pair with each other.
  • Several transversals: Identify the same transversal at both crossings before choosing a family.
  • Reversing a claim: A forward angle theorem does not itself prove its converse.

Key takeaways

  • Identify interior or exterior position and the side of the transversal before using a relationship.
  • For two distinct parallel lines, the angle postulate and a 360∘360^\circ360∘ total force both same-side interior sums to be 180∘180^\circ180∘.
  • Congruent supplements and vertical angles then establish corresponding and alternate congruence; same-side exterior pairs are supplementary.
  • Use a proved relationship to choose an equation, then check the resulting measures.
  • Without parallelism, retain local vertical-angle and linear-pair results, but do not assume relationships between crossings.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid’s fifth postulate is longer and more complicated than his first few starting assumptions. Instead of simply saying that only one parallel passes through an external point, it describes a line crossing two other lines and predicts the side on which they will meet.

For centuries, geometers tried to prove that statement from the other assumptions. Some arguments reached unfamiliar conclusions and treated their strangeness as a contradiction. In the nineteenth century, mathematicians found that geometries built with a different parallel assumption could still be consistent. The troublesome postulate had been doing real work all along.

David Joyce’s edition and commentary on Postulate 5 describe the statement and the long attempt to prove it.

Proving Lines Parallel or Perpendicular

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A drawing shows only small pieces of two lines. Looking alongside each other does not establish that they never meet. Angle information can settle that question, but only if you choose a relationship that actually tests the two lines.

Learning goals

  • Prove parallelism from a suitable angle relationship and state the required conditions
  • Distinguish sufficient angle information from relationships that hold without parallel lines
  • Justify planar parallel and perpendicular relationships using previously proved angle results
  • Identify perpendicular segments that define distances to lines and between parallels
  • Construct a parallel through an external point and justify it with an angle converse

Reverse the question

An angle relationship can establish parallelism, even when parallelism was not supplied.

Suppose a transversal cuts two distinct lines in one plane at distinct points. If a pair of corresponding angles both measure 57∘57^\circ57∘, the lines are parallel. This reverses the earlier theorem: parallel lines make corresponding angles congruent. A converse interchanges a conditional’s hypothesis and conclusion; it needs its own justification.

Here is the angle layout used below. The upper intersection is PPP on ℓ\ellℓ, the lower intersection is QQQ on mmm, and ttt crosses both. Around PPP, angles 1,2,3,41,2,3,41,2,3,4 occupy northwest, northeast, southeast, southwest sectors. Around QQQ, angles 5,6,7,85,6,7,85,6,7,8 use the same clockwise order. These positions name angle pairs; the sketch alone supplies no parallelism.

Why the angle tests work

We accept here an angle-measure foundation: given a starting ray in a plane, one chosen side of its full line, and a measure strictly between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘, there exists exactly one ray strictly on that side making that opening. This is the exact fact behind drawing an angle with a protractor. Openings of 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘ lie on the boundary line and are excluded from this statement.

Corresponding-angle converse from a unique parallel#

First run the argument with the two 57∘57^\circ57∘ corresponding angles, ∠2\angle2∠2 and ∠6\angle6∠6. Through PPP there is exactly one parallel nnn to mmm, by the accepted parallel foundation. Its corresponding angle with ttt is 57∘57^\circ57∘, by the theorem from the previous lesson. The given line ℓ\ellℓ makes that same 57∘57^\circ57∘ opening on the same side of the same ray of ttt. The two rays must coincide, so ℓ\ellℓ is nnn and is parallel to mmm.

Let distinct coplanar lines ℓ\ellℓ and mmm be cut by ttt at distinct points PPP and QQQ. Suppose their corresponding angles ∠2\angle2∠2 and ∠6\angle6∠6 are congruent. Their common measure lies strictly between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘, since these are intersections of distinct lines.

Assume for contradiction that ℓ\ellℓ is not parallel to mmm. Point PPP is outside mmm, because PPP and QQQ are distinct and ttt meets mmm only at QQQ. The accepted existence and uniqueness of a parallel gives a line nnn through PPP parallel to mmm.

Since n∥mn\parallel mn∥m while the temporary assumption says ℓ\ellℓ is not parallel to mmm, the lines nnn and ℓ\ellℓ are different. Through PPP, they therefore give different rays on the chosen side of ttt. The forward corresponding-angle theorem gives the selected angle between nnn and the upward ray of ttt the same measure as ∠6\angle6∠6. The given congruence gives ∠2\angle2∠2 that measure too. These would be two different rays on the same chosen side making the same opening with the same starting ray, contradicting the accepted angle-measure foundation. Reject the temporary assumption, so ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m.

The value 57∘57^\circ57∘ did no work beyond making the shared opening concrete. This argument establishes the converse for any valid corresponding-angle measure and any corresponding position.

∎

The other tests reduce to this converse using angle pairs you already proved.

The other transversal converses#

For example, suppose ∠4\angle4∠4 and ∠6\angle6∠6 both measure 68∘68^\circ68∘. Vertical angles make m∠2=68∘m\angle2=68^\circm∠2=68∘, so corresponding angles 222 and 666 are congruent and ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m. Or suppose same-side interior angles 444 and 555 measure 71∘71^\circ71∘ and 109∘109^\circ109∘. The lower linear pair gives m∠6=180∘−109∘=71∘m\angle6=180^\circ-109^\circ=71^\circm∠6=180∘−109∘=71∘, reducing to the preceding test.

Keep distinct coplanar lines cut by one transversal at distinct points. If alternate interior angles 444 and 666 are congruent, vertical angles give ∠2≅∠4\angle2\cong\angle4∠2≅∠4. Transitivity gives ∠2≅∠6\angle2\cong\angle6∠2≅∠6, so the corresponding-angle converse gives parallelism. The other alternate interior pair follows with the labels exchanged.

If same-side interior angles 444 and 555 are supplementary, their measures add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. The linear pair 5,65,65,6 also sums to 180∘180^\circ180∘. Subtracting m∠5m\angle5m∠5 from these equal sums gives m∠4=m∠6m\angle4=m\angle6m∠4=m∠6. The alternate interior converse just proved gives parallelism. The other side follows the same way.

If alternate exterior angles 111 and 777 are congruent, vertical angles at QQQ give ∠7≅∠5\angle7\cong\angle5∠7≅∠5. Then ∠1≅∠5\angle1\cong\angle5∠1≅∠5, a corresponding pair, so the corresponding converse gives parallelism. The other alternate exterior pair works identically.

If same-side exterior angles 111 and 888 are supplementary, the linear pair 5,85,85,8 also sums to 180∘180^\circ180∘. Subtracting m∠8m\angle8m∠8 gives m∠1=m∠5m\angle1=m\angle5m∠1=m∠5, again a corresponding pair. The other same-side exterior pair has the same argument.

∎

Worked example 1 Choose the equation that tests parallelism

Problem. In the numbered layout, the distinct coplanar lines have m∠4=(4x+9)∘m\angle4=(4x+9)^\circm∠4=(4x+9)∘ and m∠6=(6x−25)∘m\angle6=(6x-25)^\circm∠6=(6x−25)∘. Find xxx that makes ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m, find both measures, and justify the conclusion.

Solution. If ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m, the forward alternate interior theorem forces angles 444 and 666 to be congruent. Any value producing parallelism must therefore satisfy:

4x+9=6x−25,34=2x,x=17.\begin{aligned} 4x+9&=6x-25,\\ 34&=2x,\\ x&=17. \end{aligned}4x+934x​=6x−25,=2x,=17.​

Conversely, substitution of 171717 gives 4(17)+9=774(17)+9=774(17)+9=77 and 6(17)−25=776(17)-25=776(17)−25=77. Both are valid angle measures, so the alternate interior converse gives ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m. The forced equation has only one solution, so 171717 is the only suitable value.

Check that the information reaches both lines

Equal vertical angles do not test parallelism: they occur at a single intersection regardless of the second line. A linear pair is supplementary for the same local reason. Likewise, saying only that two angles are congruent omits the positions that make a converse apply.

Worked example 2 Separate a local fact from a parallelism test

Problem. Use the numbered layout, with distinct coplanar ℓ,m\ell,mℓ,m and transversal ttt. Decide whether each set of givens guarantees ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m.

Case A. m∠1=m∠3=64∘m\angle1=m\angle3=64^\circm∠1=m∠3=64∘.

Case B. m∠3=64∘m\angle3=64^\circm∠3=64∘ and m∠5=64∘m\angle5=64^\circm∠5=64∘.

Solution.

Case A. Angles 111 and 333 are vertical angles at PPP. Their equality holds for any second line mmm. Choose SSS on ℓ\ellℓ and RRR on ttt, both different from PPP, and draw mmm through them. It meets ℓ\ellℓ at SSS, preserving both 64∘64^\circ64∘ givens without parallelism.

Case B. Angles 333 and 555 are alternate interior angles. Their congruence satisfies the alternate interior converse, so ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m.

Answer choices

Parallel and perpendicular relationships in a plane

A right angle gives an angle test you can recognize immediately. If two distinct coplanar lines are perpendicular to the same transversal at distinct points, corresponding angles measure 90∘90^\circ90∘ at both crossings. Their congruence proves the two lines parallel.

A line crossing one parallel crosses the other#

Let ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m be distinct lines in one plane, and let a third coplanar line nnn cross mmm at RRR. Suppose nnn did not meet ℓ\ellℓ. These distinct coplanar lines would then be parallel. Since RRR lies on mmm, it is outside ℓ\ellℓ. Both mmm and nnn would be different parallels to ℓ\ellℓ through RRR, contradicting the accepted uniqueness of a parallel through an external point. Therefore nnn meets ℓ\ellℓ. The two crossings are distinct because ℓ\ellℓ and mmm do not meet, so nnn is a transversal.

∎

If instead ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m is given and t⊥ℓt\perp\ellt⊥ℓ, the crossing result makes ttt a transversal of both parallels. The forward corresponding-angle theorem makes the corresponding angle at mmm also 90∘90^\circ90∘. Thus t⊥mt\perp mt⊥m. This is the perpendicular transversal theorem: in a plane, a line perpendicular to one of two parallel lines is perpendicular to the other.

Two perpendiculars and two parallels#

Let ℓ\ellℓ and mmm be distinct coplanar lines perpendicular to nnn. They cannot meet nnn at the same point: on either chosen side of nnn, the unique ray making a 90∘90^\circ90∘ opening would force ℓ\ellℓ and mmm to coincide. Their feet on nnn are therefore distinct. Line nnn is a transversal, and the corresponding right angles are congruent, so the corresponding-angle converse gives ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m. The coplanar condition is essential; spatial lines can have different directions while each meets a third line at a right angle.

Now let three pairwise distinct coplanar lines ℓ,m,n\ell,m,nℓ,m,n satisfy ℓ∥n\ell\parallel nℓ∥n and m∥nm\parallel nm∥n. Suppose ℓ\ellℓ and mmm met at RRR. Point RRR would lie outside nnn, since neither parallel meets nnn. There would then be two different parallels to nnn through RRR, contradicting the accepted uniqueness of a parallel. Thus ℓ\ellℓ and mmm do not meet. As distinct coplanar lines, they are parallel.

∎

Worked example 3 Carry a perpendicular across parallels

Problem. Distinct lines ℓ\ellℓ and mmm are parallel in one plane. A transversal ttt is perpendicular to ℓ\ellℓ. At its crossing with mmm, one angle measures (7x+6)∘(7x+6)^\circ(7x+6)∘. Find xxx and explain why the angle is right.

Solution. The perpendicular transversal theorem gives t⊥mt\perp mt⊥m. Each angle at their intersection is therefore right, so

7x+6=90,7x=84,x=12.\begin{aligned} 7x+6&=90,\\ 7x&=84,\\ x&=12. \end{aligned}7x+67xx​=90,=84,=12.​

Substitution gives 7(12)+6=907(12)+6=907(12)+6=90, as required. The parallel and perpendicular givens justify the equation; the sketch’s appearance would not.

Check your understanding

Three lines lie in one plane. Lines ℓ and m are distinct and each is perpendicular to n. Why must ℓ and m be parallel?

Answer choices

Distinct coplanar perpendiculars to n have distinct feet, so n is a transversal.

A corresponding angle at each crossing measures 90∘90^\circ90∘. The corresponding-angle converse proves that ℓ and m are parallel.

Measure along a perpendicular

The distance from a point to a line is the length of the perpendicular segment from that point to the line. Its endpoint on the line is the foot of the perpendicular. If the point is on the line, the distance is zero. Why this perpendicular length is also the shortest possible joining length is proved in Sides, Angles, and Shortest Distances, lesson 5.1.

A unique perpendicular foot#

Let PPP be outside line ℓ\ellℓ in the same plane. The accepted parallel foundation gives a line ppp through PPP parallel to ℓ\ellℓ. At PPP, the accepted angle-measure foundation with measure 90∘90^\circ90∘ gives a ray perpendicular to ppp; extend it to a full line qqq. The crossing result shows that qqq meets ℓ\ellℓ, and the perpendicular transversal theorem makes that meeting perpendicular. Thus a foot exists.

If two different perpendicular lines to ℓ\ellℓ passed through PPP, the proved two-perpendiculars result would make them parallel. They would also meet at PPP, a contradiction. Hence the perpendicular line and its foot are unique. This proves existence and uniqueness; the compass-and-straightedge construction comes in lesson 9.1.

∎

For two parallel lines, measure their perpendicular distance along a segment perpendicular to both, with one endpoint on each line. The perpendicular transversal theorem establishes both right angles. The justification that every such segment has the same length comes later with triangle congruence; here, measure the specified perpendicular segment.

Worked example 4 Identify the distance segment

Problem. Point PPP is outside line mmm. Points HHH and KKK lie on mmm, and PH⊥mPH\perp mPH⊥m. The length PHPHPH is 3d+23d+23d+2 centimeters, PK=29PK=29PK=29 centimeters, and the distance from PPP to mmm is 232323 centimeters. Find ddd and name the segment whose length defines the distance.

Solution.

The perpendicular segment is PH‾\overline{PH}PH, so the distance definition gives PH=23PH=23PH=23 centimeters. Substitute the given expression:

3d+2=23,3d=21,d=7.\begin{aligned} 3d+2&=23,\\ 3d&=21,\\ d&=7. \end{aligned}3d+23dd​=23,=21,=7.​

The oblique length PKPKPK is not the length named by the definition. It is unnecessary for finding ddd.

Construct and then justify a parallel

You can turn the corresponding-angle converse into a construction. The angle-copy procedure from Circles and Construction Tools produces a congruent angle; its correctness proof is deferred to Using Congruence in Proofs, lesson 4.5. Use that copying fact here, then supply the parallelism justification now.

Worked example 5 Copy an angle through the required point

Problem. Given a line ℓ\ellℓ and a point PPP outside it in the same plane, construct a line through PPP parallel to ℓ\ellℓ using a compass and an unmarked straightedge. Retain the construction arcs and justify parallelism.

Solution.

Step 1. Choose distinct points A,BA,BA,B on ℓ\ellℓ. Draw line APAPAP, and choose CCC on it beyond PPP, so the point order is A,P,CA,P,CA,P,C. The angle to copy is ∠PAB\angle PAB∠PAB.

Step 2. Copy ∠PAB\angle PAB∠PAB at PPP, using PC→\overrightarrow{PC}PC as one side. Choose the other ray PQ→\overrightarrow{PQ}PQ​ on the same side of line APAPAP as BBB. To retain the copy record, draw equal-radius arcs at AAA and PPP, then transfer the chord between the first arc’s two crossings onto the second arc. Draw PQPQPQ through the transferred point.

Step 3. Extend PQPQPQ to a full line mmm. The copying procedure gives ∠CPQ≅∠PAB\angle CPQ\cong\angle PAB∠CPQ≅∠PAB. These are corresponding angles for transversal APAPAP, and PPP is outside ℓ\ellℓ, so the intersections are distinct. The corresponding-angle converse gives m∥ℓm\parallel\ellm∥ℓ.

The side choice prevents copying the angle into the wrong position. Measuring two openings approximately with a protractor can check a drawing, but the exact construction fact and the converse supply the justification.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reversing without proof. A forward theorem does not automatically establish its converse. Use the converse proved for the specified angle pair.
  • Checking one crossing only. Vertical angles and linear pairs hold without parallelism. They do not connect two lines.
  • Dropping the plane condition. The two-perpendiculars rule needs distinct lines in one plane; spatial directions need not match.
  • Marking the goal as given. Put parallel arrows on a starting diagram only when parallelism is supplied, not when it is the target.
  • Using an oblique distance. Point-to-line and parallel-line distances are measured along the specified perpendicular segment.

Key takeaways

  • A suitable corresponding or alternate pair of congruent angles proves two distinct coplanar lines parallel.
  • A suitable same-side pair of supplementary angles supplies another parallelism test.
  • Distinct coplanar perpendiculars to the same line are parallel; a perpendicular transversal to parallel lines is perpendicular to both.
  • Perpendicular segments define point-to-line distance and perpendicular separation between parallels.
  • An angle-copy construction produces a parallel only after the copied angles are identified as a valid converse pair.

A bit of history (optional)

In Book I of Euclid’s Elements, a reader reaches tests for parallel lines before reaching the construction of one. Proposition 27 supplies an alternate-angle test. In Proposition 31, Euclid chooses a point on the original line, joins it to the external point, and copies an angle there. The earlier test then verifies the new line. The construction is short on the page, but the angle-copy procedure it calls upon was built earlier in the book. David Joyce’s edition preserves the links between these propositions, so a reader can follow the chain of constructions and justifications rather than seeing only the finished picture. Read Euclid’s construction and its dependencies.

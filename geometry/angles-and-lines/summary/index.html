Angles and Parallel Lines: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Angle objects and measures
∠AOB\angle AOB∠AOB names an angle; m∠AOBm\angle AOBm∠AOB names its measure. Keep the vertex in the middle. Congruent angles have equal measures. Named openings are non-reflex unless specified otherwise.
Adjacent angles and linear pairs
Adjacent angles share a vertex and a side, with nonoverlapping interiors. A linear pair is adjacent with opposite noncommon rays.
Complementary and supplementary angles
Complementary measures total 90∘90^\circ90∘; supplementary measures total 180∘180^\circ180∘. Neither relationship requires adjacency.
Vertical angles
Opposite sectors formed by two distinct intersecting lines; their sides occur in opposite-ray pairs.
Parallel lines and a transversal
Parallel lines are distinct coplanar lines that never meet. A transversal crosses two coplanar lines at distinct points. Interior angles lie between the crossed lines; exterior angles lie outside that strip.
Corresponding angles
Angles in the same relative position at the two crossings of one transversal.
Alternate and same-side angles
Alternate interior or exterior pairs lie at two different crossings, on opposite sides of one transversal. Same-side interior or exterior pairs lie at two different crossings, on the same side of that transversal. Same-side interior angles are also called co-interior angles or consecutive interior angles. Family names specify positions, even without parallelism.
Converse
A statement exchanging a conditional’s hypothesis and conclusion. A forward theorem needs a separately justified converse before it can be reversed.
Perpendicular distance and foot
Point-to-line distance is the perpendicular segment’s length; its endpoint on the line is the foot. A point on the line has distance zero. Between parallels, use a specified joining segment perpendicular to both.
Triangle side classifications
A triangle has three noncollinear vertices. Scalene means no congruent sides; isosceles means at least two congruent sides; equilateral means three congruent sides. Every equilateral triangle is isosceles.
Triangle angle classifications
Acute means three acute interior angles; right means one right interior angle; obtuse means one obtuse interior angle.
Auxiliary line and angle chase
An auxiliary line is added to reveal a relationship. An angle chase reaches unknown measures through a sequence of justified angle relationships.
Exterior and remote interior angles
A triangle exterior angle lies between one side and an adjacent side’s continuation beyond their common vertex. Its remote interior angles are at the other two vertices; the interior angle at its own vertex is adjacent.

    e.g. Find the complement of 59∘59^\circ59∘: 31∘31^\circ31∘.

  • Linear-pair sum

    a+b=180∘a+b=180^\circa+b=180∘

    Use when The two angles must be adjacent with opposite noncommon rays. Conversely, adjacent supplementary angles have opposite outer rays and form a linear pair.

  • Vertical-angle theorem

    m∠AOB=m∠CODm\angle AOB=m\angle CODm∠AOB=m∠COD

    Use when Two distinct lines intersect at OOO, with A,O,CA,O,CA,O,C in that order on one and B,O,DB,O,DB,O,D in that order on the other. This is proved, not inferred from appearance.

  • Congruent complements and supplements

    Complements of congruent angles are congruent. Supplements of congruent angles are congruent.

    Use when Use the same partner type on both sides. This includes two complements, or two supplements, of the same angle.

  • Accepted angle addition and complete turns

    Consecutive parts add to the selected whole. A complete turn totals 360∘360^\circ360∘.

    Use when Count every sector exactly once, with no gap or overlapping interiors. A partial collection of angles around a point need not total a full turn.

  • Accepted Euclidean parallel foundations

    Through an external point in a line’s plane, exactly one parallel to that line exists. If same-side interior angles total less than 180∘180^\circ180∘, the crossed lines meet on that side when extended.

    Use when The angle form requires distinct coplanar lines crossed by a transversal at distinct points. These are accepted foundations; corresponding-angle congruence is a proved result.

  • Forward parallel-line angle relationships

    For parallel lines, corresponding, alternate interior, and alternate exterior angles are congruent. Same-side interior and same-side exterior angles are supplementary.

    Text description

    Two distinct parallel lines, l above m, are crossed by a transversal t that descends from upper left to lower right. At the upper intersection, the northwest, northeast, southeast and southwest sectors are one, two, three and four. At the lower intersection, those positions are five, six, seven and eight. Corresponding pairs are one and five, two and six, three and seven, and four and eight. Alternate interior pairs are three and five, and four and six. Alternate exterior pairs are one and seven, and two and eight. Same-side interior pairs are four and five, and three and six. Same-side exterior pairs are one and eight, and two and seven.

    Use when Use two distinct parallel lines and one transversal crossing at distinct points. In the locator, upper northwest, northeast, southeast, southwest sectors are 1, 2, 3, 4; lower sectors in that order are 5, 6, 7, 8.

  • Transversal converses

    One congruent corresponding or alternate pair proves parallelism. One supplementary same-side interior or exterior pair also proves parallelism.

    Use when The lines are distinct and coplanar, cut by the same transversal at distinct points. These are proved converses. A local vertical pair or linear pair does not test parallelism.

  • Planar parallel and perpendicular relationships

    Distinct coplanar perpendiculars to the same line are parallel. The perpendicular transversal theorem states that a transversal perpendicular to one of two parallels is perpendicular to the other. Two distinct lines parallel to a third are parallel to each other.

    Use when All lines involved lie in one plane. For the third claim, the three lines are pairwise distinct. These are proved results. The two-perpendiculars claim can fail without coplanarity; all three proofs here use one plane.

  • Triangle angle-sum theorem

    a+b+c=180∘a+b+c=180^\circa+b+c=180∘

    Use when a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c are the three interior measures of one nondegenerate Euclidean triangle; each is strictly between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘.

    e.g. Triangle angles are 53∘53^\circ53∘, 82∘82^\circ82∘, and ccc; find ccc: 45∘45^\circ45∘.

  • Third-angle theorem

    Two congruent angle pairs in two triangles force the remaining angle pair to be congruent.

    Use when Both triangles are nondegenerate Euclidean triangles. Match the given angle pairs first; this theorem supplies no side-length equality.

  • Right and obtuse angle restrictions

    At most one interior angle of a triangle is right or obtuse. In a right triangle, its two other angles are acute and complementary.

    Use when The triangle is nondegenerate. Use the complementary total only when a right angle is given or established.

Problem types, step by step

Classify an angle pair

  1. Check the vertex, shared side, interiors, and opposite rays for a position relationship.
  2. Separately check equality or a measure sum. More than one description may apply; a special sum supplies no adjacency.

Solve an angle equation

  1. Identify the whole opening or angle pair. A bisector supplies two equal halves.
  2. Use equality for congruent angles, 90∘90^\circ90∘ for complements, 180∘180^\circ180∘ for supplements or a linear pair, and 360∘360^\circ360∘ for a complete turn.
  3. Solve, substitute into every original expression, and check the measures against the stated geometry.

e.g. Find xxx for vertical measures (6x+5)∘(6x+5)^\circ(6x+5)∘ and (4x+51)∘(4x+51)^\circ(4x+51)∘: x=23x=23x=23, each angle 143∘143^\circ143∘.

Carry measures across parallel lines

  1. Name the particular parallel pair and transversal before locating the angle family.
  2. Use congruence for corresponding or alternate pairs, and supplementarity for same-side pairs.
  3. For several lines, transfer a matching sector one parallel pair at a time. For several transversals, follow each separately before combining adjacent parts.

e.g. Same-side interior measures of parallel lines are (8x+7)∘(8x+7)^\circ(8x+7)∘ and (9x+3)∘(9x+3)^\circ(9x+3)∘; find xxx: 101010, giving 87∘87^\circ87∘ and 93∘93^\circ93∘.

Establish parallelism or perpendicularity

  1. Check distinctness, coplanarity, and the two crossings. Identify a suitable converse pair, directly or through local angle facts.
  2. Establish the required congruence or supplementary sum and check that every sector measure is valid.
  3. Cite the proved converse. Use right angles and the planar perpendicular results when appropriate; add conclusion markings only afterward.

Identify a distance segment

  1. Find the endpoint on the line and the given perpendicular mark or established perpendicularity.
  2. Use that perpendicular segment’s length, with units, in the distance equation. An oblique joining segment does not define the distance.

Construct a parallel through an external point

  1. Work in one plane. Choose distinct A,BA,BA,B on ℓ\ellℓ. Join AAA to the external point PPP and extend to CCC in the order A,P,CA,P,CA,P,C.
  2. Copy ∠PAB\angle PAB∠PAB from ray PCPCPC, placing ray PQPQPQ on BBB’s side of APAPAP. Retain equal-radius and chord-transfer arcs.
  3. Extend PQPQPQ to a line. The congruent corresponding angles CPQCPQCPQ and PABPABPAB justify parallelism by the converse.
  4. Use the taught copying fact; its correctness proof is deferred to lesson 4.5.

Classify a triangle and find missing measures

  1. For side classification, compare stated side lengths or congruence marks. For angle classification, compare the established measures with a right angle.
  2. Form a triangle-sum equation, or a complementary equation for the two acute angles of an established right triangle. Represent ratios or differences algebraically when supplied.
  3. Solve and check all three positive interior measures and their total. Classify from the resulting measures.

Match third angles

  1. Match the two supplied congruent angle pairs, regardless of letter order.
  2. Identify the unused vertex in each triangle. Subtract the matching measures from the same triangle total to establish the remaining congruence.

Plan an angle chase

  1. Locate a triangle or opening containing the target and decide which intermediate measures it needs.
  2. Work forward from known measures using triangle sums, linear pairs, vertical angles, bisectors, or parallel-line relationships.
  3. When transferring an angle between triangles, verify that its defining rays agree. Give a reason for each move and check the whole configuration.

Calculate with an exterior angle

  1. Verify the side continuation and point order. Label the adjacent interior angle and the two remote interior angles.
  2. Add the remote measures for the exterior angle, or subtract a known remote measure from the exterior measure.
  3. Use a linear pair for the adjacent interior angle. If an interior ray divides the exterior opening, find the whole before subtracting a part.

Check exterior data or a turning record

  1. Express any unlisted interior measures using the exterior theorem and its adjacent supplement.
  2. Require every interior measure to be positive. Solve all restrictions together; a valid algebraic identity need not determine one variable value.
  3. For turning, verify the chosen traversal and consistent directions separately from the numerical total. Find a missing turn from the full-turn total and its interior neighbor from a straight-angle total.

Exam traps

  • Trap Calling supplementary angles a linear pair automatically

    Fix A linear pair also needs adjacency and opposite noncommon rays. Shared vertices alone do not exclude overlapping interiors.

  • Trap Equating a complement and a supplement of equal angles

    Fix Use two complements or two supplements for congruent partners. Mixing partner types uses different totals.

  • Trap Using an angle family name as an equation

    Fix A family describes position. For forward equalities or sums across crossings, establish parallelism first.

  • Trap Using a local fact to prove parallelism

    Fix Vertical angles and linear pairs hold at one crossing without parallelism. A converse must constrain both crossings.

  • Trap Treating a measurement or the goal’s markings as a given

    Fix Use exact givens or proved facts. Approximate readings suggest a conjecture and do not prove exact congruence.

  • Trap Dropping a plane condition or using an oblique distance

    Fix Keep coplanarity in the planar line theorems. Distance uses the specified perpendicular segment. Its shortest-length proof is deferred to 5.1; equal perpendicular separation is justified later with triangle congruence.

  • Trap Using a triangle formula on three collinear points

    Fix Triangle vertices must be noncollinear. Reject zero or negative interior measures even when an algebraic equation balances.

  • Trap Using side information as an unproved angle equality

    Fix Classify sides from stated side facts. Theorems connecting equal sides with equal opposite angles are proved in 4.3; third-angle congruence gives no side equality.

  • Trap Adding the exterior angle and its remote angles to get a straight angle

    Fix The exterior measure equals the remote sum. Only the adjacent interior angle supplements that exterior angle.

  • Trap Applying the exterior inequality to the adjacent interior angle

    Fix The exterior angle exceeds each remote interior angle separately. Its adjacent interior angle can be larger.

  • Trap Checking turn measures while mixing directions

    Fix The other exterior choice at one vertex has the same positive measure but reverses that vertex’s turn direction. A boundary turning record needs consistent choices.

Angle Bisectors and the Incenter

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Where could you put a circular disk inside a triangle so that it just reaches all three sides? Being equally far from the vertices answers a different question. Here we need equal distances to the sides, measured straight across to each side line.

Learning goals

  • Measure a point's distance to a side line along a perpendicular
  • Prove the internal angle-bisector equidistance theorem and its interior-point converse
  • Construct an angle bisector and justify the equal compass settings
  • Explain why three internal bisectors meet at the triangle's incenter
  • Determine the inradius and construct the incircle using a perpendicular foot

Distance goes straight across

The distance from a point to a line is the length of the perpendicular segment from the point to that line. Its endpoint on the line is the perpendicular foot. Any slanted segment to the line is longer, because it is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the perpendicular as a leg.

For example, if the perpendicular length is 6 and you move 8 along the line from its foot, the slanted length is 10 by Pythagoras. The distance to the line is still 6.

In a triangle, a supporting side line is the whole line containing a side segment. Extend the side when the perpendicular foot falls beyond a vertex. Distance to that line need not equal distance to the side segment: if the foot misses the segment, the nearest point of the segment is an endpoint.

Worked example 1 Choose the perpendicular measurement

Problem. Point PPP lies off line ℓ\ellℓ. Points XXX and YYY lie on ℓ\ellℓ, PXPXPX is perpendicular to ℓ\ellℓ, and the lengths are

PX=9 cmXY=12 cm.\begin{aligned} PX&=9\text{ cm}\\ XY&=12\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PXXY​=9 cm=12 cm.​

Find PYPYPY and the distance from PPP to ℓ\ellℓ.

Solution. The right triangle has legs 9 and 12.

PY2=92+122PY2=225PY=15 cm.\begin{aligned} PY^2&=9^2+12^2\\ PY^2&=225\\ PY&=15\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PY2PY2PY​=92+122=225=15 cm.​

The distance from PPP to ℓ\ellℓ is the perpendicular length, 9 cm9\text{ cm}9 cm. The longer slanted segment does not measure that distance.

Check your understanding

A perpendicular from P to a side line has length 7 cm. A slanted segment from P to that line has length 11 cm. What is P's distance to the line?

Answer choices

Distance to a line is the perpendicular length. The slanted segment reaches a different point on the line.

Equal angle halves give equal distances

An internal bisector puts a point equally far from the two side lines of an angle.

Take an angle of 60 degrees. A point on its internal bisector makes a 30-degree angle with each side ray. Dropping the two perpendiculars produces right triangles with the same hypotenuse and matching angles. They fit exactly, so their perpendicular legs have equal lengths.

The internal angle-bisector equidistance theorem says that a point on the internal bisector of an angle strictly between zero and 180 degrees has equal perpendicular distances to its two supporting side lines. The vertex itself has distance zero to both lines.

Why an internal angle bisector gives equal distances#

For a concrete case, let the full angle be 68 degrees and let OP=10OP=10OP=10. If PPP is on its internal bisector, each half-angle is 34 degrees. The perpendiculars PXPXPX and PYPYPY form right triangles OXPOXPOXP and OYPOYPOYP. Their right angles match, their 34-degree angles match, and the common side OPOPOP matches.

AAS makes the triangles congruent, so PX=PYPX=PYPX=PY.

Now let PPP be any point other than OOO on the internal bisector of ∠UOV\angle UOV∠UOV. Each half-angle is acute, so the perpendicular feet XXX and YYY lie on the forward rays OUOUOU and OVOVOV. In the two right triangles,

∠XOP≅∠POY∠OXP≅∠OYP.\begin{aligned} \angle XOP&\cong\angle POY\\ \angle OXP&\cong\angle OYP. \end{aligned}∠XOP∠OXP​≅∠POY≅∠OYP.​

Side OPOPOP is common. By AAS, the triangles are congruent. Corresponding perpendicular legs give PX=PYPX=PYPX=PY. No step depended on the chosen angle or hypotenuse length.

∎

Worked example 2 Use equality of distances

Problem. Point PPP is on the internal bisector of an angle. Its perpendicular distances to the two side lines are 2x+12x+12x+1 centimeters and 5x−85x-85x−8 centimeters. Find xxx and the common distance.

Solution. Equal distances give one equation.

2x+1=5x−89=3xx=3.\begin{aligned} 2x+1&=5x-8\\ 9&=3x\\ x&=3. \end{aligned}2x+19x​=5x−8=3x=3.​

Substitute into each given expression separately.

2(3)+1=75(3)−8=7.\begin{aligned} 2(3)+1&=7\\ 5(3)-8&=7. \end{aligned}2(3)+15(3)−8​=7=7.​

Both perpendicular lengths are 7 cm7\text{ cm}7 cm, a positive distance.

Check your understanding

P is on the internal bisector of angle UOV. X and Y are the perpendicular feet on its side lines. Which pair must have equal lengths?

Answer choices

The theorem compares perpendicular distances from P to the side lines. It does not compare P with arbitrarily chosen points U and V on the rays.

The converse needs an interior point

Equal perpendicular distances also locate an internal bisector, provided the point is inside the angle.

For instance, an interior point PPP has two perpendicular lengths of 5 and a vertex distance OP=13OP=13OP=13. Each right triangle then has its other leg equal to 12. The matching triangles show that OPOPOP divides the angle into equal halves.

The interior-point converse states that a point strictly inside an angle between zero and 180 degrees, equidistant from its two supporting side lines, lies on the internal bisector.

Why equal distances locate the internal bisector#

In the 5-12-13 case, the right triangles share hypotenuse 13 and have equal perpendicular legs 5. HL makes them congruent. When the feet are on the forward rays, their equal corresponding vertex angles are precisely the two parts of the original angle.

For a general interior point PPP, let XXX and YYY be the perpendicular feet on the full lines. Suppose PX=PYPX=PYPX=PY. First check the feet, rather than assuming their positions from a drawing.

Neither foot can be OOO. If one were, its perpendicular length would be OPOPOP, while the other perpendicular would be a leg of a nondegenerate right triangle with hypotenuse OPOPOP, hence shorter. The two feet cannot both be behind the vertex: that would make both parts of the interior angle at least 90 degrees.

If just one foot were behind the vertex, HL would still make the two right triangles congruent. The acute angle formed with that backward ray would equal the angle formed with the forward ray. The original two angle parts would then be supplementary, making the whole angle 180 degrees. This contradicts the hypothesis. Thus both feet lie on the forward rays.

The right triangles OXPOXPOXP and OYPOYPOYP have common hypotenuse OPOPOP and equal legs PX,PYPX,PYPX,PY. HL makes them congruent, giving

∠XOP≅∠POY.\angle XOP\cong\angle POY.∠XOP≅∠POY.

Those are the two interior angle parts, so ray OPOPOP is the internal bisector.

∎

For two intersecting full lines, external bisectors supply other equal-distance points. A point outside the angle cannot be put on its internal bisector by this converse. The optional Go deeper example shows the distinction on a right angle.

An exterior point can have equal perpendicular distances while missing the internal ray. The Go deeper section at the end shows one such point and where its feet land.

Worked example 3 Check the hypothesis before reversing

Problem. Point RRR is strictly inside ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC. Its perpendicular distances to lines BABABA and BCBCBC are both 4 cm4\text{ cm}4 cm. If ∠ABC=74∘\angle ABC=74^\circ∠ABC=74∘, find ∠ABR\angle ABR∠ABR and explain which condition selects the internal bisector.

Solution. The equal distances and interior location satisfy the converse. Ray BRBRBR is the internal bisector.

∠ABR=74∘2∠ABR=37∘.\begin{aligned} \angle ABR&=\frac{74^\circ}{2}\\ \angle ABR&=37^\circ. \end{aligned}∠ABR∠ABR​=274∘​=37∘.​

Being strictly inside the angle selects the internal ray. Equal distances to full lines alone would allow exterior possibilities.

Check your understanding

Which facts guarantee that OP is the internal bisector of an angle less than 180 degrees?

Answer choices

The converse needs both equal perpendicular distances and an interior point. Equal distances to chosen points concern a different locus.

Construct an angle bisector

Equal compass settings can make equal angle halves without a protractor.

  1. Center a circle arc at vertex OOO so it meets the two side rays at distinct points U,VU,VU,V. The equal radii give OU=OVOU=OVOU=OV.
  2. Keep that compass radius. Draw arcs centered at UUU and VVV. Besides OOO, they meet at a second point XXX inside the angle. Use that intersection, not OOO again.
  3. Draw ray OXOXOX with a straightedge.

Why the compass construction bisects the angle#

For an initial compass radius of 4 cm4\text{ cm}4 cm, the first arc gives equal lengths OU,OVOU,OVOU,OV. The two later arcs give equal lengths UX,VXUX,VXUX,VX, also 4 cm4\text{ cm}4 cm. The two triangles share OXOXOX, so SSS makes them congruent and their angles at OOO equal.

With any positive initial radius, the first circle gives OU=OVOU=OVOU=OV.

The equal settings in the second pair give UX=VXUX=VXUX=VX.

Side OXOXOX is common, so triangles OUXOUXOUX and OVXOVXOVX are congruent by SSS. Since XXX is the intersection inside the angle, the equal corresponding angles at OOO are its two interior halves. A zero angle or a straight angle is excluded from this construction.

∎

Worked example 4 Justify each compass equality

Problem. An arc centered at OOO meets the rays of an angle at U,VU,VU,V. Two arcs centered at U,VU,VU,V, drawn with a common radius large enough to cross inside the angle, meet at XXX. Name the three side equalities that prove ray OXOXOX bisects the angle. Must the second common radius equal the first radius?

Solution. The equalities for SSS are

OU=OVUX=VXOX=OX.\begin{aligned} OU&=OV\\ UX&=VX\\ OX&=OX. \end{aligned}OUUXOX​=OV=VX=OX.​

The first pair comes from the first arc, the second from equal settings for the two later arcs, and the third from the shared side. SSS gives equal angles at OOO. The second common radius may differ from the first, provided the arcs cross at a point strictly inside the angle. The two later settings must agree with each other.

Check your understanding

After the first arc marks U and V, which later step preserves the SSS justification?

Answer choices

The two new radii must agree to give UX=VX. Together with OU=OV and the common OX, this supplies SSS.

Three bisectors meet, and one circle fits

In any nondegenerate triangle, the three internal angle bisectors meet at one interior point. This point is the incenter.

The distance argument is short: a point on the first bisector has equal distances to two side lines. Being on a second bisector links the third distance to those two. The converse then puts the point on the third bisector.

Why the internal angle bisectors are concurrent#

First try distances of 3 cm3\text{ cm}3 cm. A point III on the bisector at AAA is 3 centimeters from ABABAB and ACACAC. If it is also on the bisector at BBB, its distance to BCBCBC is 3 centimeters too. If III is inside the triangle, the converse at CCC puts it on the third internal bisector.

Now establish the point and the general claim. The internal bisector from AAA meets segment BCBCBC at an interior point KKK. The internal bisector from BBB meets ACACAC at an interior point LLL. Points BBB and LLL lie on opposite sides of line AKAKAK, so segment BLBLBL crosses AKAKAK at a point III inside the triangle. Thus two internal bisectors really do meet there.

Let D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F be the feet from III to lines BC,CA,ABBC,CA,ABBC,CA,AB, respectively. The theorem at AAA gives IF=IEIF=IEIF=IE.

The theorem at BBB gives IF=IDIF=IDIF=ID.

Hence ID=IEID=IEID=IE. Since III lies inside angle CCC, the converse puts CICICI on its internal bisector.

The first two bisector lines are distinct, so they have only one intersection. This proves concurrency, meaning that all three pass through the same point.

∎

The common perpendicular distance is the inradius, written rrr. The incircle is the circle centered at the incenter with radius rrr; it lies inside the triangle and touches each side once.

The feet really are on the side segments. For side ABABAB, the bisector at AAA puts its foot on ray ABABAB, and the bisector at BBB puts that same foot on ray BABABA. The foot is therefore strictly between AAA and BBB. The same reasoning applies to the other sides, even when the triangle is obtuse.

To justify the circle, every point of line ABABAB other than FFF is farther from III than IFIFIF: Pythagoras makes the slanted segment longer. Thus the circle meets that side line exactly at FFF. The other two lines behave the same way. A point beyond a side line would be farther than rrr, since a segment from III to it must cross that line first. The whole circle therefore stays in the triangle.

To construct the incircle, construct any two internal bisectors and label their intersection III. Construct a perpendicular from III to line ABABAB and label its foot FFF. Set the compass to IFIFIF and draw the circle centered at III. Using IAIAIA instead would draw a circle through a vertex, not the incircle.

Use Back and Next to build the internal bisectors. Drag A or vary either base angle; BC stays fixed at 6 units. Follow the square marks to the three perpendicular feet, then compare their distances from I. These measured observations support the geometric argument above.

Worked example 5 Find the compass radius from a right triangle

Problem. III is the incenter of triangle ABCABCABC, and FFF is the perpendicular foot on side ABABAB. The lengths are

AI=13 cmAF=12 cm.\begin{aligned} AI&=13\text{ cm}\\ AF&=12\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}AIAF​=13 cm=12 cm.​

Find the inradius and the perpendicular distances from III to the other two sides. State the center and radius to use when drawing the incircle.

Solution. In right triangle AFIAFIAFI, AIAIAI is the hypotenuse.

IF2=132−122IF2=25IF=5 cm.\begin{aligned} IF^2&=13^2-12^2\\ IF^2&=25\\ IF&=5\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}IF2IF2IF​=132−122=25=5 cm.​

The inradius is 5 cm5\text{ cm}5 cm, and each other perpendicular distance is also 5 cm5\text{ cm}5 cm. Center the compass at III with radius IFIFIF, which is 5 cm5\text{ cm}5 cm.

Check your understanding

Two internal angle bisectors of triangle ABC meet at I. What completes a compass-and-straightedge construction of the incircle?

Answer choices

The radius must be the common perpendicular distance to the sides. Two internal bisectors already determine the incenter; the third need not be constructed.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Distance to a line: measure perpendicularly, not along a convenient slanted segment.
  • Converse: equal distances select the internal bisector only when the point is inside the angle.
  • Two kinds of bisector: the incenter uses angle bisectors and distances to sides. The circumcenter uses perpendicular bisectors and distances to vertices.
  • Incircle radius: use the segment to a perpendicular foot, not the segment to a vertex.
  • Earlier ratio theorem: dividing the opposite side proportionally is a different statement from equal perpendicular distances.

Key takeaways

  • An internal angle bisector gives equal perpendicular distances to the two supporting side lines.
  • An interior point with those equal distances lies on the internal bisector.
  • Equal compass settings build the congruent triangles that justify an angle-bisector construction.
  • The three internal bisectors meet at the incenter, which is inside every triangle.
  • The incircle has the incenter as center and the common perpendicular distance as radius.

Equal distances outside the angle

In a right angle, take a point QQQ above and to the left of the vertex, at perpendicular distance 4 from each supporting line. It lies outside the angle opening above and to the right. Its foot on the horizontal line is behind the vertex. It belongs to the external bisector line, so equal distances alone do not place it on the chosen internal ray.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid’s Elements sets a compass-and-straightedge challenge in its fourth book: place a circle inside a given triangle. The construction starts by bisecting two angles, then dropping three perpendiculars from their meeting point. Equal right triangles show why one compass setting reaches all three sides. The surviving text makes the compass choice a consequence of a proof, rather than a measurement taken from the picture. You can read the construction in Book IV, Proposition 4.

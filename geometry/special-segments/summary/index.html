Triangle Centers and Constructions: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Perpendicular bisector
The full line perpendicular to a segment through its midpoint. A segment bisector only passes through the midpoint; it need not be perpendicular.
Locus
The complete set of points satisfying a condition. Both theorem directions are needed to show that a proposed set has every required point and no extra points.
Supporting side line and perpendicular foot
The supporting line contains the entire side and both extensions. A perpendicular foot is where the perpendicular meets that line; it may miss the side segment.
Internal angle bisector
The ray dividing an angle into two equal interior angles. Its equal-distance property concerns perpendicular distances to the two supporting side lines.
Concurrent lines
Lines passing through one common point. Constructing two defining lines locates a candidate; the concurrence theorem places the third through that point.
Circumcenter OOO, circumcircle, circumradius RRR
The side perpendicular bisectors meet at OOO. Its circle passes through the three vertices, and its radius is a center-to-vertex distance.
Incenter III, incircle, inradius rrr
The internal angle bisectors meet at III. Its circle lies inside the triangle and touches each side once; its radius is the common perpendicular side distance.
Median and centroid GGG
A median joins a vertex to the opposite side midpoint. The three medians meet at GGG, which divides each median in the same ordered ratio.
Altitude, altitude line, orthocenter HHH
An altitude joins a vertex perpendicularly to the opposite supporting side line. The full altitude lines meet at HHH; their vertex-to-foot segments need not all contain HHH.
Uniform triangular sheet
A thin filled triangular region with mass distributed uniformly throughout, with no added weight. A wire boundary or a nonuniform sheet has a different balance model.

Formulas and theorems

  • Perpendicular-bisector theorem and converse

    For distinct A,BA,BA,B: being on the perpendicular bisector implies PA=PBPA=PBPA=PB; conversely, equal endpoint distances put PPP on that bisector.

    Use when The segment has positive length. The midpoint itself is included directly by its definition, even though connector triangles collapse there. Coincident endpoints instead give the whole plane as the equal-distance locus.

  • Internal angle-bisector equidistance theorem

    A point on an internal angle bisector has equal perpendicular distances to the two supporting side lines.

    Use when The angle is strictly between zero and 180∘180^\circ180∘. For a point other than the vertex, both perpendicular feet lie on the forward rays. At the vertex both line distances are zero.

  • Interior-point converse

    An interior point with equal perpendicular distances to the two side lines lies on the internal angle-bisector ray.

    Use when The point is strictly inside the specified angle, whose measure is between zero and 180∘180^\circ180∘. Equal distances to full lines alone also allow exterior possibilities; equal slanted distances do not meet the hypothesis.

  • Circumcenter and circumcircle

    OA=OBOB=OCR=OA\begin{gathered} OA=OB\\ OB=OC\\ R=OA \end{gathered}OA=OBOB=OCR=OA​
    Text description

    Acute triangle ABC has A and B along its lower side, with C above. M is the midpoint of AB and N is the midpoint of AC. Different tick patterns mark the two separate equal-half pairs. Dashed lines perpendicular to those sides at M and N intersect at O inside the triangle. The circle centered at O goes through all three vertices. The right-angle marks belong to the two side midpoints.

    Use when The vertices form a nondegenerate triangle. Two side perpendicular bisectors determine the unique center; the third is a consequence. The radius reaches a vertex, regardless of whether the center is inside.

  • Centroid division of a median

    AG=2GDAG=23ADGD=13AD\begin{gathered} AG=2GD\\ AG=\frac23 AD\\ GD=\frac13 AD \end{gathered}AG=2GDAG=32​ADGD=31​AD​
    Text description

    Scalene triangle ABC has side midpoints D on BC, E on CA, and F on AB. The three equal-half pairs use one, two, and three ticks respectively, so no equality between different sides is asserted. The medians AD, BE and CF meet at interior point G. On AD the order is A, G, D. The vertex portion AG is twice the midpoint portion GD. No right-angle mark or equal-angle mark is shown.

    Use when In a nondegenerate triangle, DDD is the opposite side midpoint and GGG is the centroid. The order is vertex AAA, then GGG, then midpoint DDD. A ratio on an arbitrary vertex-to-side segment is insufficient. If a median is also an altitude, its vertex lies on the opposite side’s perpendicular bisector, forcing equality of the two adjacent sides. One such median alone need not force an equilateral triangle.

  • Altitude concurrence

    The three full altitude lines have exactly one common point, the orthocenter HHH.

    Text description

    Triangle ABC has an obtuse angle at A above base BC. The altitude from A reaches D inside BC. The altitude from B reaches E on line AC extended beyond A. The altitude from C reaches F on line AB extended beyond A. Right-angle squares mark D, E and F. The complete altitude lines, including the extensions past their feet where necessary, meet at H above the triangle. H is distinct from all three feet.

    Use when The triangle is nondegenerate. Each line passes through its own vertex and is perpendicular to the opposite supporting line. Extend sides and altitude lines as required; a foot and the common intersection are different objects.

  • Center locations by angle type

    Acute: OOO and HHH are inside. Right: OOO is the hypotenuse midpoint, while HHH is the right-angle vertex. Obtuse: both are outside. The incenter III and centroid GGG remain strictly inside in all three cases.

    Use when For an obtuse angle at CCC, OOO lies across the opposite side ABABAB from CCC. Location of one altitude foot alone does not determine HHH. These statements concern nondegenerate triangles.

  • Equilateral coincidence and converse

    In a nondegenerate triangle, the centroid and orthocenter coincide exactly when the triangle is equilateral. In that case all four centers coincide.

    Use when In the equilateral case, each median line is also an internal angle bisector, altitude line, and perpendicular bisector. Conversely, GGG is interior and distinct from each vertex, so a common G=HG=HG=H makes every median line the corresponding altitude line. Each vertex then lies on the opposite side’s perpendicular bisector, giving adjacent-side equality at every vertex.

  • Geometric centroid and physical balance

    A uniform thin triangular sheet balances at its centroid.

    Use when Material fills the triangular region uniformly. Equal outline or equal total mass alone does not guarantee the same balance point for a wire frame or weighted sheet. The geometric centroid still depends on the vertices.

Problem types, step by step

Construct a segment midpoint and perpendicular bisector

  1. Use equal compass radii centered at the distinct endpoints, each radius greater than half the segment length.
  2. Label the two distinct crossings and join them as a full line. Equal radii put both crossings on the perpendicular bisector by the converse.
  3. Intersect that line with the segment to locate its midpoint. Mark equal halves and the justified right angle.

Construct a perpendicular at or from a point

  1. At a point on the line, use a circle centered there to select endpoints on opposite sides at equal distances. The given point is their midpoint.
  2. From a point off the line, choose a centered circle large enough to cut the line twice. The given point is equally far from those intersections.
  3. Construct the selected segment’s perpendicular bisector. In the off-line case, equal circles with radius to the given point supply it and a second crossing across the line.
  4. Draw the line through the given point and the off-line crossing; label its foot. A single touching point cannot supply the required two intersections.

Locate a circumcenter and draw its circle

  1. Construct perpendicular bisectors of two sides. Their nonparallel directions ensure one intersection OOO.
  2. Use endpoint equidistance twice to link all three vertex distances; the converse accounts for the third bisector.
  3. Center the compass at OOO with radius to any vertex. Check the expected acute, right, or obtuse location.

Recover the center of a given rotation

  1. Select two nonzero, nonparallel point-image chords from the same given nonidentity rotation.
  2. Construct their perpendicular bisectors. Each contains the already-given rotation center, so their unique intersection recovers it.
  3. Replace a fixed-point pair or coincident bisectors with a suitable pair. For arbitrary mappings, also check that the directed turn angles agree.

Construct an internal bisector and incircle

  1. At an angle vertex, draw an arc meeting both forward rays at distinct points. These first radii are equal.
  2. Draw equal-radius arcs from those points that meet strictly inside the angle. Join the vertex to that crossing; two equal side pairs and the shared connector justify SSS.
  3. For a triangle, construct a second internal bisector to locate III. Drop a perpendicular from III to a side line.
  4. Use the perpendicular length as the compass radius at III. The later common arc radius may differ from the first, but the paired settings must agree.

Use the correct equal-distance condition

  1. Decide whether the given equalities reach endpoints or are perpendicular lengths to side lines. Choose the matching theorem direction.
  2. For a reversed angle-bisector argument, verify interior location before identifying an internal ray. For an incenter candidate, link all three line distances.
  3. If a right triangle is available, identify its hypotenuse and any half-side before applying Pythagoras. Recover positive lengths and check the original geometric conditions.

e.g. Circumcenter OOO and side midpoint MMM have ABABAB of 22.2 cm and OMOMOM of 14.8 cm; find the radius: R=18.5R=18.5R=18.5 cm.

Find angles cut by internal bisectors

  1. Use the original triangle angle sum when a vertex angle is missing, then halve only the angles actually bisected.
  2. For an angle at III, use the angle sum in the smaller triangle containing III. Do not confuse a half-angle at a vertex with the angle at the incenter.

e.g. Triangle ABCABCABC has angles at A,BA,BA,B of 46∘46^\circ46∘ and 73∘73^\circ73∘; III is its incenter. Find ∠BIC\angle BIC∠BIC: 113∘113^\circ113∘.

Recover median parts and whole lengths

  1. Establish the opposite midpoint first, then write the order vertex, centroid, midpoint. Assign two ratio parts to the vertex portion and one to the midpoint portion.
  2. Use three parts for the whole median. Apply the division separately on different medians; their whole lengths need not agree.
  3. For expressions, solve the required ratio equation and reject zero, negative, or inconsistent lengths.

e.g. Medians AD,BEAD,BEAD,BE meet at GGG; ADADAD is 46.5 cm and BGBGBG is 17.4 cm. Find GDGDGD and BEBEBE: 15.5 cm and 26.1 cm, respectively.

Recall the dependencies in a concurrence argument

  1. Perpendicular bisectors: two endpoint equalities give the third by transitivity, then the converse supplies the remaining line.
  2. Internal bisectors: establish an interior crossing, link three perpendicular distances, then apply the interior-point converse.
  3. Medians: a midsegment gives a factor of two; AA transfers it to the median portions. Repeating the pairwise argument and using one unique division point includes the third median.
  4. Altitudes: construct the auxiliary parallels through the vertices. Parallelogram equalities and between-point order make the original vertices auxiliary-side midpoints; parallelism makes the altitudes auxiliary perpendicular bisectors. Apply concurrence to that auxiliary triangle.

Construct and interpret altitude lines

  1. From each chosen vertex construct a perpendicular to the complete opposite side line. Label the foot and retain necessary extensions.
  2. Intersect two full altitude lines to locate HHH; concurrence includes the third. Check angle type rather than assuming the crossing is inside.
  3. For lengths or angles, use the actual right triangles at the feet. For center comparisons, return to each defining line family.

e.g. Triangle ABCABCABC is right at BBB; locate HHH and OOO: HHH is BBB, while OOO is the midpoint of ACACAC.

Calculate in a right triangle made by an altitude

  1. Locate the right angle at the foot and identify the actual hypotenuse. A side portion and an altitude length are different measurements.
  2. Use Pythagoras with addition for a hypotenuse or subtraction for a leg. Take the positive root; use complements for the remaining acute angles.

e.g. Altitude ADADAD meets side BCBCBC at interior point DDD; ABABAB is 130\sqrt{130}130​ cm and BDBDBD is 7 cm. Find ADADAD: 9 cm.

Exam traps

  • Trap Treating any bisector or special segment as all the others

    Fix Require the actual midpoint, perpendicular, or equal-angle condition. A median need not be an altitude, and an altitude need not bisect the original side.

  • Trap Measuring a line distance along a slanted segment

    Fix Use the perpendicular foot on the full line. If it misses a side segment or forward ray, that line distance is not the distance to the segment or ray.

  • Trap Reversing equidistance without its hypotheses

    Fix Endpoint loci require distinct endpoints. The internal angle converse requires an interior point. Equal distances to three side lines identify the incenter only with interior location.

  • Trap Using a vertex distance as the incircle radius

    Fix Circumradii reach vertices; inradii reach perpendicular feet. Keep O,I,G,HO,I,G,HO,I,G,H for centers and give feet other names.

  • Trap Putting the two-to-one ratio in the wrong order

    Fix The vertex portion is longer. The whole has three parts; the centroid is not the median midpoint. Prove the segment is a median before using the ratio.

  • Trap Calling original altitude feet auxiliary midpoints

    Fix In the parallel-triangle proof, the original vertices are midpoints of auxiliary sides. Prove both midpoint placement and perpendicularity before invoking perpendicular-bisector concurrence.

  • Trap Using a drawing or measurement as a concurrence proof

    Fix Recall the established theorem and its dependencies. A near crossing, equal measured lengths, or one interior half-plane does not establish the full claim.

  • Trap Assuming all centers are interior or equal

    Fix Use the angle-type cases for O,HO,HO,H. Only I,GI,GI,G are always interior. Equilateral coincidence requires the equilateral condition; a right triangle already separates OOO from HHH.

  • Trap Replacing uniform sheet by uniform wire

    Fix The balance conclusion requires uniform material throughout the filled triangle. A boundary frame, added weight, or uneven sheet changes the mass distribution.

  • Trap Confusing the opposite-side ratio theorem with equidistance

    Fix The earlier angle-bisector ratio theorem divides the opposite side. Equidistance compares perpendicular distances to the two side lines.

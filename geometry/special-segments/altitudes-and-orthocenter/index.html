Altitudes and the Orthocenter

▾

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

From each corner of a triangle, draw straight across to the opposite side at a right angle. Sometimes the perpendicular reaches an extension of the side. The three resulting lines still meet at one point, but that point can move outside the triangle. The right angle controls this construction, rather than a midpoint or an equal angle split.

Learning goals

  • Construct triangle altitudes using the full supporting lines of opposite sides
  • Prove altitude concurrence by identifying perpendicular bisectors of an auxiliary triangle
  • Locate the orthocenter in acute, right, and obtuse triangles with geometric reasons
  • Calculate lengths using the right triangles created by an altitude
  • Compare four triangle centers and justify their coincidence in an equilateral triangle

A perpendicular to the opposite side line

An altitude of a nondegenerate triangle is a perpendicular segment from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side. Its endpoint on that line is the foot of the altitude. Its length is the perpendicular distance from the vertex to the line.

For example, a 5 cm perpendicular from AAA to line BCBCBC is an altitude of length 5 cm. A 7 cm slanted segment from AAA to that same line is not an altitude. The right angle, rather than the size of the length, is the defining condition.

The altitude line is the full line containing an altitude. We use these full lines when discussing their common intersection. In an obtuse triangle, some altitude segments lie outside the triangle, and their lines may need extending further to reach the common point.

Construct the foot, including an extension

Use the perpendicular-from-a-point construction from the first lesson of this chapter. Draw the complete line through the opposite side before deciding where its foot will fall. The paper’s horizontal direction has no role in the definition.

Worked example 1 Construct an exterior altitude

Problem. Triangle ABCABCABC has an obtuse angle at AAA. Construct the altitude from BBB with a compass and unmarked straightedge. Identify its foot EEE and explain why a side extension is needed.

Solution. Extend line ACACAC through AAA. Draw a circle centered at BBB with radius greater than BABABA. Since AAA lies inside this circle, line ACACAC cuts it at two distinct points, UUU and VVV.

Construct the perpendicular bisector of UVUVUV. The equal radii BUBUBU and BVBVBV put BBB on this bisector, so it is the perpendicular from BBB to line ACACAC. Its intersection with ACACAC is EEE, and BEBEBE is the required altitude.

The foot lies beyond AAA on the extension. If EEE were on ray ACACAC, the right triangle BAEBAEBAE would have the obtuse angle BACBACBAC at AAA, which is impossible. The foot cannot be AAA either, because the angle at AAA is not right.

Make a larger triangle to prove concurrence

The three altitude lines of any nondegenerate triangle are concurrent. Their common point is the orthocenter, usually labeled HHH.

To see why, build a triangle whose perpendicular bisectors are the original altitude lines. Through AAA draw the parallel to BCBCBC, through BBB the parallel to CACACA, and through CCC the parallel to ABABAB. Name their intersections so that AAA lies on QRQRQR, BBB on RPRPRP, and CCC on PQPQPQ.

Why the altitude lines meet at one point#

First take a triangle with ABABAB of 9 units, ACACAC of 10 units, and BCBCBC of 12 units. The parallels make parallelograms ABCQABCQABCQ and ACBRACBRACBR. Opposite sides in these give AQAQAQ and ARARAR the same length as BCBCBC, so each is 12 units. Thus AAA is the midpoint of the 24-unit auxiliary side QRQRQR. The two auxiliary halves at BBB are each 10 units, and those at CCC are each 9 units. Thus all three original vertices become midpoints. The altitude through each midpoint is perpendicular to the auxiliary side, so the three lines are the larger triangle’s perpendicular bisectors. Those meet at one point. The lengths 9, 10, and 12 are not needed for any step.

For any nondegenerate ABCABCABC, the three original side directions are distinct. Thus the pairwise intersections of the constructed parallels are well defined. To check their order on the line through AAA, translate AAA by the directed move from BBB to CCC. Its image is QQQ: it lies on the parallel through AAA to BCBCBC and on the parallel through CCC to BABABA. The reverse move puts RRR on the opposite ray from AAA. Hence AAA lies between QQQ and RRR. The same argument establishes the corresponding orders at BBB and CCC.

The parallel pairs make ABCQABCQABCQ, ACBRACBRACBR, and ABPCABPCABPC parallelograms. Their opposite-side equalities give

AQ=BC.AQ=BC.AQ=BC.

Also,

AR=BC.AR=BC.AR=BC.

Therefore AAA is the midpoint of QRQRQR. The remaining parallelogram equalities give

RB=AC.RB=AC.RB=AC.

And

BP=AC.BP=AC.BP=AC.

Thus BBB is the midpoint of RPRPRP. Finally,

PC=AB.PC=AB.PC=AB.

And

CQ=AB.CQ=AB.CQ=AB.

Thus CCC is the midpoint of PQPQPQ. Each side has positive length; in particular QQQ and RRR are distinct. The other auxiliary sides have different directions from QRQRQR, so PQRPQRPQR is a nondegenerate triangle.

The altitude line from AAA is perpendicular to BCBCBC. Since QRQRQR is parallel to BCBCBC, this same line is perpendicular to QRQRQR. It passes through its midpoint AAA, so it is the perpendicular bisector of QRQRQR.

Likewise, the altitude line from BBB is the perpendicular bisector of RPRPRP, and the altitude line from CCC is the perpendicular bisector of PQPQPQ.

The perpendicular-bisector concurrence theorem applies to PQRPQRPQR. Its unique circumcenter lies on all three original altitude lines. This proves both their concurrence and the uniqueness of their common point.

The orthocenter of ABCABCABC is the circumcenter of this auxiliary triangle. The proof does not identify it as the circumcenter of ABCABCABC itself.

Worked example 2 Use the auxiliary midpoint rather than an original midpoint

Problem. In the auxiliary construction above, BCBCBC is 14 cm long. Find AQAQAQ, ARARAR, and QRQRQR. Explain why the altitude line through AAA bisects QRQRQR, even though it need not bisect BCBCBC.

Solution. Opposite sides of ABCQABCQABCQ give

AQ=14 cm.AQ=14\text{ cm}.AQ=14 cm.

Opposite sides of ACBRACBRACBR give

AR=14 cm.AR=14\text{ cm}.AR=14 cm.

Since AAA is between QQQ and RRR,

QR=28 cm.QR=28\text{ cm}.QR=28 cm.

The midpoint of the auxiliary side is AAA itself. The altitude line passes through AAA and is perpendicular to QRQRQR. Nothing here establishes that its foot on the original side BCBCBC is a midpoint. Stages 2 and 3 show the two roles of the same line.

Check your understanding

What must be established before calling the altitude through A a perpendicular bisector of auxiliary side QR?

Answer choices

The midpoint condition comes from the parallelograms. Parallelism transfers perpendicularity from BC to QR. Together these give the perpendicular bisector of the auxiliary side.

The angle type decides the location

An altitude foot and the orthocenter are different points. The foot is on a supporting side line; the orthocenter is on all three altitude lines.

Worked example 3 Locate the orthocenter in three cases

Problem. Locate the orthocenter of triangle ABCABCABC in each case, and give a geometric reason.

Case (a): all three angles are acute.

Case (b): the angle at CCC is right.

Case (c): the angle at AAA is obtuse.

Solution. In case (a), the feet lie strictly inside the opposite sides. A foot beyond an endpoint would force a right triangle to have a second angle at least 90 degrees, contradicting an acute base angle. Two vertex-to-opposite-interior-point segments cross inside the triangle. Their crossing is HHH.

In case (b), ACACAC is perpendicular to BCBCBC, so the two legs are already altitude segments from AAA and BBB. They meet at CCC. The third altitude starts at CCC, so CCC is the orthocenter.

In case (c), the foot EEE from BBB lies on ACACAC extended past AAA, as in Example 1. Thus AAA and CCC lie on the same side of line BEBEBE; the line meets the triangle only at BBB. The altitude through AAA does not pass through BBB, because that would make the angle at BBB right. Their crossing HHH therefore lies outside the triangle. The altitude from CCC also has its foot beyond AAA on the other side line.

The three cases cover every nondegenerate triangle: HHH is inside for acute triangles, at the right-angle vertex for right triangles, and outside for obtuse triangles.

Check your understanding

A nondegenerate triangle has its orthocenter on its boundary. What follows?

Answer choices

The acute case puts H strictly inside and the obtuse case strictly outside. The remaining right case puts H at its right-angle vertex.

An altitude creates useful right triangles

Once a perpendicular is drawn, the earlier right-triangle tools apply. Keep the altitude length separate from the two lengths along the side.

Worked example 4 Find an altitude and the remaining side

Problem. In triangle ABCABCABC, point DDD lies between BBB and CCC, and ADADAD is perpendicular to BCBCBC. The lengths are

AB=13 cm.AB=13\text{ cm}.AB=13 cm.

Also,

BD=5 cm.BD=5\text{ cm}.BD=5 cm.

And

DC=9 cm.DC=9\text{ cm}.DC=9 cm.

Find ADADAD and ACACAC.

Solution. In right triangle ABDABDABD, ABABAB is the hypotenuse. The Pythagorean theorem gives

AD2=132−52AD2=144AD=12 cm.\begin{aligned} AD^2&=13^2-5^2\\ AD^2&=144\\ AD&=12\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}AD2AD2AD​=132−52=144=12 cm.​

Lengths are positive, so the negative square root is excluded. In right triangle ACDACDACD, ACACAC is the hypotenuse.

AC2=122+92AC2=225AC=15 cm.\begin{aligned} AC^2&=12^2+9^2\\ AC^2&=225\\ AC&=15\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}AC2AC2AC​=122+92=225=15 cm.​

Check your understanding

In Example 4, which segment is the hypotenuse of right triangle ACD?

Answer choices

The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle at D. In triangle ACD, that is AC, not the shared altitude AD.

Compare the four centers

Each center is selected by a different construction. A generic altitude has no midpoint or equal-angle promise.

CenterDefining linesWhat the point does
CircumcenterPerpendicular bisectors of the sidesIs equally far from the three vertices
IncenterInternal angle bisectorsIs inside and equally far from the supporting side lines
CentroidMediansIs inside and divides every median two to one from the vertex
OrthocenterAltitude linesIs inside, at a vertex, or outside according to the triangle’s angle type

The circumcenter is inside an acute triangle, at the hypotenuse midpoint in a right triangle, and outside an obtuse triangle. Thus a right triangle already separates the orthocenter from the circumcenter: one is its right-angle vertex, the other its hypotenuse midpoint.

Worked example 5 Explain the equilateral coincidence

Problem. Triangle ABCABCABC is equilateral, and DDD is the midpoint of BCBCBC. Prove that ADADAD is a median, an internal angle bisector, an altitude, and a perpendicular bisector. Explain why all four triangle centers coincide.

Solution. The midpoint condition makes ADADAD a median. Triangles ABDABDABD and ACDACDACD have equal side lengths ABABAB and ACACAC, equal side halves BDBDBD and DCDCDC, and the common side ADADAD. SSS makes them congruent, so the two angle parts at AAA are equal. Thus ADADAD is the internal angle bisector.

The two angles at DDD are also equal. They form a straight angle, so each is 90 degrees. Hence ADADAD is an altitude, and the line containing it is also a perpendicular bisector of BCBCBC.

Repeat at BBB and CCC. The three defining line families are now the same for all four centers, so their unique common point is shared.

Check your understanding

Which condition guarantees that all four triangle centers coincide?

Answer choices

In an equilateral triangle, each median is also an internal angle bisector, an altitude, and a perpendicular bisector. The four defining constructions therefore have the same common point.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Supporting line: extend the opposite side when the perpendicular foot lies beyond an endpoint.
  • Segment versus line: the altitude lines are concurrent even when their drawn segments do not reach the orthocenter.
  • Midpoint assumption: a right angle alone does not make an altitude a median or a perpendicular bisector of the original side.
  • Center selection: equal distances from vertices define the circumcenter. Altitude concurrence defines the orthocenter.
  • Equilateral case: the four constructions coincide here; do not carry that coincidence into every triangle.

Key takeaways

  • An altitude joins a vertex perpendicularly to the opposite supporting side line.
  • The three altitude lines meet at the orthocenter.
  • The auxiliary triangle turns those lines into perpendicular bisectors after its midpoint conditions are proved.
  • The orthocenter is inside an acute triangle, at the right-angle vertex of a right triangle, and outside an obtuse triangle.
  • The four triangle centers coincide in an equilateral triangle.

A bit of history (optional)

In Book I of Euclid’s Elements, Proposition 12 asks for a perpendicular from a point outside a line. Its construction starts with a seemingly surprising choice: pick another point on the far side of the line, then draw a circle centered at the original point through that far-side point. This makes the circle cross the line twice. Bisecting the segment between those crossings produces the required perpendicular.

The construction also shows how much a diagram can hide. David Joyce’s online edition of Proposition 12 points out that Euclid has not yet supplied all the line-and-circle intersection results needed to justify those crossings. A construction can be correct while its written foundations still leave a question to settle.

Perpendicular Bisectors and the Circumcenter

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

Place two dots on paper. Where could a third dot sit so that its distances to the first two match? One answer is the midpoint, but there are infinitely many more. Finding the whole set gives a reliable way to draw perpendiculars, locate a circle’s center, and recover a hidden rotation center.

Learning goals

  • Prove both directions of the perpendicular-bisector theorem, including its midpoint case
  • Construct segment bisectors and perpendiculars with a compass and straightedge
  • Justify concurrence of perpendicular bisectors and construct a triangle circumcircle
  • Explain circumcenter locations for acute, right, and obtuse triangles
  • Recover a given rotation center from suitable point-image pairs

Equal distances describe a line

The perpendicular bisector of a segment is the line perpendicular to it through its midpoint. A segment bisector passes through the midpoint; it need not be perpendicular.

Take a segment 8 cm long. Its midpoint is 4 cm from each end. A point 3 cm away from the midpoint along the perpendicular makes two right triangles, each with legs 3 cm and 4 cm. Pythagoras gives a 5 cm hypotenuse on each side. Moving along that perpendicular changes both distances together.

For two distinct points AAA and BBB, the perpendicular-bisector theorem says that every point on their perpendicular bisector is equally far from them. Its converse says that every point equally far from AAA and BBB lies on that line.

The perpendicular-bisector theorem and its converse#

Let MMM be the midpoint of ABABAB.

For the forward direction, first take PPP on the perpendicular bisector with PPP different from MMM. Triangles PMAPMAPMA and PMBPMBPMB share PMPMPM, have congruent halves MAMAMA and MBMBMB, and have a right included angle at MMM. SAS makes them congruent, so their corresponding sides PAPAPA and PBPBPB are equal. If PPP is MMM itself, the equal distances follow directly from the midpoint definition; there are no two triangles to compare.

For the converse, suppose first that PPP is off line ABABAB and its distances to the endpoints are equal. Triangles PMAPMAPMA and PMBPMBPMB have equal sides PAPAPA and PBPBPB, equal halves MAMAMA and MBMBMB, and common side PMPMPM. SSS makes their angles at MMM equal. Those angles form a linear pair, so each is a right angle. Thus PMPMPM is the perpendicular bisector.

If PPP is on line ABABAB, a point at or beyond either endpoint has unequal endpoint distances by segment addition. A point between the endpoints has equal distances only when it is their midpoint. The midpoint is on the perpendicular bisector.

The 3 cm and 4 cm lengths helped us see the result, but neither congruence argument depends on those lengths.

∎

A locus is the whole set of points satisfying a condition. Both directions matter when describing a locus: the perpendicular bisector is exactly the locus of points equally far from two distinct endpoints, with no missing points and no extra ones. If the endpoints coincide, every point has equal distances to them, so the locus is the whole plane instead.

Worked example 1 Use equidistance before algebra

Problem. MMM is the midpoint of ABABAB, and PMPMPM is perpendicular to ABABAB. The lengths of PAPAPA and PBPBPB, in centimeters, are given below. Find xxx and both lengths.

PA=3x+2PB=5x−12\begin{gathered} PA=3x+2\\ PB=5x-12 \end{gathered}PA=3x+2PB=5x−12​

Solution. PPP lies on the perpendicular bisector, so the two expressions measure equal lengths.

3x+2=5x−1214=2xx=7\begin{aligned} 3x+2&=5x-12\\ 14&=2x\\ x&=7 \end{aligned}3x+214x​=5x−12=2x=7​

Check your understanding

A point Q is equally far from distinct endpoints A and B. Which conclusion follows, even if Q lies on AB?

Answer choices

The converse gives the perpendicular-bisector conclusion. If Q lies on AB, it is the midpoint, which belongs to that perpendicular line too.

Build perpendiculars from equal radii

A compass can create equal distances without reading a ruler. To bisect a segment, draw two circles centered at its endpoints with the same radius, greater than half the segment length. For an 8 cm segment, a 5 cm opening works; a 4 cm opening produces only one touching point.

Call the two distinct crossings XXX and YYY. Each crossing is equally far from AAA and BBB, since the circles have equal radii. The converse puts both crossings on the perpendicular bisector. Two distinct points determine one line, so drawing line XYXYXY constructs it. Its crossing with ABABAB constructs the midpoint and therefore also bisects the segment.

Worked example 2 Draw a perpendicular at a point

Problem. A point PPP lies on a given line. Construct a perpendicular to the line at PPP using a compass and unmarked straightedge, and justify the construction.

Solution. Draw a circle centered at PPP with any positive radius. Let its intersections with the line be AAA and BBB. They lie on opposite sides of PPP with equal distances from it, so PPP is their midpoint.

From AAA and BBB, draw equal-radius arcs with an opening greater than half ABABAB. Choose a crossing XXX off the line. Draw PXPXPX.

The converse puts XXX on the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB. That bisector also passes through the known midpoint PPP, so line PXPXPX is perpendicular at PPP.

For a perpendicular from a point off a line, begin with a circle centered at that point PPP. Choose its radius large enough to cut the line in two distinct points AAA and BBB. Then PPP is equally far from them. Circles centered at AAA and BBB with radius APAPAP already pass through PPP and meet again at a second point XXX, on the other side of the line. The strict triangle inequality in nondegenerate triangle APBAPBAPB ensures that this radius is greater than half ABABAB.

Both PPP and XXX are on the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB, so PXPXPX is the required perpendicular. Its crossing with the given line is the perpendicular foot. Extend a given segment to its supporting line if necessary. A circle that merely touches the line supplies only one point and cannot start this construction.

Check your understanding

For distinct endpoints A and B, which equal compass radius guarantees two distinct circle crossings?

Answer choices

Greater than half AB makes the circles overlap in two points. Exactly half AB makes them touch once; less than half AB keeps them apart.

One circle through three vertices

Two equal-distance conditions can locate a single point. If a point is 7 cm from both AAA and BBB, and also 7 cm from CCC, it can be the center of a circle through all three. The issue is finding that point without guessing the radius.

Lines are concurrent when they pass through one common point. In every nondegenerate triangle, its three perpendicular bisectors are concurrent. Their common point is the circumcenter, and the circle centered there through the three vertices is the circumcircle. The common vertex distance is the circumradius. These distances go to vertices, not to side lines.

Why the three perpendicular bisectors concur#

Construct the perpendicular bisectors of ABABAB and ACACAC. They cannot be parallel: two lines perpendicular to parallel lines are parallel, so parallel bisectors would force ABABAB and ACACAC to be parallel. Those sides meet at AAA and are distinct because the triangle is nondegenerate. The two bisectors therefore meet at one point OOO.

The forward theorem applied to the first bisector gives

OA=OB.OA=OB.OA=OB.

Applied to the second, it gives

OA=OC.OA=OC.OA=OC.

Thus OBOBOB and OCOCOC are equal. The converse places OOO on the perpendicular bisector of BCBCBC. All three pass through OOO. Any other point equally far from all three vertices would have to lie on the first two bisectors, whose intersection is unique.

∎

Use Back to return to the triangle, then Next to build the perpendicular bisectors. Drag vertex A or change the base angles while BC stays fixed at 6 units. Before choosing a right or obtuse case, predict which side of the triangle will contain O. The displayed measurements illustrate the proof above.

To construct the circumcircle, keep the first two bisectors, set the compass to OAOAOA, and draw the circle centered at OOO. The proof establishes that it also passes through BBB and CCC. The third bisector is a useful check, but is not needed to locate OOO.

Worked example 3 Connect a radius to a right triangle

Problem. OOO is the circumcenter of triangle ABCABCABC. MMM is the midpoint of ABABAB, whose length is 24 cm. The length of OMOMOM is 5 cm. Find the circumradius and explain why the same radius reaches CCC.

Solution. The bisector through MMM is perpendicular to ABABAB. Half of the 24 cm side is 12 cm, so triangle OMAOMAOMA is right with legs 5 cm and 12 cm.

OA2=52+122OA2=169OA=13 cm\begin{aligned} OA^2&=5^2+12^2\\ OA^2&=169\\ OA&=13\text{ cm} \end{aligned}OA2OA2OA​=52+122=169=13 cm​

By the circumcenter theorem, OCOCOC has the same length as OAOAOA. Thus the circle with center OOO and radius 13 cm reaches every vertex.

Check your understanding

O lies on the perpendicular bisectors of AB and AC in a nondegenerate triangle. Which statement completes the proof for the third side?

Answer choices

The first two bisectors make OA equal OB and OA equal OC. Transitivity gives equal distances to B and C; the converse supplies the third bisector. Inside location is unnecessary.

Where the center lies

A triangle’s circumcenter need not lie inside it. In a triangle with angles 50, 60, and 70 degrees it is inside. If the largest angle opens to 90 degrees, the center reaches the opposite side’s midpoint. If it opens past 90 degrees, the center crosses that side to the outside.

Here is a justification using midpoint parallels and triangle angles. Name a longest side ABABAB, so the other vertex is CCC. The angles at AAA and BBB are acute, since the largest angle is at CCC. Let MMM and NNN be the midpoints of ABABAB and ACACAC. The midsegment theorem makes MNMNMN parallel to BCBCBC. Thus triangle AMNAMNAMN has the same three angles as triangle ABCABCABC, in the same order.

Circumcenter locations from midpoint parallels#

Write the angles at AAA, BBB, and CCC as α\alphaα, β\betaβ, and γ\gammaγ. In triangle AMNAMNAMN, the angles at AAA, MMM, and NNN have those respective measures. The circumcenter is the intersection of the perpendicular at MMM to AMAMAM and the perpendicular at NNN to ANANAN.

The perpendicular foot from NNN to ABABAB is between AAA and MMM, because both endpoint angles of ABCABCABC are acute and NNN is halfway along ACACAC. Draw the ray through NNN parallel to ABABAB toward BBB. It reaches the perpendicular through MMM. The ray from NNN perpendicular to ANANAN on that same side of ANANAN makes an acute angle of 90∘−α90^\circ-\alpha90∘−α below the parallel ray. The same-side interior angles with the perpendicular through MMM sum to less than 180 degrees, so the two rays meet on that side by the parallel postulate. Call their intersection OOO. At NNN, ray NONONO makes 90 degrees with NANANA.

If γ\gammaγ is 70 degrees, the perpendicular ray lies beyond NMNMNM by 20 degrees, across line MNMNMN from AAA. It therefore meets the perpendicular through MMM on the same side of ABABAB as NNN and CCC. In general, this gap is 90∘−γ90^\circ-\gamma90∘−γ when γ\gammaγ is acute. Equivalently, an intersection below ABABAB would make triangle MNOMNOMNO have angle 90∘+β90^\circ+\beta90∘+β at MMM and angle γ−90∘\gamma-90^\circγ−90∘ at NNN, which is impossible for acute γ\gammaγ.

If γ\gammaγ is 90 degrees, NMNMNM itself is perpendicular to ANANAN, so the intersection is MMM.

If γ\gammaγ is 110 degrees, the perpendicular ray lies inside angle ANMANMANM, 20 degrees short of NMNMNM. It meets the perpendicular through MMM across ABABAB from CCC. In general, that gap is γ−90∘\gamma-90^\circγ−90∘ when γ\gammaγ is obtuse. An intersection on CCC‘s side would instead require the negative angle 90∘−γ90^\circ-\gamma90∘−γ at NNN.

For an acute triangle, the same argument can start with each of its three sides, since all endpoint angles are acute. OOO is therefore on the vertex side of every supporting side line. The intersection of those three open half-planes is exactly the triangle’s interior. For an obtuse triangle, being across the longest side from CCC already puts OOO outside the triangle. In the right case, MMM is on its hypotenuse.

∎

Worked example 4 Recognize the right-triangle center

Problem. A right triangle has legs 9 cm and 12 cm. Locate its circumcenter and find its circumradius.

Solution. Its hypotenuse length follows from Pythagoras.

c2=92+122c2=225c=15 cm\begin{aligned} c^2&=9^2+12^2\\ c^2&=225\\ c&=15\text{ cm} \end{aligned}c2c2c​=92+122=225=15 cm​

The right-case proof puts the circumcenter at the hypotenuse midpoint. Its distance to a hypotenuse endpoint is half 15 cm, so the circumradius is 7.5 cm. This conclusion depends on the triangle being right.

Check your understanding

A triangle has an obtuse angle at C. Where is its circumcenter?

Answer choices

AB is opposite the obtuse angle and is the longest side. The midpoint-parallel argument puts the two bisectors' intersection on the opposite side of AB from C.

Recover a rotation center

A rotation keeps each point’s distance from its center. For example, if a 90 degree rotation carries a point 6 cm from the center to its image, the image remains 6 cm from the center. The center is therefore equally far from the two endpoints of their point-image chord [the segment joining the point to its image]. The converse puts the center on that chord’s perpendicular bisector.

Worked example 5 Justify the hidden-center construction

Problem. One given nonidentity rotation carries AAA to A′A'A′ and BBB to B′B'B′. Both point-image segments have nonzero length and are not parallel. Construct the center and justify why the construction recovers that rotation’s center.

Solution. Construct the perpendicular bisector of AA′AA'AA′, then that of BB′BB'BB′. Call their intersection OOO.

The given rotation has a center equally far from AAA and A′A'A′, so that center belongs to the first bisector. It is also equally far from BBB and B′B'B′, so it belongs to the second. Nonparallel chords have nonparallel perpendicular bisectors, which meet at exactly one point. The given center must therefore be OOO.

A fixed point produces a zero-length chord and cannot supply a bisector. Two unsuitable chords can have the same bisector, so choose another point-image pair if that occurs. For pairs known to come from one rotation, distinct chord bisectors already share its center; hence they cannot be distinct parallel lines.

This argument uses the fact that one rotation was given. Equal radii for two arbitrary pairs do not prove that their directed turn angles agree. Check the angle and direction as well if the task is to decide whether arbitrary pairs describe one rotation.

Check your understanding

Two arbitrary point pairs have equal endpoint distances from O. What extra check is needed before claiming one rotation about O maps both pairs?

Answer choices

A rotation has one angle and direction for every moved point. Equal radii establish possible rotation paths separately, but the two required turns may differ.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Bisection alone: Passing through a midpoint does not guarantee a right angle.
  • One compass crossing: A tangent pair supplies only one point; the segment construction needs two distinct crossings.
  • Center location: The circumcenter can be inside, on, or outside the triangle.
  • Distances to sides: Circumcenter distances go to vertices, not perpendicular feet on the sides.
  • Arbitrary rotation pairs: Equal radii alone do not establish one common directed angle.

Key takeaways

  • Equidistance: For distinct endpoints, a point lies on their perpendicular bisector exactly when its endpoint distances match.
  • Constructions: Equal-radius crossings make points satisfying that condition; two distinct points determine the constructed line.
  • Circumcenter: Two side bisectors locate the unique point equally far from all three vertices.
  • Location: Acute triangles put it inside, right triangles at the hypotenuse midpoint, and obtuse triangles outside.
  • Rotation center: Suitable point-image chords from one given nonidentity rotation locate its center through their bisectors.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid’s Book IV, Proposition 5 asks how to draw a circle around a given triangle. Its construction bisects two sides, draws perpendiculars there, and joins the intersection to the vertices. It treats the cases where that intersection is inside, on a side, and outside separately. David Joyce’s commentary notes a small gap in Euclid’s presentation: it does not justify why the two perpendiculars meet. The nonparallel-line argument in this lesson supplies that missing step. You can read the construction and the commentary in Joyce’s edition of Proposition IV.5.

Distance and Midpoint

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A sketch can make two segments look equal even when they are not. Coordinates give you a way to settle the question exactly. You have used distance and midpoint formulas in Algebra. Here the right triangle and the halfway point will explain those formulas, then help you recover missing points and check congruence.

Learning goals

  • Derive straight-line distance from perpendicular coordinate gaps and the Pythagorean theorem
  • Locate a midpoint and explain why both coordinates are averaged
  • Recover missing endpoint coordinates and check all geometric conditions
  • Justify numerical triangle congruence by matching three exact side lengths

From coordinates to a right triangle

An ordered pair gives horizontal position first, then vertical position. Use the same unit length on both perpendicular axes. For the points (−4,2)(-4,2)(−4,2) and (2,−6)(2,-6)(2,−6), the horizontal change is 666 and the vertical change is −8-8−8. The negative sign means down; the vertical length is 888.

The two perpendicular legs let you measure the direct segment with the Pythagorean theorem:

AB2=62+82,AB2=100,AB=10.\begin{aligned} AB^2&=6^2+8^2,\\ AB^2&=100,\\ AB&=10. \end{aligned}AB2AB2AB​=62+82,=100,=10.​

Adding 666 and 888 would measure a bent route through HHH, not the straight segment.

From (−2,3)(-2,3)(−2,3) to (5,−1)(5,-1)(5,−1), what are the horizontal and vertical leg lengths of the coordinate right triangle?

Answer choices

Subtract corresponding coordinates, then take the sizes of the changes.

5−(−2)=7,−1−3=−4.\begin{aligned}5-(-2)&=7,\\-1-3&=-4.\end{aligned}5−(−2)−1−3​=7,=−4.​

The movement is down four units, but a leg length is nonnegative. The lengths are 7 and 4.

Distance from perpendicular coordinate gaps#

Take a fresh pair U=(−3,1)U=(-3,1)U=(−3,1) and V=(9,6)V=(9,6)V=(9,6). The corner K=(9,1)K=(9,1)K=(9,1) gives perpendicular legs of lengths 121212 and 555. The Pythagorean theorem gives

UV2=122+52,UV2=169.\begin{aligned} UV^2&=12^2+5^2,\\ UV^2&=169. \end{aligned}UV2UV2​=122+52,=169.​

Thus the direct distance is 131313.

Take endpoints AAA at (xA,yA)(x_A,y_A)(xA​,yA​) and BBB at (xB,yB)(x_B,y_B)(xB​,yB​). The corner is HHH at (xB,yA)(x_B,y_A)(xB​,yA​). When both coordinates change, the legs are perpendicular and have lengths ∣xB−xA∣|x_B-x_A|∣xB​−xA​∣ and ∣yB−yA∣|y_B-y_A|∣yB​−yA​∣. The theorem gives

AB2=(xB−xA)2+(yB−yA)2.AB^2=(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2.AB2=(xB​−xA​)2+(yB​−yA​)2.

Absolute values disappear on squaring because a number and its opposite have the same square. Distance is nonnegative, so

AB=(xB−xA)2+(yB−yA)2.AB=\sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2}.AB=(xB​−xA​)2+(yB​−yA​)2​.

The actual coordinate values did not matter; the perpendicular gaps did. If the segment is horizontal or vertical, one gap is zero and this expression reduces to the other gap’s absolute value. No nondegenerate right triangle is needed in those cases. If the endpoints coincide, both gaps are zero and the distance is zero. Reversing the endpoints changes both signs but leaves both squares unchanged.

∎

This is the distance formula. It measures a length, so its output is a number in the coordinate unit, not an ordered pair.

Worked example 1 Keep a slanted length exact

Problem. The endpoints of segment ABABAB are (−5,−3)(-5,-3)(−5,−3) and (1,6)(1,6)(1,6). Find its exact length in simplest radical form.

Solution. The horizontal gap is 666 units and the vertical gap is 999 units. Thus

AB2=62+92,AB2=117,AB=117,AB=313.\begin{aligned} AB^2&=6^2+9^2,\\ AB^2&=117,\\ AB&=\sqrt{117},\\ AB&=3\sqrt{13}. \end{aligned}AB2AB2ABAB​=62+92,=117,=117​,=313​.​

The square factor 999 comes out of the radical as 333. The radical is an exact length; no decimal is needed.

Check your understanding

Find the exact distance between (−4,−1)(-4,-1)(−4,−1) and (0,5)(0,5)(0,5).

Answer choices

The perpendicular gaps are 4 and 6.

d2=42+62,d2=52,d=213.\begin{aligned}d^2&=4^2+6^2,\\d^2&=52,\\d&=2\sqrt{13}.\end{aligned}d2d2d​=42+62,=52,=213​.​

The sum of the squares is the squared distance. Take its nonnegative root.

Why a midpoint averages both coordinates

A midpoint is the point on a segment that divides it into two equal lengths. Halfway along a straight segment means making half its horizontal change and half its vertical change together.

For instance, from (−6,−2)(-6,-2)(−6,−2) to (4,6)(4,6)(4,6) the changes are 101010 right and 888 up. Starting at the first endpoint, half of each change gives (−1,2)(-1,2)(−1,2). A dilation centered at the first endpoint with scale factor 1/21/21/2 puts this point on the segment and makes its distance from that endpoint half the whole length. The other half is the remaining segment.

Midpoint coordinates from a half-size dilation#

For a fresh pair U=(−5,9)U=(-5,9)U=(−5,9) and V=(11,−3)V=(11,-3)V=(11,−3), the signed coordinate changes are 161616 and −12-12−12. A half-size dilation from UUU halves both changes:

xM=−5+8,xM=3,yM=9−6,yM=3.\begin{aligned} x_M&=-5+8,\\ x_M&=3,\\ y_M&=9-6,\\ y_M&=3. \end{aligned}xM​xM​yM​yM​​=−5+8,=3,=9−6,=3.​

It puts M=(3,3)M=(3,3)M=(3,3) on UVUVUV at half its length, rather than merely somewhere equally far from the endpoints.

For general distinct endpoints AAA at (xA,yA)(x_A,y_A)(xA​,yA​) and BBB at (xB,yB)(x_B,y_B)(xB​,yB​), the same dilation halves both coordinate changes. Name the midpoint MMM and its coordinates (xM,yM)(x_M,y_M)(xM​,yM​). Then

xM=xA+xB−xA2,xM=xA+xB2.\begin{aligned} x_M&=x_A+\frac{x_B-x_A}{2},\\ x_M&=\frac{x_A+x_B}{2}. \end{aligned}xM​xM​​=xA​+2xB​−xA​​,=2xA​+xB​​.​

The vertical calculation has the same form:

yM=yA+yB−yA2,yM=yA+yB2.\begin{aligned} y_M&=y_A+\frac{y_B-y_A}{2},\\ y_M&=\frac{y_A+y_B}{2}. \end{aligned}yM​yM​​=yA​+2yB​−yA​​,=2yA​+yB​​.​

No step depended on the example’s values or on the signs of the changes. A dilation works in any direction, including horizontal and vertical segments. The point stays on the segment and cuts its length in half.

∎

The midpoint formula is therefore

M=(xA+xB2,yA+yB2).M=\left(\frac{x_A+x_B}{2},\frac{y_A+y_B}{2}\right).M=(2xA​+xB​​,2yA​+yB​​).

Average matching coordinates; do not average an x-coordinate with a y-coordinate. Fractional coordinates are allowed. If the endpoints coincide, the same average returns that point, although there are no two positive half-segments.

Worked example 2 Move a vertical endpoint

Problem. Segment ABABAB has endpoints AAA at (−3,−5)(-3,-5)(−3,−5) and BBB at (−3,7)(-3,7)(−3,7). Find its length and midpoint. Then move BBB to CCC at (−3,3)(-3,3)(−3,3) and find the length and midpoint of ACACAC.

Solution. For the original segment, the x-coordinate stays −3-3−3. Call its midpoint MMM. Its length and midpoint are

AB=∣7−(−5)∣,AB=12,M=(−3,1).\begin{aligned} AB&=|7-(-5)|,\\ AB&=12,\\ M&=(-3,1). \end{aligned}ABABM​=∣7−(−5)∣,=12,=(−3,1).​

The midpoint’s height is the average of −5-5−5 and 777. Each half is 666 units.

For the moved endpoint, the segment is still vertical. Call the new midpoint NNN:

AC=∣3−(−5)∣,AC=8,N=(−3,−1).\begin{aligned} AC&=|3-(-5)|,\\ AC&=8,\\ N&=(-3,-1). \end{aligned}ACACN​=∣3−(−5)∣,=8,=(−3,−1).​

Here each half is 444 units. Moving just the upper endpoint down 444 units moved the midpoint down 222 units.

What is the midpoint of the segment joining (5,−6)(5,-6)(5,−6) and (−2,3)(-2,3)(−2,3)?

Answer choices

Average horizontal coordinates and vertical coordinates separately.

xM=(5−2)/2,xM=3/2,yM=(−6+3)/2,yM=−3/2.\begin{aligned}x_M&=(5-2)/2,\\x_M&=3/2,\\y_M&=(-6+3)/2,\\y_M&=-3/2.\end{aligned}xM​xM​yM​yM​​=(5−2)/2,=3/2,=(−6+3)/2,=−3/2.​

The midpoint is (3/2,−3/2)(3/2,-3/2)(3/2,−3/2).

Recover an endpoint

A midpoint tells you how far to continue beyond it. Repeat the horizontal and vertical changes from the known endpoint to the midpoint, continuing in the same direction.

Worked example 3 Use the midpoint in reverse

Problem. Point MMM at (2,−3)(2,-3)(2,−3) is the midpoint of ABABAB. One endpoint is AAA at (−4,5)(-4,5)(−4,5). Find BBB and check the midpoint.

Solution. The two coordinate averages must equal the given midpoint coordinates. Solve them separately:

−4+xB2=2,−4+xB=4,xB=8.\begin{aligned} \frac{-4+x_B}{2}&=2,\\ -4+x_B&=4,\\ x_B&=8. \end{aligned}2−4+xB​​−4+xB​xB​​=2,=4,=8.​5+yB2=−3,5+yB=−6,yB=−11.\begin{aligned} \frac{5+y_B}{2}&=-3,\\ 5+y_B&=-6,\\ y_B&=-11. \end{aligned}25+yB​​5+yB​yB​​=−3,=−6,=−11.​

So BBB is at (8,−11)(8,-11)(8,−11). Averaging the endpoint coordinates gives (2,−3)(2,-3)(2,−3) again. The changes from MMM to BBB are the same as from AAA to MMM, which also checks the straight continuation.

In general, multiply each midpoint coordinate by 222, then subtract the known endpoint coordinate. Check both coordinates afterward.

Check your understanding

The midpoint of ABABAB is (−1,4)(-1,4)(−1,4). Endpoint AAA is at (3,−2)(3,-2)(3,−2). Where is BBB?

Answer choices

Twice the midpoint gives the sum of the endpoints, coordinate by coordinate.

xB=2(−1)−3,xB=−5,yB=2(4)−(−2),yB=10.\begin{aligned}x_B&=2(-1)-3,\\x_B&=-5,\\y_B&=2(4)-(-2),\\y_B&=10.\end{aligned}xB​xB​yB​yB​​=2(−1)−3,=−5,=2(4)−(−2),=10.​

The average of (3,−2)(3,-2)(3,−2) and (−5,10)(-5,10)(−5,10) is (−1,4)(-1,4)(−1,4), as required.

Use exact lengths to check congruence

Coordinates can establish all three side pairs in the SSS congruence test. Equal squared lengths are enough: nonnegative lengths with equal squares are equal. Rounded decimals that merely look equal do not establish congruence.

Worked example 4 A numerical SSS check

Problem. Triangle ABCABCABC has vertices AAA at (−1,−2)(-1,-2)(−1,−2), BBB at (3,−1)(3,-1)(3,−1), and CCC at (0,2)(0,2)(0,2). Triangle DEFDEFDEF has vertices DDD at (8,0)(8,0)(8,0), EEE at (7,4)(7,4)(7,4), and FFF at (4,1)(4,1)(4,1). Decide whether △ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF in that order. Justify all three side matches.

Solution. The proposed correspondence pairs ABABAB with DEDEDE, ACACAC with DFDFDF, and BCBCBC with EFEFEF. Compute their squares:

AB2=42+12,AB2=17,DE2=(−1)2+42,DE2=17.\begin{aligned} AB^2&=4^2+1^2,\\ AB^2&=17,\\ DE^2&=(-1)^2+4^2,\\ DE^2&=17. \end{aligned}AB2AB2DE2DE2​=42+12,=17,=(−1)2+42,=17.​AC2=12+42,AC2=17,DF2=(−4)2+12,DF2=17.\begin{aligned} AC^2&=1^2+4^2,\\ AC^2&=17,\\ DF^2&=(-4)^2+1^2,\\ DF^2&=17. \end{aligned}AC2AC2DF2DF2​=12+42,=17,=(−4)2+12,=17.​BC2=(−3)2+32,BC2=18,EF2=(−3)2+(−3)2,EF2=18.\begin{aligned} BC^2&=(-3)^2+3^2,\\ BC^2&=18,\\ EF^2&=(-3)^2+(-3)^2,\\ EF^2&=18. \end{aligned}BC2BC2EF2EF2​=(−3)2+32,=18,=(−3)2+(−3)2,=18.​

Each named pair has the same exact length. Both triples form nondegenerate triangles. Their two equal sides have length 17\sqrt{17}17​, and the third has length 18\sqrt{18}18​. The two equal sides add to more than the third because 68>1868>1868>18 when those positive sums are squared. The other two inequalities each compare 17+18\sqrt{17}+\sqrt{18}17​+18​ with 17\sqrt{17}17​, so they also hold. SSS proves the stated congruence. Two matching sides alone would not have completed this check.

Check your understanding

For two nondegenerate triangles with a stated vertex correspondence, which coordinate calculation completes an SSS congruence check?

Answer choices

Compute the three corresponding squared distances. If a side pair has squared lengths u2u^2u2 and v2v^2v2, equality gives

u2=v2.u^2=v^2.u2=v2.

Side lengths are positive, so the pair has equal lengths. Doing this for all three pairs supplies SSS. Equal perimeters or only two side matches do not supply three equal side pairs.

A distance condition can allow two endpoints

A known length need not locate one endpoint uniquely. A point can lie on either side of a fixed vertical line, so keep both signs when solving for a coordinate.

Worked example 5 Find every allowed endpoint

Problem. One endpoint is AAA at (1,−4)(1,-4)(1,−4). The other is BBB at (t,2)(t,2)(t,2), where ttt is real, and ABABAB is 101010 units. Find every possible BBB and the midpoint for each segment.

Solution. The vertical gap is 666, so the distance condition gives

(t−1)2+62=100,(t−1)2=64.\begin{aligned} (t-1)^2+6^2&=100,\\ (t-1)^2&=64. \end{aligned}(t−1)2+62(t−1)2​=100,=64.​

For the left-hand possibility,

t−1=−8,t=−7.\begin{aligned} t-1&=-8,\\ t&=-7. \end{aligned}t−1t​=−8,=−7.​

This gives BBB at (−7,2)(-7,2)(−7,2) and midpoint (−3,−1)(-3,-1)(−3,−1).

For the right-hand possibility,

t−1=8,t=9.\begin{aligned} t-1&=8,\\ t&=9. \end{aligned}t−1t​=8,=9.​

This gives BBB at (9,2)(9,2)(9,2) and midpoint (5,−1)(5,-1)(5,−1). Each has horizontal gap 888 and vertical gap 666, so each length checks as 101010 units. These are all possibilities because the squared equation has only these two real solutions.

If the prescribed length were less than the fixed vertical gap, there would be no endpoint. If it equaled that gap, the horizontal gap would be zero and there would be one endpoint. This checks feasibility before you trust a sketch.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Signed changes. A change can be negative; a distance cannot. Square each complete coordinate difference.
  • Bent routes. Adding horizontal and vertical gaps measures a two-part route rather than a slanted segment.
  • Midpoints. Average matching coordinates; an equidistant point must also be on the segment to be its midpoint.
  • Missing endpoints. Double the midpoint coordinate before subtracting the known endpoint coordinate.
  • Exact checks. Retain both coordinate possibilities from a square, and use exact side lengths for congruence.

Key takeaways

  • Distance. Perpendicular coordinate gaps supply the legs in the Pythagorean theorem, including negative-coordinate cases.
  • Midpoint. A half-size dilation halves both coordinate changes, giving the two coordinate averages.
  • Reconstruction. A midpoint and one endpoint determine the other endpoint; a distance alone may allow several positions.
  • Congruence. Three exact corresponding squared distances establish the three side matches for SSS.

A bit of history (optional)

A small clay tablet in the Yale Babylonian Collection preserves a distance calculation from the Old Babylonian period, roughly 1900 to 1600 BCE. Called YBC 7289, it carries a square, its diagonals, and numbers written in a base-sixty system. One of those numbers is about 1.41421296, remarkably close to the square root of two. The calculation survived on clay even though the tablet’s original findspot is uncertain. The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative record lets you inspect its photograph and the recorded numbers.

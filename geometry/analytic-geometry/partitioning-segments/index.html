Partitioning a Segment

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

A point one third of the way along a straight segment makes one third of its horizontal change and one third of its vertical change. Using one fraction for both coordinates keeps the point on the segment. The order of the two endpoints tells us where that fraction starts.

Learning goals

  • Interpret an ordered internal ratio as a fraction measured from its first endpoint
  • Derive partition coordinates from similar triangles and explain the reversed endpoint weights
  • Locate internal division points on oblique, horizontal, and vertical segments
  • Recover a missing endpoint from a partition point and an ordered ratio
  • Check both coordinates and internality when recovering an ordered division ratio

Read the ratio from the first endpoint

An internal partition point lies strictly between two distinct endpoints. Suppose AP:PB=2:3AP:PB=2:3AP:PB=2:3. There are five equal ratio parts in the whole segment, and PPP is two of them from AAA.

Therefore AP/AB=2/5AP/AB=2/5AP/AB=2/5, not 2/32/32/3.

For example, take A(−4,1)A(-4,1)A(−4,1) and B(11,11)B(11,11)B(11,11). The whole horizontal change is 151515, and the whole vertical change is 101010. Two fifths of those changes are 666 and 444. Starting at AAA puts PPP at (2,5)(2,5)(2,5).

In the earlier distance and midpoint lesson, equal parts gave the midpoint. Here the parts can differ, so the fraction from the starting endpoint need not be one half.

For an ordered ratio AP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:v, with positive u,vu,vu,v, the fraction from AAA toward BBB is

APAB=uu+v.\frac{AP}{AB}=\frac{u}{u+v}.ABAP​=u+vu​.

The notation keeps APAPAP first and PBPBPB second. Reversing the endpoint order turns the same physical division into BP:PA=v:uBP:PA=v:uBP:PA=v:u.

Answer choices

Why both coordinates use one fraction

Similar right triangles explain why the same fraction controls both coordinate changes. In the picture above, the smaller triangle has hypotenuse APAPAP and the larger has hypotenuse ABABAB. Their right angles agree, and they share the angle at AAA, so AA similarity gives the same fraction on corresponding legs.

Why an internal ratio gives weighted coordinates#

For another numerical division, take A(−6,−2)A(-6,-2)A(−6,−2) and B(14,13)B(14,13)B(14,13) with AP:PB=3:2AP:PB=3:2AP:PB=3:2. Similarity makes the horizontal and vertical leg fractions both 3/53/53/5. The whole legs are 202020 and 151515, so the smaller legs are 121212 and 999. Adding those changes to AAA produces

xP=−6+35(20),=6,yP=−2+35(15),=7.\begin{aligned} x_P&=-6+\tfrac35(20),\\ &=6,\\ y_P&=-2+\tfrac35(15),\\ &=7. \end{aligned}xP​yP​​=−6+53​(20),=6,=−2+53​(15),=7.​

Now let A(xA,yA)A(x_A,y_A)A(xA​,yA​) and B(xB,yB)B(x_B,y_B)B(xB​,yB​) be distinct, and let PPP be internal with AP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:v, where u,vu,vu,v are positive. When both coordinate changes are nonzero, the same AA argument makes each leg change the fraction u/(u+v)u/(u+v)u/(u+v) of its whole change. If a coordinate decreases, its small and whole changes have the same negative sign, so the same positive fraction still applies.

If the segment is horizontal, the vertical change is zero throughout. If it is vertical, the horizontal change is zero throughout. Apply the fraction along the changing coordinate in either case; no division by a zero coordinate change is needed.

Thus start at AAA and add the required fraction of each signed change.

xP=xA+uu+v(xB−xA),yP=yA+uu+v(yB−yA).\begin{aligned} x_P&=x_A+\frac{u}{u+v}(x_B-x_A),\\ y_P&=y_A+\frac{u}{u+v}(y_B-y_A). \end{aligned}xP​yP​​=xA​+u+vu​(xB​−xA​),=yA​+u+vu​(yB​−yA​).​

Combining terms in each coordinate gives the weighted-coordinate formula.

xP=vxA+uxBu+v,yP=vyA+uyBu+v.\begin{aligned} x_P&=\frac{v x_A+u x_B}{u+v},\\ y_P&=\frac{v y_A+u y_B}{u+v}. \end{aligned}xP​yP​​=u+vvxA​+uxB​​,=u+vvyA​+uyB​​.​

The argument used no special property of the example’s numbers. The weight on AAA is vvv, and the weight on BBB is uuu. A larger first part APAPAP moves PPP farther toward BBB, which is why BBB receives the weight uuu.

∎

Worked example 1 Move left and up with one fraction

Problem. Let A(8,−6)A(8,-6)A(8,−6) and B(−7,4)B(-7,4)B(−7,4). Find the internal point PPP satisfying AP:PB=2:3AP:PB=2:3AP:PB=2:3.

Solution. The required fraction from AAA is 2/52/52/5. The signed coordinate changes from AAA to BBB are −15-15−15 and 101010.

xP=8+25(−15),=2,yP=−6+25(10),=−2.\begin{aligned} x_P&=8+\tfrac25(-15),\\ &=2,\\ y_P&=-6+\tfrac25(10),\\ &=-2. \end{aligned}xP​yP​​=8+52​(−15),=2,=−6+52​(10),=−2.​

So P=(2,−2)P=(2,-2)P=(2,−2). Both coordinates are between the corresponding endpoint coordinates, and both changes use the same fraction 2/52/52/5.

Keep the endpoints in order

Changing only the ratio usually changes the point. Changing both the endpoint order and the ratio order preserves the original point.

Worked example 2 Compare the two ordered divisions

Problem. Let A(−3,−2)A(-3,-2)A(−3,−2) and B(9,6)B(9,6)B(9,6).

(a) Find PPP with AP:PB=1:3AP:PB=1:3AP:PB=1:3.

(b) Find QQQ with AQ:QB=3:1AQ:QB=3:1AQ:QB=3:1.

(c) Write the ratio for the original point PPP when starting from BBB.

Solution. In (a), move one fourth of the changes 121212 and 888 from AAA.

P=(0,0).P=(0,0).P=(0,0).

In (b), move three fourths of the same changes from AAA.

Q=(6,4).Q=(6,4).Q=(6,4).

For (c), the original first and last parts exchange roles, so BP:PA=3:1BP:PA=3:1BP:PA=3:1. Starting at BBB and moving three fourths toward AAA returns to (0,0)(0,0)(0,0).

When u=vu=vu=v, the weights are equal and each coordinate is the average of its endpoint coordinates. This is the midpoint special case already established in the previous lesson.

Problem. Solve each internal division.

Recover an endpoint

An internal point and a ratio tell us how much of the full change has already happened. We can undo that fraction to recover the endpoint.

Worked example 4 Extend the known part by the required amount

Problem. Let A(−5,7)A(-5,7)A(−5,7) and P(1,1)P(1,1)P(1,1). The point PPP is internal to ABABAB and AP:PB=3:4AP:PB=3:4AP:PB=3:4. Find BBB and verify the ratio.

Solution. Three of the seven ratio parts take AAA to PPP. The known changes are 666 and −6-6−6, so one part changes each coordinate by 222 and −2-2−2. Seven parts from AAA give

xB=−5+7(2),=9,yB=7+7(−2),=−7.\begin{aligned} x_B&=-5+7(2),\\ &=9,\\ y_B&=7+7(-2),\\ &=-7. \end{aligned}xB​yB​​=−5+7(2),=9,=7+7(−2),=−7.​

The endpoint is B=(9,−7)B=(9,-7)B=(9,−7). The changes from PPP to BBB are 888 and −8-8−8, four copies of the same one-part changes. Therefore the straight subsegments have ratio 3:43:43:4, and PPP is internal because 3/73/73/7 is strictly between zero and one.

The formula gives the same endpoint by solving the two coordinate equations separately.

7(1)=4(−5)+3xB,7(1)=4(7)+3yB.\begin{aligned} 7(1)&=4(-5)+3x_B,\\ 7(1)&=4(7)+3y_B. \end{aligned}7(1)7(1)​=4(−5)+3xB​,=4(7)+3yB​.​

Check the same division in both coordinates

A candidate can sit between the endpoint coordinates in each direction and still miss the segment. Both coordinates must describe the same fraction of the journey.

Worked example 5 Recover a ratio and reject inconsistent coordinates

Problem. Let A(10,−8)A(10,-8)A(10,−8) and B(−6,16)B(-6,16)B(−6,16).

(a) Decide whether P(4,1)P(4,1)P(4,1) is internal to ABABAB. If it is, find AP:PBAP:PBAP:PB.

(b) Decide whether Q(4,2)Q(4,2)Q(4,2) is internal to ABABAB.

Solution. In (a), use each changing coordinate to recover the fraction from AAA.

4−10−6−10=38,1−(−8)16−(−8)=38.\begin{aligned} \frac{4-10}{-6-10}&=\frac38,\\ \frac{1-(-8)}{16-(-8)}&=\frac38. \end{aligned}−6−104−10​16−(−8)1−(−8)​​=83​,=83​.​

Both agree, and 0<3/8<10<3/8<10<3/8<1, so PPP is internal.

Three eighths lie before PPP and five eighths after it, giving AP:PB=3:5AP:PB=3:5AP:PB=3:5.

In (b), the first coordinate still requires 3/83/83/8 of the journey. The second coordinate gives a different fraction.

2−(−8)16−(−8)=512.\frac{2-(-8)}{16-(-8)}=\frac5{12}.16−(−8)2−(−8)​=125​.

Thus QQQ is not on ABABAB, even though each coordinate is within its endpoint range. For a vertical or horizontal segment, check that the fixed coordinate matches and use the other coordinate for the fraction. Fractions zero and one describe endpoints, not internal points.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Part and whole: for AP:PB=2:3AP:PB=2:3AP:PB=2:3, the first-part fraction is 2/52/52/5; the denominator for the whole includes both parts.
  • Endpoint weights: the weight on AAA is the second ratio number, while the weight on BBB is the first.
  • Direction: preserve signed coordinate changes when a segment goes left or down.
  • Consistency: check both coordinates, and require the common journey fraction to lie strictly between zero and one.

Key takeaways

  • Read AP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:v from AAA toward BBB, with distinct endpoints and positive ratio parts.
  • Move the fraction u/(u+v)u/(u+v)u/(u+v) of each coordinate change.
  • Equal ratio parts give the midpoint; unequal parts give unequal endpoint weights.
  • Recover missing endpoints by undoing the known fraction, then check both coordinates and internality.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1637, René Descartes published La Géométrie as an appendix to a work about his method of reasoning. He described proportions through straight lines and recorded their relations with letters. To solve a construction problem, he asked readers to name known and unknown lengths, then find relations that could become equations. He did not spell out every calculation. In his remarks, he says leaving some work would give readers the pleasure of mastering it themselves. This technical appendix expected readers who already knew geometry and algebra.

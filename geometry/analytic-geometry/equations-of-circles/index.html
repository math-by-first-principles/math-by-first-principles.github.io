Equations of Circles

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A line drawn beside a circle may look tangent, yet a small drawing cannot prove that it touches exactly once. Coordinates let you test that claim. The circle equation measures distance from the center, while the line equation restricts which points you can use. A common point must pass both tests.

Learning goals

  • Justify a circle equation by measuring distance from its center
  • Recover circle geometry and recognize point or empty graphs from equations
  • Classify points by comparing squared distance with the squared radius
  • Find all common points of a line and a circle
  • Verify tangency using incidence and a perpendicular contact radius

Distance gives the equation its meaning

A circle equation describes a fixed distance from a center. You met its algebra in Intro to Algebra; here the equation becomes a tool for checking geometric claims.

Worked example 1 Build the equation from a radius

Problem. A circle has center CCC at (−1,−3)(-1,-3)(−1,−3) and passes through PPP at (2,2)(2,2)(2,2). Write its equation and justify why it describes exactly that circle.

Solution. The horizontal and vertical gaps from CCC to PPP are 333 and 555 units. The squared radius is

r2=32+52,r2=34.\begin{aligned} r^2&=3^2+5^2,\\ r^2&=34. \end{aligned}r2r2​=32+52,=34.​

A point (x,y)(x,y)(x,y) has squared distance (x+1)2+(y+3)2(x+1)^2+(y+3)^2(x+1)2+(y+3)2 from CCC. Requiring that distance to equal the positive radius 34\sqrt{34}34​ gives

(x+1)2+(y+3)2=34.(x+1)^2+(y+3)^2=34.(x+1)2+(y+3)2=34.

Every point on the circle satisfies this equation. Conversely, any real point satisfying it has distance 34\sqrt{34}34​ from CCC, so it belongs to the circle. The distance condition works in both directions.

The center-radius equation describes a circle#

The same reasoning works for any center CCC at (h,k)(h,k)(h,k) and positive radius rrr. The horizontal and vertical gaps to PPP at (x,y)(x,y)(x,y) give

CP2=(x−h)2+(y−k)2.CP^2=(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2.CP2=(x−h)2+(y−k)2.

This distance identity also holds when one gap is zero. Therefore the center-radius equation is

(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2.(x−h)2+(y−k)2=r2.

Points at distance rrr satisfy it. Any point satisfying it has that distance, because both CPCPCP and rrr are nonnegative. Nothing in the numerical argument depended on the chosen center or radius.

∎

Try moving the center and the point on the rim separately. Predict which parts of the equation will change, then compare the readout. Finally, put the two marked points together: the graph then has one point, rather than a positive-radius circle.

Recover the geometry before drawing

Completing the square rewrites an equation without changing its real solutions. Once it has center-radius form, read the center and take the positive square root of the right side.

Worked example 2 Recover and graph a circle

Problem. Find the center and radius of the following graph. Sketch it using four points horizontally or vertically aligned with its center.

x2+y2−2x+8y+1=0.x^2+y^2-2x+8y+1=0.x2+y2−2x+8y+1=0.

Solution. Move the constant, then add 111 and 161616 to complete the two squares.

(x2−2x)+(y2+8y)=−1,(x−1)2+(y+4)2=16.\begin{aligned} (x^2-2x)+(y^2+8y)&=-1,\\ (x-1)^2+(y+4)^2&=16. \end{aligned}(x2−2x)+(y2+8y)(x−1)2+(y+4)2​=−1,=16.​

The center is (1,−4)(1,-4)(1,−4) and the radius is 444. Four radius steps give L at (−3,−4)(-3,-4)(−3,−4), R at (5,−4)(5,-4)(5,−4), U at (1,0)(1,0)(1,0), and D at (1,−8)(1,-8)(1,−8). Draw a round curve through them with equal coordinate scales.

Each of these points makes one square zero and the other 161616, checking the sketch against the equation.

The sign of the completed-square right side matters. For example,

(x−3)2+(y+5)2=0(x-3)^2+(y+5)^2=0(x−3)2+(y+5)2=0

forces both squares to be zero, so its graph is the single point (3,−5)(3,-5)(3,−5). Replacing the right side by −2-2−2 gives no real points, since real squares cannot have a negative sum. A circle needs a positive radius. A zero or negative completed-square right side does not describe a positive-radius circle.

Compare a point with the radius

A point lies inside, on, or outside a positive-radius circle according to whether its center distance is less than, equal to, or greater than the radius. Compare squared distances directly: squaring preserves the order of nonnegative numbers, so you can avoid square roots.

Worked example 3 Prove three point positions

Problem. Classify each point relative to the circle below, using exact squared distances.

(x−4)2+(y−1)2=10.(x-4)^2+(y-1)^2=10.(x−4)2+(y−1)2=10.

Point A is (5,3)(5,3)(5,3).

Point B is (7,2)(7,2)(7,2).

Point D is (2,−2)(2,-2)(2,−2).

Solution. The center is C=(4,1)C=(4,1)C=(4,1). For A,

CA2=12+22,CA2=5.\begin{aligned} CA^2&=1^2+2^2,\\ CA^2&=5. \end{aligned}CA2CA2​=12+22,=5.​

Since 5<105<105<10, A is inside.

For B,

CB2=32+12,CB2=10.\begin{aligned} CB^2&=3^2+1^2,\\ CB^2&=10. \end{aligned}CB2CB2​=32+12,=10.​

The equality puts B on the circle.

For D,

CD2=(−2)2+(−3)2,CD2=13.\begin{aligned} CD^2&=(-2)^2+(-3)^2,\\ CD^2&=13. \end{aligned}CD2CD2​=(−2)2+(−3)2,=13.​

Since 13>1013>1013>10, D is outside.

A line adds a second condition

Intersections must satisfy both equations. For a nonvertical line, substitute its expression for yyy into the circle equation. For a vertical line, substitute its fixed xxx instead. Solve, then recover and check both coordinates of each point.

Worked example 4 Find both intersections exactly

Problem. Find every common point of the circle and line below.

(x+1)2+(y−2)2=8,y=x+1.\begin{aligned} (x+1)^2+(y-2)^2&=8,\\ y&=x+1. \end{aligned}(x+1)2+(y−2)2y​=8,=x+1.​

Solution. Substitute the line expression for yyy.

(x+1)2+(x−1)2=8,2x2+2=8,x2=3.\begin{aligned} (x+1)^2+(x-1)^2&=8,\\ 2x^2+2&=8,\\ x^2&=3. \end{aligned}(x+1)2+(x−1)22x2+2x2​=8,=8,=3.​

The two points are

A=(−3,1−3),B=(3,1+3).\begin{aligned} A&=(-\sqrt3,1-\sqrt3),\\ B&=(\sqrt3,1+\sqrt3). \end{aligned}AB​=(−3​,1−3​),=(3​,1+3​).​

Each pair satisfies the line. For either root, the circle’s left side simplifies to 2x2+22x^2+22x2+2, which is 888. Both are genuine intersections, and the equation for xxx has no other roots.

For a positive-radius circle, a line has zero, one, or two common points. A tangent meets it once; a secant meets it twice. These are distinct points, so a repeated algebraic root still gives one intersection.

For the circle centered at (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) with radius 222, substituting the three horizontal lines in the figure gives the following cases.

Line at height 444 gives x2=−5x^2=-5x2=−5, with no real roots.

Line at height 333 gives x2=0x^2=0x2=0, with just one real root.

Line at height 111 gives x2=4x^2=4x2=4, with two real roots.

Verify the contact radius

The tangent-radius theorem from Circle Geometry gives a second way to check a tangent: the line must pass through a point on the circle and be perpendicular to the radius ending there. Checking slopes without checking the contact point leaves part of the argument missing.

Worked example 5 Verify a slanted tangent

Problem. Verify that the line below is tangent to the circle at T=(4,2)T=(4,2)T=(4,2).

(x−2)2+(y+2)2=20,x+2y=8.\begin{aligned} (x-2)^2+(y+2)^2&=20,\\ x+2y&=8. \end{aligned}(x−2)2+(y+2)2x+2y​=20,=8.​

Solution. The circle has center C=(2,−2)C=(2,-2)C=(2,−2). The point T is on it because

(4−2)2+(2+2)2=20.(4-2)^2+(2+2)^2=20.(4−2)2+(2+2)2=20.

T also lies on the line because 4+2(2)=84+2(2)=84+2(2)=8. The contact radius has slope

mCT=2−(−2)4−2.m_{CT}=\frac{2-(-2)}{4-2}.mCT​=4−22−(−2)​.

This is 222. The line can be written as

y=−12x+4.y=-\frac12x+4.y=−21​x+4.

Both slopes are defined and nonzero, and their product is −1-1−1. Thus the line is perpendicular to CT at its circle endpoint, proving tangency.

A horizontal radius instead has a vertical tangent at its endpoint. A vertical radius has a horizontal tangent. Handle these orientations directly; an undefined slope is not a number you can multiply.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Radius and squared radius. The positive square root of the right side is the radius. Check the sign before taking that root.
  • A single squared comparison. Comparing a point’s x-coordinate with the radius ignores its vertical gap. Use both coordinate differences from the center.
  • Missing a second point. A positive square can have two real roots. Recover a y-coordinate from the line for each x-coordinate.
  • Incomplete tangency checks. A perpendicular direction alone does not locate the tangent. Verify that the proposed contact lies on both the line and the circle.
  • Undefined slopes. Treat horizontal and vertical directions as a separate perpendicular pair.

Key takeaways

  • A positive-radius circle consists exactly of points at its radius distance from its center.
  • Completing both squares reveals the center and the sign of the squared radius; zero gives one point and a negative value gives no real points.
  • Compare a point’s squared center distance with the squared radius to classify its position.
  • Substitute a line condition into the circle equation and check every real intersection.
  • A line through a circle point is tangent there when it is perpendicular to the contact radius.

A bit of history (optional)

The Roman writer Cicero tells a story about searching for Archimedes’ tomb while serving as an official in Sicily. Local people told him that no such monument remained. Cicero had a clue they had overlooked: he remembered hearing that the grave carried a sphere and a cylinder.

Among the crowded monuments near a city gate, he noticed a small column rising above the bushes with those shapes on it. Workers cut a path through the growth. Once the party could reach the pedestal, Cicero found an inscription, although parts of its verses had already worn away.

This is Cicero’s own account, written long after Archimedes’ lifetime. In his telling, a remembered geometric carving led him to a monument that the city had forgotten. Read sections 64 to 66 of the Tusculan Disputations in C. D. Yonge’s 1877 translation, hosted by Attalus.

Coordinate Geometry: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Coordinate proof
An argument that represents geometric conditions with coordinates and uses exact calculations to establish a conclusion.
Midpoint
A point on a segment that divides it into equal lengths. Equal endpoint distances alone do not establish a midpoint.
Ordered internal ratio
AP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:vAP:PB=u:v names the first and remaining lengths from AAA toward BBB. An internal point lies strictly between distinct endpoints; both parts are positive.
Slope
The signed rise divided by the signed run of a nonvertical line. Horizontal slope is zero; vertical slope is undefined.
Median, altitude, perpendicular bisector
A median joins a vertex to the opposite midpoint. An altitude passes through a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side line. A perpendicular bisector passes through a segment midpoint perpendicular to its line.
Centroid
The common point of a triangle’s medians, lying two thirds of the way from each vertex toward the opposite midpoint.
Locus
The set of all points satisfying a stated geometric condition.
Secant and tangent
For a positive-radius circle, a secant line has two distinct common points; a tangent line has exactly one.
Parabola, focus, directrix, vertex
A parabola consists of points equally distant from a fixed focus and a directrix line not containing it. Its vertex bisects their perpendicular spacing; its axis passes through the focus perpendicular to the directrix.

  • Parallel-line criterion

    m1=m2\begin{gathered} m_1=m_2 \end{gathered}m1​=m2​​

    Use when For two distinct nonvertical lines, equal slopes are equivalent to parallelism. Equal slopes with a shared point give coincidence. Distinct vertical lines are parallel too.

  • Perpendicular-line criterion

    m1m2=−1\begin{gathered} m_1m_2=-1 \end{gathered}m1​m2​=−1​

    A counterclockwise quarter-turn sends a nonzero step with signed run aaa and rise bbb to run −b-b−b and rise aaa.

    Use when The product test is equivalent to perpendicularity for two nonvertical, nonhorizontal lines. Separately, every horizontal line is perpendicular to every vertical line; undefined slope is never multiplied.

  • Quadrilateral classification tests

    Diagonals bisecting each other establish a parallelogram. In a parallelogram, one right angle or congruent diagonals establishes a rectangle; congruent adjacent sides establish a rhombus. A rectangle that is also a rhombus is a square.

    Use when Convex quadrilaterals with vertices in boundary order. Match exact coordinate evidence to every hypothesis. Squares also belong to both larger families; every parallelogram is a trapezoid under the inclusive definition.

    Use when Nondegenerate triangle ABC; M and N are the midpoints of AC and BC. The midsegment line is distinct from the opposite side line.

    Use when Noncollinear vertices A, B, C. In the ratio, D is the midpoint of BC and the order starts at vertex A. Average three coordinates separately.

    e.g. For vertices (−7,5)(-7,5)(−7,5), (2,−7)(2,-7)(2,−7), and (11,8)(11,8)(11,8), find the centroid: (2,2)(2,2)(2,2).

    Perimeter adds outside edge lengths. Area adds pieces with nonoverlapping interiors, or subtracts missing pieces from a containing region. For a triangle and rectangle respectively:

    Atriangle=12bhArectangle=bh\begin{gathered} A_{\text{triangle}}=\frac12bh\\ A_{\text{rectangle}}=bh \end{gathered}Atriangle​=21​bhArectangle​=bh​

    Use when A simple polygon with vertices in boundary order. Pieces must fill the intended region exactly. Use perpendicular base-height pairs and matching units; area uses square units. Internal cuts are absent from perimeter.

  • Inside, on, or outside a circle

    d2=(x−h)2+(y−k)2d2<r2: insided2=r2: ond2>r2: outside\begin{gathered} d^2=(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2\\ d^2<r^2:\ \text{inside}\\ d^2=r^2:\ \text{on}\\ d^2>r^2:\ \text{outside} \end{gathered}d2=(x−h)2+(y−k)2d2<r2: insided2=r2: ond2>r2: outside​
    Text description

    A circle has center C. Point A lies strictly inside it, B is its rightmost boundary point, and D lies outside to the upper right. The radius from C to B provides the boundary distance used to compare the other points.

    Use when A positive-radius circle and a real point at distance d from its center. Compare squared distances exactly, using both coordinate gaps.

  • Line-circle intersection count

    A line has two common points when its perpendicular center distance is less than the radius, one when equal, and none when greater.

    Text description

    Three distinct dashed horizontal lines are compared with the same circle. The highest line misses it and has zero common points. The next line touches its top and has one common point. The lowest goes through its center C and has two common points.

    Use when A positive-radius circle and an entire line. Find the perpendicular foot using line equations. A line through the center has distance zero and is a secant. A repeated algebraic root counts once.

Problem types, step by step

Reconstruct endpoints from midpoint and distance data

  1. Use each midpoint equation to express unknown endpoint coordinates. Apply any fixed-coordinate or axis conditions.
  2. Substitute into squared distance; keep every real coordinate possibility, including both signs of a nonzero square.
  3. With one free coordinate and one fixed gap, lengths shorter than that gap are impossible; equality gives one position, larger lengths give two. Check every original condition and noncollinearity if a triangle is required.

Check coordinate triangle congruence

  1. List side pairs from the proposed vertex correspondence.
  2. Compare all three exact corresponding squared lengths. Verify nondegenerate triangles and invoke SSS; two sides or equal perimeters alone are insufficient.

Locate a partition point or recover an endpoint

  1. Read the ordered positive ratio from the named starting endpoint; divide the first part by the sum of both parts.
  2. Apply that fraction to both signed changes. For a missing endpoint, solve the weighted-coordinate equations instead.
  3. Check both coordinates, endpoint order, and internality.

e.g. From AAA at (13,−9)(13,-9)(13,−9) to BBB at (−11,9)(-11,9)(−11,9) with ratio 3:53:53:5, find PPP: (4,−9/4)(4,-9/4)(4,−9/4).

Recover an ordered ratio or reject a candidate

  1. From each nonzero coordinate change, divide the change to P by the full change from A to B. A fixed coordinate must match.
  2. Require one common fraction strictly between zero and one. Compare that fraction with its leftover to obtain AP:PB; disagreement means the point is off the segment.
  3. For two points on the same segment, their separation occupies the absolute difference of their journey fractions.

Build geometric lines and find their intersections

  1. Choose the required point and direction: opposite midpoint for a median, vertex and opposite perpendicular direction for an altitude, midpoint and perpendicular direction for a perpendicular bisector.
  2. Separate horizontal and vertical cases before using slope. Write each equation and solve the system.
  3. Check the point in both equations and each geometric definition. Distinct parallels have no common point; coincident lines have infinitely many.

e.g. For endpoints (−8,2)(-8,2)(−8,2) and (4,8)(4,8)(4,8), find the perpendicular bisector: y=−2x+1y=-2x+1y=−2x+1.

Choose coordinates for a general proof

  1. Select the shortest suitable geometric test. Put a vertex at the origin and a known side on an axis when useful.
  2. Keep all free dimensions arbitrary, with positivity and nondegeneracy conditions. Do not impose an unprovided right angle or symmetry.
  3. Calculate only the needed slopes, squared distances, or midpoints; state the geometric conclusion. One numerical instance does not prove a universal claim.

Classify a quadrilateral or disprove a claim

  1. Establish the base family with a valid test, such as common diagonal midpoints or both opposite side pairs congruent. To recover a missing parallelogram vertex, equate the two diagonal midpoints.
  2. Check right angles and side equalities for stronger classifications; justify exclusions with exact evidence.
  3. For a counterexample, verify every hypothesis and show a failed conclusion. Equal or perpendicular diagonals alone do not force a rectangle or rhombus.

Use midsegment or centroid constraints

  1. Locate the specified side midpoints or average the corresponding coordinates of the three vertices.
  2. Translate the required parallelism, perpendicularity, length, or line membership into an equation.
  3. Solve and check all coordinate and nondegeneracy conditions. For a centroid on a median, start the two-to-one ratio at the vertex.

Measure or recover a polygon

  1. Trace the given simple boundary in order; add its exact edge lengths for perimeter.
  2. Choose a complete nonoverlapping area split or a containing region with specified cuts. Use perpendicular heights.
  3. For an unknown dimension, solve the area or perimeter constraint, then check positivity and boundary order.

Recover circle geometry and sketch

  1. Normalize equal nonzero squared-term coefficients if necessary, then complete both squares.
  2. Classify the right side before taking a root. For a proper circle, read its center and positive radius; a known diameter supplies its midpoint and half-length.
  3. Plot four radius steps from the center on equal-scale axes. Check proposed boundary points by substitution.

e.g. For x2+y2−14x+6y+45=0x^2+y^2-14x+6y+45=0x2+y2−14x+6y+45=0, recover the radius: 13\sqrt{13}13​.

Solve circle intersections and tangency constraints

  1. Recover center-radius form; substitute the line’s height expression or fixed first coordinate.
  2. Solve for every real point and check both equations. Restrict to the segment if a segment was requested; use its endpoints for chord length or midpoint.
  3. For specified tangency, check contact on both objects and perpendicularity to its radius. To recover a center, place it on that perpendicular through the contact and apply the remaining data.

e.g. For center (−3,4)(-3,4)(−3,4) and radius 10\sqrt{10}10​, intersect the vertical line at first coordinate −2-2−2: (−2,1)(-2,1)(−2,1) and (−2,7)(-2,7)(−2,7).

Write, recover, or translate a parabola

  1. From focus and directrix, find the perpendicular foot and their midpoint vertex. Alternatively, complete the squared coordinate in the equation.
  2. Identify the axis orientation and signed vertex-to-focus step. Divide the unsquared-gap multiplier by four when reading p from an equation.
  3. Locate focus and directrix on opposite sides of the vertex; give the opening direction. A translation moves the whole configuration and preserves p.

e.g. For (y+5)2=−10(x−4)(y+5)^2=-10(x-4)(y+5)2=−10(x−4), recover the focus: (3/2,−5)(3/2,-5)(3/2,−5).

Test locus membership or recover missing parabola data

  1. Compute focus distance and perpendicular directrix distance for the given point; equate them to find unknown data.
  2. Square nonnegative distances if helpful. Keep all real candidates, then exclude a focus on the directrix and any candidate violating the specified direction.
  3. Check both original distances and the recovered equation. For a sketch, use the vertex and checked symmetric points.

e.g. For a vertical-axis parabola with vertex (1,−3)(1,-3)(1,−3) through (7,0)(7,0)(7,0), find the signed parameter: p=3p=3p=3.

Exam traps

  • Trap Replacing distance with a bent route or signed change

    Fix Square complete coordinate differences, add their squares, and take the nonnegative root. Keep radicals exact until rounding is requested.

  • Trap Reversing partition weights or ignoring direction

    Fix The first ratio number weights B, the second weights A. Preserve signed changes, check both coordinates, and require a strictly internal fraction.

  • Trap Using equal slopes without checking distinctness

    Fix A shared slope and point give coincidence. Distinct vertical lines are parallel with undefined slopes, not infinite slopes.

  • Trap Changing only the sign for a perpendicular slope

    Fix Use the negative reciprocal for nonzero defined slopes. Use the separate horizontal-vertical case when a slope is zero or undefined.

  • Trap Proving a special drawing or using an incomplete classification test

    Fix Keep arbitrary dimensions and nondegeneracy guards. Establish the required family before applying its converse tests; a square remains both rectangle and rhombus.

  • Trap Reading the squared radius as the radius

    Fix Complete both squares and inspect the right-side sign. Only a positive value gives a proper circle; zero is one point and a negative value gives none.

  • Trap Proving tangent direction without its contact

    Fix Verify that the contact lies on both the circle and the line. Then verify the perpendicular radius, including horizontal and vertical cases.

  • Trap Treating p or four times p as a distance

    Fix Recover signed p from the multiplier. Distances use ∣p∣|p|∣p∣; spacing uses 2∣p∣2|p|2∣p∣. Exclude zero, and identify which coordinate is squared before choosing the axis.

  • Trap Measuring diagonally to the directrix

    Fix Use its perpendicular coordinate gap with an absolute value. Equal squared distances imply equal distances because both are nonnegative.

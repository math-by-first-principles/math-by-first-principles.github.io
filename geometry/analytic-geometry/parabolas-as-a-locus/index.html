Parabolas as a Distance Locus

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

A circle keeps a fixed distance from one point. What shape keeps its distance from a point equal to its distance from a line? Place the point above the line. Near the middle, the two distances balance halfway between them. Farther sideways, a point must rise to keep the distances equal. The trace is the familiar parabola, now described by a geometric rule.

Learning goals

  • Test whether a point is equally distant from a focus and directrix
  • Derive a parabola equation from its defining equality of distances
  • Write translated equations for vertical and horizontal parabola axes
  • Recover the focus, directrix, vertex, and opening direction from an equation
  • Verify recovered geometry using both distances and the equation

Equal distance to a point and a line

Distance to a line follows the perpendicular, shortest route. For PPP at (4,2)(4,2)(4,2) and the horizontal line y=−2y=-2y=−2, that route ends at HHH at (4,−2)(4,-2)(4,−2). Its length is 444. If the fixed point is FFF at (0,2)(0,2)(0,2), the horizontal segment PFPFPF also has length 444.

A locus is the set of all points satisfying a stated condition. A parabola is the locus of points equally distant from a fixed point, its focus, and a fixed line, its directrix. The focus must be off the directrix. We consider horizontal or vertical directrices here.

The figure traces three exact points. The middle point VVV at (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) is 222 units from both FFF and the directrix. Each of PPP at (4,2)(4,2)(4,2) and QQQ at (−4,2)(-4,2)(−4,2) is 444 units from both. The perpendicular feet are HHH at (4,−2)(4,-2)(4,−2) and III at (−4,−2)(-4,-2)(−4,−2). Each pair of distances is checked separately; all the segments do not have the same length.

For a horizontal line y=cy=cy=c, the distance from (x,y)(x,y)(x,y) is ∣y−c∣|y-c|∣y−c∣.

For a vertical line x=cx=cx=c, it is ∣x−c∣|x-c|∣x−c∣. Absolute values keep these distances nonnegative, even below or left of the line.

Check your understanding

For PPP at (7,−1)(7,-1)(7,−1) and directrix y=−5y=-5y=−5, what is the perpendicular foot?

Answer choices

A perpendicular to a horizontal line is vertical, so the x-coordinate stays 7. The foot has the line's y-coordinate, giving (7,−5)(7,-5)(7,−5). The distance is 4 units.

Worked example 1 Test the distance condition

Problem. A parabola has focus FFF at (0,2)(0,2)(0,2) and directrix y=−2y=-2y=−2. Decide whether each point lies on it.

Point PPP at (4,2)(4,2)(4,2).

Point BBB at (2,1)(2,1)(2,1).

Solution. Point PPP is 444 units from the directrix. Its focus distance is

PF=(4−0)2+(2−2)2.PF=\sqrt{(4-0)^2+(2-2)^2}.PF=(4−0)2+(2−2)2​.

This is 444, so PPP lies on the locus, as in the figure.

Point BBB is 333 units from the directrix. Its focus distance is

BF=(2−0)2+(1−2)2.BF=\sqrt{(2-0)^2+(1-2)^2}.BF=(2−0)2+(1−2)2​.

This is 5\sqrt55​, which differs from 333. Point BBB is off the locus. A point being near the drawn curve is not the test; the equality is.

An equation records the distance condition

The vertex lies halfway from the focus to its perpendicular foot on the directrix. The line through those two points is the axis of symmetry.

With focus (0,3)(0,3)(0,3) and directrix y=−3y=-3y=−3, the halfway point is (0,0)(0,0)(0,0). On the axis, a point’s distances balance only at that midpoint. Reflection across the axis preserves the focus and directrix and preserves both distances, so it preserves the locus. This explains both the vertex and the axis of symmetry in the familiar graph.

Worked example 2 Derive an equation and check the reverse

Problem. Find an equation for the locus with focus (0,3)(0,3)(0,3) and directrix y=−3y=-3y=−3. Explain why every point satisfying your equation meets the original distance condition.

Solution. For a possible point (x,y)(x,y)(x,y), write the two distances and equate them.

x2+(y−3)2=∣y+3∣.\sqrt{x^2+(y-3)^2}=|y+3|.x2+(y−3)2​=∣y+3∣.

Both sides are nonnegative. Squaring and expanding gives

x2+(y−3)2=(y+3)2,x2+y2−6y+9=y2+6y+9,x2=12y.\begin{aligned} x^2+(y-3)^2&=(y+3)^2,\\ x^2+y^2-6y+9&=y^2+6y+9,\\ x^2&=12y. \end{aligned}x2+(y−3)2x2+y2−6y+9x2​=(y+3)2,=y2+6y+9,=12y.​

This is the equation. Solving it for yyy gives the quadratic graph y=x2/12y=x^2/12y=x2/12.

For the reverse check, start with x2=12yx^2=12yx2=12y. Substitution gives

x2+(y−3)2=12y+(y−3)2,x2+(y−3)2=(y+3)2.\begin{aligned} x^2+(y-3)^2&=12y+(y-3)^2,\\ x^2+(y-3)^2&=(y+3)^2. \end{aligned}x2+(y−3)2x2+(y−3)2​=12y+(y−3)2,=(y+3)2.​

Taking nonnegative square roots returns the distance equality, including the absolute value on the line distance. Thus the equation describes exactly the locus, without extra points from squaring.

A translated vertical axis

The parameter measures a signed step from the vertex toward the focus. For vertex (2,−1)(2,-1)(2,−1) and focus (2,0)(2,0)(2,0), that step is one unit and the directrix is y=−2y=-2y=−2. A point’s squared focus distance is (x−2)2+y2(x-2)^2+y^2(x−2)2+y2; its squared line distance is (y+2)2(y+2)^2(y+2)2. Their equality simplifies to

(x−2)2=4(y+1).(x-2)^2=4(y+1).(x−2)2=4(y+1).

The coordinate gaps from the vertex have replaced the plain coordinates. This is how a translation enters the equation.

The translated vertical distance locus#

First take vertex (−2,1)(-2,1)(−2,1), focus (−2,3)(-2,3)(−2,3), and directrix y=−1y=-1y=−1. The squared-distance equality becomes

(x+2)2+(y−3)2=(y+1)2,(x+2)2+y2−6y+9=y2+2y+1,(x+2)2=8(y−1).\begin{aligned} (x+2)^2+(y-3)^2&=(y+1)^2,\\ (x+2)^2+y^2-6y+9&=y^2+2y+1,\\ (x+2)^2&=8(y-1). \end{aligned}(x+2)2+(y−3)2(x+2)2+y2−6y+9(x+2)2​=(y+1)2,=y2+2y+1,=8(y−1).​

Conversely, replacing (x+2)2(x+2)^2(x+2)2 by 8(y−1)8(y-1)8(y−1) in the squared focus distance gives (y+1)2(y+1)^2(y+1)2. Taking nonnegative square roots recovers the equality of distances.

The same steps work at any vertex (h,k)(h,k)(h,k). Let ppp be a nonzero signed number. Take focus (h,k+p)(h,k+p)(h,k+p) and directrix y=k−py=k-py=k−p.

For a point (x,y)(x,y)(x,y), equidistance means

(x−h)2+(y−k−p)2=∣y−k+p∣.\sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k-p)^2}=|y-k+p|.(x−h)2+(y−k−p)2​=∣y−k+p∣.

Squaring is reversible because these are nonnegative distances. Expanding the squares gives

(x−h)2+(y−k−p)2=(y−k+p)2,(x−h)2=4p(y−k).\begin{aligned} (x-h)^2+(y-k-p)^2&=(y-k+p)^2,\\ (x-h)^2&=4p(y-k). \end{aligned}(x−h)2+(y−k−p)2(x−h)2​=(y−k+p)2,=4p(y−k).​

The matching square and constant terms cancel; the two opposite cross terms give four times p(y−k)p(y-k)p(y−k). Nothing depended on the chosen numerical coordinates. Conversely, the last equation gives

(x−h)2+(y−k−p)2=(y−k+p)2.(x-h)^2+(y-k-p)^2=(y-k+p)^2.(x−h)2+(y−k−p)2=(y−k+p)2.

Nonnegative square roots give equal focus and perpendicular line distances. Both directions hold for either sign of ppp.

∎

For a vertical axis, the equation is therefore

(x−h)2=4p(y−k),p≠0.\begin{gathered}(x-h)^2=4p(y-k),\\p\ne0.\end{gathered}(x−h)2=4p(y−k),p=0.​

The vertex is (h,k)(h,k)(h,k) and the axis is x=hx=hx=h.

The focus is (h,k+p)(h,k+p)(h,k+p) and the directrix is y=k−py=k-py=k−p. The vertex-focus distance is ∣p∣|p|∣p∣, while the focus-directrix spacing is 2∣p∣2|p|2∣p∣.

For positive ppp, the nonnegative square forces y≥ky\ge ky≥k, so the curve opens up.

For negative ppp, it forces y≤ky\le ky≤k, so it opens down. The vertex is the curve’s point on its axis in either case. A negative ppp indicates direction, not a negative distance. Zero is excluded because it would put the focus on the directrix.

Check your understanding

The locus has equation x2=20yx^2=20yx2=20y. What is the vertex-focus distance?

Answer choices

The multiplier is four times the signed parameter.

4p=20,p=5.\begin{aligned}4p&=20,\\p&=5.\end{aligned}4pp​=20,=5.​

The vertex-focus distance is 5.

Worked example 3 Use a focus below its directrix

Problem. The focus is (3,−5)(3,-5)(3,−5) and the directrix is y=1y=1y=1. Find the vertex, axis, signed parameter, equation, and opening direction.

Solution. The foot from the focus is (3,1)(3,1)(3,1). Their midpoint is (3,−2)(3,-2)(3,−2), the vertex. The axis is x=3x=3x=3.

The focus is 333 units below the vertex, so p=−3p=-3p=−3. The equation is

(x−3)2=−12(y+2).(x-3)^2=-12(y+2).(x−3)2=−12(y+2).

It opens down. The distance from vertex to focus is 333, and the spacing from focus to directrix is 666.

At PPP at (9,−5)(9,-5)(9,−5) and QQQ at (−3,−5)(-3,-5)(−3,−5), the focus distances are 666 and the perpendicular distances to y=1y=1y=1 are also 666. These exact points guide the sketch on both sides of the axis.

Which is the focus of (x+4)2=−6(y−2)(x+4)^2=-6(y-2)(x+4)2=−6(y−2)?

Answer choices

The vertex is (−4,2)(-4,2)(−4,2). Divide the multiplier by four to obtain p=−3/2p=-3/2p=−3/2. Move down 3/23/23/2 from the vertex, so the focus is (−4,1/2)(-4,1/2)(−4,1/2).

A horizontal axis

A vertical directrix makes the perpendicular distance horizontal. For example, with focus (−3,2)(-3,2)(−3,2) and directrix x=1x=1x=1, the vertex is (−1,2)(-1,2)(−1,2). Here the signed step from vertex to focus is −2-2−2 along the x-direction.

Worked example 4 Turn the distance calculation sideways

Problem. Find the equation for focus (−3,2)(-3,2)(−3,2) and directrix x=1x=1x=1. Verify that (−3,6)(-3,6)(−3,6) is on the locus.

Solution. The distance equality is

(x+3)2+(y−2)2=∣x−1∣.\sqrt{(x+3)^2+(y-2)^2}=|x-1|.(x+3)2+(y−2)2​=∣x−1∣.

Square and cancel the matching x-squared terms.

(x+3)2+(y−2)2=(x−1)2,x2+6x+9+(y−2)2=x2−2x+1,(y−2)2=−8(x+1).\begin{aligned} (x+3)^2+(y-2)^2&=(x-1)^2,\\ x^2+6x+9+(y-2)^2&=x^2-2x+1,\\ (y-2)^2&=-8(x+1). \end{aligned}(x+3)2+(y−2)2x2+6x+9+(y−2)2(y−2)2​=(x−1)2,=x2−2x+1,=−8(x+1).​

The curve opens left from (−1,2)(-1,2)(−1,2). At (−3,6)(-3,6)(−3,6), the focus distance is 444 and the perpendicular distance to x=1x=1x=1 is also 444.

In general, the horizontal form is

(y−k)2=4p(x−h),p≠0.\begin{gathered}(y-k)^2=4p(x-h),\\p\ne0.\end{gathered}(y−k)2=4p(x−h),p=0.​

The vertex is (h,k)(h,k)(h,k) and the axis is y=ky=ky=k.

The focus is (h+p,k)(h+p,k)(h+p,k) and the directrix is x=h−px=h-px=h−p. To derive this form, equate squared distances as above:

(x−h−p)2+(y−k)2=(x−h+p)2,(y−k)2=4p(x−h).\begin{aligned} (x-h-p)^2+(y-k)^2&=(x-h+p)^2,\\ (y-k)^2&=4p(x-h). \end{aligned}(x−h−p)2+(y−k)2(y−k)2​=(x−h+p)2,=4p(x−h).​

Reversing these steps and taking nonnegative square roots proves the reverse direction, just as for the vertical form. For positive ppp, x≥hx\ge hx≥h and the curve opens right.

For negative ppp, x≤hx\le hx≤h and it opens left. In both orientations, the opening points from the vertex toward the focus.

Here the vertex is (1,1)(1,1)(1,1), the focus is (3,1)(3,1)(3,1), and the directrix is x=−1x=-1x=−1.

The equation is

(y−1)2=8(x−1).(y-1)^2=8(x-1).(y−1)2=8(x−1).

The points (3,5)(3,5)(3,5) and (3,−3)(3,-3)(3,−3) are each 444 units from both the focus and the line. This right-opening case uses the same distance rule as the left-opening case.

Recover and verify the geometry

Read the multiplier of the unsquared coordinate gap as 4p4p4p, then divide by four. If the equation is expanded, complete the square first.

Worked example 5 Recover a hidden focus and check a point

Problem. A parabola has equation

y2−6y+12x+21=0.y^2-6y+12x+21=0.y2−6y+12x+21=0.

Find its vertex, axis, focus, directrix, and opening direction. Verify PPP at (−4,9)(-4,9)(−4,9) using the defining distances.

Solution. Complete the y-square.

y2−6y=−12x−21,(y−3)2=−12x−12,(y−3)2=−12(x+1).\begin{aligned} y^2-6y&=-12x-21,\\ (y-3)^2&=-12x-12,\\ (y-3)^2&=-12(x+1). \end{aligned}y2−6y(y−3)2(y−3)2​=−12x−21,=−12x−12,=−12(x+1).​

The vertex is (−1,3)(-1,3)(−1,3) and the axis is y=3y=3y=3.

Since 4p=−124p=-124p=−12, the signed parameter is −3-3−3.

The focus is (−4,3)(-4,3)(−4,3) and the directrix is x=2x=2x=2. The curve opens left.

Point PPP is 666 units above the focus and 666 units left of the directrix. Thus both defining distances are 666. Substitution into the completed equation also gives 363636 on each side.

(y−1)2=−8(x−2).(y-1)^2=-8(x-2).(y−1)2=−8(x−2).

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Line distance: use the perpendicular distance to the directrix, not a slanted segment to an arbitrary point on it.
  • The multiplier: the equation contains 4p4p4p; divide by four before locating the focus.
  • Signed parameter: ppp can be negative, but the vertex-focus distance is ∣p∣|p|∣p∣.
  • Orientation: an x-square pairs with a vertical axis; a y-square pairs with a horizontal axis.
  • Squaring: retain nonnegative distances, including the absolute value for distance to the line, when checking the reverse direction.

Key takeaways

  • A parabola consists exactly of points equally distant from a focus and a directrix, with the focus off the line.
  • The vertex is halfway from the focus to its perpendicular foot on the directrix; the axis passes through them.
  • For a vertical axis, use (x−h)2=4p(y−k)(x-h)^2=4p(y-k)(x−h)2=4p(y−k) with nonzero signed ppp.
  • For a horizontal axis, use (y−k)2=4p(x−h)(y-k)^2=4p(x-h)(y−k)2=4p(x−h) with nonzero signed ppp.
  • Recover the signed parameter and verify the geometry with perpendicular distances.

A bit of history (optional)

In ancient Greece, doubling a cube meant finding the side of a new cube with twice the original volume. Doubling the side would make eight times the volume, so that simple move failed.

Menaechmus, a mathematician of the fourth century BCE, found a way to reach the required length using intersections of curves. A much later account by Eutocius preserves two solutions associated with him; one uses two parabolas. This was a different construction route from using only a straightedge and compass.

The surviving account leaves questions about how Menaechmus drew the curves. Modern equations can explain the intersections, but they are not evidence that he used coordinate algebra. His work survives through later writers rather than his own original text. MacTutor’s biography follows both the construction and the uncertainties in its transmission.

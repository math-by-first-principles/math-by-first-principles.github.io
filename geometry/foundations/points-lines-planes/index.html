Points, Lines, and Planes

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

The edges of a box may cross in a photograph even though the edges never touch. A sketch gives you a view, but you need precise words to describe what is actually there. Geometry starts with locations, straight paths, and flat surfaces, then states which relationships you may rely on.

Learning goals

  • Name points, lines, rays, segments, and planes using precise notation
  • Identify collinear and coplanar points from stated geometric information
  • Apply incidence foundations with their distinct-point and noncollinearity conditions
  • Distinguish intersecting, parallel, perpendicular, and skew lines in space
  • Classify two planes as intersecting, parallel, or coincident

Three starting objects

Imagine marking a location on a tabletop, tracing a straight path through it, and considering the whole flat surface of the table. These suggest a point, a line, and a plane.

We take point, line, and plane as undefined terms: starting ideas whose behavior we describe and accept, rather than define using simpler geometric objects. The descriptions below help you picture them. They are not definitions built from other terms.

ObjectHow to picture itHow to name it
PointAn exact location with no sizeA capital letter, such as AAA
LineA straight path with no thickness, extending without end in both directionsTwo distinct points, as in AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}AB, or a lowercase letter such as ℓ\ellℓ
PlaneA flat surface with no thickness, extending without end in every direction along itA script letter, such as P\mathcal PP, or three noncollinear points

A pencil dot has width, but the point it represents does not. A drawn line stops at the edge of the page, but the geometric line continues. A slanted parallelogram can represent a plane; its outline is a drawing boundary, not an edge of the plane.

Points are collinear when one line contains them. They are coplanar when one plane contains them. Three points that do not all lie on one line are noncollinear.

Check your understanding

Plane P\mathcal PP contains AAA, BBB, and CCC. The distinct points AAA and BBB lie on line ℓ\ellℓ, but CCC does not. Which description is correct?

Answer choices

All three points belong to the same plane, so they are coplanar.

The unique line through AAA and BBB is ℓ\ellℓ, which does not contain CCC. Thus the three points are noncollinear.

A line, a ray, and a segment

On a straight road, you could describe the whole path, the part beginning at one location and continuing in one direction, or just the part between two locations. Geometry gives each a different name.

We take the order of points along a line, including betweenness, as another starting idea. For distinct points occurring in order A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C, the point BBB is between AAA and CCC. This order is available before we define a segment.

A segment AB‾\overline{AB}AB consists of endpoints AAA and BBB and all points between them. A ray AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB begins at endpoint AAA, passes through the distinct point BBB, and continues without end in that direction.

NotationRead it asWhat changing the order does
AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}ABLine ABABABBA↔\overleftrightarrow{BA}BA is the same line
AB‾\overline{AB}ABSegment ABABABBA‾\overline{BA}BA is the same segment
AB→\overrightarrow{AB}ABRay ABABABBA→\overrightarrow{BA}BA has a different endpoint and is a different ray

The first letter of a ray matters: it names the endpoint. The second chooses a direction, not a stopping place. If AAA, BBB, and CCC occur in that order on a line, rays AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB and AC→\overrightarrow{AC}AC are the same ray. Rays BA→\overrightarrow{BA}BA and BC→\overrightarrow{BC}BC are opposite rays: they share endpoint BBB and together form the whole line.

Worked example 1 Name the object, not just the letters

Problem. Distinct points JJJ, KKK, and LLL occur in that order on one line. Name each object:

  1. The segment from JJJ to LLL.
  2. The ray starting at KKK through LLL. State whether it contains JJJ.
  3. The whole line, using two different names.

Solution.

  1. The segment contains all three points and is JL‾\overline{JL}JL.

  2. The ray is KL→\overrightarrow{KL}KL. It starts at KKK and continues through LLL, so it does not contain JJJ.

  3. The whole line is JK↔\overleftrightarrow{JK}JK, also named JL↔\overleftrightarrow{JL}JL because either pair consists of distinct points on it.

Check your understanding

Distinct points RRR, SSS, and TTT occur in that order on one line. Which ray equals RS→\overrightarrow{RS}RS?

Answer choices

Both RS→\overrightarrow{RS}RS and RT→\overrightarrow{RT}RT start at RRR and extend in the direction of SSS and TTT.

The second letter selects the direction; it is not a second endpoint.

Facts we accept at the start

Two distinct pinholes fix where a straight thread must run. Three pinholes all on that thread still leave a flat sheet free to turn around it. A third pinhole off the thread fixes the sheet’s position. These models help explain why the conditions below matter, although a physical model is not a proof.

An incidence relationship says which points belong to which lines or planes. We accept the following as starting facts, also called postulates. A postulate is a fact we agree to use without proving it within this course.

Accepted foundationConditions you must keep
Exactly one line contains two given pointsThe two points are distinct
Exactly one plane contains three given pointsThe three points are noncollinear
A line lies entirely in a plane if two of its points lie in that planeThe two points are distinct
If two planes intersect, all their common points form exactly one lineThe planes are distinct

We also accept that every line contains at least two distinct points, every plane contains at least three noncollinear points, and three-dimensional space contains points that do not all lie in one plane. These prevent the objects from collapsing into smaller ones.

One point alone does not determine a unique line. Three collinear points do not determine a unique plane: many planes can turn around their common line.

Worked example 2 Use the condition that makes the fact apply

Problem. Distinct points UUU and VVV belong to plane Q\mathcal QQ. Point WWW is also in Q\mathcal QQ but off line UVUVUV. Decide whether the whole line UVUVUV lies in Q\mathcal QQ and whether U,V,WU,V,WU,V,W name that plane uniquely. Explain what changes if WWW lies on UVUVUV.

Solution.

The line UV↔\overleftrightarrow{UV}UV lies entirely in Q\mathcal QQ, by the line-in-plane foundation. If point WWW is also in Q\mathcal QQ but off that line, UUU, VVV, and WWW name this plane uniquely. Merely adding another point on UV↔\overleftrightarrow{UV}UV would not do that.

Two distinct lines share at most one point. Two distinct points already fix one line, so they leave no choice of a second different line through both. This is a proved result from accepted line uniqueness.

The full proof explains why two different lines can share one point but cannot share two. It is in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Which information determines exactly one plane?

Lines in space

Two straight lines drawn on a sheet may meet or stay apart. Space gives us another possibility: they can pass in different directions without sharing a plane.

  • Intersecting lines share exactly one point. If their meeting makes a square corner, they are perpendicular, written ℓ⊥m\ell \perp mℓ⊥m. The term right angle also names that square corner; the next angle lesson develops its measurement.
  • Parallel lines are distinct, coplanar lines with no common point, written ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m.
  • Skew lines are lines that do not lie together in any plane. They are not parallel, and we prove below that they cannot intersect.
  • Coincident lines are two names for the same line. They share every point, rather than exactly one point.

The rectangular prism below lets you compare actual relationships. For this model, take the familiar box facts as givens: every face is a rectangle with parallel opposite edge lines and square corners, and opposite faces lie in parallel planes. Its edges are segments, but statements about their lines refer to the complete lines extending beyond the edges.

Worked example 3 Explain line relationships on a box

Problem. A rectangular box has front face ABFEABFEABFE, back face DCGHDCGHDCGH, and connecting edges AD,BC,FG,EHAD,BC,FG,EHAD,BC,FG,EH, as shown above. Every face is a rectangle, and opposite faces lie in parallel planes. Classify and justify each pair of complete edge lines:

  1. ABABAB and BFBFBF.
  2. ABABAB and FGFGFG.
  3. ABABAB and EFEFEF.

Solution.

  1. Lines ABABAB and BFBFBF meet at BBB, a given square corner of rectangular face ABFEABFEABFE, so they are perpendicular. The noncollinear points A,B,FA,B,FA,B,F name that front-face plane. It contains two distinct points of each line, so both complete lines lie in it by the line-in-plane foundation. Here two meeting lines are coplanar.

  2. For lines ABABAB and FGFGFG, the only candidate for a common plane is that front-face plane: a common plane must contain the noncollinear points A,B,FA,B,FA,B,F. Point GGG lies in the parallel back-face plane DCGHDCGHDCGH, so it is outside the front-face plane. The only candidate fails to contain GGG. Thus no plane contains both lines, so they are skew.

  3. By contrast, lines ABABAB and EFEFEF are parallel: they are the opposite edge lines of the rectangular front face, covered by the given box facts.

More generally, we can prove that two distinct intersecting lines are coplanar. Take their meeting point VVV, a point XXX on the first line other than VVV, and a point YYY on the second line other than VVV.

Distinct lines already share VVV and can have no other common point, so YYY is off the first line. The unique line through VVV and XXX is that first line, which misses YYY. Thus V,X,YV,X,YV,X,Y are noncollinear.

Their unique plane contains both lines by the line-in-plane foundation. A meeting therefore requires a common plane: lines that are not coplanar cannot meet.

A crossing of strokes in a perspective picture does not establish an intersection. Use the stated shape, labels, givens, and markings. Do not infer a square corner or parallelism from appearance alone.

Check your understanding

Two distinct lines have no common point. What additional information establishes that they are parallel?

Answer choices

Parallel lines must be coplanar as well as distinct and disjoint.

If the lines are not coplanar, they are skew instead. A flat sketch alone cannot settle this.

Two planes

The floor and a wall suggest two different planes meeting along a line. Two shelves at different heights suggest parallel planes. Two names for the plane of one shelf describe coincident planes.

  • Intersecting planes are distinct planes whose intersection, the set of all their common points, is a line. They cannot share just one isolated point or have another common point off that line.
  • Parallel planes are distinct planes with no common point.
  • Coincident planes are the same plane under different names, so every point of either belongs to both.

In ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space, those are the possibilities for two planes. The word distinct is essential: the intersection-line foundation does not say that a plane meets itself in just a line.

For the prism, the planes of front face ABFEABFEABFE and right face BCGFBCGFBCGF meet along line BFBFBF. The planes of front face ABFEABFEABFE and back face DCGHDCGHDCGH are parallel. Plane ABFABFABF and plane AEFAEFAEF are coincident: each triple is noncollinear and lies in the same front-face plane.

Check your understanding

Two distinct planes share point XXX. Which conclusion follows from the accepted plane-intersection foundation?

Answer choices

The shared point means that the planes intersect.

Because they are distinct, the accepted foundation says that all their common points form a line. That line contains XXX.

What supports each statement?

This reference will grow through the course. A definition fixes a word’s meaning; an accepted foundation supplies a starting fact; a proved result follows from facts already available. An experimental observation reports what a drawing or measurement suggests.

DefinitionsSegment, ray; collinear, coplanar; the line and plane relationships above
Accepted foundationsUnique line through two distinct points; unique plane through three noncollinear points; line containment in a plane; all common points of distinct intersecting planes form a line; the minimum-point facts

We work in ordinary Euclidean space. The accepted foundations describe that setting; the pictures help you see it, but do not replace the conditions.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Ray order: AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB starts at AAA; reversing the letters changes the endpoint.
  • Naming a plane: Three collinear points do not single out one plane. Use three noncollinear points.
  • No intersection: Disjoint lines in space can be parallel or skew. Check whether they are coplanar.
  • A drawing’s limits: Arrowheads and plane outlines represent continuation, not finite boundaries. A visual crossing alone is not a given intersection.
  • Distinct planes: The intersection of a plane with itself is the whole plane, not just a line.

Key takeaways

  • A point, line, and plane are starting objects described by their accepted behavior.
  • A segment has two endpoints; a ray has one endpoint; a line continues both ways.
  • Collinear means on one line; coplanar means in one plane.
  • Keep the hypotheses: two distinct points determine a line, and three noncollinear points determine a plane.
  • Parallel lines are coplanar and disjoint; skew lines are noncoplanar. Distinct planes either share a line or are parallel; coincident planes are the same plane.

Why distinct lines cannot share two points

Why distinct lines cannot share two points#

In the prism, lines ABABAB and BFBFBF share BBB. A second common point would fix a single line together with BBB, leaving no different line through both.

For the general argument, let rrr and sss be distinct lines. Temporarily suppose they shared two distinct points HHH and JJJ. Exactly one line contains HHH and JJJ, by the accepted unique-line foundation. Both rrr and sss would be that line, so they would be the same line. This contradicts their stated distinctness. Thus two distinct lines cannot share two distinct points, which means they share at most one point.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

The opening of Euclid’s Elements describes a point as something with no parts and a line as length without breadth. Those short descriptions invite the reader to picture objects that no pencil can draw exactly. A real dot has size; the geometric location it stands for does not.

Euclid then gives starting assumptions and builds arguments from them. His first postulate permits a straight line to be drawn from one point to another. The organization mattered as much as the individual facts: a reader could ask which earlier statement supported a later one.

The surviving text has a long history of copying and editing, so the exact wording of every opening definition cannot be confidently assigned to Euclid himself. David Joyce’s edition and notes at Clark University explain both the mathematical starting point and that uncertainty.

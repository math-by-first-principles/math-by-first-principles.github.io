  1. Problem 1 Read the notation

    For distinct points MMM and NNN, name the endpoint or endpoints of MN→\overrightarrow{MN}MN and MN‾\overline{MN}MN. State which of these objects contains the other.

    1. Hint 1

      The mark above the letters tells you whether an object stops.

    2. Hint 2

      A ray starts at its first letter and continues through its second.

    Answer

    MN→\overrightarrow{MN}MN has endpoint MMM; MN‾\overline{MN}MN has endpoints MMM and NNN. The ray contains the segment.

    Full solution

    The ray begins at MMM and continues through and beyond NNN, so it has only one endpoint.

    The segment stops at both MMM and NNN.

    Every point between them belongs to the ray, so the ray contains the segment.

    Answer

    MN→\overrightarrow{MN}MN has endpoint MMM; MN‾\overline{MN}MN has endpoints MMM and NNN. The ray contains the segment.

    Key idea

    Read the mark and the order together to identify a geometric object.

  2. Problem 2 Same direction

    Distinct points AAA, BBB, CCC, and DDD occur in that order on one line.

    Among BA→\overrightarrow{BA}BA, BC→\overrightarrow{BC}BC, and BD→\overrightarrow{BD}BD, identify equal rays and a pair of opposite rays.

    Text description of this figure

    The labeled points lie on one line in the stated order. Drawn spacing adds no length or equality information.

    1. Hint 1

      First compare the endpoints, then the directions.

    2. Hint 2

      From BBB, points CCC and DDD are on the same side, while AAA is on the other side.

    Answer

    BC→=BD→\overrightarrow{BC}=\overrightarrow{BD}BC=BD. BA→\overrightarrow{BA}BA is opposite to either of those rays.

    Full solution

    All three rays begin at BBB.

    The ray toward CCC also passes through DDD, so the two forward rays are equal.

    The ray toward AAA extends the other way on the same line.

    Together with either forward ray, it forms the entire line.

    Answer

    BC→=BD→\overrightarrow{BC}=\overrightarrow{BD}BC=BD. BA→\overrightarrow{BA}BA is opposite to either of those rays.

    Key idea

    A ray's second named point selects its direction, not its extent.

    ▾

  3. Problem 3 Choose valid plane names

    Plane S\mathcal SS contains distinct collinear points D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F and a point ZZZ off their line.

    Among the triples DEFDEFDEF, DEZDEZDEZ, DFZDFZDFZ, and EFZEFZEFZ, which name S\mathcal SS uniquely?

    Explain why the remaining triple does not.

    Text description of this figure

    Plane S contains the complete line through distinct D and F, and point Z off that line. Point E is also on the drawn line. The parallelogram represents only a patch of the unlimited plane.

    1. Hint 1

      A plane name needs three noncollinear points.

    2. Hint 2

      Compare the triple made entirely from the line with each triple containing two line points and ZZZ.

    Answer

    DEZDEZDEZ, DFZDFZDFZ, and EFZEFZEFZ name S\mathcal SS uniquely. The collinear triple DEFDEFDEF does not.

    Full solution

    The points D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F lie on one line, so their triple does not determine a unique plane.

    Each remaining triple contains two distinct points on that line and the point ZZZ off it.

    Each triple is therefore noncollinear.

    All its points lie in S\mathcal SS, so the unique-plane foundation makes each of DEZDEZDEZ, DFZDFZDFZ, and EFZEFZEFZ a valid name for S\mathcal SS.

    Answer

    DEZDEZDEZ, DFZDFZDFZ, and EFZEFZEFZ name S\mathcal SS uniquely. The collinear triple DEFDEFDEF does not.

    Key idea

    Select a plane name by checking its points, not by counting three letters.

    ▾

  4. Problem 4 Containment beyond a segment

    A plane contains distinct points PPP and QQQ.

    Point RRR lies on PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​ beyond QQQ.

    Must the plane contain RRR?

    Give the accepted foundation that supports your answer.

    1. Hint 1

      Decide whether the foundation concerns a segment or an entire line.

    2. Hint 2

      The line has two distinct points in the plane.

    Answer

    Yes. The line-in-plane foundation puts all of PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​, including RRR, in the plane.

    Full solution

    The plane contains two distinct points of PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​.

    The accepted containment foundation therefore puts the entire line in the plane.

    The location of RRR beyond QQQ does not matter, because the foundation applies beyond the segment as well.

    Answer

    Yes. The line-in-plane foundation puts all of PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​, including RRR, in the plane.

    Key idea

    Two distinct points of a line in a plane force the whole line into that plane.

  5. Problem 5 Three planes through one line

    Three pairwise distinct planes A\mathcal AA, B\mathcal BB, and C\mathcal CC all contain line ttt. Describe the intersection of each pair. Can any pair be parallel?

    1. Hint 1

      Every pair shares the whole given line.

    2. Hint 2

      Apply the intersection foundation to distinct planes that share points.

    Answer

    Each pair intersects in line ttt. No pair is parallel.

    Full solution

    Planes A\mathcal AA and B\mathcal BB share line ttt, so they intersect.

    Their distinctness makes all their common points form one line kkk.

    Choose two distinct points U,VU,VU,V of ttt.

    Both lie on kkk too, so the unique-line foundation identifies kkk as ttt.

    The same reasoning applies to the other two pairs.

    Parallel planes have no common points, so no pair here is parallel.

    Answer

    Each pair intersects in line ttt. No pair is parallel.

    Key idea

    Several distinct planes can contain the same line.

    ▾

  6. Problem 6 Relationships on a box

    A rectangular box has front face ABFEABFEABFE, back face DCGHDCGHDCGH, and connecting edges ADADAD, BCBCBC, FGFGFG, and EHEHEH.

    Use the box facts that each face is a rectangle and opposite faces lie in parallel planes. Classify each pair of complete lines:

    Pair A: BFBFBF and EHEHEH.

    Pair B: BCBCBC and FGFGFG.

    Pair C: EHEHEH and HGHGHG.

    Text description of this figure

    The front face is ABFE and the back face is DCGH. Connecting edges are AD, BC, FG and EH. Only hidden edges AD, DC and DH are dashed. Use the given rectangular-face and parallel-plane facts, rather than perspective appearance.

    1. Hint 1

      Check which vertices a common plane would have to contain, and use the stated rectangular faces.

    2. Hint 2

      For the first pair, compare the plane through B,F,EB,F,EB,F,E with the location of HHH. For the other pairs, examine opposite and adjacent edges of their shared faces.

    Answer

    BFBFBF and EHEHEH are skew; BCBCBC and FGFGFG are parallel; EHEHEH and HGHGHG are perpendicular.

    Full solution

    Pair A: Any plane containing BFBFBF and EHEHEH contains B,F,EB,F,EB,F,E.

    These are noncollinear vertices of the rectangular front face, so that face is the only candidate plane.

    Point HHH lies in the distinct parallel back-face plane, outside the front-face plane.

    The only candidate fails to contain HHH, so BFBFBF and EHEHEH are skew.

    Pair B: Lines BCBCBC and FGFGFG are opposite edge lines of rectangular face BCGFBCGFBCGF, so they are parallel.

    Pair C: Lines EHEHEH and HGHGHG are adjacent edge lines of the rectangular top face.

    They meet at the square corner HHH, so they are perpendicular.

    Answer

    BFBFBF and EHEHEH are skew; BCBCBC and FGFGFG are parallel; EHEHEH and HGHGHG are perpendicular.

    Key idea

    Classify line pairs using incidence and the given relationships of a solid's faces.

    ▾

  7. Problem 7 Two planes containing two lines

    Planes P\mathcal PP and Q\mathcal QQ both contain two distinct intersecting lines rrr and sss.

    Must the planes coincide?

    Explain using the incidence foundations.

    1. Hint 1

      Find three noncollinear points that both planes must contain.

    2. Hint 2

      Choose the lines' meeting point and one different point on each line.

    Answer

    Yes. The planes coincide.

    Full solution

    Let VVV be the meeting point.

    Choose XXX on rrr and YYY on sss, each different from VVV.

    Distinct lines already share VVV and can have no other common point.

    Thus YYY is off rrr, the unique line through VVV and XXX, making V,X,YV,X,YV,X,Y noncollinear.

    Both planes contain the three points.

    Exactly one plane contains that noncollinear triple, so P\mathcal PP and Q\mathcal QQ are the same plane.

    Answer

    Yes. The planes coincide.

    Key idea

    Two distinct intersecting lines supply a noncollinear triple and determine one plane.

  8. Problem 8 Disjoint does not settle it

    A rectangular box has front face ABFEABFEABFE, back face DCGHDCGHDCGH, and connecting edges ADADAD, BCBCBC, FGFGFG, and EHEHEH.

    Each face is a rectangle, and opposite faces lie in parallel planes.

    The claim is: any two lines with no common point are parallel.

    Use a pair of the box's complete edge lines to give a counterexample, explain why it meets the premise, and identify the missing condition.

    Text description of this figure

    The front face is ABFE and the back face is DCGH. Connecting edges are AD, BC, FG and EH. Only hidden edges AD, DC and DH are dashed. Use the given rectangular-face and parallel-plane facts, rather than perspective appearance.

    1. Hint 1

      A plane containing both selected lines would have to contain all their endpoints.

    2. Hint 2

      Compare a front vertical edge with a back horizontal edge. Which face plane would three of their endpoints determine?

    Answer

    For example, lines AEAEAE and CDCDCD are skew. They have no common point but are not parallel; the claim is missing coplanarity.

    Full solution

    Take lines AEAEAE and CDCDCD.

    Any common plane would contain the noncollinear points A,C,DA,C,DA,C,D, so it would be the bottom-face plane.

    But EEE is in the parallel top-face plane, outside the bottom-face plane.

    Thus the two lines are noncoplanar.

    They are skew and have no common point, since noncoplanar lines cannot meet.

    They satisfy the claim's premise but fail its parallelism conclusion.

    Requiring the distinct lines to be coplanar makes the claim correct.

    Answer

    For example, lines AEAEAE and CDCDCD are skew. They have no common point but are not parallel; the claim is missing coplanarity.

    Key idea

    A self-contained counterexample satisfies the hypothesis while failing the conclusion.

  9. Problem 9 A wall and a shelf

    Model a wall and a shelf as distinct planes sharing a line.

    A straight rail contains distinct points RRR and SSS on that shared line.

    Must the rail's entire line belong to both planes?

    Explain, and name the intersection line.

    1. Hint 1

      The two distinct points RRR and SSS determine the rail's line.

    2. Hint 2

      Apply line containment separately to the wall plane and the shelf plane.

    Answer

    Yes. Their intersection is RS↔\overleftrightarrow{RS}RS, the rail's line, which lies in both planes.

    Full solution

    The rail's distinct points RRR and SSS lie on the shared intersection line.

    Line uniqueness identifies both the rail's line and the intersection line as RS↔\overleftrightarrow{RS}RS.

    Both planes contain that entire line by the line-in-plane foundation.

    This conclusion concerns the ideal line extending beyond the physical rail.

    Answer

    Yes. Their intersection is RS↔\overleftrightarrow{RS}RS, the rail's line, which lies in both planes.

    Key idea

    Two shared distinct points can name the intersection line of distinct planes.

  10. Problem 10 Enough information to decide

    In each case, decide whether the information forces a unique plane.

    Case A: three distinct points on one line.

    Case B: a line and a point off that line.

    Case C: two distinct intersecting lines.

    Explain the decisive condition in each case.

    1. Hint 1

      Try to locate three noncollinear points in each case.

    2. Hint 2

      For Case C, choose the intersection and one other point from each line.

    Answer

    Case A: no. Case B: yes. Case C: yes.

    Full solution

    Case A supplies only collinear points.

    Many planes contain their common line, so no unique plane is forced.

    In Case B, choose distinct points J,KJ,KJ,K on the given line and name the off-line point TTT.

    The given line is the unique line through J,KJ,KJ,K, and TTT is off it, so J,K,TJ,K,TJ,K,T are noncollinear.

    Their unique plane contains the entire given line by the line-in-plane foundation.

    Any plane containing that line and TTT contains the same noncollinear triple, so it must be this plane.

    In Case C, let the distinct lines r,sr,sr,s meet at VVV.

    Choose XXX on rrr and YYY on sss, each different from VVV.

    Distinct lines already share VVV and can have no other common point, so YYY is off rrr, the unique line through V,XV,XV,X.

    Thus V,X,YV,X,YV,X,Y are noncollinear.

    Their unique plane contains both lines by the line-in-plane foundation.

    Any plane containing both lines contains the same noncollinear triple, so it must be this plane.

    Answer

    Case A: no. Case B: yes. Case C: yes.

    Key idea

    Sufficient spatial information often reduces to finding three noncollinear points.

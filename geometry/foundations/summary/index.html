Foundations and Reasoning: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Point, line, and plane
Undefined starting terms. A point is pictured as a location without size, a line as a straight path extending both ways, and a plane as an unlimited flat surface. Drawn dots, strokes, and plane outlines have physical limits the objects do not.
Line, ray, segment, and plane notation
AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}AB names the whole line; AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB starts at AAA and continues through BBB; AB‾\overline{AB}AB has endpoints A,BA,BA,B. Use distinct named points. Reversing ray letters changes its endpoint. Opposite rays share an endpoint and form a line. Write ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m for parallel lines and ℓ⊥m\ell\perp mℓ⊥m for perpendicular lines. Name a plane with a script letter such as P\mathcal PP or three noncollinear points.
Collinear and coplanar
Collinear points lie on one line; coplanar points lie in one plane. A noncollinear triple does not lie on one line.
Objects, measures, and congruence
ABABAB is the length of AB‾\overline{AB}AB; m∠AOBm\angle AOBm∠AOB is the selected angle's measure. Congruent segments have equal lengths; congruent angles have equal measures. Write AB‾≅CD‾\overline{AB}\cong\overline{CD}AB≅CD for objects and AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD for lengths.
Betweenness, midpoint, and segment bisector
Betweenness requires three distinct collinear points in the stated order. A midpoint lies on its segment and makes equal halves. A segment bisector is a line, ray, segment, or plane intersecting the segment only at its midpoint; perpendicularity is not required.
Angle, vertex, and selected opening
An angle is formed by two rays with a common endpoint, its vertex. The middle letter names the vertex. Three-letter notation selects an opening of at most a half turn unless a different opening is specified; name a reflex opening explicitly.
Circle and disk
A circle is the set of points in a plane at one fixed positive distance from its center. A disk includes that boundary and its interior, including the center.
Radius, chord, diameter, and arc
Radius names the fixed distance or a center-to-circle segment. A chord joins two circle points; a diameter is a chord through the center. An arc is a portion of a circle.
Construction tools
A compass draws a circle or arc with a chosen positive radius; the compass used here retains its opening when moved. An unmarked straightedge draws through two distinct points without reading a measurement scale.
Definition, postulate, theorem, observation
A definition fixes meaning. A postulate, or axiom, is accepted as a foundation. A theorem has a proof from available facts. A measurement or moving figure supplies an experimental observation, not an exact general proof.
Inductive and deductive reasoning
Inductive reasoning uses examples to suggest a conjecture. Deductive reasoning uses accepted facts and valid steps to justify a conclusion. Repeated successful tests alone do not prove a universal conjecture.
Conditional and negation
In p→qp\to qp→q, ppp is the hypothesis and qqq the conclusion: if ppp, then qqq. Negation ¬p\neg p¬p says that the entire statement ppp is false.
Counterexample
An allowed case with a true hypothesis and a false conclusion refutes a universal conditional. An object outside the hypothesis does not refute it.
Inclusive square and rectangle definitions
A rectangle is a quadrilateral with four right angles. A square is a rectangle with four congruent sides, so every square is a rectangle.
Equilateral triangle
A triangle with three equal side lengths.
Givens, goal, and proof formats
Givens are supplied facts; the goal is the claim to establish. Paragraph proofs connect reasons in sentences; two-column proofs pair statements with reasons; flow proofs show dependencies with arrows. All require a complete argument.

Formulas and theorems

  • Accepted incidence foundations

    Two distinct points determine exactly one line. Three noncollinear points determine exactly one plane. A line with two distinct points in a plane lies entirely in that plane. Two distinct intersecting planes share exactly one line.

    Use when Keep distinctness and noncollinearity. Every line contains at least two distinct points; every plane contains at least three noncollinear points; space contains noncoplanar points.

  • Line relationships

    Parallel lines are distinct, coplanar, and disjoint. Skew lines are noncoplanar. Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle. Coincident lines are the same line; distinct lines share at most one point (proved from the unique-line foundation).

    Text description

    A rectangular prism has front face A B F E and back face D C G H. The complete front bottom and front top lines are parallel. The front bottom and front right vertical lines are perpendicular. The front bottom line and the top right line extending toward the back are skew: they do not meet and do not share a plane.

    Use when Use complete lines, including extensions. In the labeled rectangular prism, lines AB and EF are parallel, AB and BF are perpendicular, and AB and FG are skew.

  • Plane relationships

    Distinct planes either intersect in one line or are parallel with no common points. Coincident planes are the same plane and share every point.

    Use when Ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space. The intersection-line claim excludes coincident planes.

  • Number-line distance and midpoint coordinate

    AB=∣b−a∣m=a+b2\begin{gathered} AB=|b-a|\\ m=\frac{a+b}{2} \end{gathered}AB=∣b−a∣m=2a+b​​

    Use when a,ba,ba,b are endpoint coordinates on one number line with a fixed unit; mmm is the midpoint coordinate. Distance is an accepted foundation; the midpoint formula follows from equal halves. Length is nonnegative; a coordinate may be negative.

    e.g. Endpoints −21-21−21 and 161616 give length 373737 and midpoint coordinate −2.5-2.5−2.5.

  • Accepted segment addition

    AB+BC=ACAB+BC=ACAB+BC=AC

    Use when BBB is between AAA and CCC; the distinct points are collinear. All lengths use the same unit.

    Use when MMM is the midpoint of the nonzero segment AB‾\overline{AB}AB. Equal distances alone do not supply its location on the segment.

  • Angle types and turns

    Acute: 0∘<m<90∘0^\circ<m<90^\circ0∘<m<90∘. Right: m=90∘m=90^\circm=90∘. Obtuse: 90∘<m<180∘90^\circ<m<180^\circ90∘<m<180∘. Straight: m=180∘m=180^\circm=180∘. Reflex: 180∘<m<360∘180^\circ<m<360^\circ180∘<m<360∘. Full turn: 360∘360^\circ360∘.

    Use when mmm is the selected opening's measure. One degree is one three-hundred-sixtieth of a full turn. A zero turn and a full turn end in the same direction but record different turns.

    Use when Ray OB lies inside the selected opening AOC, and the selected parts fill it with no overlapping interiors. Specify a reflex whole explicitly.

  • Circle and disk distance tests

    Circle membership means OP=rOP=rOP=r; disk membership means OP≤rOP\le rOP≤r. A point with OP>rOP>rOP>r is outside the disk.

    Use when Points lie in the circle's plane; OOO is its center and r>0r>0r>0. The center belongs to the disk, not the circle.

  • Diameter and radius

    d=2r d=2rd=2r

    Use when ddd is a diameter's length, not an arbitrary chord's length; rrr is the positive radius.

  • Equal-radius construction results

    If Q lies on the circle centered at P with retained compass opening AB, then PQ=ABPQ=ABPQ=AB. An intersection C of circles centered at A and B with common radius AB gives AC=ABAC=ABAC=AB and BC=ABBC=ABBC=AB.

    Use when Work in one plane with distinct A,BA,BA,B and retained positive compass settings. Accept the unique center-ray intersection and the two intersections for each specified equilateral or angle-copy circle pair; the latter needs a source opening strictly between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘.   • Four conditional forms

    Use when Keep the meanings of p,qp,qp,q and the allowed objects fixed when changing order or negating.

  • Logical equivalence and biconditionals

    A conditional is logically equivalent to its contrapositive; its converse is logically equivalent to its inverse. The biconditional p↔qp\leftrightarrow qp↔q requires both p→qp\to qp→q and q→pq\to pq→p.

    Use when A true original does not establish its converse or inverse. Check every geometric condition in each direction.

  • Detachment and syllogism

    Detachment: p→qp\to qp→q and a matching fact ppp give qqq. Syllogism: p→qp\to qp→q and q→rq\to rq→r give p→rp\to rp→r.

    Use when Use valid conditionals in their stated directions. A conditional chain alone does not supply a starting fact about a particular object.

  • Equality and congruence properties

    Reflexive: a=aa=aa=a. Symmetric: a=ba=ba=b gives b=ab=ab=a. Transitive: a=ba=ba=b and b=cb=cb=c give a=ca=ca=c. Substitute equals and apply equal operations to both sides. Addition is commutative.

    Use when Here a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c are lengths or measures. Divide only by a nonzero number. Segment and angle congruence inherit reflexive, symmetric, and transitive properties from equal measures.

  • Two proved geometric results

    For distinct collinear points in order A,B,C,D, AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD gives AC=BDAC=BDAC=BD; conversely, AC=BDAC=BDAC=BD gives AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD by subtracting BCBCBC. Concentric circles with unequal positive radii have no common point.

    Use when Keep the point order for both directions of the first result. For the second, concentric means the same center; equal radii give coincident circles when the circles also lie in the same plane.

Problem types, step by step

Identify an object or spatial relationship

  1. Read notation, named points, stated shape, and given markings.
  2. Check distinctness, noncollinearity, and coplanarity before applying the relevant definition or incidence foundation.
  3. For prism edges, classify their complete lines; do not infer intersections from projected crossings.

Find lengths, endpoints, or a midpoint

  1. Identify the point order, whole segment, equal parts, and units.
  2. Use coordinate difference, segment addition, or the midpoint condition to form an equation.
  3. For a positive distance ddd from coordinate aaa, check a−da-da−d and a+da+da+d unless a direction is specified.
  4. With one endpoint coordinate aaa and midpoint coordinate mmm, use 2m=a+b2m=a+b2m=a+b and solve for the missing endpoint bbb.
  5. Solve, check positive part lengths, and distinguish a midpoint coordinate from a half length.

Measure or draw a selected angle

  1. Put the protractor center on the vertex and align its baseline with one ray.
  2. Follow the scale starting at that ray's zero; read or mark the required measure.
  3. For a reflex opening, subtract the smaller opening from a full turn. Extending drawn rays changes no measure.

Use angle addition and bisection

  1. Select the whole opening and verify which nonoverlapping parts fill it.
  2. Add part measures for the whole, or subtract a known part. A bisector supplies equal part measures.
  3. Solve expressions and check that the measures fit the selected opening.

Copy a nonzero segment onto a ray

  1. Set the compass to the source segment's endpoint distance.
  2. Keep that opening and draw a circle centered at the target ray's endpoint.
  3. Mark its intersection with the ray; the new segment has the retained radius.

Copy an angle with compass and straightedge

  1. For a source opening strictly between zero and a half turn, draw a vertex-centered circle meeting its rays at A and B.
  2. Keep its radius to draw the target circle at P, meeting the target ray at D.
  3. Set the compass to chord AB and draw an arc centered at D, meeting the target circle at E on the specified side.
  4. Draw ray PE. Preserve helper arcs; the full angle-copy proof is deferred to lesson 4.5.

Construct an equilateral triangle

  1. Draw circles centered at distinct endpoints A and B, both with radius AB.
  2. Choose either circle intersection as C and join AC and BC.
  3. Retain the circles and mark the equal sides, using each circle's radius condition.

Test a logical claim

  1. State the hypothesis, conclusion, allowed objects, and needed definitions.
  2. Seek an allowed case with true hypothesis and false conclusion; one refutes a universal claim.
  3. Check reverse implications separately; an if-and-only-if claim needs both directions. Apply detachment or connect matching forward links when justified.

Plan and write a geometric proof

  1. Separate givens from goal and annotate only supplied or justified facts.
  2. Midpoints or bisectors suggest equal parts; betweenness, segment addition; an interior ray, angle addition; expressions, substitution.
  3. Plan backward from the goal to needed facts, then write the proof forward from the givens. A direct proof follows justified steps to the goal.
  4. Consider contradiction for impossibility or at-most-one goals. Proof by contradiction is a form of indirect proof: keep the givens, assume the goal's negation, derive a named conflict with known facts, and reject that assumption.
  5. Supply every necessary reason in paragraph, two-column, or flow form. Cite a given or prove a needed geometric condition; otherwise report a gap. Never invent givens.

Exam traps

  • Trap Dropping distinctness or noncollinearity

    Fix Use two distinct points for a unique line and three noncollinear points for a unique plane.

  • Trap Calling every disjoint pair of lines parallel

    Fix Parallel lines must be coplanar as well as distinct and disjoint. Skew lines are noncoplanar.

  • Trap Using equality of parts without their location

    Fix Midpoint requires a point on the segment; segment addition requires betweenness. Equal distances alone do not supply either.

  • Trap Treating a bisector as automatically perpendicular

    Fix Bisection means equal halves. A square-corner marking establishes perpendicularity, not equal halves.

  • Trap Reading an endpoint instead of measuring a gap

    Fix Subtract ruler readings, count intervals, and convert to one unit. Match reported precision to the measuring tool.

  • Trap Reading the wrong protractor scale or opening

    Fix Start at the zero on the baseline ray. Specify the selected opening before adding, halving, or finding its reflex partner.

  • Trap Treating a circle as its filled interior

    Fix Circle points have exactly the radius distance; a disk also includes smaller distances. Not every chord is a diameter.

  • Trap Changing the wrong compass setting

    Fix Retain the radius when transferring it. In an angle copy, change to the source chord only for the chord-transfer step.

  • Trap Reversing a conditional from its conclusion

    Fix Knowing qqq does not establish ppp from p→qp\to qp→q. The converse needs its own justification; examples alone are insufficient.

  • Trap Promoting a sketch or measurement to a proof

    Fix Use definitions, accepted foundations, prior proved results, and valid algebra. Do not mark the goal as a given.

  • Trap Stopping after assuming the opposite

    Fix A contradiction proof must identify an impossibility conflicting with known facts, then reject the temporary assumption and conclude the goal.

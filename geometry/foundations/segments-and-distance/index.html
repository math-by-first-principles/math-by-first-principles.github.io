Segments, Length, and Distance

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A ruler can measure a segment even when its endpoint is not at zero. What matters is the gap between the two readings. That same idea connects measurement to coordinates, and lets you combine lengths without trusting how long a sketch looks.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish a segment from its length and express segment congruence correctly
  • Find number-line distances using coordinates, including negative coordinates
  • Use betweenness and segment addition to find missing lengths
  • Locate a midpoint and decide whether an object bisects a segment
  • Read measurements and compare lengths using consistent units

The object and its length

Draw a segment joining AAA and BBB. The segment is a set of points. Its length is a nonnegative number describing how far apart its endpoints are, measured in a chosen unit.

We write AB‾\overline{AB}AB for the segment and ABABAB for its length. Thus AB=6AB=6AB=6 centimeters describes a measurement; AB‾\overline{AB}AB names the object being measured. Reversing the letters changes neither: AB‾=BA‾\overline{AB}=\overline{BA}AB=BA as sets of points, and AB=BAAB=BAAB=BA as lengths.

Two segments are congruent when their lengths are equal. If AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD, write AB‾≅CD‾\overline{AB}\cong\overline{CD}AB≅CD. The segments can occupy different places or point in different directions. Matching tick marks in a diagram declare congruent segments; appearance alone does not.

For distinct endpoints the length is positive. The distance from a point to itself is zero. A coordinate can be negative, but a length cannot.

Check your understanding

The segments JK‾\overline{JK}JK and RS‾\overline{RS}RS have equal lengths. Which statement correctly expresses their congruence?

Answer choices

Congruence compares the geometric segments, so place the bars over the letters. Equality compares their numerical lengths: JK=RSJK=RSJK=RS. Equal lengths do not require the segments to have the same endpoints.

Distance on a number line

From coordinate −4-4−4 to coordinate 777, you travel 444 units to zero and another 777 units to the right. The distance is 111111 units. Subtracting the left reading from the right gives that same gap:

7−(−4)=11.7-(-4)=11.7−(−4)=11.

Traveling back from 777 to −4-4−4 changes the direction but not the distance. The subtraction −4−7=−11-4-7=-11−4−7=−11 records a signed change, so take its absolute value to obtain the length.

We use the number-line distance foundation, often called the ruler postulate: real-number coordinates can be assigned so that each point on the line has exactly one coordinate, each real number is the coordinate of exactly one point on the line, and the distance between two points is the absolute difference of their coordinates. The coordinate scale fixes the unit of length.

For points with coordinates aaa and bbb:

distance=∣b−a∣.\text{distance}=|b-a|.distance=∣b−a∣.

You may instead subtract the smaller coordinate from the larger. Both methods give a nonnegative distance and work on either side of zero.

Worked example 1 Distance is unchanged when you reverse direction

Problem. Points PPP and QQQ have number-line coordinates 131313 and −4-4−4. Find the distance PQPQPQ by subtracting in both orders, and explain why the distance is unchanged.

Solution.

Using the coordinates in the order Q−PQ-PQ−P gives

PQ=∣−4−13∣=∣−17∣=17.PQ=|-4-13|=|-17|=17.PQ=∣−4−13∣=∣−17∣=17.

Using the opposite order gives ∣13−(−4)∣=17|13-(-4)|=17∣13−(−4)∣=17 too. The signed changes are opposites, but their absolute values are equal. The segment’s length is 171717 units in either direction.

Check your understanding

Points UUU and VVV have coordinates −12-12−12 and −5-5−5. What is UVUVUV?

Answer choices

Subtract the left coordinate from the right: −5−(−12)=7-5-(-12)=7−5−(−12)=7. Both endpoints are left of zero, but the distance between them is positive. Adding their absolute values would count a trip to zero and back instead of the direct gap.

Betweenness and segment addition

On the aligned number lines, 777 lies between −4-4−4 and 131313. Traveling from −4-4−4 to 131313 through 777 covers the whole segment once, with no gap or overlap. The two part lengths therefore add:

11+6=17.11+6=17.11+6=17.

The point order introduced in 1.1 can be expressed with coordinates. We accept that coordinates follow this order: for distinct collinear points A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C with coordinates a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c on one number line, point BBB is between AAA and CCC exactly when a<b<ca<b<ca<b<c or c<b<ac<b<ac<b<a. Either endpoint may come first on the coordinate scale.

We accept the segment addition foundation: if BBB is between AAA and CCC, then

AB+BC=AC.AB+BC=AC.AB+BC=AC.

The middle letter of the stated order identifies the joining point. A sketch that places a point near the segment is not enough: the betweenness condition includes collinearity.

For four distinct points in order A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D, apply segment addition twice. First, AC=AB+BCAC=AB+BCAC=AB+BC. Then AD=AC+CDAD=AC+CDAD=AC+CD, giving AD=AB+BC+CDAD=AB+BC+CDAD=AB+BC+CD. This joins three adjacent parts without introducing a new addition rule.

Worked example 2 Find the missing part of a whole

Problem. Point TTT lies between RRR and SSS. The whole segment has length RS=24RS=24RS=24 centimeters, and RT=9RT=9RT=9 centimeters. Find TSTSTS and check that the two parts make the whole.

Solution.

Segment addition gives

RT+TS=RS,9+TS=24,TS=15.\begin{aligned} RT+TS&=RS,\\ 9+TS&=24,\\ TS&=15. \end{aligned}RT+TS9+TSTS​=RS,=24,=15.​

So TS=15TS=15TS=15 centimeters. Check the roles: 9+15=249+15=249+15=24 adds two parts to the stated whole. Adding 9+249+249+24 would count the first part twice.

Check your understanding

Distinct points FFF, GGG, and HHH occur in that order on a line. If FG=8FG=8FG=8 and GH=19GH=19GH=19, what is FHFHFH?

Answer choices

The order says that GGG is between FFF and HHH. Add the adjacent parts: FH=FG+GH=8+19=27FH=FG+GH=8+19=27FH=FG+GH=8+19=27. Subtraction would find a missing part if the whole were known.

Midpoints and bisectors

If a segment is 202020 units long, a point halfway along it leaves 101010 units on each side. Both the location and the equal lengths matter.

A midpoint of AB‾\overline{AB}AB is a point MMM on the segment such that AM=MBAM=MBAM=MB. When AAA and BBB differ, MMM cannot be either endpoint: one part would have length zero while the other has the positive length ABABAB. Thus MMM is between them, and segment addition shows that each half has length AB/2AB/2AB/2.

On a number line, endpoints −4-4−4 and 777 are 111111 units apart. Half that gap is 5.55.55.5, so the midpoint is −4+5.5=1.5-4+5.5=1.5−4+5.5=1.5. It is also the average of the endpoint coordinates:

−4+72=1.5.\frac{-4+7}{2}=1.5.2−4+7​=1.5.

For general coordinates aaa and bbb, move halfway from aaa toward bbb. The signed change is b−ab-ab−a, which may be negative, so half that change gives a+(b−a)/2=(a+b)/2a+(b-a)/2=(a+b)/2a+(b−a)/2=(a+b)/2. Thus the midpoint’s number-line coordinate is (a+b)/2(a+b)/2(a+b)/2 regardless of endpoint order. This statement concerns coordinates on one line, not a two-coordinate formula in the plane.

A segment bisector is a line, ray, segment, or plane that intersects a segment at its midpoint, sharing just that midpoint with the segment. It cuts the segment into two congruent parts. A bisector need not be perpendicular to the segment. Crossing at a square corner and cutting into equal halves are separate conditions.

Worked example 3 Turn a midpoint condition into an equation

Problem. Point MMM is the midpoint of AB‾\overline{AB}AB. In the same unit, AM=3x+2AM=3x+2AM=3x+2 and MB=5x−6MB=5x-6MB=5x−6. Find xxx, both part lengths, and the whole length ABABAB. Check the midpoint conditions.

Solution.

Equal halves give

3x+2=5x−6,8=2x,x=4.\begin{aligned} 3x+2&=5x-6,\\ 8&=2x,\\ x&=4. \end{aligned}3x+28x​=5x−6,=2x,=4.​

Then AM=3(4)+2=14AM=3(4)+2=14AM=3(4)+2=14 and MB=5(4)−6=14MB=5(4)-6=14MB=5(4)−6=14. Both lengths are positive and equal, so the segment has total length AB=28AB=28AB=28. The algebra fits the geometric conditions.

Check your understanding

A line crosses PQ‾\overline{PQ}PQ​ at MMM, with PM=QM=12PM=QM=12PM=QM=12. It is not perpendicular to PQ‾\overline{PQ}PQ​. Which conclusion is correct?

Answer choices

The crossing point is on the segment and divides it into equal lengths, so it is the midpoint. The crossing line is a bisector even without perpendicularity. Segment addition gives PQ=12+12=24PQ=12+12=24PQ=12+12=24.

Measurement and units

Suppose one endpoint of a strip aligns with the 2.32.32.3 centimeter mark of a ruler and the other with 8.18.18.1 centimeters. The length is the difference, not the final reading:

8.1−2.3=5.8 cm.8.1-2.3=5.8\text{ cm}.8.1−2.3=5.8 cm.

A ruler measures intervals. The marks at 000, 111, 222, and 333 enclose three unit intervals, not four. If the zero end is worn, using two other readings and subtracting still measures the gap.

Use the same unit before comparing or adding lengths. Since 111 centimeter is 101010 millimeters, 5.85.85.8 centimeters is 585858 millimeters. A statement such as 5.8+58=63.85.8+58=63.85.8+58=63.8 cannot combine those measurements as lengths until their units match.

A physical measurement is approximate. If a ruler supports only a nearest-millimeter reading, report that precision rather than invent extra decimal places. An exact length supplied by a geometry problem is different: you may use the stated value exactly. A drawing labeled “not to scale” provides relationships and labels, not a reliable measurement from the page.

Check your understanding

The endpoints of a segment align with the 141414 mm and 474747 mm ruler marks. Which description gives its measured length?

Answer choices

Subtract the readings: 47−14=3347-14=3347−14=33 millimeters. Divide by 101010 to express the same length in centimeters: 333333 millimeters is 3.33.33.3 centimeters.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Segment or length: Write AB‾\overline{AB}AB for the object and ABABAB for its numerical length. Use congruence for segments and equality for lengths.
  • Negative coordinates: A negative location does not make a distance negative. Use the absolute difference.
  • The joining point: Add AB+BCAB+BCAB+BC to get ACACAC only with BBB between AAA and CCC.
  • Bisector or perpendicular: A bisector goes through the midpoint. It need not meet the segment at a square corner, and a square corner alone does not establish bisection.
  • Ruler readings: Subtract the endpoint readings, count intervals, and keep units consistent.

Key takeaways

  • Congruent segments have equal lengths, regardless of their locations or directions.
  • Number-line distance is ∣b−a∣|b-a|∣b−a∣ for coordinates aaa and bbb.
  • Segment addition gives AB+BC=ACAB+BC=ACAB+BC=AC when BBB is between AAA and CCC.
  • A midpoint gives two equal halves; a segment bisector meets the segment only at that point. The midpoint coordinate is (a+b)/2(a+b)/2(a+b)/2.
  • Subtract ruler readings and state a unit. Physical measurements are approximate.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1792, the astronomers Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain began surveying a route between Dunkirk and Barcelona. Their measurements supported an ambitious plan: tie a new unit, the meter, to the size of Earth rather than to a particular person’s arm or foot. The work took about seven years, and the resulting standard was embodied in a platinum bar.

Later standards used the distance between two fine lines on a platinum-iridium bar. That choice protected the measurement if the bar’s ends were damaged. But even a carefully kept object can be difficult to copy and compare. In 1960 the definition moved to a specified wavelength of light, and in 1983 it moved to the distance light travels in a specified fraction of a second.

The goal through these changes was to preserve the unit’s size while making it more reproducible. NIST’s account of the meter tells the survey and bar story; the BIPM’s historical account records the later definitions.

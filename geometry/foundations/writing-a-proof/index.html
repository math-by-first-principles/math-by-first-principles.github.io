Writing a Geometric Proof

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Two overlapping segments can look equally long even when the sketch is inaccurate. To settle the claim, identify the pieces they contain and explain why those lengths match. A geometric proof makes that explanation checkable: each step needs a reason, and the reasons must lead from the information supplied to the result you want.

Learning goals

  • Separate givens from the goal and annotate a diagram without adding assumptions
  • Justify geometric steps using definitions, equality properties, congruence, and substitution
  • Compare paragraph, two-column, and flow versions of the same argument
  • Choose and carry out direct reasoning or proof by contradiction
  • Repair an argument that relies on an unstated condition

Build one complete argument

Two segments made from matching end pieces and the same middle piece have equal lengths. Take four distinct points in the order A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D on a line. Suppose AB=CD=18AB=CD=18AB=CD=18 and BC=26BC=26BC=26, with every length in centimeters. Segment addition gives:

AC=18+26=44,BD=26+18=44.\begin{aligned} AC&=18+26=44,\\ BD&=26+18=44. \end{aligned}ACBD​=18+26=44,=26+18=44.​

Both lengths are 444444 centimeters. The reason is the matching end pieces and the shared middle length, not how the picture was drawn.

Now remove the particular measurements. The givens are the facts supplied: the four distinct points have that order on one line, and AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD. The goal is the statement to establish: AC‾≅BD‾\overline{AC}\cong\overline{BD}AC≅BD. A segment is the object; its length is the number, so AC=BDAC=BDAC=BD will establish the segment congruence.

When you draw the general case, label the points in the stated order and put matching tick marks on ABABAB and CDCDCD. Do not mark ACACAC and BDBDBD as congruent yet. That is the goal, not a given. The numerical labels belong to the particular example above; the following proof uses no particular lengths.

Equal end pieces around a common middle segment#

Because BBB lies between AAA and CCC, segment addition gives AC=AB+BCAC=AB+BCAC=AB+BC. Because CCC lies between BBB and DDD, it also gives BD=BC+CDBD=BC+CDBD=BC+CD. The given equality AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD lets us substitute CDCDCD for ABABAB in the first sum, obtaining AC=CD+BCAC=CD+BCAC=CD+BC. Changing the order of addition gives AC=BC+CDAC=BC+CDAC=BC+CD. Symmetry rewrites BD=BC+CDBD=BC+CDBD=BC+CD as BC+CD=BDBC+CD=BDBC+CD=BD. Transitivity with AC=BC+CDAC=BC+CDAC=BC+CD then gives AC=BDAC=BDAC=BD. Equal lengths mean AC‾≅BD‾\overline{AC}\cong\overline{BD}AC≅BD.

∎

This is a paragraph proof, a complete argument in connected sentences. The numbers 181818 and 262626 were not needed in its steps, so it applies to any positive end and middle lengths satisfying the givens.

Reasons you can use

The paragraph proof uses familiar algebra to handle geometric lengths. For example, 18+26=26+1818+26=26+1818+26=26+18 is the commutative property of addition: the same two lengths have the same sum in either order. A reason states why a step follows from the facts already available.

Equality concerns numbers. For lengths or angle measures a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c, the following properties are available from Algebra I; they are not new conjectures based on drawings.

PropertyWhat it allows
Reflexivea=aa=aa=a: a length or measure equals itself
SymmetricFrom a=ba=ba=b, write b=ab=ab=a
TransitiveFrom a=ba=ba=b and b=cb=cb=c, conclude a=ca=ca=c
Addition or subtractionAdd or subtract the same quantity on both sides of an equality
Multiplication or divisionMultiply both sides by the same number, or divide both by the same nonzero number
SubstitutionReplace a quantity in an expression with an equal quantity

For instance, if two whole lengths are both 444444 and you remove the same length 262626 from each, the remainders are equal:

44−26=18.44-26=18.44−26=18.

In a general proof, AC=BDAC=BDAC=BD similarly permits AC−BC=BD−BCAC-BC=BD-BCAC−BC=BD−BC. To identify these differences with particular segments, you still need betweenness. Algebra alone does not tell you which piece lies inside which whole.

Segment congruence means equality of lengths. Angle congruence means equality of the selected angle measures. Because their measures obey equality properties, congruence of segments and congruence of angles are also reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. For example, if ∠U≅∠V\angle U\cong\angle V∠U≅∠V and ∠V≅∠W\angle V\cong\angle W∠V≅∠W, their measures are equal in succession, so ∠U≅∠W\angle U\cong\angle W∠U≅∠W. Use unambiguous angle labels when a diagram has several angles at one vertex.

Worked example 1 Connect a geometric given to an equation

Problem. Point MMM is the midpoint of PQ‾\overline{PQ}PQ​. The lengths in the same unit are PM=3x+1PM=3x+1PM=3x+1 and MQ=2x+9MQ=2x+9MQ=2x+9. Find xxx and the whole length PQPQPQ, justifying the geometric equations.

Solution.

The midpoint definition gives PM‾≅MQ‾\overline{PM}\cong\overline{MQ}PM≅MQ​. Congruent segments have equal lengths, so PM=MQPM=MQPM=MQ. Substitution gives:

3x+1=2x+9,x+1=9,x=8.\begin{aligned} 3x+1&=2x+9,\\ x+1&=9,\\ x&=8. \end{aligned}3x+1x+1x​=2x+9,=9,=8.​

The second line subtracts 2x2x2x from both sides. The third subtracts 111. Substituting 888 into each length gives 252525 for both.

The midpoint condition also places MMM between PPP and QQQ. Segment addition therefore gives PQ=PM+MQPQ=PM+MQPQ=PM+MQ. Substituting the two part lengths gives

PQ=25+25=50.PQ=25+25=50.PQ=25+25=50.

The midpoint given earned both the equality of the parts and the betweenness needed to add them. The appearance of a centered point would not have earned either condition.

No addition or doubling is part of that translation.

The same proof in three formats

The complete paragraph argument came first. Two other formats display its structure without changing what must be justified.

A two-column proof pairs each statement with its reason. Here the givens are still four distinct collinear points in order A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D, with AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD.

StatementReason
AB=CDAB=CDAB=CDGiven
AC=AB+BCAC=AB+BCAC=AB+BCSegment addition; BBB is between AAA and CCC
BD=BC+CDBD=BC+CDBD=BC+CDSegment addition; CCC is between BBB and DDD
AC=CD+BCAC=CD+BCAC=CD+BCSubstitute CDCDCD for the equal length ABABAB
AC=BC+CDAC=BC+CDAC=BC+CDCommutative property of addition
AC=BDAC=BDAC=BDSymmetric and transitive properties of equality; both equal BC+CDBC+CDBC+CD
AC‾≅BD‾\overline{AC}\cong\overline{BD}AC≅BDEqual lengths give congruent segments

A flow proof places statements with their reasons in boxes. Arrows show which facts support the next conclusion. The first two boxes supply the substitution. Commutativity then reorders that sum. A separate segment-addition statement supplies the same sum for BDBDBD. Symmetry and transitivity join the two equalities, after which equal lengths give segment congruence. The arrows belong to the proof, rather than adding geometric givens.

Paragraph form keeps the reasoning connected. Two-column form makes a missing reason easy to spot. Flow form makes dependencies easy to see. You need to recognize the same argument in all three; learning three different mathematical arguments is unnecessary.

Check your understanding

A paragraph proof and a two-column proof use the same givens and the same justified steps. What changes between them?

Answer choices

Each version must connect the same givens to the same goal.

Moving a statement beside its reason changes the organization, not what makes the inference valid. A flow proof can display those same dependencies with arrows.

Choose a route before writing

In a direct proof, start with the givens and follow justified steps toward the goal. The overlapping-segment proof is direct. Its goal concerns two whole lengths, and segment addition expresses both as sums of parts.

Planning can run backward. Ask what would establish the goal, then ask which givens could supply it. For a segment-congruence goal, an equality of lengths would suffice. For an equality of whole lengths, equal sums of their parts might suffice. Write the finished proof forward from facts already known; planning backward does not permit assuming the goal.

Choose a route by the information supplied. A midpoint or bisector given suggests equal parts. A betweenness condition suggests segment addition. A dividing ray inside a selected angle suggests angle addition. An equality with expressions suggests substitution and algebra. These are possible starting points, not guarantees that a particular tool will finish every proof.

A positive goal may follow directly from an available definition or equation. A goal saying that something does not exist, cannot happen, or occurs at most once often suggests contradiction: its negation supplies a possible object or extra case to test against the givens. These are planning cues; more than one approach can give a valid proof.

Proof by contradiction

Sometimes the useful starting point is the opposite of the goal. Suppose two circles in one plane have the same center OOO, one of radius 111111 and one of radius 191919. They cannot share a point. If a point PPP were on both, the same distance OPOPOP would have to be both 111111 and 191919. Those unequal numbers cannot both be that one length.

This method is proof by contradiction, a form of indirect proof. Keep the givens, temporarily assume the negation of the goal, and deduce an impossibility. The negation is the statement that the goal is false. Since that assumption cannot coexist with the givens, reject it and conclude the original goal.

For a goal saying that no common point exists, its negation says that at least one common point exists. You must rule out that whole possibility, not just show that a poorly chosen point fails.

Worked example 2 Prove that unequal concentric circles have no common point

Problem. Two concentric circles, circles in one plane that share center OOO, have positive radii rrr and sss, with r≠sr\ne sr=s. Prove by contradiction that they have no common point, and explain why the unequal-radius condition matters.

Solution.

Assume for contradiction that a point PPP lies on both circles. By the definition of the first circle, OP=rOP=rOP=r. By the definition of the second, OP=sOP=sOP=s.

Symmetry and transitivity of equality give r=sr=sr=s. This contradicts the given r≠sr\ne sr=s. Therefore the assumed common point does not exist.

This repeats the 111111-and-191919 argument without particular values. Positivity makes the radius choices valid circles; inequality of the radii drives the contradiction. If the radii were equal, these concentric circles would be the same circle, so the no-common-point conclusion would fail.

Another contradiction may conflict with a uniqueness postulate rather than with arithmetic. The same rule applies: name the exact given, definition, or accepted foundation that the temporary assumption violates. Failure to find a direct proof is not itself a contradiction.

Lesson 1.1 established that two distinct lines have at most one common point. Here is a self-contained proof by contradiction: suppose two distinct lines share distinct points PPP and QQQ. The accepted unique-line postulate says that exactly one line passes through those two distinct points, so both proposed lines would be that same line. This contradicts their given distinctness. Therefore two distinct lines cannot share two distinct points.

Check your understanding

You want to prove that two distinct lines cannot have two distinct common points. Which is a suitable contradiction setup?

Answer choices

The negation of the goal asserts that two distinct common points exist.

The accepted incidence postulate says that exactly one line passes through those two distinct points. Both proposed lines would therefore be the same line, contradicting that they are distinct.

Repair assumptions and track foundations

An argument may use correct algebra after an unjustified geometric step. Suppose you know only AB=BCAB=BCAB=BC and claim that BBB is the midpoint of AC‾\overline{AC}AC. The lengths match, but the midpoint definition also needs BBB to lie on AC‾\overline{AC}AC. A bent configuration shows what the equality alone permits.

Repair the proof by citing the fact that BBB lies on AC‾\overline{AC}AC, if the problem gives it, or by proving that location from other givens. If neither is available, the midpoint conclusion is not established. Drawing a straight configuration does not repair a missing condition in the original problem.

Check your understanding

Given only AB = BC, which additional condition justifies that B is the midpoint of segment AC?

Answer choices

The midpoint definition requires the point to lie on the segment and split it into equal lengths.

AB = BC supplies equality. B lying on AC supplies the missing location condition. The bent diagram shows why the equality alone is insufficient.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the goal. Mark only givens or already justified facts on the starting diagram.
  • Naming a property without its conditions. Segment addition needs betweenness; division needs a nonzero divisor.
  • Confusing objects and measures. Segments or angles are congruent; their lengths or angle measures are equal.
  • Stopping at the temporary assumption. A contradiction proof must reach and identify an impossibility, then reject that assumption.
  • Trusting a format. A neat table or flow diagram cannot supply a missing reason.

Key takeaways

  • Separate the givens from the goal before annotating a diagram.
  • Justify each consequential step with a definition, accepted foundation, prior result, or valid algebra.
  • Paragraph, two-column, and flow proofs can present the same argument.
  • Direct reasoning moves from givens to goal; proof by contradiction rejects the negation after it conflicts with known facts.
  • Check every geometric condition before using an equation or classification.

A bit of history (optional)

Abraham Lincoln studied geometry after he was already a lawyer and had served in Congress. In an autobiographical account prepared in 1860 for a campaign biographer, he described his formal schooling as amounting to less than a year in total. Books remained a way to fill the gaps.

The account reports that he studied and nearly mastered six books of Euclid after his time in Congress. That was an ambitious reading project for an adult whose daily work was law rather than mathematics: Euclid’s books build arguments through definitions, accepted starting points, and earlier propositions.

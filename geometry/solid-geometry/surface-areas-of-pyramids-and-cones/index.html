Surface Areas of Pyramids and Cones

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

To cover a pointed roof with paper, you need the area of its outside, not the space beneath it. Cut along a few edges and spread the covering flat. A pyramid gives triangles; a right cone gives a circular sector. The flat pieces tell you which lengths measure the covering.

Learning goals

  • Derive regular-pyramid and right-cone lateral areas from their unfolded surfaces
  • Distinguish slant height from perpendicular height and calculate the needed length
  • Select the surfaces included in an open or closed boundary
  • Reconstruct a right cone and its sector pattern from linked measurements

Triangles around a regular pyramid

A regular pyramid has a convex regular polygon as its base and its apex directly above the base’s center. Its triangular side faces are congruent. Their common face altitude, measured from the apex perpendicular to a base edge within that face, is the slant height, written ℓ\ellℓ. This is different from an edge running from the apex to a base vertex.

Its lateral area is the combined area of the triangular side faces, excluding the base. A closed pyramid’s total surface area also includes its one polygonal base.

Worked example 1 Add the unfolded faces

Problem. A regular square pyramid has base side 666 cm and slant height 555 cm. Find its lateral area and its closed total surface area.

Solution. Each triangle has base 666 cm and perpendicular face altitude 555 cm. Its area is 151515 square centimeters. There are four triangles.

L=4(12⋅6⋅5)L=60 cm2.\begin{aligned} L&=4\left(\tfrac12\cdot6\cdot5\right)\\ L&=60\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}LL​=4(21​⋅6⋅5)=60 cm2.​

The central square is the base. Add it once.

B=62B=36 cm2,S=60+36S=96 cm2.\begin{aligned} B&=6^2\\ B&=36\text{ cm}^2,\\ S&=60+36\\ S&=96\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}BBSS​=62=36 cm2,=60+36=96 cm2.​

The side lengths used as triangle bases add to the base perimeter. In the example that sum is 242424 cm, and half of 242424 times 555 gives the same lateral area. The argument works for any regular pyramid: every side triangle has the same face altitude, so factor half that altitude from the sum of their areas.

With PPP for base perimeter and BBB for base area,

L=12Pℓ,Sclosed=12Pℓ+B.\begin{aligned} L&=\tfrac12P\ell,\\ S_{\text{closed}}&=\tfrac12P\ell+B. \end{aligned}LSclosed​​=21​Pℓ,=21​Pℓ+B.​

These are the regular-pyramid shortcuts. If face altitudes differ, add the individual triangle areas with their own base lengths and altitudes. One chosen slant length cannot stand for every face.

L=12(32)(9)L=144 cm2.\begin{aligned}L&=\tfrac12(32)(9)\\L&=144\text{ cm}^2.\end{aligned}LL​=21​(32)(9)=144 cm2.​

Find the height that lies on the surface

The solid’s perpendicular height hhh runs from the apex to the base plane. It measures height through the interior. The slant height lies on a triangular face and measures that face’s area.

For a regular pyramid, join the base center to the midpoint of a base side. That segment is the base’s apothem aaa. It meets the edge perpendicularly, and the apex-to-midpoint segment is the face altitude. These three segments form a right triangle with hypotenuse ℓ\ellℓ.

Worked example 2 Use a section through a face midpoint

Problem. A regular square pyramid has perpendicular height 121212 cm and base side 101010 cm. Find its slant height and closed surface area.

Solution. The square’s center is 555 cm from a side midpoint, half its side length. In the true right triangle VOMVOMVOM, the perpendicular height and the 555 cm base distance are legs.

ℓ2=122+52ℓ=13 cm.\begin{aligned} \ell^2&=12^2+5^2\\ \ell&=13\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ℓ2ℓ​=122+52=13 cm.​

Use the face altitude 131313 cm for lateral area, then add the square base.

L=12(40)(13)L=260 cm2,S=260+100S=360 cm2.\begin{aligned} L&=\tfrac12(40)(13)\\ L&=260\text{ cm}^2,\\ S&=260+100\\ S&=360\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}LLSS​=21​(40)(13)=260 cm2,=260+100=360 cm2.​

Nothing depended on the particular height or side length. The same right triangle gives, for a regular pyramid,

ℓ2=h2+a2.\ell^2=h^2+a^2.ℓ2=h2+a2.

The base apothem aaa goes to a side midpoint, not a vertex. For a square it is half a side; for other regular bases, use the base geometry already learned.

A regular square pyramid has base side 202020 cm and perpendicular height 242424 cm. What is its slant height?

The base center is 101010 cm from a side midpoint. The slant height is the hypotenuse.

ℓ2=242+102ℓ=26 cm.\begin{aligned}\ell^2&=24^2+10^2\\\ell&=26\text{ cm}.\end{aligned}ℓ2ℓ​=242+102=26 cm.​

A right cone unfolds into a sector

Cut a right circular cone along one straight segment from apex to rim and flatten its curved side. Predict which length becomes the sector radius: the base radius, the perpendicular height, or the slant height.

Every straight apex-to-rim segment of a right cone has the same length ℓ\ellℓ. When flattened, those segments become radii of a sector. The sector’s curved arc goes once around the base rim, so its length is the base circumference.

For the illustrated cone, the slant height is 666 cm and the base radius is 333 cm. The arc length is 6π6\pi6π cm. A full circle of radius 666 cm has circumference 12π12\pi12π cm, so the needed sector is half that circle. Its area is 18π18\pi18π square centimeters.

For a general right cone, the sector radius is ℓ\ellℓ and its arc length is 2πr2\pi r2πr. The fraction of the full radius-ℓ\ellℓ circle is therefore r/ℓr/\ellr/ℓ. Apply that fraction to the full circle’s area.

L=rℓ⋅πℓ2L=πrℓ.\begin{aligned} L&=\frac{r}{\ell}\cdot\pi\ell^2\\ L&=\pi r\ell. \end{aligned}LL​=ℓr​⋅πℓ2=πrℓ.​

The same fraction gives the sector angle θ\thetaθ in degrees.

θ=360∘rℓ.\theta=360^\circ\frac{r}{\ell}.θ=360∘ℓr​.

The curved surface is the cone’s lateral surface; it is not a planar face. A closed right cone includes one base disk, so

Sclosed=πrℓ+πr2.S_{\text{closed}}=\pi r\ell+\pi r^2.Sclosed​=πrℓ+πr2.

These formulas need a right circular cone with positive radius and positive perpendicular height. Then ℓ>r\ell>rℓ>r, and the sector angle lies strictly between zero and a full turn. An oblique cone has different apex-to-rim lengths; its curved surface is not this circular sector, and πrℓ\pi r\ellπrℓ is not its surface shortcut.

A sector of radius 121212 cm forms the curved side of a right cone without overlap. Its selected arc is 8π8\pi8π cm long. What is the base radius?

Answer choices

The selected arc becomes the base circumference, not the base radius.

2πr=8πr=4 cm.\begin{aligned}2\pi r&=8\pi\\r&=4\text{ cm}.\end{aligned}2πrr​=8π=4 cm.​

Use the cone’s right triangle and its boundary

A plane through the axis of a right cone gives an isosceles triangle. Half of it has perpendicular height hhh and base radius rrr as its legs. The slant height is its hypotenuse.

Worked example 3 From an axial section to curved area

Problem. A right circular cone has base radius 555 cm and perpendicular height 121212 cm. Find its slant height, lateral area, and closed total surface area exactly.

Solution. In half of the axial section, apply Pythagoras to the two legs.

ℓ2=122+52ℓ=13 cm.\begin{aligned} \ell^2&=12^2+5^2\\ \ell&=13\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ℓ2ℓ​=122+52=13 cm.​

The curved side uses the slant height. The base is one disk.

L=π(5)(13)L=65π cm2,S=65π+25πS=90π cm2.\begin{aligned} L&=\pi(5)(13)\\ L&=65\pi\text{ cm}^2,\\ S&=65\pi+25\pi\\ S&=90\pi\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}LLSS​=π(5)(13)=65π cm2,=65π+25π=90π cm2.​

In general the half-section gives

ℓ2=h2+r2.\ell^2=h^2+r^2.ℓ2=h2+r2.

Surface area depends on which surfaces actually exist in the boundary. An opening has no material disk to add. The pointed apex has no extra cap.

Worked example 4 Count the included surfaces

Problem. A thin right-conical shade has radius 444 cm and slant height 999 cm. Ignore thickness, seams, and overlap. Find the material area in each case.

Case A: the shade includes its entire curved side and has an open circular rim, with no disk across it.

Case B: the same curved side also has a circular disk across that rim.

Solution. Case A includes only the sector-shaped curved side.

L=36π cm2.L=36\pi\text{ cm}^2.L=36π cm2.

Case B includes that side and one radius-444 disk.

S=36π+16πS=52π cm2.\begin{aligned} S&=36\pi+16\pi\\ S&=52\pi\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}SS​=36π+16π=52π cm2.​

The circle around the opening is an edge of the ideal covering. Its circumference is a length, not an extra area. This model counts one layer of material, rather than separately coating its inner and outer faces.

Only the curved side belongs to this covering.

L=π(2)(5)L=10π cm2.\begin{aligned}L&=\pi(2)(5)\\L&=10\pi\text{ cm}^2.\end{aligned}LL​=π(2)(5)=10π cm2.​

There is no base disk to add.

Reconstruct a cone and check the pattern

Area data can determine lengths, but those lengths must still fit a real right cone. Recover the base radius and slant height, check that the slant height is longer, then match the sector arc to the base circumference.

Worked example 5 Recover a cone from two surface measurements

Problem. A closed right cone has total surface area 150π150\pi150π square centimeters. Its curved side alone has area 125π125\pi125π square centimeters. Find its radius, slant height, perpendicular height, and sector angle exactly. Check the sector’s arc length.

Solution. The difference between total and lateral area is one base disk.

πr2=150π−125πr2=25r=5 cm.\begin{aligned} \pi r^2&=150\pi-125\pi\\ r^2&=25\\ r&=5\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}πr2r2r​=150π−125π=25=5 cm.​

Use the curved area to recover the slant height.

125π=π(5)ℓℓ=25 cm.\begin{aligned} 125\pi&=\pi(5)\ell\\ \ell&=25\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}125πℓ​=π(5)ℓ=25 cm.​

Since 252525 exceeds 555, the perpendicular height is positive. Subtract the radius-square from the slant-height square.

h2=252−52h=106 cm.\begin{aligned} h^2&=25^2-5^2\\ h&=10\sqrt6\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}h2h​=252−52=106​ cm.​

The sector radius is 252525 cm. Its angle occupies 5/255/255/25 of a full turn.

θ=72∘.\theta=72^\circ.θ=72∘.

The arc check uses the same fraction of the full circumference.

s=72360⋅2π(25)s=10π cm.\begin{aligned} s&=\tfrac{72}{360}\cdot2\pi(25)\\ s&=10\pi\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ss​=36072​⋅2π(25)=10π cm.​

That is the circumference of the recovered radius-555 base. The sector area is one fifth of 625π625\pi625π, agreeing with the given lateral area.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Height: use the perpendicular height for volume and a face altitude or slant height for these lateral areas.
  • Pyramid edge: an apex-to-vertex edge is not the altitude of a triangular face.
  • Cone pattern: its sector radius is the slant height; its arc matches the base circumference.
  • Boundary: add one base only when it is included; an open rim contributes no disk.
  • Restrictions: the shortcuts here require a regular pyramid or a right circular cone.

Key takeaways

  • A regular pyramid’s triangular sides have area L=12PℓL=\tfrac12P\ellL=21​Pℓ altogether.
  • A right cone’s curved side unfolds to a sector and has area L=πrℓL=\pi r\ellL=πrℓ.
  • Add the base area once for a closed boundary, and omit any surface explicitly excluded.
  • Find slant height in the appropriate right triangle before using it in an area formula.
  • A cone sector has radius ℓ\ellℓ and arc length 2πr2\pi r2πr; verify both when reconstructing a net.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1525, Albrecht Dürer published a geometry book in Nuremberg with an unusual way of showing solids. Its flat patterns laid out the faces of three-dimensional bodies on the page. Instead of trying to see through a perspective picture, a reader could inspect the polygons and their connections directly.

The first edition described fourteen solid bodies using planar nets. Dürer continued working in his own copy, adding nets for two more bodies. Those additions appeared in the 1538 edition, after his death. A printed book could preserve an artist’s revisions as well as his finished designs.

The Metropolitan Museum’s original book record identifies the 1525 edition and its printer. Vera Viana’s historical study, section 2.3 traces the nets and the later additions.

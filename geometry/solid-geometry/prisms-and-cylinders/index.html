Prisms and Cylinders

▾

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

Slide the top of a stack of cards sideways. The stack leans, but no card has disappeared. Its volume stays the same, while its outside shape changes. Layers explain volume; unfolding the outside explains surface area. These are different measurements, even when they use the same dimensions.

Learning goals

  • Derive prism and cylinder volumes from layers and matching parallel sections
  • Apply Cavalieri's principle using equal section areas at every common height
  • Calculate right prism and cylinder surface areas from their labeled nets
  • Recover dimensions from capacity and surface information with explicit boundary assumptions

From unit cubes to base area

Volume counts the space a solid occupies. A cubic unit is the volume of a cube with edges one unit long. A box with a 444 cm by 333 cm base holds 121212 unit cubes in one 111 cm layer. Two such layers hold 242424 cubes.

The layer count gives the product. The 121212 is the base area measured in square centimeters; multiplying by the 222 cm height gives cubic centimeters.

Fractional lengths follow the same idea with smaller cubes. For example, subdividing each centimeter into halves gives cubes of volume 1/81/81/8 cubic centimeter. Finer subdivisions extend the measurement to any positive lengths.

A right prism can have a triangular or other polygonal base. Pair copies of a triangular base to make a parallelogram. The familiar cut-and-slide rearrangement turns that parallelogram into a rectangle of equal area. Extend these same cuts through the prism height: they rearrange the paired prism into a rectangular box. Each triangular prism is half the pair, so its volume is its own base area times the height. Splitting any polygonal base into triangles and adding their volumes gives the same rule for a right prism.

Write BBB for the area of one base and hhh for the perpendicular height, the distance between the parallel base planes. For every prism,

V=Bh.V=Bh.V=Bh.

The oblique case needs a section argument, which comes next. The height is not automatically an edge that joins the bases.

Worked example 1 Use the triangular base

Problem. A right prism has right triangular bases with perpendicular legs 777 cm and 444 cm. The distance between the bases is 999 cm. Find its volume.

Solution. First find the area of one triangular base.

B=12(7)(4)B=14 cm2.\begin{aligned} B&=\tfrac12(7)(4)\\ B&=14\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}BB​=21​(7)(4)=14 cm2.​

Repeat that base through the 999 cm height.

V=14(9)V=126 cm3.\begin{aligned} V&=14(9)\\ V&=126\text{ cm}^3. \end{aligned}VV​=14(9)=126 cm3.​

Why leaning preserves volume

Sliding base-parallel layers changes their positions without changing their areas. Consider a prism with base area 121212 square units and perpendicular height 666 units. Predict its volume after the top shifts 222 units, then 444 units sideways. What happens to a section halfway up?

In each position, the halfway section has area 121212. So does a section at one third of the height, or at any other height. Each section is just a translated copy of the base. All three volumes are 727272 cubic units.

This illustrates Cavalieri’s principle: suppose two ordinary solids lie between the same two parallel planes. If every plane parallel to those boundary planes cuts the solids in sections of equal area, the solids have equal volumes. Equivalently, corresponding section areas must agree at every common perpendicular height, across the full height.

Imagine approximating the solids by thinner and thinner layers. Equal section areas give matching layer-volume approximations. Refining the layers makes the discrepancies as small as desired. This is the same kind of shrinking-error reasoning used for circle area. A finite stack is an illustration; the principle concerns every height, not just the few cuts drawn.

An oblique prism and a right prism with equal base areas and equal perpendicular heights meet this test: every base-parallel section has area BBB. Therefore both have volume BhBhBh. The sideways shift and the slanted edge length do not enter that volume.

Equal base area and equal height alone do not justify a comparison of arbitrary solids. Their sections between the bases must also be checked.

Worked example 2 Choose the perpendicular height

Problem. An oblique prism has base area 303030 square centimeters. Its base planes are 888 cm apart, and its slanted joining edges are 101010 cm long. Find its volume and explain why 101010 is not the height used.

Solution. The 888 cm perpendicular distance measures the full range of parallel sections. Each has area 303030 square centimeters.

V=30(8)V=240 cm3.\begin{aligned} V&=30(8)\\ V&=240\text{ cm}^3. \end{aligned}VV​=30(8)=240 cm3.​

The 101010 cm edge follows the lean. It does not measure the distance perpendicular to the base planes.

Check your understanding

Two solids lie between the same parallel planes. Their section areas agree at the bottom, middle, and top. What more is needed to apply Cavalieri?

Answer choices

The three sampled levels leave other levels unchecked. Cavalieri requires equality of the base-parallel section areas at every common height.

Cylinders use the same volume idea

A circular cylinder also repeats its base area through the perpendicular height. For radius 222 cm and height 777 cm, every base-parallel section is a disk of area 4π4\pi4π square centimeters. Compare it with a rectangular prism of the same height and base area 4π4\pi4π square centimeters. Their sections match in area at every height, even though the section shapes differ. Both volumes are 28π28\pi28π cubic centimeters.

Nothing in that comparison depended on those particular measurements. A circular cylinder of positive radius rrr and perpendicular height hhh has

B=πr2,V=πr2h.\begin{aligned} B&=\pi r^2,\\ V&=\pi r^2h. \end{aligned}BV​=πr2,=πr2h.​

This works for right and oblique circular cylinders. For the oblique cylinder, base-parallel sections are translated disks, and hhh still measures the perpendicular distance between base planes. Here and below, a circular cylinder means one with circular bases.

Capacity is the greatest volume a container can hold. Use its inside dimensions. A liter equals 1,0001{,}0001,000 cubic centimeters, or one cubic decimeter. A milliliter equals one cubic centimeter. Wall thickness, when relevant, makes inside and outside dimensions differ.

Check your understanding

A right cylindrical jar has inside radius 222 cm and inside height 101010 cm. What is its capacity?

Answer choices

The disk area is 4π4\pi4π square centimeters. Multiply by the inside height.

V=40π cm3.V=40\pi\text{ cm}^3.V=40π cm3.

One cubic centimeter equals one milliliter.

A right prism unfolds into rectangles

Surface area measures the boundary, in square units. The lateral area excludes the two chosen bases; total surface area includes every surface in the stated boundary.

A closed right rectangular prism with a 333 cm by 222 cm base and height 444 cm unfolds as follows. Each side rectangle has one edge from the base perimeter and the other equal to the height.

The four rectangle areas total 404040 square centimeters. That is the base perimeter 101010 cm multiplied by the common height 444 cm. The two bases add 121212 square centimeters, giving 525252 in total.

For any right prism, the lateral rectangles all have height hhh. Their widths add to the base perimeter PPP. Thus

L=Ph,Sclosed=Ph+2B.\begin{aligned} L&=Ph,\\ S_{\text{closed}}&=Ph+2B. \end{aligned}LSclosed​​=Ph,=Ph+2B.​

The restriction to a right prism matters: in an oblique prism, lateral faces are generally parallelograms, and their face altitudes need not equal the solid’s perpendicular height. Compute those actual face areas instead of applying this shortcut.

Worked example 3 Read all five faces of a net

Problem. A closed right triangular prism has base sides 555, 121212, and 131313 cm, with a right angle between the 555 cm and 121212 cm sides. Its prism height is 444 cm. Find its lateral area and total surface area.

Solution. The three rectangles have widths equal to the three triangle sides. Their common height is 444 cm.

P=5+12+13P=30 cm,L=30(4)L=120 cm2.\begin{aligned} P&=5+12+13\\ P&=30\text{ cm},\\ L&=30(4)\\ L&=120\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}PPLL​=5+12+13=30 cm,=30(4)=120 cm2.​

Each triangular base has area 303030 square centimeters. Include both in the closed boundary.

B=12(5)(12)B=30 cm2,S=120+2(30)S=180 cm2.\begin{aligned} B&=\tfrac12(5)(12)\\ B&=30\text{ cm}^2,\\ S&=120+2(30)\\ S&=180\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}BBSS​=21​(5)(12)=30 cm2,=120+2(30)=180 cm2.​

A closed right prism has base perimeter 171717 cm, base area 121212 square cm, and height 555 cm. What is its total surface area?

Answer choices

The side rectangles total 17(5)=8517(5)=8517(5)=85 square centimeters. Add two bases.

S=85+2(12)S=109 cm2.\begin{aligned}S&=85+2(12)\\S&=109\text{ cm}^2.\end{aligned}SS​=85+2(12)=109 cm2.​

Unroll a right cylinder and count its ends

A right cylinder’s curved side unrolls into a rectangle. With radius 333 cm and height 888 cm, the rectangle is 6π6\pi6π cm wide and 888 cm high. The width wraps once around the circular base; it is the circumference, not the diameter.

The side area is 48π48\pi48π square centimeters. Each end adds 9π9\pi9π square centimeters. Replacing 333 and 888 by arbitrary positive rrr and hhh gives, for a right circular cylinder,

L=2πrh,Sclosed=2πrh+2πr2.\begin{aligned} L&=2\pi rh,\\ S_{\text{closed}}&=2\pi rh+2\pi r^2. \end{aligned}LSclosed​​=2πrh,=2πrh+2πr2.​

These surface shortcuts require a right cylinder. An oblique cylinder’s side does not unroll into that rectangle, even though its volume is still πr2h\pi r^2hπr2h.

Worked example 4 Specify the boundary before adding

Problem. A right cylindrical container has radius 333 cm and height 888 cm. Ignore thickness and overlap. Find its volume and the material area in each case.

Case A: it is closed with two circular ends.

Case B: it has a bottom but no top.

Solution. The net above supplies the side area 48π48\pi48π and each base area 9π9\pi9π, in square centimeters. In both cases the enclosed capacity is

V=72π cm3.V=72\pi\text{ cm}^3.V=72π cm3.

Case A includes the side and two disks.

SA=66π cm2.S_A=66\pi\text{ cm}^2.SA​=66π cm2.

Case B includes the side and one disk.

SB=57π cm2.S_B=57\pi\text{ cm}^2.SB​=57π cm2.

Removing a top changes the material area, while the volume it can hold up to the rim stays the same. For an ideal sleeve open at both ends, only the curved side remains. These are single-surface, zero-thickness models; a thick wall has additional surfaces.

Worked example 5 Recover dimensions from two measurements

Problem. An ideal right cylindrical cup has capacity 128π128\pi128π cubic centimeters. Its curved side alone uses 64π64\pi64π square centimeters of material. The cup has a bottom and no top; ignore thickness and seams. Find its radius, height, and total material area.

Solution. Let rrr and hhh be the positive radius and height in centimeters. Divide volume by curved-side area. The common factors cancel.

VL=πr2h2πrh128π64π=r2r=4 cm.\begin{aligned} \frac{V}{L}&=\frac{\pi r^2h}{2\pi rh}\\ \frac{128\pi}{64\pi}&=\frac r2\\ r&=4\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}LV​64π128π​r​=2πrhπr2h​=2r​=4 cm.​

Use the side-area measurement to recover the height.

64π=2π(4)hh=8 cm.\begin{aligned} 64\pi&=2\pi(4)h\\ h&=8\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}64πh​=2π(4)h=8 cm.​

Check the capacity: a 16π16\pi16π square-centimeter base repeated through 888 cm gives 128π128\pi128π cubic centimeters. Add one bottom disk to the curved side.

S=80π cm2.S=80\pi\text{ cm}^2.S=80π cm2.

Counting two disks would describe a closed can, not this cup.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Height: volume uses the perpendicular distance between base planes, not a slanted joining edge.
  • Cavalieri: equal base areas are only the first section check; corresponding sections must match at every height.
  • Surface shortcuts: PhPhPh and 2πrh2\pi rh2πrh are the lateral-area shortcuts for right prisms and right cylinders.
  • Boundary: list the side and each included end before adding areas; an opening contributes no disk.
  • Units: area uses square units, volume uses cubic units, and capacity uses inside dimensions.

Key takeaways

  • Prism and circular-cylinder volume is base area times perpendicular height, including oblique cases.
  • Cavalieri compares section areas at every corresponding height between common parallel planes.
  • A right prism’s side rectangles total PhPhPh; a right cylinder’s unrolled side has area 2πrh2\pi rh2πrh.
  • Add only the bases actually included in the stated boundary.
  • Check recovered dimensions in both the volume and the surface information.

A bit of history (optional)

Bonaventura Cavalieri was an Italian mathematician who worked on geometry while also serving in a religious order. In December 1627 he told Galileo and Cardinal Federico Borromeo that his geometry book was complete. It would not appear in print until 1635.

The manuscript helped him before publication. When a mathematics position opened in Bologna, Galileo supported Cavalieri’s application, and Cavalieri sent his geometry manuscript as evidence of his work. He received the position in 1629.

His method of indivisibles compared plane regions through parallel lines and solids through parallel sections. It let him attack area and volume questions in a new way, but it also provoked arguments about what those infinitely thin pieces meant. The book’s delayed publication did not stop its ideas from helping establish his career.

Volumes of Pyramids and Cones

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A prism keeps the same cross-section all the way up. A pyramid narrows to a point, so base area times height counts too much space. An exact cut-and-compare argument will tell us how much to keep. Matching sections will then carry that result from polygonal bases to circular ones.

Learning goals

  • Explain the one-third volume factor using an exact prism dissection
  • Compare parallel section areas using similarity and Cavalieri's principle
  • Calculate pyramid and cone volumes with the actual perpendicular height
  • Recover missing dimensions and verify them against geometric information

How the sections shrink

Sections parallel to a pyramid’s base shrink in a predictable way. Take a pyramid with base area 121212 square units and perpendicular height 888 units. A section 222 units below the apex is one quarter of the full height away from that apex. Every section length is therefore one quarter of its corresponding base length, by similar triangles along the sides. Its area uses the square of that factor.

A=12(28)2.A=12\left(\frac28\right)^2.A=12(82​)2.

This is 3/43/43/4 square unit. A section 444 units below the apex instead has half the base lengths and area 333 square units. Equal vertical steps do not give equal area changes.

The numbers 121212 and 888 were only an example. For a pyramid with positive base area BBB and perpendicular height hhh, let ttt be the perpendicular distance from the apex down to a base-parallel section. Similar triangles give section-to-base length factor t/ht/ht/h, so

A(t)=B(th)2.A(t)=B\left(\frac th\right)^2.A(t)=B(ht​)2.

Here ttt runs from 000 at the apex to hhh at the base. This works even when the apex leans away from the base center. Every ray from the apex meets the cutting plane at the same fraction of its route to the base, so the whole section is a smaller similar copy of the base.

Recall Cavalieri’s principle: two ordinary solids between the same parallel planes have equal volumes if their section areas agree in every plane parallel to those planes. Two pyramids with equal base areas and equal perpendicular heights pass that test. At every distance ttt below their apex levels, both section areas are the same squared fraction of their common base area. Their sections need not have the same shape.

We have established this equality of volumes without knowing a pyramid-volume formula. We will use it to justify the equal pieces in a dissection.

Where one third comes from

A triangular prism can be cut into three pyramids of equal volume. A tetrahedron is a pyramid with a triangular base. Its four vertices can be named in any order; choosing a different face as its base does not change the solid.

For a triangular prism with base area 181818 square units and height 777 units, the prism volume is 126126126 cubic units. Once we justify the three equal pieces, each must occupy 424242 cubic units. The equality of the pieces needs a reason, not a guess from the picture.

For a numerical check of the comparison, take a right prism whose base triangle is right at BBB, with ABABAB and BCBCBC both 666 units long. Its prism height is 777 units. Both side faces BCFEBCFEBCFE and ABEDABEDABED are 666 by 777 rectangles. Each diagonal splits a 424242 square-unit face into two bases of area 212121 square units.

In the first pair, common apex AAA is 666 units from the face plane. In the second pair, common apex FFF is also 666 units from its face plane. Halfway from either apex to its chosen base, each section has area 21/421/421/4 square units. At any other fraction of the height, both sections use that same fraction squared. The first two pieces therefore have equal volumes, and so do the second and third. The whole prism has volume 126126126 cubic units, shared equally among the three pieces.

Three equal-volume tetrahedra fill a triangular prism#

Let ABCABCABC and DEFDEFDEF be the two triangular bases of any nondegenerate prism, with corresponding vertices A,DA,DA,D; B,EB,EB,E; and C,FC,FC,F. The joining edges are parallel and equal. The prism may be right or oblique.

First cut along triangle ABFABFABF. One side is tetrahedron ABCFABCFABCF. The remainder is a pyramid with apex FFF and parallelogram base ABEDABEDABED. Cut that base along diagonal AEAEAE, extending the cut to FFF. This gives tetrahedra ABEFABEFABEF and ADEFADEFADEF. The cuts cover the prism with no gaps and no overlapping interiors.

Compare ABCFABCFABCF and ABEFABEFABEF using bases BCFBCFBCF and BEFBEFBEF. These are the two equal-area halves of parallelogram BCFEBCFEBCFE, split by BFBFBF. Both tetrahedra have apex AAA, so their perpendicular heights to that same face plane agree. The matching-section argument just proved gives equal volumes.

Now compare ABEFABEFABEF and ADEFADEFADEF using bases ABEABEABE and ADEADEADE. These are equal-area halves of parallelogram ABEDABEDABED, split by AEAEAE. Their common apex is FFF, so their heights to that face plane agree too. The same section argument gives equal volumes.

Thus all three pieces have equal volumes. The prism volume is BhBhBh, where BBB is the area of ABCABCABC and hhh is the perpendicular separation of the prism’s base planes. Tetrahedron ABCFABCFABCF has that same base and height. Each piece takes one third of the prism, so

VABCF=13Bh.V_{ABCF}=\frac13Bh.VABCF​=31​Bh.

Any triangular pyramid with positive base area BBB and height hhh can be compared with this tetrahedron by matching every parallel section. It therefore has the same volume. Finally, split a simple polygonal base into triangles and join each to the pyramid’s apex. Those triangular pyramids have common height hhh; adding their volumes gives one third of the whole base area times hhh.

∎

For any pyramid with a simple polygonal base of positive area and an apex outside its base plane,

V=13Bh.V=\frac13Bh.V=31​Bh.

The height is the perpendicular distance from the apex to the base plane, even if the perpendicular foot is outside the base. A slanted edge is not that distance.

Worked example 1 Use the dissection

Problem. A triangular prism has base area 181818 square centimeters and perpendicular height 777 cm. It is cut into the three pieces described above. Find the prism volume and the volume of each piece.

Solution. The prism repeats its base area through the full height.

Vprism=18(7)Vprism=126 cm3.\begin{aligned} V_{\text{prism}}&=18(7)\\ V_{\text{prism}}&=126\text{ cm}^3. \end{aligned}Vprism​Vprism​​=18(7)=126 cm3.​

The dissection proof establishes three equal volumes, so divide by three.

Vpiece=42 cm3.V_{\text{piece}}=42\text{ cm}^3.Vpiece​=42 cm3.

The three volumes add back to 126126126 cubic centimeters.

Find the whole base area first

The BBB in the formula is an area, not a base edge length. For a triangular base, first use its own base and perpendicular triangle height. That triangle height lies in the base plane and differs from the pyramid’s height.

Worked example 2 Separate the two heights

Problem. A pyramid has a trapezoidal base with parallel sides 666 cm and 121212 cm. Their perpendicular separation within the base is 555 cm. The apex is 888 cm perpendicularly from the base plane. Find the pyramid’s volume.

Solution. Average the two parallel sides, then multiply by their separation.

B=6+122(5)B=45 cm2.\begin{aligned} B&=\frac{6+12}{2}(5)\\ B&=45\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}BB​=26+12​(5)=45 cm2.​

Use the solid’s 888 cm perpendicular height for volume.

V=13(45)(8)V=120 cm3.\begin{aligned} V&=\frac13(45)(8)\\ V&=120\text{ cm}^3. \end{aligned}VV​=31​(45)(8)=120 cm3.​

V=13(18)(5).V=\frac13(18)(5).V=31​(18)(5).

Carry the result to cones

A cone’s parallel sections shrink by the same length fraction as a pyramid’s. Compare a cone and a pyramid, each with base area 363636 square units and perpendicular height 121212 units. At 444 units below either apex, the length fraction is one third, so each section has area 444 square units. Halfway down, each section has area 999 square units.

Those two cuts illustrate the comparison; they do not prove it. At every distance ttt below the apex level, both section areas are

A(t)=36(t12)2.A(t)=36\left(\frac t{12}\right)^2.A(t)=36(12t​)2.

Place their bases in one plane and their apexes in a second parallel plane. Every intervening parallel plane gives equal section areas, so Cavalieri gives equal volumes. Both volumes are 144144144 cubic units.

For any positive base area BBB and perpendicular height hhh, the same reasoning uses B(t/h)2B(t/h)^2B(t/h)2 in both solids. It works for right and oblique cones and pyramids. Moving the apex sideways changes the section positions, but not that area at each height.

For a circular cone of positive radius rrr, substitute the disk area for BBB.

B=πr2,V=13πr2h.\begin{aligned} B&=\pi r^2,\\ V&=\frac13\pi r^2h. \end{aligned}BV​=πr2,=31​πr2h.​

Worked example 3 Start from a diameter

Problem. A right circular cone has base diameter 141414 cm and perpendicular height 999 cm. Find its exact volume.

Solution. Halve the diameter to obtain radius 777 cm. Square that radius for the base area, then use one third of base area times height.

V=13π(7)2(9)V=147π cm3.\begin{aligned} V&=\frac13\pi(7)^2(9)\\ V&=147\pi\text{ cm}^3. \end{aligned}VV​=31​π(7)2(9)=147π cm3.​

Keeping π\piπ gives an exact answer.

Recover the perpendicular height

For a right circular cone, the slant height ℓ\ellℓ is the straight distance along the side from the apex to the base rim. The apex lies directly above the base center, so the radius, perpendicular height, and slant height form a right triangle. The slant height is its hypotenuse.

A pyramid’s edge or side-face altitude is also a different measurement from the perpendicular height of the solid. The next lesson uses slanted measurements to find surface areas. Here they help us recover the height needed for volume.

Worked example 4 Read the axial right triangle

Problem. A right circular cone has radius 888 cm and slant height 171717 cm. Find its perpendicular height and exact volume.

Solution. Subtract the radius square from the slant-height square.

h2=172−82h2=225h=15 cm.\begin{aligned} h^2&=17^2-8^2\\ h^2&=225\\ h&=15\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}h2h2h​=172−82=225=15 cm.​

Now use that perpendicular height.

V=13π(8)2(15)V=320π cm3.\begin{aligned} V&=\frac13\pi(8)^2(15)\\ V&=320\pi\text{ cm}^3. \end{aligned}VV​=31​π(8)2(15)=320π cm3.​

Missing dimensions can also come from the volume measurement. For positive base area, solving V=Bh/3V=Bh/3V=Bh/3 for the height gives

h=3VB.h=\frac{3V}{B}.h=B3V​.

Worked example 5 Check a recovered height against the geometry

Problem. An oblique pyramid has base area 545454 square centimeters and volume 288288288 cubic centimeters. Its apex is SSS, and HHH is the perpendicular foot on the base plane. A point QQQ in that plane is 121212 cm from HHH, and SQSQSQ is 202020 cm. Find the perpendicular height and check whether both measurements support it.

Solution. Recover the perpendicular height from the volume.

h=3(288)54h=16 cm.\begin{aligned} h&=\frac{3(288)}{54}\\ h&=16\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}hh​=543(288)​=16 cm.​

Because SHSHSH is perpendicular to the base plane, it is perpendicular to HQHQHQ. Check the right triangle.

162+122=400,202=400.\begin{aligned} 16^2+12^2&=400,\\ 20^2&=400. \end{aligned}162+122202​=400,=400.​

The geometric measurement supports the recovered height. Substituting it into the volume rule gives 288288288 cubic centimeters again. The 202020 cm slanted segment would not give that volume.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Base area: BBB is the whole polygon or disk area, measured in square units.
  • One-third factor: a pyramid or cone uses one third of the matching prism or cylinder volume.
  • Height: use the perpendicular distance to the base plane, not a slanted edge or slant height.
  • Section distance: the fraction t/ht/ht/h measures from the apex, and section area uses its square.
  • Cavalieri: equal base areas and heights justify this comparison because every intermediate section area matches.

Key takeaways

  • A prism dissection and matching sections establish the one-third factor without assuming it.
  • A pyramid’s volume is Bh/3Bh/3Bh/3; a circular cone’s volume is πr2h/3\pi r^2h/3πr2h/3.
  • Both formulas use perpendicular height and apply to right and oblique cases.
  • Sections parallel to the base have the square of their apex-distance fraction in area.
  • Check a recovered dimension in the volume relation and any supplied geometric constraints.

A bit of history (optional)

An Egyptian scribe left a small geometric puzzle in the document now called the Moscow Mathematical Papyrus. Problem 14 concerns a square pyramid with its pointed top cut off. The lower square is 444 cubits across, the upper square is 222 cubits across, and the remaining height is 666 cubits. A cubit was a unit of length.

The scribe gives a recipe: square each width, multiply the two widths together, add those three results, and multiply by one third of the height. The recorded volume is 565656 cubic cubits. This is a correct calculation for that truncated pyramid.

The surviving recipe tells us what was calculated, but it does not supply a dissection proof explaining how the rule was discovered. That missing explanation is part of the document’s interest: a practical answer survived while its route of discovery did not.

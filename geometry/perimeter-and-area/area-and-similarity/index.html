Area and Similarity

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

Enlarge a rectangle so that every edge becomes three times as long. Its outline needs three times the length, but its inside has room for three times as many rows and three times as many columns. That second multiplication is why area changes faster than length.

Learning goals

  • Choose a positive length scale factor by dividing an image length by its corresponding original length
  • Explain perimeter and area scaling using corresponding lengths and triangle partitions
  • Calculate areas of similar polygons from corresponding lengths or perimeters
  • Recover a positive length scale factor from an area ratio when similarity is known
  • Distinguish equal area, similarity, and congruence using geometric evidence

Keep the direction of the comparison

Similar figures have the same shape, with corresponding lengths multiplied by one positive number. The dilation lesson established length scaling and angle preservation; here we use those results to study area.

We call the figure before resizing the original and the resized figure the image. The length scale factor is the image length divided by its corresponding original length:

k=image lengthoriginal length.k=\frac{\text{image length}}{\text{original length}}.k=original lengthimage length​.

We require k>0k>0k>0. Keep image over original when calculating a scale factor. If a given area ratio is written in the order original:image, reverse it before using the image-over-original formula. A factor greater than 111 enlarges; a factor between 000 and 111 reduces. A factor of 111 preserves size.

We write AoriginalA_{\text{original}}Aoriginal​ and AimageA_{\text{image}}Aimage​ for the two areas, and use corresponding subscripts on PPP for the perimeters.

Worked example 1 Scale every boundary side

Problem. A triangle has side lengths 4.54.54.5, 666, and 7.57.57.5 cm. A similar image has a 666 cm side corresponding to the original 4.54.54.5 cm side. Find the length scale factor taking the original to its image, and find both perimeters.

Solution. Divide corresponding lengths in image-over-original order:

k=64.5,k=43.\begin{aligned} k&=\frac{6}{4.5},\\ k&=\frac43. \end{aligned}kk​=4.56​,=34​.​

The original perimeter is the sum of its three sides:

Poriginal=4.5+6+7.5,Poriginal=18 cm.\begin{aligned} P_{\text{original}}&=4.5+6+7.5,\\ P_{\text{original}}&=18\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}Poriginal​Poriginal​​=4.5+6+7.5,=18 cm.​

The image sides are 666, 888, and 101010 cm, so its perimeter is 242424 cm. This is also the original perimeter multiplied by 43\tfrac4334​.

Adding scaled sides scales their sum. For any polygon with original side lengths s1,…,sns_1,\ldots,s_ns1​,…,sn​, the image perimeter is

Pimage=ks1+⋯+ksn,Pimage=k(s1+⋯+sn),Pimage=kPoriginal.\begin{aligned} P_{\text{image}}&=ks_1+\cdots+ks_n,\\ P_{\text{image}}&=k(s_1+\cdots+s_n),\\ P_{\text{image}}&=kP_{\text{original}}. \end{aligned}Pimage​Pimage​Pimage​​=ks1​+⋯+ksn​,=k(s1​+⋯+sn​),=kPoriginal​.​

Worked example 2 Three times the rows and columns

Problem. A rectangle is 333 units by 222 units. Its similar image is made with length scale factor 333. Find the image dimensions and compare the two areas.

Solution. The image dimensions are 999 units and 666 units. The original has 666 unit squares; the image has 545454 unit squares.

AimageAoriginal=546,AimageAoriginal=9.\begin{aligned} \frac{A_{\text{image}}}{A_{\text{original}}}&=\frac{54}{6},\\ \frac{A_{\text{image}}}{A_{\text{original}}}&=9. \end{aligned}Aoriginal​Aimage​​Aoriginal​Aimage​​​=654​,=9.​

There are three times as many columns and three times as many rows, giving an area factor of 323^232.

This grid explains an integer enlargement of this rectangle. It does not show that every polygon can fill its own enlargement with identical small copies. For noninteger factors and other polygon shapes, triangles give a general argument.

Why every triangle contributes the squared factor

The triangle area formula uses a chosen base and its perpendicular height. Similarity scales both of those lengths, even when the height meets an extended base line.

Area scaling from triangles to polygons#

First take a triangle with base 101010 cm and perpendicular height 777 cm. Its area is 353535 square cm. Under length scale factor 222, the base becomes 202020 cm and the height 141414 cm, so

Aimage=12⋅20⋅14,Aimage=140 cm2.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{image}}&=\frac12\cdot20\cdot14,\\ A_{\text{image}}&=140\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}Aimage​Aimage​​=21​⋅20⋅14,=140 cm2.​

The area factor is 444 because both the base and height were multiplied by 222.

Now let the original base and height be any positive lengths bbb and hhh. Similarity carries the base line and its perpendicular altitude to a base line and perpendicular altitude. Thus the corresponding lengths are kbkbkb and khkhkh. Rigid motions preserve these lengths and areas, so a turn or reflection does not affect the argument.

Aimage=12(kb)(kh),Aimage=k2(12bh),Aimage=k2Aoriginal.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{image}}&=\frac12(kb)(kh),\\ A_{\text{image}}&=k^2\left(\frac12bh\right),\\ A_{\text{image}}&=k^2A_{\text{original}}. \end{aligned}Aimage​Aimage​Aimage​​=21​(kb)(kh),=k2(21​bh),=k2Aoriginal​.​

Nothing used the specific base 101010, height 777, or factor 222. The reasoning applies to every nondegenerate triangle and every positive length scale factor.

For a convex pentagon, draw the two diagonals from one vertex to its nonadjacent vertices. Convexity keeps both diagonals inside. They meet only at that vertex, and their three triangular regions fill the pentagon with no overlapping interiors. Carry those exact diagonals into the image, rather than choosing a different partition.

For a numerical partition, suppose the three original triangle areas are 666, 131313, and 171717 square units. Under factor 12\tfrac1221​, the image triangle areas are 64\tfrac6446​, 134\tfrac{13}4413​, and 174\tfrac{17}4417​. Adding gives 999 square units, one quarter of the original 363636 square units.

In general, call those three areas a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c. Each image triangle has the squared area factor. Area addition gives

Aimage=k2a+k2b+k2c,Aimage=k2(a+b+c),Aimage=k2Aoriginal.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{image}}&=k^2a+k^2b+k^2c,\\ A_{\text{image}}&=k^2(a+b+c),\\ A_{\text{image}}&=k^2A_{\text{original}}. \end{aligned}Aimage​Aimage​Aimage​​=k2a+k2b+k2c,=k2(a+b+c),=k2Aoriginal​.​

For this L-shaped polygon, extend the horizontal notch edge leftward inside the region. The cut separates two rectangles, and one diagonal in each rectangle gives four triangles. Their interiors do not overlap, and together they fill the L.

The same area argument works for any finite nonoverlapping triangle partition. Here is a way to make such a partition even when the outline has a notch.

In the six-vertex example below, choose vertical cuts. None follows an edge, and no cut contains two vertices. The six vertex lines enclose five strips. In the middle strip, the notch separates the polygon into an upper piece UUU and a lower piece LLL.

Follow the top and bottom edges of UUU across that strip. Neither edge ends inside the strip, so those same two edges bound UUU all the way from one cut to the next. Together with the two parallel cuts, they form a trapezoid. The separate piece LLL is bounded by a different pair of edges and the same cuts, so it is another trapezoid.

Across the whole figure, three connected pieces are already triangles and four are trapezoids. A dotted diagonal inside each trapezoid divides it into two triangles. The final count is

3+2⋅4=11.3+2\cdot4=11.3+2⋅4=11.

For any simple polygon, choose parallel cuts through its vertices. Turn the cut direction if it would follow an edge or put two vertices on one cut. Between consecutive cuts there is no vertex where a bounding edge could end or change. The edges cannot cross there because the polygon is simple. Thus each connected piece stays between two fixed edges and the parallel cuts, making a convex triangle or trapezoid.

Divide every trapezoid by an interior diagonal, just as in the example. Keep the pieces of positive area; their interiors are disjoint and they fill the polygon. Shared cut segments add no area. Similarity carries these exact cuts and triangles to the image, so the same coverage and nonoverlap hold there. Applying the triangle area factor to each corresponding piece and adding proves the polygon area factor.

This proves the area rule for simple polygons with positive area, convex or concave. A self-crossing outline is outside these hypotheses. If a region has polygonal holes, apply the result to the outer region and each hole, then subtract their areas as in Composite and Shaded Figures.

∎

For similar simple polygons, with positive areas and positive length scale factor,

AimageAoriginal=k2.\frac{A_{\text{image}}}{A_{\text{original}}}=k^2.Aoriginal​Aimage​​=k2.

Use length information to compare areas

Worked example 3 Reduce a polygon

Problem. A simple polygon has area 112 cm2112\text{ cm}^2112 cm2 and perimeter 484848 cm. A similar image has length scale factor 34\tfrac3443​. Find its area and perimeter.

Solution. Perimeter uses one factor:

Pimage=34⋅48,Pimage=36 cm.\begin{aligned} P_{\text{image}}&=\frac34\cdot48,\\ P_{\text{image}}&=36\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}Pimage​Pimage​​=43​⋅48,=36 cm.​

Area uses the square of that factor:

Aimage=(34)2⋅112,Aimage=63 cm2.\begin{aligned} A_{\text{image}}&=\left(\frac34\right)^2\cdot112,\\ A_{\text{image}}&=63\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}Aimage​Aimage​​=(43​)2⋅112,=63 cm2.​

Both measurements decrease, but not by the same fraction. The perimeter is 34\tfrac3443​ of its original value; the area is 916\tfrac9{16}169​ of its original value.

Reverse an area ratio

When similarity is given or proved, an area ratio determines the positive length scale factor. For example, an image with 916\tfrac9{16}169​ of the original area has 34\tfrac3443​ of every corresponding length, because 34\tfrac3443​ squared is 916\tfrac9{16}169​. In general,

k=AimageAoriginal.k=\sqrt{\frac{A_{\text{image}}}{A_{\text{original}}}}.k=Aoriginal​Aimage​​​.

Use the positive square root because lengths and the length scale factor are positive. An area ratio is dimensionless only after both areas have been expressed in the same square unit.

himage=75⋅10,himage=14 cm.\begin{aligned} h_{\text{image}}&=\frac75\cdot10,\\ h_{\text{image}}&=14\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}himage​himage​​=57​⋅10,=14 cm.​

Equal area, similarity, and congruence

Equal area records how much region is enclosed. Similarity records shape. Congruence records shape and size.

Worked example 5 Equal areas with different shapes

Problem. Compare a 333 cm by 888 cm rectangle with a 444 cm by 666 cm rectangle. Do they have equal areas? Are they similar or congruent? Justify each conclusion.

Solution. The first area is 3⋅83\cdot83⋅8, or 24 cm224\text{ cm}^224 cm2. The second is 4⋅64\cdot64⋅6, also 24 cm224\text{ cm}^224 cm2.

For similar rectangles, the ratio of longer side to shorter side must agree. Here those ratios are 83\tfrac8338​ and 64\tfrac6446​, which differ. The rectangles are not similar, so they are not congruent either.

Thus equal area alone implies neither similarity nor congruence. Congruent polygons do have equal areas, by the area postulate. Similar polygons need not have equal areas: their area factor depends on the length factor.

There is a useful conclusion when both conditions are supplied. If two similar polygons with positive area also have equal areas, their squared factor is 111. The positive length factor must then be 111, so their similarity uses only size-preserving motions and they are congruent. Dropping the similarity hypothesis would invalidate that conclusion.

Check your understanding

Two simple polygons with positive area are known to be similar and to have equal areas. What follows?

Answer choices

Their image-over-original area ratio is 111. Since they are similar, the length factor is its positive square root, also 111.

A similarity with length factor 111 preserves shape and size, so the polygons are congruent.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using a length factor for area. Both a base and its perpendicular height scale, so area uses the squared factor.
  • Reversing just one ratio. Keep image over original for lengths, perimeters, and areas in the same calculation.
  • Taking an area ratio as evidence of similarity. Establish similarity before recovering a length factor from areas.
  • Confusing units. A length in centimeters and an area in square centimeters describe different measurements.

Key takeaways

  • For similar polygons, perimeter uses the positive length scale factor and area uses its square.
  • Corresponding nonoverlapping triangle partitions explain the polygon area rule.
  • When similarity is known, take the positive square root of the image-over-original area ratio to recover the length factor.
  • Equal area alone does not determine shape or corresponding lengths.

A bit of history (optional)

In Book VI of the Elements, Euclid compared similar polygons by dividing them into corresponding triangles. His Proposition 20 describes their area ratio as the “duplicate ratio” of corresponding sides. The phrase predates our convenient exponent notation: a side ratio is used twice, just as a base and a height each contribute a factor today.

The original argument worked with ratios of geometric magnitudes rather than a formula written with a squared letter. A reader following its diagram could pass from matching triangles to the whole polygons without measuring either polygon’s area. You can see that structure in the text and diagram of Proposition 20.

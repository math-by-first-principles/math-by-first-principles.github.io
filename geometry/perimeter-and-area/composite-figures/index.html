Composite and Shaded Figures

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A room can bend around a stairwell, and a courtyard can leave a hole in a floor plan. Neither region needs a new area formula. The useful question is which familiar pieces fill the region, and which edges you would actually walk along to go around it.

Learning goals

  • Decompose polygonal regions into nonoverlapping pieces or subtract contained regions
  • Recover hidden lengths from aligned sides and auxiliary segments
  • Trace exterior and hole boundaries without counting shared internal edges
  • Solve missing dimensions from a composite region's area
  • Convert perimeter and area using linear and square units correctly

Account for every piece once

A composite figure is a region assembled from simpler figures. To find its area, account for every included part exactly once. Two rectangles covering 666 and 151515 square units, with no overlapping interiors, cover 212121 square units together. If a 444-square-unit part is then removed, 171717 square units remain. A shared edge has no area, so pieces may meet along their edges.

The area addition principle from Area from Rectangles to Triangles justifies this accounting. Use the triangle and quadrilateral formulas already established there and in Areas of Quadrilaterals.

An auxiliary segment is an added line that helps reveal familiar pieces. It does not add material or create a new outside edge. Choose cuts that leave pieces whose bases and perpendicular heights you can determine.

Worked example 1 Two ways to measure a floor plan

Problem. A floor plan has horizontal and vertical sides. Its total width is 171717 m, total height 111111 m, upper horizontal side 777 m, and lower right side 444 m. The upper right rectangular corner is absent. Find the floor area in two ways and its outside perimeter.

Solution. The missing width and height come from aligned sides. Across the full width, the upper side and missing corner span the same distance as the bottom. Vertically, the missing corner and lower right side span the full height.

missing width=17−7=10 mmissing height=11−4=7 m.\begin{aligned} \text{missing width}&=17-7\\ &=10\text{ m}\\ \text{missing height}&=11-4\\ &=7\text{ m}. \end{aligned}missing widthmissing height​=17−7=10 m=11−4=7 m.​

The vertical auxiliary segment divides the floor into a 777 m by 111111 m rectangle and a 101010 m by 444 m rectangle. Their interiors do not overlap, and together they fill the floor.

A=7(11)+10(4)A=117 m2.\begin{aligned} A&=7(11)+10(4)\\ A&=117\text{ m}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=7(11)+10(4)=117 m2.​

Alternatively, complete the 171717 m by 111111 m rectangle and subtract the absent 101010 m by 777 m corner.

A=17(11)−10(7)A=117 m2.\begin{aligned} A&=17(11)-10(7)\\ A&=117\text{ m}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=17(11)−10(7)=117 m2.​

The six solid edges form the outside boundary. The auxiliary segment inside the floor does not contribute.

P=7+7+10+4+17+11P=56 m.\begin{aligned} P&=7+7+10+4+17+11\\ P&=56\text{ m}. \end{aligned}PP​=7+7+10+4+17+11=56 m.​

The inward corner makes this a concave polygon, a simple polygon with an interior angle greater than 180∘180^\circ180∘. Concavity does not change the addition principle. The cuts must still cover the region without gaps or overlapping interiors. Do not assume that drawing from a concave vertex to any other vertex stays inside the polygon.

Subtract only what is absent

Subtraction is useful when completing a familiar outside shape is easier than splitting the retained region. The removed parts must lie inside that completed shape. If you subtract several parts separately, check that their interiors do not overlap, or the overlap would be removed twice.

Worked example 2 A shaded polygon with a slanted boundary

Problem. Start with a 191919 cm by 121212 cm rectangle. Remove its upper right corner along a diagonal. The removed corner is a right triangle with perpendicular legs 777 cm and 666 cm. Find the retained shaded area by subtraction and by addition.

Solution. The missing triangle has half the product of its perpendicular legs as its area.

A=19(12)−12(7)(6)A=207 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=19(12)-\tfrac12(7)(6)\\ A&=207\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=19(12)−21​(7)(6)=207 cm2.​

For an additive split, draw a perpendicular segment from the left end of the diagonal to the bottom. The left rectangle is 121212 cm wide because the full width is 191919 cm and the removed horizontal leg is 777 cm. The right trapezoid has parallel vertical sides 121212 cm and 666 cm, separated by 777 cm.

A=12(12)+12+62(7)A=207 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=12(12)+\frac{12+6}{2}(7)\\ A&=207\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=12(12)+212+6​(7)=207 cm2.​

The diagonal is an actual boundary edge. The vertical segment in the additive split is internal. Neither the removed top edge nor the removed upper right edge belongs to the shaded polygon’s perimeter.

The same accounting handles overlapping figures. If two figures have areas 303030 and 222222 square units and overlap in 555 square units, adding gives 525252 but counts those 555 twice. Their union, meaning all points in either figure, has area 474747 square units. In general, add the two areas and subtract their common area once. This uses no new formula for the overlap: find that area with a familiar decomposition.

Trace the boundary, including the requested holes

Perimeter measures boundary length, not the lengths of all lines in a drawing. Shared internal edges disappear when pieces are joined. For example, polygons with perimeters 262626 cm and 424242 cm joined along exactly one 888 cm segment have union perimeter 525252 cm: the sum counts the shared segment twice, and neither copy remains exposed.

When polygonal pieces have disjoint interiors and meet only along shared boundary segments, subtract twice the total shared length from the sum of their perimeters. Do not use this shortcut for overlapping interiors without first identifying the actual union boundary.

A hole is an excluded region completely surrounded by the retained region. The remaining region has an outer boundary and an inner boundary; it is not one simple polygon. For a length around only the outside, omit the hole edges. For the total boundary length of the retained region, include them. A task about fencing or trim must identify which boundary is being covered.

Worked example 3 A courtyard gives two different boundary questions

Problem. A building occupies a 232323 m by 151515 m outer rectangle except for a 999 m by 555 m rectangular courtyard strictly inside it. Find the occupied area, the outside boundary length, and the total boundary length including the courtyard.

Solution. The courtyard contributes no occupied area.

A=23(15)−9(5)A=300 m2.\begin{aligned} A&=23(15)-9(5)\\ A&=300\text{ m}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=23(15)−9(5)=300 m2.​

The outside boundary is the outer rectangle.

Poutside=2(23+15)Poutside=76 m.\begin{aligned} P_{\text{outside}}&=2(23+15)\\ P_{\text{outside}}&=76\text{ m}. \end{aligned}Poutside​Poutside​​=2(23+15)=76 m.​

Square units contain two lengths

Changing the unit does not enlarge a region. It changes how many units describe the same region. A meter is 100100100 centimeters long, but a square meter is a square with that length in each direction.

Why a square unit uses the length conversion twice#

A square 222 m on each side has area 444 square meters. In centimeters, each side is 200200200 cm, so its area is 40,00040{,}00040,000 square centimeters. Each of its four one-meter squares therefore contains 10,00010{,}00010,000 one-centimeter squares.

More directly, each one-meter side divides into 100100100 one-centimeter lengths. The one-meter square contains 100100100 rows with 100100100 small squares in each row.

1 m2=100(100) cm2.1\text{ m}^2=100(100)\text{ cm}^2.1 m2=100(100) cm2.

Nothing depends on the particular unit names. If one larger length unit equals ccc smaller length units, where ccc is positive, a square of one larger unit has ccc smaller units along each side. By the rectangle area formula, one larger square unit equals c2c^2c2 smaller square units. This applies to the square units used to measure any polygonal area, whether or not that polygon tiles into whole unit squares.

Worked example 4 Convert an area and a perimeter

Problem. A polygonal region has area 0.370.370.37 square meters and perimeter 3.93.93.9 meters. Express its area in square centimeters and its perimeter in centimeters. Use 111 m equal to 100100100 cm.

Solution. The area conversion uses two factors of 100100100 because a square unit contains two length directions.

0.37 m2=3700 cm2.0.37\text{ m}^2=3700\text{ cm}^2.0.37 m2=3700 cm2.

The perimeter is a length, so it uses one factor of 100100100.

3.9 m=390 cm.3.9\text{ m}=390\text{ cm}.3.9 m=390 cm.

To reverse these conversions, divide by 10,00010{,}00010,000 for square centimeters to square meters, and by 100100100 for centimeters to meters. Put all lengths in a common unit before using an area formula; do not multiply a length in meters directly by one in centimeters and label the result square centimeters.

Work backward from an area

A given area can determine an unknown length. Define the length, express the included and excluded areas in the same square unit, and solve the resulting equation. Then check that the length fits the geometry, not just the equation.

Worked example 5 Recover the width of a notch

Problem. A 242424 cm by 131313 cm rectangle has a rectangular notch removed from its upper right corner. The notch is 555 cm deep and xxx cm wide. The retained area is 267267267 square centimeters. Find xxx and the perimeter of the retained polygon.

Solution. The whole rectangle has area 312312312 square centimeters. The notch area is 5x5x5x square centimeters.

312−5x=2675x=45x=9.\begin{aligned} 312-5x&=267\\ 5x&=45\\ x&=9. \end{aligned}312−5x5xx​=267=45=9.​

The notch width is 999 cm. It is positive and less than 242424 cm; its depth 555 cm is less than 131313 cm, so the described corner notch is possible. Substituting gives the required retained area.

The remaining top side is 151515 cm, and the remaining right side is 888 cm. Trace those, the two notch edges, the bottom, and the left side.

P=15+5+9+8+24+13P=74 cm.\begin{aligned} P&=15+5+9+8+24+13\\ P&=74\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PP​=15+5+9+8+24+13=74 cm.​

Here the notch replaces a 999 cm top segment and a 555 cm right segment with equal horizontal and vertical lengths, so this corner removal leaves the original rectangle’s perimeter unchanged. A notch opening into the middle of a side has two added depth edges and behaves differently.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Overlapping pieces: adding their areas counts the overlap twice; subtract the common area once.
  • Hidden dimensions: use aligned full lengths and their parts, not a measurement from a schematic drawing.
  • Internal edges: an auxiliary cut or shared join is not part of the exposed boundary.
  • Hole edges: decide whether the question asks for the outside boundary or all boundary components.
  • Square conversions: derive the area factor from both side lengths rather than using a linear factor once.

Key takeaways

  • Split into familiar pieces whose interiors do not overlap, or complete a figure and subtract absent parts.
  • Check that every retained part is counted once and every needed height is perpendicular to its base.
  • Trace the requested boundary; count shared internal edges zero times and included hole edges once.
  • Use a known area to form an equation, then check whether its solution fits the figure.
  • A length conversion acts once on perimeter and twice on the two directions in a square unit.

A bit of history (optional)

The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus looks more like a collection of working problems than a modern geometry textbook. Its copyist recorded that he was writing in the thirty-third year of King Apophis and that his source was an older text. The British Museum describes the problems as practical calculations connected with administration, building, and geometric shapes.

The document’s modern name comes from Alexander Henry Rhind, a Scottish lawyer who acquired it in Thebes in the 1850s. Even its reverse side preserves a different kind of record: a note about the taking of Egyptian towns. The king referred to in that note is uncertain. A document kept for mathematical work also became a small, incomplete witness to political events.

The British Museum’s description of the papyrus distinguishes what its inscriptions say from the uncertainty about that later historical note.

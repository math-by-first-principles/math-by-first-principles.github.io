Areas of Quadrilaterals

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A quadrilateral can be hard to measure as one region but easy to measure as a few triangles. Another approach is to join it to a congruent copy. The useful formula depends on which lengths meet at right angles and which sides are parallel, not on how the outline happens to look.

Learning goals

  • Derive the trapezoid area formula by joining two congruent copies
  • Calculate rhombus and kite areas using perpendicular diagonals
  • Select an area formula whose geometric conditions are satisfied
  • Decompose a convex quadrilateral into triangles with known heights
  • Recover missing bases, heights, and diagonal lengths from area

Two trapezoids make one parallelogram

A trapezoid has at least one pair of parallel sides; choose such a pair as its bases, with lengths b1b_1b1​ and b2b_2b2​. Its height hhh is the perpendicular distance between their supporting lines, and all lengths here are positive.

In Area from Rectangles to Triangles, we established that parallelogram area is bhbhbh and triangle area is 12bh\tfrac12 bh21​bh, with perpendicular height. We can use those results and addition of nonoverlapping areas here.

Joining a trapezoid to a turned copy makes its area visible inside a parallelogram.

The trapezoid area formula#

Take a convex trapezoid with bases 111111 and 555 units, separated by 333 units. Turn a congruent copy halfway around the midpoint of one leg and join the copies along that leg.

The top edges are collinear because the bases are parallel and a half turn reverses their directions. Each outside slanted side is the half-turn image of the other, so the joined outside boundary is a parallelogram. The copies lie on opposite sides of the joining leg, with no overlap of their interiors.

The parallelogram has base 11+511+511+5 units and height 333 units. Its area is

2A=(11+5)⋅3,2A=48,A=24.\begin{aligned} 2A&=(11+5)\cdot3,\\ 2A&=48,\\ A&=24. \end{aligned}2A2AA​=(11+5)⋅3,=48,=24.​

Nothing in the arrangement depended on these particular lengths. For any convex trapezoid with chosen parallel bases b1,b2b_1,b_2b1​,b2​ and perpendicular height hhh, the two congruent copies make a parallelogram with base b1+b2b_1+b_2b1​+b2​ and height hhh:

2A=(b1+b2)h,A=b1+b22h.\begin{aligned} 2A&=(b_1+b_2)h,\\ A&=\frac{b_1+b_2}{2}h. \end{aligned}2AA​=(b1​+b2​)h,=2b1​+b2​​h.​

∎

The fraction is the average of the base lengths. A parallelogram is included in our trapezoid definition: its chosen bases have equal length, and averaging them gives the familiar base-times-height formula.

Worked example 1 Choose the perpendicular measurement

Problem. A convex trapezoid has parallel bases 131313 cm and 777 cm, perpendicular height 444 cm, and a slanted leg of 555 cm. Find its area.

Solution. The 444 cm distance is perpendicular to the bases. The 555 cm leg is not the required height.

A=13+72⋅4,A=40 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac{13+7}{2}\cdot4,\\ A&=40\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=213+7​⋅4,=40 cm2.​

Two congruent copies of a convex trapezoid join along a leg to form a parallelogram. Each trapezoid has parallel bases 111111 m and 666 m and perpendicular height 555 m. If AAA is the area of one trapezoid in square meters, which equation describes the joined parallelogram?

Answer choices

The joined parallelogram contains two congruent trapezoids. Its base is the sum of the original parallel bases.

2A=(11+6)⋅5,2A=85,A=42.5.\begin{aligned} 2A&=(11+6)\cdot5,\\ 2A&=85,\\ A&=42.5. \end{aligned}2A2AA​=(11+6)⋅5,=85,=42.5.​

Work backward from a trapezoid’s area

An area equation can determine a missing measurement. First identify the parallel bases and their perpendicular separation; then solve for the unknown length and check it in the original equation.

Worked example 2 Recover a parallel base

Problem. A convex trapezoid has area 959595 square cm, perpendicular height 555 cm, and one parallel base of 121212 cm. Find the other parallel base.

Solution. Let xxx be the missing base length in cm.

95=12+x2⋅5,38=12+x,x=26.\begin{aligned} 95&=\frac{12+x}{2}\cdot5,\\ 38&=12+x,\\ x&=26. \end{aligned}9538x​=212+x​⋅5,=12+x,=26.​

The other base is 262626 cm. Substitution checks the area:

12+262⋅5=95.\frac{12+26}{2}\cdot5=95.212+26​⋅5=95.

A convex trapezoid has area 666666 square cm and parallel bases 888 cm and 141414 cm. What is its perpendicular height?

Answer choices

The average base length is 111111 cm.

h=66÷11,h=6.\begin{aligned} h&=66\div11,\\ h&=6. \end{aligned}hh​=66÷11,=6.​

Rhombi and their diagonals

A rhombus has perpendicular diagonals that bisect each other, as proved in Chapter 8. This gives four right triangles whose areas add to half the product of the full diagonal lengths.

The rhombus diagonal area formula#

For a rhombus with diagonals 181818 and 888 units, each right triangle has legs 999 and 444 units. Each has area 12⋅9⋅4\tfrac12\cdot9\cdot421​⋅9⋅4, so all four together have area 727272 square units.

Now let d1d_1d1​ and d2d_2d2​ be the full diagonal lengths of any nondegenerate rhombus. Perpendicularity supplies the right angles, and bisection supplies the half lengths. The four triangles cover the rhombus without overlapping interiors:

A=4(12⋅d12⋅d22),A=12d1d2.\begin{aligned} A&=4\left(\frac12\cdot\frac{d_1}{2}\cdot\frac{d_2}{2}\right),\\ A&=\frac12 d_1d_2. \end{aligned}AA​=4(21​⋅2d1​​⋅2d2​​),=21​d1​d2​.​

The same partition worked for the numerical lengths; their values were not part of the reason it worked.

∎

Worked example 3 Use full diagonal lengths

Problem. The diagonals of a rhombus measure 141414 m and 101010 m. Find its area.

Solution. The formula uses the full lengths, not their halves.

A=12⋅14⋅10,A=70 m2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot14\cdot10,\\ A&=70\text{ m}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅14⋅10,=70 m2.​

Check your understanding

A rhombus has diagonals 111111 cm and 888 cm. What is its area?

Answer choices

The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular.

A=12⋅11⋅8,A=44.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot11\cdot8,\\ A&=44. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅11⋅8,=44.​

Kites need not split into four equal areas

A kite here is a convex quadrilateral with two disjoint pairs of adjacent congruent sides, each side in one pair. Rhombi and squares qualify; a kite’s diagonals are perpendicular, but in a general kite only the diagonal joining the equal-side vertices must bisect the other.

The kite diagonal area formula#

In the kite below, the horizontal diagonal is 121212 units and is bisected into two 666 unit parts. The vertical diagonal is 999 units, split into 222 and 777 units. The four right triangles are visible, but the two upper triangles are smaller than the two lower ones.

Combine the upper pair into triangle ABDABDABD, using base BDBDBD. Then do the same with triangle CBDCBDCBD:

A=12⋅12⋅2+12⋅12⋅7,A=54.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot12\cdot2+\frac12\cdot12\cdot7,\\ A&=54. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅12⋅2+21​⋅12⋅7,=54.​

For the general argument, let the perpendicular diagonals ACACAC and BDBDBD of a convex quadrilateral meet at OOO. Let the full length of BDBDBD be d1d_1d1​, and let the parts AOAOAO and OCOCOC have lengths xxx and yyy. Then the full diagonal length d2d_2d2​ of ACACAC is x+yx+yx+y. The triangles on the two sides of BDBDBD have perpendicular heights xxx and yyy:

A=12d1x+12d1y,A=12d1(x+y),A=12d1d2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12 d_1x+\frac12 d_1y,\\ A&=\frac12 d_1(x+y),\\ A&=\frac12 d_1d_2. \end{aligned}AAA​=21​d1​x+21​d1​y,=21​d1​(x+y),=21​d1​d2​.​

The numerical split into 222 and 777 did not matter. This argument applies to every convex quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals, so it includes every kite and rhombus; it does not require both diagonals to bisect each other.

∎

Worked example 4 A diagonal split and an area

Problem. In convex kite ABCDABCDABCD, the equal-side pairs meet at AAA and CCC. Diagonals ACACAC and BDBDBD meet at OOO. The full diagonal ACACAC measures 161616 cm, its part AOAOAO measures 555 cm, and the full diagonal BDBDBD measures 111111 cm. Find OCOCOC and the kite’s area.

Solution. For the missing part, subtract along ACACAC:

OC=16−5,OC=11 cm.\begin{aligned} OC&=16-5,\\ OC&=11\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}OCOC​=16−5,=11 cm.​

For the area, use the two full diagonals. Chapter 8’s kite theorem guarantees their perpendicularity.

A=12⋅16⋅11,A=88 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot16\cdot11,\\ A&=88\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅16⋅11,=88 cm2.​

Check your understanding

A convex quadrilateral has diagonals of 101010 cm and 171717 cm. Which extra fact justifies an area of 858585 square cm?

Answer choices

Perpendicularity makes the diagonal parts usable as triangle heights. Then half the product is 858585 square cm.

Equal diagonals alone do not supply perpendicular heights. A rectangle generally has equal diagonals without perpendicular diagonals, so equal lengths alone do not justify taking half their product.

Decompose when a special formula does not apply

A diagonal divides any convex quadrilateral into two triangles with nonoverlapping interiors. Use that diagonal as a base and find each vertex’s perpendicular distance to its line; these heights need not be parts of the other diagonal.

Worked example 5 Two different heights to one diagonal

Problem. In convex quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD, diagonal ACACAC is 181818 cm. The perpendicular distances from BBB and DDD to line ACACAC are 333 cm and 888 cm, respectively. Find the quadrilateral’s area.

Solution. Let A1A_1A1​ be the area of triangle ABCABCABC and A2A_2A2​ the area of triangle ACDACDACD. They lie on opposite sides of ACACAC, so add their areas.

A1=12⋅18⋅3,A1=27 cm2.\begin{aligned} A_1&=\frac12\cdot18\cdot3,\\ A_1&=27\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}A1​A1​​=21​⋅18⋅3,=27 cm2.​A2=12⋅18⋅8,A2=72 cm2.\begin{aligned} A_2&=\frac12\cdot18\cdot8,\\ A_2&=72\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}A2​A2​​=21​⋅18⋅8,=72 cm2.​A=27+72,A=99 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=27+72, \\ A&=99\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=27+72,=99 cm2.​

We did not need a pair of parallel sides or perpendicular diagonals. The two marked distances were the required triangle heights.

Check your understanding

A diagonal of a convex quadrilateral is 999 cm. The other vertices have perpendicular distances 444 cm and 666 cm to its line. What is the total area?

Answer choices

The diagonal splits the convex quadrilateral into two triangles.

A=12⋅9⋅4+12⋅9⋅6,A=45.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot9\cdot4+\frac12\cdot9\cdot6,\\ A&=45. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅9⋅4+21​⋅9⋅6,=45.​

When a problem asks for a missing dimension, use the same condition check before forming its area equation. An equation solved correctly cannot repair an unjustified formula.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Height versus leg: a trapezoid’s slanted leg is not its perpendicular height.
  • Full diagonals: the half-product formula uses both full diagonal lengths, even when half lengths are supplied.
  • Perpendicularity: equal diagonal lengths do not justify the half-product formula.
  • Kite bisection: do not halve both diagonals unless a rhombus or other given property justifies it.

Key takeaways

  • Two congruent trapezoids join into a parallelogram, giving half the base sum times the perpendicular height.
  • A convex quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals has area half their product.
  • Rhombi and kites satisfy that diagonal condition; squares belong to both families.
  • A diagonal also lets you add two triangle areas using their separate perpendicular heights.
  • Check the geometric conditions before solving an area equation for a missing length.

A bit of history (optional)

The Indian mathematician and astronomer Brahmagupta wrote the Brahmasphutasiddhanta in the seventh century. Alongside astronomical calculations, it contained rules for lengths and areas, including a remarkable quadrilateral rule. His mathematical instructions were written in verse, a compact form that could be remembered and passed on.

That quadrilateral rule later prompted a disagreement among historians. It works for quadrilaterals whose four vertices lie on one circle, but Brahmagupta did not explicitly state that restriction. Some readers regard this as an omission; others think the intended setting was already understood. The surviving words leave room for both readings. MacTutor’s biography describes the debate.

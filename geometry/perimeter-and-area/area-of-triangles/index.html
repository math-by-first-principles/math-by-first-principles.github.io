Area from Rectangles to Triangles

▾

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

A length of cord can follow a triangle’s edge. A sheet of paper can cover its inside. Those are different measurements. To measure the inside, we will start with squares, then cut and move pieces until triangles can be compared with rectangles.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish perimeter from area and choose the appropriate measurement units
  • Justify rectangle area by tiling with unit squares and rational subdivisions
  • Derive parallelogram and triangle areas using congruent pieces and area addition
  • Identify the perpendicular height matching any chosen side of a triangle
  • Calculate areas and recover missing base or height measurements

What area is allowed to do

The perimeter of a polygon is the total length of its boundary. The area is the measure of the region it encloses. We write PPP for perimeter and AAA for area. Lengths use units such as centimeters; areas use square units such as square centimeters, written cm2\text{cm}^2cm2.

A rectangle 444 units long and 333 units wide has 121212 unit squares inside. Its boundary length is 141414 units. Counting squares and adding edge lengths answer different questions.

We accept three area postulates, the starting assumptions for our arguments.

  • Unit square. A square with side length one unit has area one square unit.
  • Congruence. Congruent regions have equal areas. Sliding, turning, or reflecting a piece preserves its area.
  • Addition. If finitely many pieces fill a region without gaps and their interiors do not overlap, their areas add to the whole area. Shared boundary segments add no area.

These assumptions explain why cutting and reconstructing a region preserves area: each moved piece keeps its area, and the same piece areas add before and after. They say nothing about preserving the outer boundary length.

Check your understanding

Which measurement describes the region inside a triangle?

Answer choices

Area measures the enclosed region, so it uses square centimeters. Perimeter measures the boundary length and uses centimeters.

Rectangles from square tiles

A rectangle’s area comes from counting equal square pieces. A 555 by 222 rectangle contains two rows of five unit squares. Addition gives 101010 square units. In general, positive integer side lengths bbb and hhh give hhh rows of bbb squares, so their product counts the tiles.

Fractions need smaller square tiles. Consider a rectangle 53\tfrac5335​ units long and 111 unit high. Divide a unit square into three rows and three columns. Its nine small squares are congruent, so each has area 19\tfrac1991​ square unit. The rectangle contains five columns and three rows of those small squares. Its area is 159\tfrac{15}{9}915​, or 53\tfrac5335​, square units.

Rectangle area for rational side lengths#

For another numerical case, take a rectangle 74\tfrac7447​ units by 32\tfrac3223​ units. A unit square splits into 161616 congruent squares of side 14\tfrac1441​ unit. The rectangle has 777 columns and 666 rows of them, so its area in square units is

A=42⋅116,A=218.\begin{aligned} A&=42\cdot\frac1{16},\\ A&=\frac{21}{8}. \end{aligned}AA​=42⋅161​,=821​.​

This agrees with multiplying the two side lengths. The counting does not depend on these particular fractions. For any two positive rational lengths, choose a positive integer NNN so both can be written with denominator NNN. Write the lengths as mN\tfrac mNNm​ and nN\tfrac nNNn​.

A unit square contains N2N^2N2 congruent squares of side 1N\tfrac1NN1​, each with area 1N2\tfrac1{N^2}N21​. Our rectangle has mmm columns and nnn rows, so addition gives

A=mnN2,A=mN⋅nN.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac{mn}{N^2},\\ A&=\frac mN\cdot\frac nN. \end{aligned}AA​=N2mn​,=Nm​⋅Nn​.​

∎

Thus a rectangle with positive rational base bbb and perpendicular height hhh has area

A=bh.A=bh.A=bh.

We accept the extension of this rectangle formula to positive irrational side lengths here. For example, a rectangle with sides 222 and 3\sqrt33​ units has area 232\sqrt323​ square units. A finite rational grid does not tile such a side exactly. The extension is an additional accepted foundation, not something the finite tiling proof has established. No limiting argument is being assumed.

Worked example 1 Count rational tiles and measure the boundary

Problem. A rectangle has side lengths 73\tfrac7337​ cm and 53\tfrac5335​ cm. Find its area by counting squares of side 13\tfrac1331​ cm, and find its perimeter.

Solution. A unit square contains nine of these small squares, so each has area 19 cm2\tfrac19\text{ cm}^291​ cm2. Seven columns and five rows contain 353535 small squares.

A=359 cm2.A=\frac{35}{9}\text{ cm}^2.A=935​ cm2.

The perimeter counts two copies of each side length.

P=2(73+53),P=8 cm.\begin{aligned} P&=2\left(\frac73+\frac53\right),\\ P&=8\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PP​=2(37​+35​),=8 cm.​

Slide a parallelogram into a rectangle

Choose a side of a parallelogram as its base, of length bbb. Its corresponding height, of length hhh, is the perpendicular distance between the lines containing that base and the opposite side. A slanted side usually has a different length.

For a parallelogram with base 999 units and height 555 units, cut down the perpendicular from its upper left corner. Slide the left triangular piece 999 units to the right. It completes a rectangle 999 units wide and 555 units high, without gaps or overlaps. Both regions therefore have area 454545 square units.

The parallelogram cut and slide#

The same move works for a base of 777 units and height 333 units when the sideways shift is no more than 777 units. The cut-off right triangle has the same height and sideways width as the empty corner on the right. A translation by 777 units makes it fit that corner exactly. The resulting rectangle has area 212121 square units.

For general positive bbb and hhh, when the sideways shift is at most bbb, translation by bbb fits the cut-off triangle into the opposite corner. Parallel sides give matching perpendicular heights and matching sideways widths. The remaining piece and the moved triangle have disjoint interiors and fill a rectangle with sides bbb and hhh. The postulates give equal total areas.

If the parallelogram leans left, reflect this construction. A rectangle itself needs no cut.

If the sideways shift is greater than the base, one vertical cut might miss the opposite side segment. For instance, a parallelogram with base 333, height 666, and sideways shift 666 can be cut into three equal-height horizontal strips. Each is a parallelogram with base 333, height 222, and sideways shift 222. Rearrange each strip as above. Their three areas of 666 square units add to 181818 square units.

In general, choose a finite number nnn of equal-height strips large enough that the shift of each strip is at most bbb. Parallel-line proportionality makes each shift the original shift divided by nnn. Each strip has height hn\tfrac hnnh​ and base bbb. Adding their rectangle areas gives

A=n(bhn).A=n\left(b\frac hn\right).A=n(bnh​).

Therefore every nondegenerate parallelogram has area

A=bh.A=bh.A=bh.

This uses the rectangle formula, including its accepted irrational extension when needed, and only finitely many cuts.

∎

Worked example 2 Use the height for area and the side for perimeter

Problem. A parallelogram has base 131313 cm, perpendicular height 666 cm, and adjacent side 101010 cm. Find its area and perimeter.

Solution. The area uses the base and its perpendicular height.

A=13⋅6,A=78 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=13\cdot6,\\ A&=78\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=13⋅6,=78 cm2.​

The boundary has two sides of each given side length.

P=2(13+10),P=46 cm.\begin{aligned} P&=2(13+10),\\ P&=46\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PP​=2(13+10),=46 cm.​

Check your understanding

A parallelogram is cut into pieces that fill a rectangle without gaps or interior overlaps. The rectangle has the same base and perpendicular height. Why do the areas agree?

Answer choices

Congruence preserves each piece area. Addition gives the original area and the reconstructed rectangle area as the same sum. The rectangle formula therefore gives the parallelogram area.

A=bh.A=bh.A=bh.

Pair two triangles

A triangle is half of a suitable parallelogram. Take a triangle with base 666 units and height 444 units. Turn a congruent copy halfway around the midpoint of one of its other sides. The two copies fill a parallelogram with the same base and height. Its area is 242424 square units, so each triangle has area 121212 square units.

Why a triangle has half the base times the height#

For a triangle with base 555 and height 333, the half-turn construction makes a parallelogram with area 151515 square units. Congruence gives equal areas for its two triangular pieces. Addition gives 2A=152A=152A=15, so one has area 152\tfrac{15}{2}215​ square units.

Now take any triangle ABCABCABC whose vertices are not collinear, with chosen base ABABAB of length bbb and perpendicular height hhh from CCC to line ABABAB. Turn the triangle halfway around the midpoint of BCBCBC. The images of BBB and CCC exchange places; call the image of AAA point DDD.

The image of ABABAB is DCDCDC, parallel and equal to ABABAB. The image of ACACAC is DBDBDB, parallel and equal to ACACAC. Thus the union is parallelogram ABDCABDCABDC. The copies lie on opposite sides of BCBCBC, meet only along that side, and fill the parallelogram. Its base is bbb and its height is hhh.

Let AAA be the original triangle’s area. The postulates and the parallelogram formula give

2A=bh,A=12bh.\begin{aligned} 2A&=bh,\\ A&=\frac12bh. \end{aligned}2AA​=bh,=21​bh.​

The half turn works whether the triangle is acute, right, or obtuse. No rectangle or trapezoid formula for a larger enclosing shape is needed.

∎

Worked example 3 Find an area with an exterior height

Problem. Triangle ABCABCABC has an 111111 cm base ABABAB. The perpendicular from CCC meets line ABABAB at HHH beyond BBB, and its length is 666 cm. Find the triangle’s area.

Solution. The height is the perpendicular distance to the base’s line, so the exterior segment CHCHCH is the correct height. The extension BHBHBH is not part of the base.

A=12⋅11⋅6,A=33 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot11\cdot6,\\ A&=33\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅11⋅6,=33 cm2.​

Check your understanding

A triangle has a 15 cm base. Its opposite vertex is 8 cm from the base line, measured perpendicularly, with the foot outside the base. What is the area?

Answer choices

The exterior foot does not change the perpendicular distance or the triangle formula.

A=12⋅15⋅8,A=60 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot15\cdot8,\\ A&=60\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅15⋅8,=60 cm2.​

Any side can be the base

The base is a choice, not a permanent label for the lowest or longest side. Changing the base changes which altitude supplies the height. The triangle itself and its area stay unchanged.

For triangle ABCABCABC, base ABABAB uses the altitude from CCC; base ACACAC uses the altitude from BBB; base BCBCBC uses the altitude from AAA. In an obtuse triangle, two of these feet lie on extensions of sides. The third lies on a side segment. In a right triangle, choosing either leg makes the other leg the height; choosing the hypotenuse requires the perpendicular from the right-angle vertex.

Worked example 4 Use three base-and-height pairs

Problem. A triangle has sides 131313 cm, 202020 cm, and 212121 cm. Its perpendicular height to the 212121 cm side is 121212 cm. Find its area, then find the heights to the other two sides.

Solution. First use the supplied matching pair.

A=12⋅21⋅12,A=126 cm2.\begin{aligned} A&=\frac12\cdot21\cdot12,\\ A&=126\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AA​=21​⋅21⋅12,=126 cm2.​

For the 131313 cm base, call the height h13h_{13}h13​.

126=12⋅13h13,h13=25213 cm.\begin{aligned} 126&=\frac12\cdot13h_{13},\\ h_{13}&=\frac{252}{13}\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}126h13​​=21​⋅13h13​,=13252​ cm.​

For the 202020 cm base, call the height h20h_{20}h20​.

126=12⋅20h20,h20=635 cm.\begin{aligned} 126&=\frac12\cdot20h_{20},\\ h_{20}&=\frac{63}{5}\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}126h20​​=21​⋅20h20​,=563​ cm.​

The three matching products all equal 252 cm2252\text{ cm}^2252 cm2, twice the triangle’s area.

Worked example 5 Recover a height from the enclosed area

Problem. A triangle has area 65 cm265\text{ cm}^265 cm2 and a 101010 cm chosen base. Find the corresponding height and check it.

Solution. Substitute the known area and base into the triangle formula.

65=12⋅10h,65=5h,h=13 cm.\begin{aligned} 65&=\frac12\cdot10h,\\ 65&=5h,\\ h&=13\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}6565h​=21​⋅10h,=5h,=13 cm.​

Check by substituting the height back into the area calculation.

12⋅10⋅13=65 cm2.\frac12\cdot10\cdot13=65\text{ cm}^2.21​⋅10⋅13=65 cm2.

Area and one base determine the corresponding height. They do not supply all the side lengths needed for perimeter.

Check your understanding

A triangle has area 48 square units and a chosen base of 6 units. Which height belongs to that base?

Answer choices

Double the area to recover base times height, then divide by the chosen base.

6h=96,h=16.\begin{aligned} 6h&=96,\\ h&=16. \end{aligned}6hh​=96,=16.​

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Boundary versus interior. Add side lengths for perimeter; use an area formula for the enclosed region. Keep length units and square units distinct.
  • Slanted side versus height. A height must be perpendicular to the chosen base’s supporting line.
  • Exterior foot. Extend the line when necessary, but keep the original side length as the base.
  • Changed base. Pair the new base with its own perpendicular height.
  • Missing half. Two congruent triangles fill the parallelogram, so one receives half its area.

Key takeaways

  • Foundations. Unit-square area, congruence, and addition justify finite dissections.
  • Rectangles. Tiling proves base times height for rational sides; the irrational extension is accepted here.
  • Parallelograms. A finite cut and slide gives A=bhA=bhA=bh for positive base and perpendicular height.
  • Triangles. Pairing congruent copies gives A=12bhA=\tfrac12bhA=21​bh for a nondegenerate triangle.
  • Base and height. Any side can be chosen, with the perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to its line.

A bit of history (optional)

In Euclid’s Elements, a diagonal of a parallelogram is called its “diameter.” The word can surprise a modern reader expecting a circle. In Book I, Proposition 34, Euclid first uses parallel-line angles to compare the two triangles formed by that diagonal. Their congruence supplies equal sides and angles, then equal areas. The proposition bundles properties that a modern textbook might spread across several pages. There is no numerical area calculation in the argument: matching pieces establishes the equality directly.

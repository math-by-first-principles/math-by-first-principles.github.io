Same Base, Same Height, and Area Ratios

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

Hold a triangle’s base still and move its top corner sideways without raising or lowering it. The triangle changes shape, but its area stays fixed. The reason is that the area formula measures the base and the perpendicular height, rather than the lengths of both sloping sides.

Learning goals

  • Identify the base and height conditions that guarantee equal triangle areas
  • Calculate area ratios for triangles whose perpendicular heights are equal
  • Derive shared-angle area ratios from corresponding perpendicular height ratios
  • Justify area bisection by a median using equal bases and a common height
  • Find areas in the six equal regions formed by the three medians

Equal bases and equal heights

Equal base lengths and equal corresponding heights guarantee equal triangle areas. For instance, a base of 9 cm and a perpendicular height of 4 cm give 18 square cm, wherever the height meets the base’s supporting line. The area formula from Area from Rectangles to Triangles applies to acute, right, and obtuse triangles.

Write AABCA_{ABC}AABC​ for the area of triangle ABCABCABC; the subscript names the region. All triangles here are nondegenerate, so their bases, heights, and areas are positive.

Why equal bases and heights give equal areas#

First, with base 12 and height 7, either triangle has area 12(12)(7)\frac12(12)(7)21​(12)(7), or 42 square units. Changing the apex sideways changes neither factor.

In general, two triangles with equal chosen base lengths bbb and equal corresponding perpendicular heights hhh both have area

A=12bh.A=\frac12 bh.A=21​bh.

The numbers 12 and 7 had no special role. The formula depends only on these two measurements.

∎

In this figure, the common base is BCBCBC. Because ADADAD is parallel to BCBCBC, the perpendicular distances from AAA and DDD to the line BCBCBC are equal. The foot from DDD lies outside segment BCBCBC, but it still gives the height. Thus the two triangles have equal areas.

The bases may instead be separate segments with equal lengths; equality of their matching heights still suffices. Conversely, equal areas alone do not establish equal bases and equal heights. A base of 3 with height 12 and a base of 6 with height 6 both give 18 square units.

Worked example 1 Move an apex along a parallel

Problem. Segment BCBCBC is 11 cm long. Points AAA and DDD lie on a line parallel to BCBCBC, at perpendicular distance 6 cm from it. The perpendicular from DDD meets the line BCBCBC beyond CCC. Find AABCA_{ABC}AABC​ and ADBCA_{DBC}ADBC​.

Solution. Both triangles use base BCBCBC and height 6 cm, including the triangle with the outside foot.

AABC=12(11)(6)AABC=33 cm2.\begin{aligned} A_{ABC}&=\frac12(11)(6)\\ A_{ABC}&=33\text{ cm}^2. \end{aligned}AABC​AABC​​=21​(11)(6)=33 cm2.​

The second triangle has the same two factors, so

ADBC=33 cm2.A_{DBC}=33\text{ cm}^2.ADBC​=33 cm2.

The parallel configuration is the one shown above; the new measurements do not change its reasoning.

Which pair of conditions guarantees equal triangle areas?

Answer choices

The formula uses both the chosen base and its perpendicular height. Matching those two factors guarantees equal areas; none of the other conditions fixes their product.

A divided base transfers its ratio to area

Triangles with equal heights have areas in the ratio of their bases. For example, bases 3 and 8 with a common height of 4 give areas 6 and 16. Both areas are the base multiplied by 2, so their ratio is 3:83:83:8.

Let DDD lie strictly between BBB and CCC, and let AAA lie off line BCBCBC. The two triangles use bases BDBDBD and DCDCDC. Their common height is the perpendicular distance from AAA to the same line BCBCBC, whether its foot is inside or outside either base segment.

Why common-height areas follow the base ratio#

For a numerical run, bases 7 and 10 with common height 6 give areas 21 and 30. Dividing cancels the shared factor 3, leaving 7/107/107/10.

For arbitrary positive bases b1,b2b_1,b_2b1​,b2​ and common positive height hhh,

A1A2=12b1h12b2h.\frac{A_1}{A_2}=\frac{\frac12 b_1h}{\frac12 b_2h}.A2​A1​​=21​b2​h21​b1​h​.

Cancel the nonzero common factor 12h\frac12 h21​h:

A1A2=b1b2.\frac{A_1}{A_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}.A2​A1​​=b2​b1​​.

Here A1A_1A1​ and A2A_2A2​ name the two areas in the same order as the bases. In the partition above, this gives

AABDAADC=BDDC.\frac{A_{ABD}}{A_{ADC}}=\frac{BD}{DC}.AADC​AABD​​=DCBD​.

No particular height was needed, only equality of the two heights. Likewise, for equal positive bases, the area ratio equals the corresponding height ratio, by canceling the common factor 12b\frac12 b21​b instead.

Worked example 2 Use a ratio without finding the height

Problem. In triangle ABCABCABC, DDD lies on BCBCBC, with BD=5BD=5BD=5 cm and DC=9DC=9DC=9 cm. The total area is 63 square cm. Find AABDA_{ABD}AABD​ and AADCA_{ADC}AADC​.

Solution. The common-height ratio is 5:95:95:9. There are 14 equal ratio parts, so one part is

63÷14=4.5 cm2.63\div14=4.5\text{ cm}^2.63÷14=4.5 cm2.

The area beside the 5 cm base is

AABD=5(4.5)AABD=22.5 cm2.\begin{aligned}A_{ABD}&=5(4.5)\\A_{ABD}&=22.5\text{ cm}^2.\end{aligned}AABD​AABD​​=5(4.5)=22.5 cm2.​

The area beside the 9 cm base is

AADC=9(4.5)AADC=40.5 cm2.\begin{aligned}A_{ADC}&=9(4.5)\\A_{ADC}&=40.5\text{ cm}^2.\end{aligned}AADC​AADC​​=9(4.5)=40.5 cm2.​

The areas add to 63 square cm. The partition figure above shows which region goes with each base.

Check your understanding

D lies inside BC in triangle ABC. The ratio BD to DC is 4 to 11. If triangle ABD has area 18 square cm, what is the area of triangle ADC?

Answer choices

Both heights are the distance from A to line BC. One ratio part is 18 divided by 4, or 4.5 square cm. The other triangle contains 11 such parts, giving 49.5 square cm.

A shared angle lets us compare heights

When two triangles have equal included angles, their areas are in the ratio of the products of the two sides enclosing those angles. The angle condition is essential: two specified side lengths alone leave the height free to change.

For a concrete configuration, take two rays from OOO. On the first ray, let OA=7OA=7OA=7 and OC=10OC=10OC=10. On the second ray, let OB=6OB=6OB=6 and OD=9OD=9OD=9. Suppose the perpendicular heights from BBB and DDD to the first ray’s line are 4 and 6. Similar right triangles explain the matching ratios 4:64:64:6 and 6:96:96:9. The areas are 14 and 30, so their ratio is 7:157:157:15, also the ratio of the products 7(6)7(6)7(6) and 10(9)10(9)10(9).

Shared-angle area ratios through heights#

First use enclosing side pairs 4 and 10, then 9 and 15. If the first perpendicular height is 6, AA similarity of the right triangles gives a second height of 9: their height ratio is 2:32:32:3, the same as 10:1510:1510:15. The areas are 12 and 40.5, whose ratio is 8:278:278:27. The enclosing side products are 40 and 135, with that same ratio. These particular lengths are not needed in the following argument.

Arrange the two nondegenerate triangles by a rigid motion so their equal included angles occupy the same two rays from OOO. Write a,b,c,da,b,c,da,b,c,d for the enclosing side lengths OA,OB,OC,ODOA,OB,OC,ODOA,OB,OC,OD, respectively. Let the perpendicular heights from BBB and DDD to the full line OCOCOC be hBh_BhB​ and hDh_DhD​.

Acute included angle. Let the feet be HHH and KKK. Right triangles OBHOBHOBH and ODKODKODK have equal acute angles at OOO, so AA makes them similar. Their corresponding heights and hypotenuses give

hBhD=bd.\frac{h_B}{h_D}=\frac{b}{d}.hD​hB​​=db​.

Right included angle. The feet coincide with OOO. There are no small right triangles to compare: the heights are the sides OBOBOB and ODODOD themselves, so the same ratio follows directly.

Obtuse included angle. The feet HHH and KKK lie on the opposite ray of the base line. Right triangles OBHOBHOBH and ODKODKODK have equal acute angles at OOO, each supplementary to the given obtuse angle. AA again gives the same positive height ratio.

These cases include a foot beyond the end of a chosen base segment: the height always reaches its supporting line. Using the area formula, then the height ratio, gives

AOABAOCD=ahBchDAOABAOCD=abcd.\begin{aligned} \frac{A_{OAB}}{A_{OCD}}&=\frac{a h_B}{c h_D}\\ \frac{A_{OAB}}{A_{OCD}}&=\frac{ab}{cd}. \end{aligned}AOCD​AOAB​​AOCD​AOAB​​​=chD​ahB​​=cdab​.​

The shared factor from the angle cancels through similar right triangles. We did not need its numerical measure.

Worked example 3 Match the included angle before multiplying

Problem. Two triangles have equal included angles of 110∘110^\circ110∘. The sides enclosing that angle in the first triangle are 9 cm and 14 cm; those in the second are 6 cm and 21 cm. Find the first-to-second area ratio.

Solution. The two sides in each product enclose the equal angle, so the height argument applies, including its obtuse case.

A1A2=9(14)6(21).\frac{A_1}{A_2}=\frac{9(14)}{6(21)}.A2​A1​​=6(21)9(14)​.

Both products are 126, giving area ratio 1:11:11:1. Equal areas do not require the individual enclosing sides to match.

Check your understanding

Two triangles have equal included angles. Their enclosing sides are 4 cm and 15 cm in the first triangle, and 6 cm and 10 cm in the second. What is the first-to-second area ratio?

Answer choices

The side products are both 60, so the areas are equal. The height ratio accounts for the second side; comparing only 4 with 6 would leave out that change.

A median halves area

A median gives two equal areas because it cuts the opposite base into equal lengths. For a triangle of area 54 square units, the two regions made by any median therefore have area 27 each. This does not say the two triangles are congruent.

Why a median bisects triangle area#

For example, if BDBDBD and DCDCDC are each 6 units, their common height of 5 gives area 15 square units on each side of ADADAD.

In any nondegenerate triangle ABCABCABC, let DDD be the midpoint of BCBCBC. The bases BDBDBD and DCDCDC are equal. The two heights both measure the distance from AAA to line BCBCBC. Equal-base, equal-height areas give

AABD=AADC.A_{ABD}=A_{ADC}.AABD​=AADC​.

The triangles fill ABCABCABC, so each is half its area. No perpendicular or angle-bisector condition on ADADAD was used.

Conversely, if DDD is inside BCBCBC and ADADAD gives equal subareas, their common-height ratio forces BD=DCBD=DCBD=DC. In this specific configuration, equal-area bisection implies that ADADAD is a median.

∎

Worked example 4 Bisect area in an obtuse triangle

Problem. Triangle ABCABCABC is obtuse and has area 86 square cm. Segment ADADAD is a median. Find the two subareas and explain whether the obtuse angle changes the answer.

Solution. The midpoint supplies equal bases, and both heights are measured from AAA to line BCBCBC. Thus

AABD=43 cm2.A_{ABD}=43\text{ cm}^2.AABD​=43 cm2.

Also,

AADC=43 cm2.A_{ADC}=43\text{ cm}^2.AADC​=43 cm2.

The argument uses a distance to a line, so it remains valid for an obtuse triangle. A median need not be a perpendicular height.

Check your understanding

D lies strictly inside BC, and AD divides triangle ABC into two equal areas. What must follow?

Answer choices

The two triangles share the height from A to line BC. Equal areas force equal bases BD and DC. This gives the midpoint condition but does not force the other three properties.

The centroid gives six equal areas

The three medians divide a triangle into six equal-area triangles. A total area of 72 square units, for example, gives 12 in each small region. The equality follows from the median and centroid facts already proved: the medians meet inside the triangle, and a centroid divides each median in the vertex-to-midpoint ratio 2:12:12:1.

Why the six median regions have equal areas#

Let D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F be the midpoints of BC,CA,ABBC,CA,ABBC,CA,AB, and let GGG be the centroid. First run the argument with a total area of 108. Median ADADAD leaves area 54 in triangle ABDABDABD. Inside that triangle, use bases GDGDGD and ADADAD, both on the median’s line, with common vertex BBB. Since GDGDGD is one third of ADADAD, triangle BGDBGDBGD has area 18. Equal bases BDBDBD and DCDCDC, with common vertex GGG, give area 18 in GDCGDCGDC too. Repeating at the other side midpoints gives 18 in each of the six regions.

For the general argument, let the whole area be TTT. The median theorem gives

AABD=T2.A_{ABD}=\frac{T}{2}.AABD​=2T​.

The earlier centroid theorem gives GD/AD=1/3GD/AD=1/3GD/AD=1/3. Triangles BGDBGDBGD and BADBADBAD share the height from BBB to line ADADAD, so

ABGD=13ABADABGD=T6.\begin{aligned} A_{BGD}&=\frac13 A_{BAD}\\ A_{BGD}&=\frac{T}{6}. \end{aligned}ABGD​ABGD​​=31​ABAD​=6T​.​

Triangles GBDGBDGBD and GDCGDCGDC have equal bases on BCBCBC and common vertex GGG, so they have equal areas. Repeating the same argument with median BEBEBE gives T/6T/6T/6 for CGECGECGE and EGAEGAEGA; with median CFCFCF it gives T/6T/6T/6 for AGFAGFAGF and FGBFGBFGB. These six nonoverlapping triangles fill ABCABCABC because GGG is inside and each side has been divided at its midpoint.

Worked example 5 Reassemble regions around the centroid

Problem. In triangle ABCABCABC, all three medians meet at GGG, with D,E,FD,E,FD,E,F the midpoints of BC,CA,ABBC,CA,ABBC,CA,AB. Triangle GBDGBDGBD has area 7.5 square cm. Find the whole area, the area of BGCBGCBGC, and the area of quadrilateral AEGFAEGFAEGF.

Solution. Each small region has the given area, so the whole area is

AABC=6(7.5)AABC=45 cm2.\begin{aligned}A_{ABC}&=6(7.5)\\A_{ABC}&=45\text{ cm}^2.\end{aligned}AABC​AABC​​=6(7.5)=45 cm2.​

Triangle BGCBGCBGC combines two small regions:

ABGC=2(7.5)ABGC=15 cm2.\begin{aligned}A_{BGC}&=2(7.5)\\A_{BGC}&=15\text{ cm}^2.\end{aligned}ABGC​ABGC​​=2(7.5)=15 cm2.​

Quadrilateral AEGFAEGFAEGF combines AEGAEGAEG and AGFAGFAGF, also two small regions:

Check your understanding

The three medians of a triangle form six small triangles. If the whole triangle has area 102 square cm, what is the area of one small triangle?

Answer choices

All six regions have equal areas, so divide 102 by 6. One small triangle has area 17 square cm.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Missing condition: equal base lengths alone do not give equal areas; the corresponding heights must also match.
  • Wrong height: use perpendicular distance to the base line, including an outside foot when needed.
  • Ratio order: match each area with its own base, or its own product of sides enclosing the equal angle.
  • Median versus altitude: a median halves area because of the midpoint, without needing a right angle.
  • Equal area versus congruence: equal areas alone do not establish matching sides and angles.

Key takeaways

  • Equal chosen bases and equal corresponding perpendicular heights give equal triangle areas.
  • For a common positive height, the ordered area ratio equals the ordered base ratio.
  • Equal included angles make the area ratio equal the ratio of enclosing side products.
  • A median divides a triangle into two equal areas.
  • All three medians divide a triangle into six equal areas.

A bit of history (optional)

In Book I of Euclid’s Elements, a word translated as “equal” sometimes refers to area rather than matching shape. This can surprise a reader who expects equal triangles to be congruent. Proposition 37 considers triangles standing on the same base between the same parallel lines, even though their top vertices can be in different places.

Euclid’s proof surrounds each triangle with a parallelogram. He first compares those larger regions, then uses the fact that a diagonal halves a parallelogram. The triangles inherit equal areas from the equal areas of their doubles. The text of Proposition 37 preserves that geometric route without giving the triangles numerical measurements.

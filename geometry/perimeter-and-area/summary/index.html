Perimeter and Area: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Perimeter PPP and area AAA
Perimeter measures boundary length in length units. Area measures the enclosed region in square units; equal areas do not imply equal perimeters.
Base and corresponding height
A triangle base is any chosen side. Its height is the perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to that side’s supporting line, including an exterior foot. A parallelogram height separates its opposite parallel side lines.
Area postulates
A unit square has area one. Congruent regions have equal areas. Finitely many pieces filling a region without gaps or interior overlaps have areas adding to the whole; shared edges add no area.
Inclusive trapezoid and kite
A trapezoid has at least one pair of parallel sides, so parallelograms qualify. A kite is convex with two disjoint pairs of adjacent congruent sides, each side in one pair; rhombi and squares qualify.
Named-region area AABCA_{ABC}AABC​
The subscript names the region: this notation denotes triangle ABC’s area. Subscripts on compared areas must follow the stated order of the corresponding bases or sides.
Convex regular polygon
A simple, nondegenerate polygon with congruent sides and interior angles. Its center is OOO, integer side count is nnn, and side length is sss.
Apothem aaa and radius rrr
The apothem runs perpendicularly from the regular polygon’s center to a side midpoint. A radius runs to a vertex. The central angle lies between radii to adjacent vertices.
Composite figure, auxiliary segment, and hole
A composite figure combines simpler regions. An auxiliary segment exposes useful pieces. A hole is an excluded region completely surrounded by retained material; its outline is an inner boundary.
Concave polygon
A simple polygon with an interior angle greater than 180 degrees. A proposed cut must remain in the region to give an interior decomposition.
Length scale factor kkk
The positive multiplier taking every original length to its corresponding image length in similar figures. Values above one enlarge, values between zero and one reduce, and one preserves size.

Problem types, step by step

Count rational square tiles

  1. Choose a common subdivision for both rational side lengths.
  2. Count rows and columns, then multiply the tile count by one tile’s area.
  3. Keep the tile’s side length separate from its square-unit area.

e.g. For a rectangle 11/5 by 9/5 units, find area using fifth-unit tiles: A=99/25A=99/25A=99/25 square units.

Find area or recover a matching height

  1. Choose a side and identify its perpendicular height, extending the supporting line if needed.
  2. Use the appropriate area rule; if a height is missing, an available right triangle may supply it.
  3. For a new base of the same triangle, divide twice its unchanged area by that new base.

e.g. For a triangle with 17.5 cm base and 6.4 cm perpendicular height, find area: A=56 cm2A=56\text{ cm}^2A=56 cm2.

Select a quadrilateral rule and reverse it

  1. Check parallel bases, perpendicular diagonals, or a diagonal with two known perpendicular distances.
  2. Form the justified area equation and solve for the requested length.
  3. Reassemble full diagonals from their parts and check positive lengths in the original equation.

e.g. For a trapezoid of area 62.4 square cm, height 4.8 cm, and one base 9.5 cm, find the other base: 16.5 cm.

Transfer a divided-base area ratio

  1. Identify the common supporting line and perpendicular height.
  2. Match the ordered base ratio to the ordered area ratio.
  3. Use the total or difference to find one ratio part; repeat with the new supporting line for a nested partition.

e.g. For an interior base split 11:16 and total triangle area 94.5 square cm, find the two subareas: 38.5 and 56 square cm.

Compare areas around equal included angles

  1. Verify that the stated equal angles lie between the two specified sides in each triangle.
  2. Compare the side products in the requested order.
  3. Transfer a known area or solve the resulting equation for a positive missing side.

e.g. For equal included angles with enclosing side pairs 5, 17 and 10, 12 cm, find the first-to-second area ratio: 17:2417:2417:24.

Regroup median and centroid regions

  1. Mark the actual side midpoints and medians.
  2. Use halves for one median and sixths for all three medians.
  3. Count the small regions in the requested triangle, quadrilateral, or remaining portion.

e.g. For a triangle of area 141 square cm with all three medians, find one small region’s area: 23.5 square cm.

Calculate a regular polygon’s area

  1. Find the side count and half-central angle.
  2. Use special right triangles or degree-mode trig to obtain the apothem and any needed side.
  3. Multiply half the full perimeter by the apothem; retain exact expressions or calculator precision until final rounding.

e.g. For an equilateral triangle of side 26 cm, find exact area: A=1693 cm2A=169\sqrt3\text{ cm}^2A=1693​ cm2.

Check regular-polygon dimension compatibility

  1. Fit radius, apothem, and half-side to one right triangle.
  2. Recover the acute half-central angle from the given side-to-apothem or apothem-to-radius ratio.
  3. Require 180 degrees divided by that angle to be an integer at least three; never round an exact noninteger into a side count.

Decompose a composite region or reverse its area

  1. Convert lengths to one unit and recover hidden lengths from aligned full lengths and parts.
  2. Add a complete nonoverlapping split, or subtract contained missing parts from a convenient whole.
  3. For an unknown dimension, solve the resulting area equation and check containment and positivity.

e.g. From a 28 by 16 cm rectangle remove a corner rectangle 7 cm deep; retained area is 371 square cm. Find the removed width: 11 cm.

Trace the requested perimeter

  1. Follow each exposed boundary once, including hole outlines only when requested.
  2. Exclude internal joins and auxiliary cuts; use a right triangle for an unknown slanted boundary if available.
  3. Distinguish a corner notch from a notch opening into the middle of a side.

e.g. Two polygons of perimeters 43 and 57 cm meet only along a 6.5 cm segment with disjoint interiors. Find union perimeter: 87 cm.

Convert area and length measurements

  1. Identify the conversion direction and a true length-unit factor.
  2. Use one factor for perimeter and its square for area.
  3. Combine measurements only after their units match.

e.g. Using 100 cm per meter, convert 0.043 square meters to square centimeters: 430 square cm.

Scale similar figures or reverse an area ratio

  1. Establish similarity and match original measurements with their images.
  2. Obtain the positive length factor from matching lengths or perimeters, or the positive square root of the area ratio.
  3. Scale any corresponding length once and area twice; subtract one from a multiplying factor when finding a percentage increase.

e.g. Similar polygons have original and image areas 125 and 245 square cm; original perimeter is 55 cm. Find image perimeter: 77 cm.

Exam traps

  • Trap Using the slanted side as a height

    Fix Find the perpendicular distance to the chosen base line. An exterior foot is valid; a base extension is not added to the original side.

  • Trap Keeping the old height after changing the base

    Fix The area stays fixed, but each chosen side needs its own perpendicular height.

  • Trap Halving an already halved diagonal

    Fix The diagonal area rule uses two full diagonals. In a general kite, only the diagonal joining equal-side vertices must bisect the other.

  • Trap Inferring area equality from just one matched factor

    Fix Equal bases alone or equal heights alone are insufficient. Match both, or use the applicable ordered ratio.

  • Trap Treating side products as absolute areas

    Fix Equal included angles give a ratio of products. Without the angle’s height information, those products do not determine each area.

  • Trap Assuming a median is an altitude or that equal-area pieces are congruent

    Fix A median reaches a midpoint. It halves area without requiring perpendicularity, angle bisection, or congruent pieces.

  • Trap Substituting a radius for an apothem

    Fix Use the perpendicular center-to-side distance as the height. The right half-triangle uses half the central angle and half a side.

  • Trap Multiplying by the side count twice

    Fix The full perimeter already includes every center-triangle base. Delay rounding until the final requested results.

  • Trap Counting overlaps, joins, or holes incorrectly

    Fix Count each included area once. Trace actual boundary components separately; a shared internal edge contributes no perimeter.

  • Trap Using a linear factor for square units or similar areas

    Fix Square the appropriate length factor. Unit conversion renames one region; a similarity resizes it. A dimensionless area multiplier does not change square units into fourth-power units.

  • Trap Reversing only one ratio or confusing “more” with “of”

    Fix Keep the same comparison direction. An increase of p percent gives a factor of one plus p divided by 100, before any square root.

  • Trap Inferring similarity or congruence from equal areas

    Fix Equal area alone determines neither shape nor corresponding lengths. If positive-area polygons are already similar and also have equal areas, their positive length factor is one and they are congruent.

Tangents and Secants

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

A straight bar across a circular frame can cut the frame twice, just touch it, or miss it. A bar that just touches has a precise direction: the radius to the contact point meets it at a right angle. That right angle turns a circle problem into a right-triangle problem.

Learning goals

  • Classify a line using its intersections with a circle
  • Prove and apply radius-tangent perpendicularity and its converse
  • Find and justify equal tangent segment lengths from one external point
  • Distinguish internal and external contact between two circles
  • Count common tangents using circle positions and the centers' sides

Count intersections first

A secant is a line meeting a circle at two distinct points. A tangent is a line meeting it at exactly one point, called the point of tangency or contact point. A line with no intersection is neither. These definitions concern the entire line, even when a drawing shows only part of it.

For example, take a circle of radius 555 units. A line 666 units from its center misses it. A line 555 units from the center touches once. A line 333 units from the center cuts twice: the perpendicular from the center leaves a right triangle with hypotenuse 555 and one leg 333 on each side of its foot.

To see why these are the only possibilities, let HHH be the perpendicular foot from center OOO to a line. Every other point XXX on the line is farther from OOO than HHH is. If OHOHOH exceeds the radius, no point can be on the circle. If OHOHOH equals the radius, only HHH is on it. If OHOHOH is smaller, Pythagoras gives one equal positive distance from HHH in each direction along the line, hence two circle points. This includes a line through OOO, whose intersections are the endpoints of a diameter.

Worked example 1 Classify three lines without trusting the sketch

Problem. A circle has radius 777 cm. The perpendicular distances from its center to lines aaa, bbb, and ccc are 777 cm, 999 cm, and 222 cm, respectively. Classify each line and give its number of circle intersections.

Solution. For line aaa, the distance equals the radius. It is a tangent with one intersection.

For line bbb, the distance is greater than the radius. It misses the circle and has no intersections.

For line ccc, the distance is smaller than the radius. It is a secant with two intersections.

Check your understanding

A circle has radius 666 units. A line is 222 units from its center. How does the line meet the circle?

Answer choices

The perpendicular distance is smaller than the radius. The line reaches the circle on both sides of the perpendicular foot, so it has two intersections and is a secant.

A tangent and its contact radius

A tangent meets its contact radius at a right angle. The contact point matters: the tangent need not be perpendicular to another radius.

A tangent is perpendicular to its contact radius#

First consider a radius-131313 circle and a line whose perpendicular distance from the center is 555. Pythagoras gives 121212 units from the foot HHH to either intersection. Reflecting one intersection across the perpendicular through HHH gives the other. A line whose foot differs from its circle intersection therefore cannot touch only once.

Now let a tangent meet a positive-radius circle at TTT, and let HHH be the perpendicular foot from OOO to that line. Suppose HHH differs from TTT. Reflect in the line through OOO perpendicular to the tangent. This reflection fixes OOO, carries the tangent line onto itself, and sends TTT to a different point UUU on that line. Distance preservation gives a second point of the circle because OUOUOU equals radius OTOTOT. This contradicts the definition of a tangent. Thus HHH is TTT, and the radius is perpendicular to the tangent. If HHH is OOO, the same reflection still works; it exchanges the two ends of a diameter. Nothing depended on radius 131313.

∎

The reverse claim is useful when tangency is not given.

A perpendicular at a radius endpoint is a tangent#

Take a radius of 666 units and a line perpendicular to it at its endpoint TTT on the circle. A point XXX on the line, 888 units from TTT, is 101010 units from the center by Pythagoras, so it is outside. The same comparison works for every point other than TTT.

In general, let TTT be on a circle with center OOO and positive radius rrr. A line through TTT is perpendicular to OTOTOT. For any other point XXX on that line, the right triangle gives

OX2=r2+TX2,OX2>r2.\begin{aligned} OX^2&=r^2+TX^2,\\ OX^2&>r^2. \end{aligned}OX2OX2​=r2+TX2,>r2.​

Hence XXX is outside the circle, while TTT is on it. The line meets the circle exactly once and is a tangent. The numerical values above did not matter; the positive extra square did.

∎

Together these results say: a line through a point on a circle is tangent exactly when it is perpendicular to the radius at that point. Perpendicularity somewhere else on the radius is not enough.

Worked example 2 Find a finite tangent segment

Problem. A circle centered at OOO has radius 999 cm. Point PPP is 151515 cm from OOO, and line PTPTPT is tangent at TTT. Find the length of segment PTPTPT.

Solution. Radius OTOTOT is perpendicular to tangent PTPTPT, so OPOPOP is the hypotenuse of right triangle OTPOTPOTP. Use the Pythagorean theorem:

PT2+92=152,PT2=144,PT=12 cm.\begin{aligned} PT^2+9^2&=15^2,\\ PT^2&=144,\\ PT&=12\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PT2+92PT2PT​=152,=144,=12 cm.​

Choose the positive root because PTPTPT is a length. We have measured a finite segment from PPP to TTT, not an infinite line.

Check your understanding

Point TTT is on a circle centered at OOO. A line through TTT is perpendicular to OTOTOT. Why is it tangent?

Answer choices

For any other point XXX of the line, OTXOTXOTX is a right triangle. Its hypotenuse OXOXOX is longer than radius OTOTOT. Thus all points of the line except TTT are outside, leaving exactly one intersection.

Two tangent segments from one external point

A strict external point is farther from the center than the radius. From such a point PPP, a circle has two distinct tangent lines, touching at TTT and UUU. The two finite segments PTPTPT and PUPUPU have equal lengths.

For example, if the radius is 888 and OPOPOP is 101010, both right triangles have the same hypotenuse 101010 and a radius leg 888. Pythagoras gives a tangent length of 666 in each triangle.

Tangent segments from a common external point are equal#

For the numerical case, both triangles are right at their contact points; each has hypotenuse 202020 and a leg 121212. The HL congruence criterion makes the triangles congruent, so their remaining sides both have length 161616.

For any positive-radius circle and strict external point PPP, join OTOTOT, OUOUOU, and OPOPOP. The radius-tangent theorem gives right angles at TTT and UUU. The radius legs are equal, and OPOPOP is the common hypotenuse. HL gives

△OTP≅△OUP.\triangle OTP\cong\triangle OUP.△OTP≅△OUP.

Corresponding sides therefore give

PT=PU.PT=PU.PT=PU.

The proof did not depend on 121212 or 202020. It does depend on both segments being tangent to the same circle from the same strict external point.

∎

Compare the two tangent segments

Point PPP must stay strictly outside for two distinct positive tangent segments. A point on the circle has one tangent line at that point; it does not give two positive contact segments. A point strictly inside has no tangent line through it, since every tangent line’s closest distance from OOO is the radius. The familiar two-tangent picture therefore has a real boundary condition.

Worked example 3 Use equality before solving

Problem. The tangent segments PTPTPT and PUPUPU from the same strict external point touch the same circle at TTT and UUU. Their lengths, in centimeters, are 3x+23x+23x+2 and 5x−105x-105x−10, respectively. Find xxx and both segment lengths.

Solution. Both tangent segments meet the theorem’s conditions, so set their lengths equal:

3x+2=5x−10,12=2x,x=6.\begin{aligned} 3x+2&=5x-10,\\ 12&=2x,\\ x&=6. \end{aligned}3x+212x​=5x−10,=2x,=6.​

Substitute into each expression:

PT=20 cm,PU=20 cm.\begin{aligned} PT&=20\text{ cm},\\ PU&=20\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PTPU​=20 cm,=20 cm.​

Both are positive, as the geometry requires.

When two circles touch

Two distinct circles are tangent circles when they share exactly one point. At that point their tangent lines agree, and their contact radii lie on the same line. If the radii were not collinear, reflection in the line of centers would give a second shared point.

With radii 555 and 222 units, external contact puts the contact point between the centers and gives center distance 777. Internal contact puts the smaller center between the larger center and the contact point, giving distance 333.

Let the larger radius be RRR, the smaller be rrr, and the center distance be ddd. Both radii are positive.

External contact has the circles outside one another:

d=R+r.d=R+r.d=R+r.

Internal contact has one circle inside the other and requires unequal radii:

d=R−r.d=R-r.d=R−r.

Equal radii with the same center produce coincident circles, which share the whole circle, not a single contact point.

Worked example 4 Distinguish two kinds of contact

Problem. Two circles have radii 101010 cm and 444 cm. Classify their contact in each case.

Case A: their centers are 141414 cm apart.

Case B: their centers are 666 cm apart.

Solution. Case A: the sum of the radii is 141414 cm, so the circles are externally tangent. The contact point lies between the centers.

Case B: the difference of the radii is 666 cm, so the circles are internally tangent. The smaller circle lies inside the larger, touching it once.

Check your understanding

Two circles have radii 999 cm and 555 cm, and their centers are 444 cm apart. Which description fits?

Answer choices

The difference of the radii is 444 cm, matching the center distance. One circle is inside the other and touches it once, so the contact is internal.

Common tangents depend on both centers

A common tangent is one line tangent to both circles. It is a common external tangent when the two centers lie on the same side of that line. It is a common internal tangent when the centers lie on opposite sides. These names concern sides of a line; they do not say whether the circles touch externally or internally.

For radii 555 and 333, a common external tangent leaves a perpendicular radius difference of 222 between the centers. An internal tangent leaves a perpendicular separation of 888, the sum of the radii. The center distance must be at least that perpendicular separation. For example, center distance 666 permits external tangents but is too short for an internal one.

Here is why the thresholds also give the counts. Assume first that the centers are distinct. For a common external tangent, both contact radii point toward the same side. The perpendicular part of the center separation is the radius difference. If the center distance is larger than this difference, a right triangle has a positive along-line leg. Its two mirror placements give two external tangents. At equality that leg disappears, leaving one. Below the difference no such triangle is possible.

For a common internal tangent, the contact radii point in opposite directions. Replace the difference with the radius sum in the same argument. Larger center distance gives two internal tangents; equality gives one; smaller distance gives none. The two mirror choices exhaust the possibilities because a right triangle with the fixed hypotenuse and perpendicular leg has only those two placements. For equal radii and distinct centers, the external tangents are the two parallels to the line of centers. This is the zero-difference case.

If the centers coincide but the radii differ, a line cannot be at two different perpendicular distances from the same center, so there is no common tangent. If both center and radius agree, the circles coincide and every tangent to one is tangent to the other.

Worked example 5 Count the tangent lines at a boundary

Problem. Two circles have radii 666 cm and 222 cm. Give the number of common external tangents, common internal tangents, and all common tangents in each case.

Case A: center distance 999 cm.

Case B: center distance 888 cm.

Solution. Case A: 999 exceeds the radius sum, 888. The circles are separate. There are two external and two internal common tangents, for four distinct lines.

Case B: the center distance equals the radius sum. The circles touch externally. The two external tangents remain, but there is just one internal tangent at the contact point, for three distinct lines.

Check your understanding

Two circles have the same center and the same positive radius. How many common tangent lines do they have?

Answer choices

The circles coincide. Every tangent of either circle is also a tangent of the other. A different contact point gives a different tangent line, so there are infinitely many.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Counting a segment instead of a line. Extend the whole line mentally before calling it a secant or tangent.
  • Putting the right angle at the wrong point. It belongs between the tangent and the radius to its contact point.
  • Equalizing arbitrary segments. Equal tangent lengths require one circle, one strict external starting point, and two tangent contact segments.
  • Mixing the two uses of internal and external. Circle contact describes the circles’ positions; a common tangent’s name describes the centers’ sides of the line.
  • Ignoring coincident circles. Same center and same radius gives infinitely many shared points and common tangents.

Key takeaways

  • A line meets a circle in zero, one, or two points; one means tangent and two means secant.
  • A line through a circle point is tangent exactly when it is perpendicular to the radius there.
  • The two finite tangent segments from one strict external point to one circle are equal.
  • Tangent circles meet once; their center distance is the radius sum for external contact or the positive difference for internal contact.
  • Classify common tangents by the centers’ sides, and include nested, concentric, and coincident cases when counting.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid treated tangency in Book III of the Elements, devoted to circles. Proposition 18 proves that the center-to-contact segment is perpendicular to a touching line. Its argument supposes a different perpendicular and uses a right triangle to reach an impossibility. A modern reflection proof reaches the same conclusion by producing a second intersection.

Read Proposition 18 in David Joyce’s edition of Euclid.

Segment Products and Power of a Point

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Two bars cross inside a circular frame. One bar is split into lengths 333 and 888; another is split into 444 and 666. Their sums differ, but both products are 242424. This is a circle constraint: the split lengths cannot vary independently. Similar triangles explain the shared product and show what changes when the crossing point is outside.

Learning goals

  • Identify the segments in an inside or outside product using endpoint order
  • Derive the three segment product rules from correctly matched similar triangles
  • Distinguish signed power from positive products inside, outside, and on a circle
  • Calculate missing lengths using chord, secant, and tangent products
  • Check algebraic roots against every actual length and the required point order

Products inside the circle

Multiplying the two parts of a chord through a fixed interior point gives the same result for every such chord. Throughout this lesson, symbols such as PAPAPA mean ordinary positive lengths when the endpoints differ.

Let chords ABABAB and CDCDCD meet at PPP, strictly inside one circle. The orders are A,P,BA,P,BA,P,B and C,P,DC,P,DC,P,D. For lengths 333 and 888 on the first chord, and 444 and 666 on the second, each product is 242424.

Join ACACAC and DBDBDB. The angles at PPP are vertical angles. The angles PACPACPAC and PDBPDBPDB intercept the same arc BCBCBC, so they are equal by Inscribed Angles. This gives AA similarity, using the criteria from Similarity and Proportion.

Intersecting-chord products from similarity#

First use a different numerical instance: the lengths from PPP to A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D are 2,12,3,82,12,3,82,12,3,8. The angle equalities just described match triangle APCAPCAPC to triangle DPBDPBDPB. Thus the ratios of corresponding sides give

28=312,2(12)=3(8).\begin{aligned} \frac{2}{8}&=\frac{3}{12},\\ 2(12)&=3(8). \end{aligned}82​2(12)​=123​,=3(8).​

Now keep the same point orders but allow any lengths. Because PPP lies between the endpoints on both chords, ∠PAC\angle PAC∠PAC is ∠BAC\angle BAC∠BAC, and ∠PDB\angle PDB∠PDB is ∠CDB\angle CDB∠CDB. Both intercept arc BCBCBC excluding their vertices. Also ∠APC\angle APC∠APC and ∠DPB\angle DPB∠DPB are vertical angles.

AA gives the ordered correspondence

△APC∼△DPB.\triangle APC\sim\triangle DPB.△APC∼△DPB.

Here AAA matches DDD, PPP matches PPP, and CCC matches BBB. In particular, PAPAPA matches PDPDPD, and PCPCPC matches PBPBPB.

PAPD=PCPB,PA⋅PB=PC⋅PD.\begin{aligned} \frac{PA}{PD}&=\frac{PC}{PB},\\ PA\cdot PB&=PC\cdot PD. \end{aligned}PDPA​PA⋅PB​=PBPC​,=PC⋅PD.​

Nothing in the angle reasoning depended on the numerical lengths. The two chords must be distinct and meet strictly inside; then the triangles are nondegenerate and all denominators are positive.

∎

This is the intersecting-chord product rule. Pair the two lengths on the same straight chord, not two adjacent pieces from different chords.

Worked example 1 Find a part, then the whole chord

Problem. Chords ABABAB and CDCDCD intersect at interior point PPP, with orders A,P,BA,P,BA,P,B and C,P,DC,P,DC,P,D. The lengths from PPP to A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C are 333 cm, 101010 cm, and 555 cm, respectively. Find PDPDPD and CDCDCD.

Solution. The two parts of each chord form a product.

3(10)=5(PD),PD=6 cm.\begin{aligned} 3(10)&=5(PD),\\ PD&=6\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}3(10)PD​=5(PD),=6 cm.​

Point PPP lies between CCC and DDD, so add the parts.

CD=5+6,CD=11 cm.\begin{aligned} CD&=5+6,\\ CD&=11\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}CDCD​=5+6,=11 cm.​

Both parts are positive, and their product is 303030, matching the first chord.

Chords AB,CDAB,CDAB,CD meet at interior PPP. The lengths PA,PB,PCPA,PB,PCPA,PB,PC are 4,15,54,15,54,15,5, respectively. Find PDPDPD.

Answer choices

Multiply the two parts of AB, then divide by PC.

4(15)=5(PD),PD=12.\begin{aligned}4(15)&=5(PD),\\PD&=12.\end{aligned}4(15)PD​=5(PD),=12.​

Products outside the circle

From an external point, the useful pair is the external part and the whole secant segment. Suppose two secant rays meet the same circle in orders P,A,BP,A,BP,A,B and P,C,DP,C,DP,C,D.

The external segment is PAPAPA. The whole secant segment is PBPBPB, which includes both PAPAPA and the chord ABABAB. For example, an external part of 333 and an inside chord of 555 give a whole segment of 888. Its product with the external part is 242424, not 151515.

Two-secant products from similarity#

For a numerical instance, take PAPAPA of 222, PBPBPB of 151515, PCPCPC of 333, and PDPDPD of 101010. Join ADADAD and CBCBCB. The two triangles share their angle at PPP. Their angles at DDD and BBB intercept the same arc ACACAC. Therefore triangle PADPADPAD matches triangle PCBPCBPCB, and corresponding sides give

23=1015,2(15)=3(10).\begin{aligned} \frac{2}{3}&=\frac{10}{15},\\ 2(15)&=3(10). \end{aligned}32​2(15)​=1510​,=3(10).​

For arbitrary lengths in those same orders, ∠APD\angle APD∠APD and ∠CPB\angle CPB∠CPB use the same two rays. At DDD, ray DPDPDP passes through CCC, so ∠PDA\angle PDA∠PDA is ∠CDA\angle CDA∠CDA. At BBB, ray BPBPBP passes through AAA, so ∠PBC\angle PBC∠PBC is ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC. These inscribed angles intercept the same arc ACACAC excluding B,DB,DB,D.

AA gives

△PAD∼△PCB.\triangle PAD\sim\triangle PCB.△PAD∼△PCB.

The matches are PPP to PPP, AAA to CCC, and DDD to BBB.

PAPC=PDPB,PA⋅PB=PC⋅PD.\begin{aligned} \frac{PA}{PC}&=\frac{PD}{PB},\\ PA\cdot PB&=PC\cdot PD. \end{aligned}PCPA​PA⋅PB​=PBPD​,=PC⋅PD.​

The numerical argument used this same correspondence. The hypotheses are two distinct secant rays from one strict external point to one circle. Each whole segment must be longer than its external part.

∎

This is the two-secant product rule. If a problem gives the external part and the inside chord, first add them.

PB=PA+AB.PB=PA+AB.PB=PA+AB.

Worked example 2 Build both whole secant segments

Problem. Two secants meet a circle in orders P,A,BP,A,BP,A,B and P,C,DP,C,DP,C,D. The external lengths PA,PCPA,PCPA,PC are 333 cm and 444 cm. Chord ABABAB is 171717 cm. Find PDPDPD and chord CDCDCD.

Solution. On the first secant, add the external and inside lengths.

PB=3+17,PB=20 cm.\begin{aligned} PB&=3+17,\\ PB&=20\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PBPB​=3+17,=20 cm.​

Use external times whole on each ray.

3(20)=4(PD),PD=15 cm.\begin{aligned} 3(20)&=4(PD),\\ PD&=15\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}3(20)PD​=4(PD),=15 cm.​

Remove the second external part from its whole.

CD=15−4,CD=11 cm.\begin{aligned} CD&=15-4,\\ CD&=11\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}CDCD​=15−4,=11 cm.​

The whole length 151515 exceeds the external length 444, as the order requires.

Check your understanding

Secants have orders P,A,BP,A,BP,A,B and P,C,DP,C,DP,C,D. The lengths PA,AB,PCPA,AB,PCPA,AB,PC are 2,19,32,19,32,19,3. Find the whole segment PDPDPD.

Answer choices

The first whole secant is 21. Use external times whole.

2(21)=3(PD),PD=14.\begin{aligned}2(21)&=3(PD),\\PD&=14.\end{aligned}2(21)PD​=3(PD),=14.​

A tangent and a secant

The square of a tangent segment’s length equals external times whole on a secant from the same strict external point. For example, external length 222 and whole length 888 give product 161616. A tangent segment from that point has length 444, whose square is 161616.

The tangent-secant product from similarity#

In a numerical instance, let tangent segment PTPTPT have length 666, while secant lengths PA,PBPA,PBPA,PB are 3,123,123,12. Join TATATA and TBTBTB. The tangent-chord theorem matches the angle PTAPTAPTA to angle PBTPBTPBT, using arc TATATA that excludes BBB. The angle at PPP is shared, giving triangle PTAPTAPTA similar to triangle PBTPBTPBT.

612=36,62=3(12).\begin{aligned} \frac{6}{12}&=\frac{3}{6},\\ 6^2&=3(12). \end{aligned}126​62​=63​,=3(12).​

For any tangent at TTT and genuine secant in order P,A,BP,A,BP,A,B, the same angle reasoning applies. Ray TPTPTP, together with chord TATATA, selects the arc TATATA excluding BBB. By the tangent-chord theorem, its angle equals the inscribed angle TBATBATBA, which is also ∠PBT\angle PBT∠PBT. The angles TPATPATPA and BPTBPTBPT use the same two rays.

AA gives

△PTA∼△PBT.\triangle PTA\sim\triangle PBT.△PTA∼△PBT.

The matches are PPP to PPP, TTT to BBB, and AAA to TTT.

PTPB=PAPT,PT2=PA⋅PB.\begin{aligned} \frac{PT}{PB}&=\frac{PA}{PT},\\ PT^2&=PA\cdot PB. \end{aligned}PBPT​PT2​=PTPA​,=PA⋅PB.​

All three lengths are positive because PPP is strictly outside and A,BA,BA,B are distinct. This proof uses the tangent-chord theorem, rather than treating a nearly tangent secant drawing as proof.

∎

Worked example 3 Recover the tangent length

Problem. Segment PTPTPT is tangent to a circle at TTT. A secant from PPP meets it in order P,A,BP,A,BP,A,B, with external length PAPAPA of 777 cm and inside chord ABABAB of 212121 cm. Find PTPTPT.

Solution. The whole secant is 282828 cm. The tangent-secant rule gives

PT2=7(28),PT2=196,PT=14 cm.\begin{aligned} PT^2&=7(28),\\ PT^2&=196,\\ PT&=14\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}PT2PT2PT​=7(28),=196,=14 cm.​

Although the equation for a real number squared has roots 141414 and −14-14−14, PTPTPT itself is a length and must be positive.

Check your understanding

The ordered similarity is △PTA∼△PBT\triangle PTA\sim\triangle PBT△PTA∼△PBT. Its matches are P to P, T to B, and A to T. Which product equality follows from corresponding sides?

Answer choices

The matched sides give the proportion below. Multiplying by both positive denominators gives the product equality.

PTPB=PAPT,PT2=PA⋅PB.\begin{aligned}\frac{PT}{PB}&=\frac{PA}{PT},\\PT^2&=PA\cdot PB.\end{aligned}PBPT​PT2​=PTPA​,=PA⋅PB.​

One signed value for a point

The three products belong to one idea, but the inside case needs a sign. For a circle with center OOO and positive radius rrr, define the signed power of PPP by

Pow⁡(P)=OP2−r2.\operatorname{Pow}(P)=OP^2-r^2.Pow(P)=OP2−r2.

For radius 666 and center distance 444, this signed value is −20-20−20. The positive product of two chord parts is 202020. For the same radius and center distance 101010, signed power is 646464, equal to an external-times-whole product.

The link comes from a diameter, without a coordinate equation.

Why the products equal signed power or its negative#

First take radius 555 and center distance 333. The diameter through O,PO,PO,P has endpoints U,VU,VU,V, with PPP between them. The distances from PPP to the ends are 888 and 222, so their product is 161616. The signed value is 32−523^2-5^232−52, or −16-16−16.

For arbitrary radius rrr and interior center distance ddd, the diameter parts are r+dr+dr+d and r−dr-dr−d. The chord rule makes every chord product equal to their product.

PA⋅PB=(r+d)(r−d),PA⋅PB=r2−d2,PA⋅PB=−Pow⁡(P).\begin{aligned} PA\cdot PB&=(r+d)(r-d),\\ PA\cdot PB&=r^2-d^2,\\ PA\cdot PB&=-\operatorname{Pow}(P). \end{aligned}PA⋅PBPA⋅PBPA⋅PB​=(r+d)(r−d),=r2−d2,=−Pow(P).​

If PPP is the center, choose any diameter: both parts are rrr. This also gives the displayed result with ddd zero.

Next take radius 555 and exterior center distance 131313. Along the diameter ray toward OOO, the external and whole lengths are 888 and 181818. Their product is 144144144, which also equals 132−5213^2-5^2132−52.

For arbitrary exterior center distance ddd, the diameter ray has external length d−rd-rd−r and whole length d+rd+rd+r. The two-secant rule compares any other secant with this one.

PA⋅PB=(d−r)(d+r),PA⋅PB=d2−r2,PA⋅PB=Pow⁡(P).\begin{aligned} PA\cdot PB&=(d-r)(d+r),\\ PA\cdot PB&=d^2-r^2,\\ PA\cdot PB&=\operatorname{Pow}(P). \end{aligned}PA⋅PBPA⋅PBPA⋅PB​=(d−r)(d+r),=d2−r2,=Pow(P).​

For a tangent segment, the contact radius is perpendicular to it, as proved in Tangents and Secants. In the numerical exterior case, the right triangle gives a tangent square of 132−5213^2-5^2132−52, or 144144144. In general, the Pythagorean theorem gives

PT2+r2=d2,PT2=d2−r2,PT2=Pow⁡(P).\begin{aligned} PT^2+r^2&=d^2,\\ PT^2&=d^2-r^2,\\ PT^2&=\operatorname{Pow}(P). \end{aligned}PT2+r2PT2PT2​=d2,=d2−r2,=Pow(P).​

Both diameter arguments use differences of squares; the choice of numerical radius did not matter.

∎

Strictly outsidePositiveExternal times whole, or the tangent length squared

At a point on the circle, the center distance equals the radius. A secant through that point has one intersection at PPP itself, so that factor is zero. This is a boundary case, not the strict external configuration: there is one tangent line at PPP, and no positive segment from PPP to a different tangent contact point.

Compare products through an interior point

For a fixed circle and a fixed interior point, rotating a chord changes its two parts but preserves their product. Measurements or a moving drawing illustrate the rule; the similarity argument proves it.

Try all five chord directions. Predict which lengths change and whether their positive product changes, then compare signed power separately.

Compare products through an exterior point

Keep the exterior point and circle fixed. Different genuine secants have different external and whole lengths but the same product. A drawing supplies evidence for this invariance; the ordered similarity proof establishes it.

Try all five secant directions through fixed PPP. Track the external length PAPAPA and whole length PBPBPB, then test whether their product stays fixed.

Worked example 4 Separate the sign from the lengths

Problem. A circle has radius 111111 units. Use the following separate points.

Case A: OPOPOP is 777 units. Find signed power and the positive product for a chord through PPP.

Case B: OQOQOQ is 131313 units. Find signed power and the tangent length from QQQ.

Case C: OROROR is 111111 units. Find signed power and describe the tangent at RRR.

Solution. Case A is inside.

Pow⁡(P)=72−112,Pow⁡(P)=−72.\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Pow}(P)&=7^2-11^2,\\ \operatorname{Pow}(P)&=-72. \end{aligned}Pow(P)Pow(P)​=72−112,=−72.​

The positive chord product is 727272 square units.

Case B is outside.

Pow⁡(Q)=132−112,Pow⁡(Q)=48.\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Pow}(Q)&=13^2-11^2,\\ \operatorname{Pow}(Q)&=48. \end{aligned}Pow(Q)Pow(Q)​=132−112,=48.​

The tangent length is the positive square root, 434\sqrt343​ units.

Case C is on the circle. Its power is zero. There is one tangent line at RRR; there are not two positive tangent contact segments starting at RRR.

Answer choices

The point is inside. Subtract radius squared from center distance squared.

Pow⁡(P)=42−82,Pow⁡(P)=−48.\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Pow}(P)&=4^2-8^2,\\\operatorname{Pow}(P)&=-48.\end{aligned}Pow(P)Pow(P)​=42−82,=−48.​

The positive chord-part product is the negative of this value, 48.

Algebra must fit the lengths

A root solves an equation; a geometric solution also makes every actual length positive and preserves the stated order. For an external secant, the whole must exceed the external part. If a variable itself names a length, it must be positive. If it is only a parameter inside length expressions, a negative value can be valid.

Worked example 5 Keep a valid negative parameter

Problem. From a strict external point PPP, a secant meets a circle in order P,A,BP,A,BP,A,B. In centimeters, the external length is x+5x+5x+5 and the whole length is x+7x+7x+7. A tangent segment from PPP has length 222\sqrt222​ cm. Find every geometrically valid xxx, then find ABABAB. Here xxx is a parameter, not itself a length.

Solution. The tangent square is 888. Use external times whole.

(x+5)(x+7)=8,x2+12x+27=0,(x+3)(x+9)=0.\begin{aligned} (x+5)(x+7)&=8,\\ x^2+12x+27&=0,\\ (x+3)(x+9)&=0. \end{aligned}(x+5)(x+7)x2+12x+27(x+3)(x+9)​=8,=0,=0.​

The algebraic candidates are −3-3−3 and −9-9−9. Check them separately.

For x=−3x=-3x=−3, the external length is 222 cm and the whole is 444 cm. Both are positive, the whole is longer, and their product is 888. This negative parameter is valid.

For x=−9x=-9x=−9, the expressions give −4-4−4 cm and −2-2−2 cm. These are not ordinary lengths, so this candidate is invalid.

Only x=−3x=-3x=−3 fits. The inside chord is

AB=4−2,AB=2 cm.\begin{aligned} AB&=4-2,\\ AB&=2\text{ cm}. \end{aligned}ABAB​=4−2,=2 cm.​

Sometimes more than one algebraic candidate passes every length and order check. Keep every admissible candidate; the sign of a parameter alone cannot decide.

Check your understanding

A secant has external length x+4x+4x+4 and whole length x+10x+10x+10. A tangent from the same point has length 333\sqrt333​. Here xxx is a parameter. Which value is geometrically valid?

Answer choices

The product equation factors as shown.

(x+4)(x+10)=27,(x+1)(x+13)=0.\begin{aligned}(x+4)(x+10)&=27,\\(x+1)(x+13)&=0.\end{aligned}(x+4)(x+10)(x+1)(x+13)​=27,=0.​

At x=-1 the lengths are 3 and 9, both positive and correctly ordered. At x=-13 they are -9 and -3, so that candidate is invalid.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • External times inside. For an outside secant, multiply external by whole, adding the inside chord first if necessary.
  • Mixed chord pieces. Inside, multiply the two opposite parts on one chord.
  • A sign lost inside. Positive chord lengths have a positive product, while signed power is negative.
  • Roots without a geometry check. Substitute each candidate into every length expression and check the point order.
  • A boundary treated as outside. Zero power places the point on the circle, where the two-positive-tangent picture does not apply.

Key takeaways

  • Inside, the products of the two parts of intersecting chords are equal.
  • Outside, external times whole is the same on every genuine secant and equals a tangent segment’s squared length.
  • Signed power is center distance squared minus radius squared; inside products equal its negative.
  • Similarity proves the product rules through ordered triangle correspondences.
  • Accept all algebraic candidates that satisfy the actual positive lengths and required order.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid described products as areas rather than writing algebraic formulas. In Book III, Proposition 35, two crossing chords produce equal rectangles when their parts are used as side lengths. Proposition 36 compares the rectangle formed by a whole secant and its outside part with the square on a tangent. The diagrams turned a multiplication question into a question about shapes that could be cut, added, and compared. The modern product notation is shorter, but those rectangles explain the older wording.

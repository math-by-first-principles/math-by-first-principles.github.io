Cyclic Quadrilaterals

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

Four corners can slide around a circular frame while their side lengths change. Their angles still have to fit the same full turn around the circle. We will use that restriction in both directions: to find angles when a circle is given, and to decide whether four corners can share a circle.

Learning goals

  • Identify cyclic quadrilaterals using four distinct vertices in convex boundary order
  • Prove that opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary
  • Justify cyclicity using the converse opposite-angle test
  • Relate an exterior angle to the opposite interior angle
  • Transfer angle information through diagonals after establishing cyclicity

Four vertices on one circle

A cyclic quadrilateral is a convex quadrilateral whose four distinct vertices lie on one circle. We name its vertices in boundary order, such as A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D, so its sides are AB,BC,CD,DAAB,BC,CD,DAAB,BC,CD,DA. No three vertices are collinear, and every interior angle is strictly between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘.

The vertices must lie on the circle itself. Merely placing a quadrilateral inside a disk does not make it cyclic. Joining four circle points in a crossing order also falls outside the convex quadrilaterals studied here.

An opposite-angle pair has vertices that do not share a side. Here the pairs are ∠DAB\angle DAB∠DAB with ∠BCD\angle BCD∠BCD, and ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC with ∠CDA\angle CDA∠CDA. We sometimes shorten these whole interior angles to A,CA,CA,C and B,DB,DB,D.

Check your understanding

Four distinct points A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D occur in that order on a circle. Which joining order gives the convex cyclic quadrilateral?

Answer choices

The sides must follow the circle's boundary order. The order A, B, C, D does this; the other listed orders cross two sides.

Why opposite angles add to a straight angle

Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary: their measures add to a straight angle. For example, if arc BCDBCDBCD measures 154∘154^\circ154∘, the angle at AAA measures 77∘77^\circ77∘. The other arc from BBB to DDD measures 206∘206^\circ206∘, so the angle at CCC measures 103∘103^\circ103∘. The two angles total 180∘180^\circ180∘ because their arcs total 360∘360^\circ360∘.

The inscribed-angle theorem makes each angle half the arc between its side endpoints that excludes its own vertex. Here opposite vertices exclude opposite arcs between the same endpoints.

Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary#

First take intercepted arcs of 182∘182^\circ182∘ and 178∘178^\circ178∘. Halving gives 91∘91^\circ91∘ and 89∘89^\circ89∘, and their sum is 180∘180^\circ180∘. We now follow the same steps without fixing the arc measures.

Let A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D be distinct points in convex order on a circle. Write ppp for the measure of arc BCDBCDBCD and qqq for the measure of arc DABDABDAB. These are exactly the two arcs from BBB to DDD, so

p+q=360∘.p+q=360^\circ.p+q=360∘.

The inscribed-angle theorem gives

m∠DAB=p/2,m∠BCD=q/2.\begin{gathered} m\angle DAB=p/2,\\ m\angle BCD=q/2. \end{gathered}m∠DAB=p/2,m∠BCD=q/2.​

Adding the two angle measures therefore gives

m∠DAB+m∠BCD=(p+q)/2,m∠DAB+m∠BCD=180∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle DAB+m\angle BCD&=(p+q)/2,\\ m\angle DAB+m\angle BCD&=180^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠DAB+m∠BCDm∠DAB+m∠BCD​=(p+q)/2,=180∘.​

Using the two arcs from AAA to CCC instead gives

m∠ABC+m∠CDA=180∘.m\angle ABC+m\angle CDA=180^\circ.m∠ABC+m∠CDA=180∘.

Neither step depended on the numerical arcs chosen first.

∎

Worked example 1 Use both opposite pairs

Problem. Convex cyclic quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD has m∠DAB=67∘m\angle DAB=67^\circm∠DAB=67∘ and m∠ABC=112∘m\angle ABC=112^\circm∠ABC=112∘. Find the whole angles BCDBCDBCD and CDACDACDA.

Solution. The angle opposite AAA is at CCC.

m∠BCD=180∘−67∘,m∠BCD=113∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle BCD&=180^\circ-67^\circ,\\ m\angle BCD&=113^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠BCDm∠BCD​=180∘−67∘,=113∘.​

The angle opposite BBB is at DDD.

m∠CDA=180∘−112∘,m∠CDA=68∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle CDA&=180^\circ-112^\circ,\\ m\angle CDA&=68^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠CDAm∠CDA​=180∘−112∘,=68∘.​

Worked example 2 An equation from cyclicity

Problem. In convex cyclic quadrilateral PQRSPQRSPQRS, the whole angles at PPP and RRR measure (2x+17)∘(2x+17)^\circ(2x+17)∘ and (4x+7)∘(4x+7)^\circ(4x+7)∘. Find xxx and both angle measures.

Solution. Opposite angles are supplementary.

(2x+17)+(4x+7)=180,6x+24=180,x=26.\begin{aligned} (2x+17)+(4x+7)&=180,\\ 6x+24&=180,\\ x&=26. \end{aligned}(2x+17)+(4x+7)6x+24x​=180,=180,=26.​

Substitute into each expression.

m∠P=69∘,m∠R=111∘.\begin{gathered} m\angle P=69^\circ,\\ m\angle R=111^\circ. \end{gathered}m∠P=69∘,m∠R=111∘.​

Both measures are between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘, and their sum checks the equation.

Explore opposite-angle sums

Predict which angle must decrease when its opposite angle increases while all four vertices remain on the circle. Compare this with moving one vertex off the circle while preserving convex order. Measurements can test the prediction; the arc argument supplies the proof.

Answer choices

B and D are opposite vertices. Their angles total 180 degrees.

m∠CDA=180∘−123∘,m∠CDA=57∘.\begin{aligned}m\angle CDA&=180^\circ-123^\circ,\\m\angle CDA&=57^\circ.\end{aligned}m∠CDAm∠CDA​=180∘−123∘,=57∘.​

The converse decides cyclicity

A supplementary pair of opposite angles also forces a convex quadrilateral to be cyclic. For example, opposite angles of 76∘76^\circ76∘ and 104∘104^\circ104∘ pass this test. Consecutive angles of 76∘76^\circ76∘ and 104∘104^\circ104∘ alone do not tell us about cyclicity.

The converse cyclicity test says: if a convex quadrilateral has four distinct vertices, no three collinear, and one pair of opposite interior angles sums to 180∘180^\circ180∘, all four vertices lie on one circle. A quadrilateral’s interior angles total 360∘360^\circ360∘, so one supplementary opposite pair also makes the other pair supplementary.

We need to prove that the fourth point lies on the circle through the first three. The existence and uniqueness of that circumcircle were established earlier.

The converse opposite-angle test for cyclicity#

The comparison can first be rehearsed with numbers. Suppose ABCEABCEABCE is cyclic, its angles at A,CA,CA,C are 85∘,95∘85^\circ,95^\circ85∘,95∘, and XXX is the intersection of its diagonals. A point DDD strictly between XXX and EEE gives smaller angles at both AAA and CCC, so their sum is less than 180∘180^\circ180∘. A point beyond EEE on ray BXBXBX gives larger angles at both vertices, so their sum exceeds 180∘180^\circ180∘. Only D=ED=ED=E can give the required sum. The following argument explains each comparison and constructs EEE.

Let convex ABCDABCDABCD have a supplementary opposite pair. Since its four angles total 360∘360^\circ360∘, we have

m∠BAD+m∠BCD=180∘.m\angle BAD+m\angle BCD=180^\circ.m∠BAD+m∠BCD=180∘.

Let diagonals AC,BDAC,BDAC,BD meet at XXX. Convexity puts XXX strictly inside both segments: A,X,CA,X,CA,X,C are in order, and B,X,DB,X,DB,X,D are in order. Draw the circle through noncollinear A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C.

First, XXX is strictly inside this circle. To see this, let MMM be the perpendicular foot from its center OOO to chord line ACACAC. If ACACAC is not a diameter, MMM is the chord midpoint, and MX<MAMX<MAMX<MA. Pythagoras compares the right triangles with common leg OMOMOM, giving OX<OAOX<OAOX<OA. If X=MX=MX=M, this follows directly from OM<OAOM<OAOM<OA. If ACACAC is a diameter, OOO is its midpoint and an interior point XXX has OX<OAOX<OAOX<OA directly.

Line BXBXBX therefore has a second circle intersection EEE, with B,X,EB,X,EB,X,E in order. Here is the existence and order check: let HHH be the perpendicular foot from OOO to this line. Because XXX is strictly inside, OHOHOH is less than the radius. The line-circle cases give two intersections, equidistant from HHH. Pythagoras makes points strictly between those intersections exactly the points of the line inside the circle, so XXX is between them. One is BBB; the other is EEE. If H=OH=OH=O, these are simply the ends of a diameter.

The point EEE is on the opposite side of ACACAC from BBB. Thus ABCEABCEABCE is convex in that order, and the theorem already proved gives

m∠BAE+m∠BCE=180∘.m\angle BAE+m\angle BCE=180^\circ.m∠BAE+m∠BCE=180∘.

Both DDD and EEE lie on ray BXBXBX beyond XXX. If they differ, there are exactly two orders to examine.

Case 1: X,D,EX,D,EX,D,E are in order. Then DDD is strictly inside triangle ACEACEACE. At AAA, ray ADADAD lies strictly between ACACAC and AEAEAE. Diagonal ACACAC is inside both convex whole angles BADBADBAD and BAEBAEBAE, so the positive extra angle DAEDAEDAE gives

m∠BAD<m∠BAE.m\angle BAD<m\angle BAE.m∠BAD<m∠BAE.

At CCC, ray CDCDCD lies strictly between CACACA and CECECE, giving

m∠BCD<m∠BCE.m\angle BCD<m\angle BCE.m∠BCD<m∠BCE.

Their sum is less than 180∘180^\circ180∘, contradicting the given sum.

Case 2: X,E,DX,E,DX,E,D are in order. Now EEE is strictly inside triangle ACDACDACD. The same angle additions, with D,ED,ED,E exchanged, give

m∠BAD>m∠BAE,m∠BCD>m∠BCE.\begin{gathered} m\angle BAD>m\angle BAE,\\ m\angle BCD>m\angle BCE. \end{gathered}m∠BAD>m∠BAE,m∠BCD>m∠BCE.​

Their sum exceeds 180∘180^\circ180∘, again contradicting the hypothesis.

Both unequal positions are excluded, so D=ED=ED=E and DDD lies on the circle through A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C. The numerical rehearsal used only these strict comparisons, not special values of the two angles.

∎

Worked example 3 Accept or reject the circle test

Problem. Each case describes a convex quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD in boundary order with four distinct vertices and no three collinear. Decide whether it is cyclic, giving a reason.

Case A: m∠DAB=82∘m\angle DAB=82^\circm∠DAB=82∘ and m∠BCD=98∘m\angle BCD=98^\circm∠BCD=98∘.

Case B: m∠ABC=105∘m\angle ABC=105^\circm∠ABC=105∘ and m∠CDA=76∘m\angle CDA=76^\circm∠CDA=76∘.

Solution. Case A: the opposite angles sum to 180∘180^\circ180∘, so the converse proves cyclicity.

Case B: the opposite angles sum to 181∘181^\circ181∘. A cyclic quadrilateral would have a sum of 180∘180^\circ180∘, so this quadrilateral is not cyclic.

Check your understanding

A convex quadrilateral has four distinct vertices in boundary order A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D. Which fact is sufficient to prove it cyclic?

Answer choices

A and C are opposite. Their supplementary sum is precisely the converse's hypothesis. The other choices by themselves do not impose that opposite-angle condition.

An exterior angle matches the opposite interior angle

Extending a side turns the supplementary rule into an equality. Extend BCBCBC through CCC to EEE, so B,C,EB,C,EB,C,E are collinear in that order. The exterior angle DCEDCEDCE and interior angle BCDBCDBCD form a linear pair.

For example, an interior angle of 54∘54^\circ54∘ at CCC makes its exterior angle 126∘126^\circ126∘. In a cyclic quadrilateral, the opposite interior angle at AAA is also 126∘126^\circ126∘.

The exterior and opposite interior angles of a cyclic quadrilateral#

For the numerical instance above, the linear pair and the opposite-angle theorem each give 180∘−54∘180^\circ-54^\circ180∘−54∘, or 126∘126^\circ126∘. In general, the linear pair gives

m∠DCE=180∘−m∠BCD.m\angle DCE=180^\circ-m\angle BCD.m∠DCE=180∘−m∠BCD.

For convex cyclic ABCDABCDABCD, the opposite-angle theorem gives

m∠BAD=180∘−m∠BCD.m\angle BAD=180^\circ-m\angle BCD.m∠BAD=180∘−m∠BCD.

Thus

m∠DCE=m∠BAD.m\angle DCE=m\angle BAD.m∠DCE=m∠BAD.

Conversely, for a convex quadrilateral with the same side extension, this equality and the linear pair make the opposite interior angles supplementary. The converse test then proves cyclicity.

∎

Worked example 4 Read the specified extension

Problem. Convex cyclic ABCDABCDABCD has BCBCBC extended through CCC to EEE. Given m∠DCE=121∘m\angle DCE=121^\circm∠DCE=121∘ and m∠ABC=78∘m\angle ABC=78^\circm∠ABC=78∘, find the whole interior angles at A,C,DA,C,DA,C,D.

Solution. The exterior angle equals the opposite interior angle.

m∠BAD=121∘.m\angle BAD=121^\circ.m∠BAD=121∘.

The linear pair at CCC gives

m∠BCD=180∘−121∘,m∠BCD=59∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle BCD&=180^\circ-121^\circ,\\ m\angle BCD&=59^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠BCDm∠BCD​=180∘−121∘,=59∘.​

Finally, DDD is opposite BBB.

m∠CDA=180∘−78∘,m∠CDA=102∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle CDA&=180^\circ-78^\circ,\\ m\angle CDA&=102^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠CDAm∠CDA​=180∘−78∘,=102∘.​

Check your understanding

In convex cyclic ABCDABCDABCD, side BCBCBC is extended through CCC to EEE. If m∠BAD=87∘m\angle BAD=87^\circm∠BAD=87∘, find m∠DCEm\angle DCEm∠DCE.

Establish the circle before transferring angles

Once cyclicity is justified, a diagonal can expose two angles intercepting the same chord. As proved in Inscribed Angles, two inscribed angles intercepting the same chosen arc are equal. Name the endpoints and check that the vertices exclude the same arc before transferring a value.

Worked example 5 From a circle test to two angle transfers

Problem. Convex quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD has diagonals AC,BDAC,BDAC,BD. Given m∠BAC=43∘m\angle BAC=43^\circm∠BAC=43∘, m∠CAD=66∘m\angle CAD=66^\circm∠CAD=66∘, and m∠BCD=71∘m\angle BCD=71^\circm∠BCD=71∘, decide whether it is cyclic. Then find m∠CBDm\angle CBDm∠CBD and m∠BDCm\angle BDCm∠BDC.

Solution. Convexity places ACACAC inside the angle at AAA, so

m∠BAD=43∘+66∘,m∠BAD=109∘.\begin{aligned} m\angle BAD&=43^\circ+66^\circ,\\ m\angle BAD&=109^\circ. \end{aligned}m∠BADm∠BAD​=43∘+66∘,=109∘.​

The opposite angles total 109∘+71∘109^\circ+71^\circ109∘+71∘, which is 180∘180^\circ180∘. The converse proves that all four vertices share a circle.

Angles CADCADCAD and CBDCBDCBD both intercept the arc CDCDCD excluding A,BA,BA,B, so

m∠CBD=66∘.m\angle CBD=66^\circ.m∠CBD=66∘.

Angles BACBACBAC and BDCBDCBDC both intercept the arc BCBCBC excluding A,DA,DA,D, so

m∠BDC=43∘.m\angle BDC=43^\circ.m∠BDC=43∘.

Check your understanding

Convex ABCDABCDABCD has m∠BAD=96∘m\angle BAD=96^\circm∠BAD=96∘ and m∠BCD=84∘m\angle BCD=84^\circm∠BCD=84∘. Diagonal ACACAC gives m∠BAC=38∘m\angle BAC=38^\circm∠BAC=38∘. What is m∠BDCm\angle BDCm∠BDC?

Answer choices

The opposite-angle sum proves cyclicity. Angles BAC and BDC then intercept the same arc BC, giving equal measures.

m∠BDC=38∘.m\angle BDC=38^\circ.m∠BDC=38∘.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Inside instead of on. Every vertex must lie on the same circle, not merely inside a disk.
  • Consecutive instead of opposite. A supplementary consecutive pair alone does not establish cyclicity.
  • A whole angle instead of a part. The opposite-angle theorem uses the whole interior angles; add diagonal pieces first.
  • An unspecified exterior angle. Name the extended side and its point order before using the exterior equality.
  • A circle assumed too soon. Establish cyclicity before using same-arc angle transfers on a quadrilateral with no given circle.

Key takeaways

  • Four distinct vertices on one circle, in convex boundary order, form a cyclic quadrilateral.
  • Each pair of opposite interior angles is supplementary because its intercepted arcs make a full circle.
  • For a convex quadrilateral, one supplementary opposite pair is enough to prove cyclicity.
  • An exterior angle formed by extending a side equals the opposite interior angle, with the same convexity conditions.
  • After cyclicity is established, diagonals let you transfer angles intercepting the same arc.

A bit of history (optional)

Euclid’s Elements presents the opposite-angle property in Book III, among its circle propositions. Proposition 22 describes the sum as two right angles. Its construction instruction is only four words: “Join AC and BD.”

Those two straight lines let the proof call on earlier propositions about triangles and angles on the same circle segment. The short construction is the turning point of a proof whose explanation is spread across earlier results. Reading the proposition beside those references gives a glimpse of how the Elements was organized: a new circle statement arrives with much of its reasoning already prepared.

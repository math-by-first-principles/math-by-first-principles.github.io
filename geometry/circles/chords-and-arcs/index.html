Circles, Chords, and Arcs

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Slide a straight bar across a circular window. Near the center, a long bar fits; near the edge, only a short one fits. To explain that change, join the center to the bar’s ends. The round frame has supplied equal sides, and a question about a circle has become a question about triangles.

Learning goals

  • Describe circle parts and distinguish concentric, congruent, and similar circles
  • Prove that translations and dilations map any positive-radius circle onto another
  • Calculate minor, major, and semicircle arc measures using central angles and addition
  • Justify chord and arc congruence with the same-circle or congruent-circle hypothesis
  • Apply perpendicular chord bisection and center distance, including the diameter case

One center, one fixed distance

A circle is the set of all points in a plane at one fixed positive distance from a fixed point, its center. A radius is a segment from the center to a point on the circle; the same word names its length. A chord joins two distinct points on the circle. A diameter is a chord through the center. It consists of two radii, so its length is twice the radius. The circle is the boundary; its interior is not part of that set of points.

Two circles are concentric when they have the same center. Distinct concentric circles have different radii. Two circles are congruent when a rigid motion maps one onto the other. This happens exactly when their radii are equal: rigid motions preserve distance, and translating one center to the other then matches every point at that radius. Equal-radius circles may have different centers; congruent and concentric describe different relationships.

Why every circle is similar to every other

A change of size can carry an entire circle onto another circle.

A circle of radius 222 can be translated to share the center of a circle of radius 777. A dilation by 72\tfrac7227​ about that center then sends every distance of 222 to a distance of 777. Reversing that dilation sends every point of the second circle back to the first. Thus the match covers both whole circles, not just a few chosen points.

Use the accepted transformation properties from Dilations and Scale Factors: a dilation about its center multiplies all distances from that center by its positive scale factor.

All positive-radius circles are similar#

For radius-333 and radius-888 circles, translate the first center to the second center. Every point on the translated circle is 333 units from that center. Dilation by 83\tfrac8338​ sends it to distance 888. Conversely, every point at distance 888 has one preimage on its ray at distance 333, obtained by the factor 38\tfrac3883​.

Now take any circles with centers OOO and PPP and positive radii rrr and sss. Translate OOO to PPP. Dilate about PPP by the positive factor s/rs/rs/r. Each point at distance rrr goes to distance sss because

r(sr)=s.r\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)=s.r(rs​)=s.

For any point of the target circle, the inverse dilation by r/sr/sr/s produces a point at distance rrr, and the inverse translation returns it to the original circle. These inverse steps also show that different points cannot merge. This is a one-to-one match onto the whole second circle, made from allowed similarity transformations. Nothing depended on the particular radii 333 and 888.

∎

Worked example 1 Map two whole circles

Problem. Circle OOO has radius 444 cm. Circle PPP has radius 101010 cm. Describe a similarity carrying circle OOO onto circle PPP, and decide whether they are congruent.

Solution. Translate OOO to PPP, then dilate about PPP by the factor

k=104,k=52.\begin{aligned}k&=\frac{10}{4},\\k&=\frac52.\end{aligned}kk​=410​,=25​.​

Every original radius becomes 101010 cm. The inverse factor 25\tfrac2552​ takes every target point back, so the image is the whole target circle. The circles are similar. They are not congruent because their radii differ.

Arc measure records a turn

An arc is one of the two portions of a circle between distinct endpoints. A central angle has its vertex at the center and its sides along radii to those endpoints. Arc measure records the turn about the center, in degrees; it does not report the length along the curved boundary.

For example, if the smaller central angle from OAOAOA to OBOBOB is 80∘80^\circ80∘, the short arc has measure 80∘80^\circ80∘. The other way around measures 280∘280^\circ280∘, since the two turns complete 360∘360^\circ360∘.

A minor arc measures less than 180∘180^\circ180∘. Two letters name it when the short route is unambiguous, as in AB^\widehat{AB}AB. A major arc measures more than 180∘180^\circ180∘ and less than 360∘360^\circ360∘. Use three letters to select its route, as in ACB^\widehat{ACB}ACB, with CCC on that major arc. If the endpoints are opposite ends of a diameter, each route is a semicircle, measuring 180∘180^\circ180∘. A third point selects which semicircle is meant; neither is a minor or major arc.

The degree measure of a minor arc is defined to be the measure of its smaller central angle. A major arc has the remaining measure in a full turn. These definitions use angle turns, so changing the radius does not change an arc’s degree measure. Equal degree measures on different-sized circles do not make the arcs congruent as figures.

Arcs that meet only at endpoints and trace a larger arc without overlap have measures that add. This follows from angle addition at the center. If BBB lies on the selected arc from AAA to CCC, then

m(ABC^)=m(AB^)+m(BC^).m(\widehat{ABC})=m(\widehat{AB})+m(\widehat{BC}).m(ABC)=m(AB)+m(BC).

Here the two smaller arcs are the routes through BBB in that selected arc. Do not replace them by other routes between the same endpoints.

Worked example 2 Select the route before adding

Problem. Points A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D occur in that order around a circle. The consecutive minor arcs AB^\widehat{AB}AB and BC^\widehat{BC}BC measure 74∘74^\circ74∘ and 59∘59^\circ59∘. Find the minor arc AC^\widehat{AC}AC and the major arc ADC^\widehat{ADC}ADC.

Solution. Add the adjacent arcs through BBB.

m(AC^)=74∘+59∘,m(AC^)=133∘.\begin{aligned}m(\widehat{AC})&=74^\circ+59^\circ,\\m(\widehat{AC})&=133^\circ.\end{aligned}m(AC)m(AC)​=74∘+59∘,=133∘.​

This is below 180∘180^\circ180∘, so it is the minor arc. The route through DDD is the rest of the circle.

m(ADC^)=360∘−133∘,m(ADC^)=227∘.\begin{aligned}m(\widehat{ADC})&=360^\circ-133^\circ,\\m(\widehat{ADC})&=227^\circ.\end{aligned}m(ADC)m(ADC)​=360∘−133∘,=227∘.​

Equal chords and their arcs

In one circle, or in congruent circles, equal chord lengths go with equal minor arc measures. The reverse is also true. The equal-radius condition is essential.

Imagine two chords of length 121212 in circles of radius 888. Each chord and its two endpoint radii form a triangle with side lengths 8,8,128,8,128,8,12. SSS gives equal central angles, hence equal minor arc measures. Starting instead with equal central angles and two radius-888 circles, SAS gives equal chords.

Equal chords and equal arcs in equal-radius circles#

Two 9,9,109,9,109,9,10 triangles are congruent by SSS, so their angles opposite the 101010-unit sides are equal. These are the smaller central angles. For the reverse direction, two 70∘70^\circ70∘ central angles with adjacent radius lengths 999 determine congruent triangles by SAS, so their chords are equal.

For the general case, let ABABAB and CDCDCD be non-diameter chords in the same circle or in circles of the same positive radius. Join each center to both endpoints. If the chords have equal lengths, the two radius pairs and the chords give SSS congruence. The central angles, and therefore the minor arc measures, are equal.

Conversely, equal minor arc measures give equal central angles by the definition of arc measure. Together with the equal radii, these give SAS congruence and equal chord lengths. No step depended on the numbers 999, 101010, or 707070.

If a chord is a diameter, its endpoints give semicircles. Equal-length chords in equal-radius circles must then both be diameters, since no non-diameter chord reaches twice the radius (the triangle inequality is strict). Conversely, any two diameters of equal-radius circles have equal lengths. Their corresponding semicircles each measure 180∘180^\circ180∘.

∎

Equal minor arcs also leave equal major arc measures after subtraction from 360∘360^\circ360∘. Equal major arc measures give equal minor arc measures in the reverse direction. In equal-radius circles, arcs of the same type with equal measure are congruent arcs: move the centers together, rotate one endpoint onto its partner, and reflect if needed to match the selected route. The entire selected arc matches. A minor and a major arc sharing one chord are not congruent to each other.

Worked example 3 Transfer chord equality to arc measures

Problem. In one circle, chords ABABAB and CDCDCD are congruent. Their minor arcs have the following measures, in degrees. Find xxx and the major arc from CCC to DDD.

m(AB^)=(4x+6)∘,m(CD^)=(7x−24)∘.\begin{aligned} m(\widehat{AB})&=(4x+6)^\circ,\\ m(\widehat{CD})&=(7x-24)^\circ. \end{aligned}m(AB)m(CD)​=(4x+6)∘,=(7x−24)∘.​

Solution. The same-circle hypothesis lets us equate the minor arc measures.

4x+6=7x−24,30=3x,x=10.\begin{aligned} 4x+6&=7x-24,\\ 30&=3x,\\ x&=10. \end{aligned}4x+630x​=7x−24,=3x,=10.​

Substitute into either expression, then subtract from a full turn.

m(CD^)=46∘.m(\widehat{CD})=46^\circ.m(CD)=46∘.

The major arc from CCC to DDD therefore measures

360∘−46∘=314∘.360^\circ-46^\circ=314^\circ.360∘−46∘=314∘.

The 46∘46^\circ46∘ result is between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘, as a minor arc must be.

A perpendicular through the center

A line through the center of a circle that is perpendicular to a chord bisects that chord. For a non-diameter chord, the segment from the center to its midpoint is also perpendicular to the chord.

For instance, take a chord of length 101010 in a circle of radius 131313. Its midpoint is 555 units from each endpoint. The center is 131313 units from each endpoint. Both points lie on the chord’s perpendicular bisector by the perpendicular-bisector locus, so the line joining them is that perpendicular bisector.

Perpendicular chord bisection and its converse#

With radius 101010 and chord length 141414, let MMM be the midpoint. Both OOO and MMM are equally far from the chord endpoints, so both lie on the perpendicular bisector. They are distinct, and hence determine that line. Conversely, the perpendicular line through OOO is that same unique perpendicular bisector and meets the chord at MMM.

Now let ABABAB be any non-diameter chord with midpoint MMM and center OOO. The endpoint radii have equal lengths. Thus OOO lies on the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB. The midpoint MMM lies on it too. Since OOO and MMM are distinct, line OMOMOM is that perpendicular bisector. Conversely, the unique perpendicular to line ABABAB through OOO must be the perpendicular bisector already found, so its intersection with ABABAB is MMM. The numeric lengths played no part beyond showing equal endpoint distances.

For a diameter ABABAB, its midpoint is OOO because the two opposite radii have equal lengths. Every line through OOO bisects that diameter, so a line through its midpoint need not be perpendicular. The segment OMOMOM now has zero length and determines no line. The perpendicular line through OOO still exists, and still bisects ABABAB at OOO.

∎

For a non-diameter chord, the two right triangles on either side of the perpendicular have equal hypotenuses (radii) and the common leg OMOMOM. HL congruence shows that OMOMOM also bisects the smaller central angle. The diameter line containing OMOMOM therefore bisects the minor arc, and its opposite ray bisects the major arc. For example, a 110∘110^\circ110∘ minor arc splits into two 55∘55^\circ55∘ arcs; the 250∘250^\circ250∘ major arc splits into two 125∘125^\circ125∘ arcs. If ABABAB itself is a diameter, the perpendicular diameter makes four 90∘90^\circ90∘ central angles, so it bisects both semicircles.

Worked example 4 Find a full chord from one right triangle

Problem. A circle has radius 131313 cm. The perpendicular distance from its center OOO to chord ABABAB is 555 cm. Find ABABAB.

Solution. Let MMM be the perpendicular foot. Chord bisection gives two equal halves. In right triangle OMAOMAOMA, the radius is the hypotenuse.

h2+52=132,h2=144,h=12.\begin{aligned} h^2+5^2&=13^2,\\ h^2&=144,\\ h&=12. \end{aligned}h2+52h2h​=132,=144,=12.​

A length is positive, so use the positive square root. The full chord contains both halves.

AB=2(12)=24 cm.AB=2(12)=24\text{ cm}.AB=2(12)=24 cm.

Center distance compares chords

The distance from the center to a chord means the perpendicular distance to the line containing that chord. Its foot lies at the chord’s midpoint, so this is also the shortest distance to the chord itself.

A radius-252525 circle with a chord 242424 units from its center gives a right triangle with hypotenuse 252525 and one leg 242424. Its other leg is 777, since the squares are 625625625 and 576576576, leaving 494949. The full chord is 141414 units. This is the same Pythagorean reasoning used for any right triangle.

Chord length and perpendicular center distance#

For a radius-171717 circle with center distance 888, half the chord satisfies

h2=172−82,h2=225,h=15.\begin{aligned} h^2&=17^2-8^2,\\ h^2&=225,\\ h&=15. \end{aligned}h2h2h​=172−82,=225,=15.​

Generally let the radius be rrr, the perpendicular center distance be ddd, and half the chord be hhh. For a non-diameter chord, bisection and Pythagoras give

h2+d2=r2,h=r2−d2,chord length=2r2−d2.\begin{aligned} h^2+d^2&=r^2,\\ h&=\sqrt{r^2-d^2},\\ \text{chord length}&=2\sqrt{r^2-d^2}. \end{aligned}h2+d2hchord length​=r2,=r2−d2​,=2r2−d2​.​

The numeric argument used the radius and perpendicular distance only, so the same steps work for any positive radius and a non-diameter chord. For a diameter, ddd is zero and the length is 2r2r2r directly from the definition. The formula agrees in this boundary case without pretending there is a nondegenerate right triangle.

A genuine chord has two distinct endpoints, so the permitted distances are

0≤d<r.0\le d<r.0≤d<r.

At distance rrr, the formula would give zero, not a chord with distinct endpoints.

In the same circle or congruent circles, equal perpendicular center distances give equal values of r2−d2r^2-d^2r2−d2 and hence equal chord lengths. Conversely, equal chord lengths give equal half lengths; subtracting their equal squares from the same radius square gives equal distance squares. Distances are nonnegative, so the distances themselves are equal. This includes two diameters at distance zero.

With a fixed radius, increasing the nonnegative distance makes its square larger. That makes the positive half-chord square smaller and the chord shorter. A diameter, at distance zero, is therefore a longest chord.

∎

A measurement or moving diagram can suggest this comparison. The right-triangle argument establishes it for every chord satisfying the hypotheses.

Worked example 5 Recover a distance, then transfer it

Problem. Two congruent circles have radius 151515 cm. In the first, chord EFEFEF is 181818 cm long. In the second, chord GHGHGH is also 181818 cm long. Find each chord’s perpendicular center distance and explain the transfer.

Solution. Half of EFEFEF is 999 cm. If its center distance is ddd cm, Pythagoras gives

d2+92=152,d2=144,d=12.\begin{aligned} d^2+9^2&=15^2,\\ d^2&=144,\\ d&=12. \end{aligned}d2+92d2d​=152,=144,=12.​

The chord GHGHGH has the same length in a circle of the same radius. Equal chords in congruent circles have equal perpendicular center distances, so each distance is 121212 cm.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Arc measure versus length. Degrees record a central turn. A number of degrees is not a length in centimeters.
  • Missing equal radii. Equal degree measures give equal chord lengths only in the same circle or congruent circles.
  • Stopping at a half chord. The right triangle contains half the chord; double that leg to recover the full chord.
  • Using a slanted distance. The center-to-chord distance is perpendicular, not a segment to an arbitrary point of the chord.
  • Forgetting the diameter boundary. Its midpoint is the center, so the segment joining them does not define a line.

Key takeaways

  • All positive-radius circles are similar through a translation and a positive dilation; equal radii characterize congruence.
  • Select the arc’s route before using central-angle measure, arc addition, or a full 360∘360^\circ360∘ turn.
  • In one circle or congruent circles, equal chords correspond to equal minor arcs, equal major arcs, and equal perpendicular center distances.
  • A perpendicular through the center bisects a chord. The center-to-midpoint converse requires a non-diameter chord.
  • Use the radius, half chord, and perpendicular distance in a right triangle; handle a diameter directly.

A bit of history (optional)

A circle can be drawn without marking its center. That leaves a practical puzzle for someone given only its outline: where should a compass point go to reproduce it?

The opening proposition of Book III of Euclid’s Elements takes up that puzzle. It starts with an arbitrary chord, bisects it, and draws a perpendicular through the midpoint. That perpendicular continues across the circle. Bisecting the resulting diameter locates the center.

The construction does not need the center as an input. The proof is what certifies that the point found by the ruler-and-compass steps is the center, rather than a point that merely looks central. A modern translation and diagram are available in David Joyce’s edition of Book III, Proposition 1.

Designing Under Constraints

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Watch the video explainer

Intuition

The biggest garden on a sketch might be the first one you rule out. If fencing that layout costs more than you have, its extra planting space is out of reach. Which layout would you choose if one of its sides already had a fence?

Learning goals

  • Identify constraints, feasible designs, and an objective in a design problem
  • Compare fencing layouts under a fixed budget and a changed site assumption
  • Count the most items that fit within a specified packing layout family
  • Justify a proven optimum over a stated set of possible dimensions
  • Revise a packaging recommendation when an estimate of waste changes

Find the designs you can use

A constraint is a requirement a design must meet. For a garden, a spending limit and the available space are both constraints. A feasible design satisfies every constraint. The objective is what you want to make as large or as small as possible among the feasible designs. For the garden, it could be the area available to plant.

An assumption helps turn the real situation into something you can calculate. If you count the whole area inside the fence as plantable, for instance, you are assuming the model can ignore paths. When the design changes, keep track of which requirements and assumptions your calculation uses.

Worked example 1 Compare three garden plans

Problem. A school has a fencing budget of 408408408 dollars for a rectangular garden. The two edges parallel to the sidewalk cost 121212 dollars per meter each. The two edges perpendicular to it cost 888 dollars per meter each. Assume the site can fit all three proposals below, fence can be bought in the exact lengths needed, and the full enclosed area is plantable.

  • Plan A. 121212 m parallel to the sidewalk by 666 m perpendicular.
  • Plan B. 101010 m parallel by 101010 m perpendicular.
  • Plan C. 121212 m parallel by 999 m perpendicular.

Find the cost and area of each plan. Which feasible proposal has the most planting area?

Check your understanding

A design costs exactly the budget, fits the site, and meets every other requirement. Its area is smaller than another feasible design's. How should you classify it?

Answer choices

The budget is a cap, so spending exactly that much is allowed. All requirements are met, making the design feasible.

The larger feasible design rules out an area optimum for the smaller one.

Recheck feasibility when the site changes

A best tested design wins among the feasible designs you actually compared. A proven optimum wins over every design allowed by the model. When the allowed choices really are a short list, checking every one can prove the optimum. If your list is a sample, you need another argument to cover the designs you left out.

Worked example 2 Use an existing fence

Problem. The school reconsiders the same three garden plans. It still has a 408408408 dollar cap. Previously, each of the two edges parallel to the sidewalk cost 121212 dollars per meter and each of the two perpendicular edges cost 888 dollars per meter. An existing fence can supply one whole parallel edge of any proposal at no cost. The other rates, dimensions, and planting areas stay the same.

  • Plan A. 121212 m parallel by 666 m perpendicular. Its old cost was 384384384 dollars.
  • Plan B. 101010 m parallel by 101010 m perpendicular. Its old cost was 400400400 dollars.
  • Plan C. 121212 m parallel by 999 m perpendicular. Its old cost was 432432432 dollars.

Find the new costs and choose the feasible proposal with the most planting area. What can you conclude if the three plans are only a sample?

Solution. Each plan saves 121212 dollars for every meter of one parallel edge. The savings are 144144144 dollars for A and C and 120120120 dollars for B.

C now meets the budget. It is the best tested plan because its area is greater than the other two areas. Its area was already greatest of the three before the change. Using the existing fence made that area affordable.

If the list is a sample, the recommendation applies to these three plans. To claim a proven optimum, we would need to know the full range of layouts the site permits and show that none of them can do better.

Check your understanding

Four layouts are tested for a room. They all fit, and the fourth seats the most people. What extra fact would make the seating maximum a proven optimum from the table alone?

Answer choices

If the table includes every permitted layout, its largest feasible count is the maximum over that whole set. How the winner ranks on cost, floor area, or number of rows cannot establish that coverage.

Count a packing family

A layout family is a set of layouts that follow the same placement rules. Rules about rows, orientation, and clearance can turn a packing question into a count you can check completely.

Worked example 3 Pack tiles in two kinds of rows

Problem. A tray is 272727 cm wide and 191919 cm deep inside. Each rectangular tile is 888 cm by 555 cm. Use one layer with edges parallel to the tray edges. Each row runs across the tray width and has just one tile orientation. Rows may differ in orientation. Tiles can touch, and rows cannot overlap. What is the most tiles that fit under these rules?

Solution. A 555 cm deep row holds 333 tiles across. Three widths of 888 cm use 242424 cm, and a fourth would need 323232 cm. An 888 cm deep row holds 555 tiles across. Five widths of 555 cm use 252525 cm, and a sixth would need 303030 cm.

At most two 888 cm deep rows can fit in 191919 cm. For each possible number, fill the remaining depth with as many 555 cm deep rows as will fit.

Prove a minimum when dimensions can vary

When dimensions may be any positive real numbers that satisfy the constraints, a few test sizes can suggest a choice. To prove it is best, look for a bound that applies to every feasible size and check whether one feasible size reaches it.

Worked example 4 Find the least board for a divided box

Problem. A closed rectangular box with a lid needs 480 cm3480\text{ cm}^3480 cm3 of total inside volume and a height of 555 cm. It has a full-height divider spanning the width. The box width can be any positive length up to 999 cm, and there is no separate length limit. Ignore board thickness, overlaps, and cutting waste. Find the length and width that use the least board for the six outside faces and the divider. Prove the minimum and give the board area.

Check your understanding

A proof gives a lower bound of 432432432 square centimeters for a box design, attained only at width 888 cm. A revised rule allows widths up to 777 cm. What does that proof still establish?

Answer choices

The equality width is no longer permitted. The bound still applies to the remaining designs, but a different argument must identify the best one among them.

Revise the cost calculation

Material and cost can rank two designs differently. A supplier might charge for cutting as well as for the board itself. And if a cut wastes material, the paid-for area can exceed the area that ends up in the box.

Worked example 5 Price the board that gets thrown away

Problem. A shop can order either of two closed rectangular packages. It needs exactly 480 cm3480\text{ cm}^3480 cm3 of capacity, and both designs fit the shelf. Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Compare designs after checking constraints. A proposal with more area is unusable if its fencing exceeds the budget.
  • Check the scope of a best result. A sample can identify the best tested design. A complete list or a bound attained by a feasible design can prove an optimum.
  • Count paid-for material. Include waste and fees in the cost model. Use inside dimensions for the capacity requirement.

Key takeaways

  • Write the requirements, what can vary, and the quantity you want to maximize or minimize.
  • State which alternatives your calculation covers, especially when a packing rule limits the layout family.
  • When a price, material estimate, or site condition changes, check both feasibility and the objective again.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1785, the Continental Congress debated how to survey western land acquired from Indigenous nations. One draft called for square townships seven miles on a side. William Grayson moved to reduce the side to six miles, and James Monroe seconded him. The final ordinance set the side at six miles. A full township could be divided into thirty-six one-mile-square lots.

The approved plan told surveyors to run lines north and south and cross them at right angles. It also allowed surveyors to depart from the grid where the boundaries of land purchases from Indigenous nations made the grid impracticable. The same ordinance that set out the square grid told surveyors when they could depart from it. Congressional debate, volume 28, pages 327 to 328 and Land Ordinance of May 20, 1785

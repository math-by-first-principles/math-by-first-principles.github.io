Percent Increase and Decrease

Intuition

A jacket that was 808080 dollars is now 606060 dollars, a drop of 202020 dollars. A stock that was 444 dollars is now 555 dollars, a rise of only 111 dollar. Next to the jacket’s twenty-dollar drop, the stock’s one-dollar rise looks tiny. But compare each change with what it started from:

−2080=−25%,14=25%.\frac{-20}{80} = -25\%, \qquad \frac{1}{4} = 25\%.80−20​=−25%,41​=25%.

The jacket fell by 25%25\%25% and the stock rose by 25%25\%25%: equal in size, opposite in direction, even though their dollar changes are twenty times apart. Comparing a change to what you started with, and writing that comparison as a percent, is a percent change. It is how you tell whether a change is actually big or small.

Learning goals

  • Divide the change by the original, never by the new value
  • Build one multiplier for any increase or decrease
  • Match discounts to decreases and tax, tip and markup to increases
  • Recover the original by dividing by the multiplier
  • Apply two percent changes one after another, using the value each step actually starts from

Why we measure change against the original

Suppose a price moves from one amount to another. The plain amount of change is just the difference:

change=new value−original value.\text{change} = \text{new value} - \text{original value}.change=new value−original value.

If the new value is bigger, the change is positive and the quantity went up. If the new value is smaller, the change is negative and the quantity went down. That difference alone does not tell you how big a deal the change is, because the same difference can be huge or tiny depending on the size of the thing it happened to. A raise of 1,0001{,}0001,000 dollars is life-changing on a 2,0002{,}0002,000 dollar salary and barely noticeable on a 200,000200{,}000200,000 dollar salary.

So compare the change with a baseline: the original value, the amount you had before the change happened. Percent change is the change compared with that original, written as a percent, and it asks the same kind of question as before, what percent one number is of another, now applied to the change and the original:

percent change=changeoriginal value×100%.\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{change}}{\text{original value}} \times 100\%.percent change=original valuechange​×100%.

A positive result is a percent increase; a negative result is a percent decrease.

The original goes on the bottom because it is the amount the change is being measured against, the whole the change is a part of. Putting the new value on the bottom instead answers a different question. Doing that is the single most common error in this whole topic.

Worked example 1 A price rises from 404040 to 505050 dollars. Find the percent increase.

First find the amount of change by subtracting the original from the new value:

change=50−40=10.\text{change} = 50 - 40 = 10.change=50−40=10.

The price went up, so this is an increase. Now ask what percent 101010 is of the original 404040, by making the ratio of change to original and writing it over 100100100:

1040×100%=0.25×100%=25%.\frac{10}{40} \times 100\% = 0.25 \times 100\% = 25\%.4010​×100%=0.25×100%=25%.

The price rose by 25%25\%25%. Notice the 101010 is compared to the original 404040, not to the new 505050. If you divided by 505050 you would get 20%20\%20%, the answer to a different question.

Worked example 2 A class shrinks from 303030 to 242424 students. Find the percent decrease.

Find the change first, new value minus original:

change=24−30=−6.\text{change} = 24 - 30 = -6.change=24−30=−6.

The change is negative, so this is a decrease. Compare it to the original 303030:

−630×100%=−20%.\frac{-6}{30} \times 100\% = -20\%.30−6​×100%=−20%.

A negative percent change marks a decrease, so the class had a 20%20\%20% decrease.

Answer choices

First find the change, then compare it to the original 252525, not the new 303030.

The amount of change is 30−25=530 - 25 = 530−25=5. Now ask what percent 555 is of the original 252525:

(The original must not be 000: dividing by 000 is undefined, so a quantity that starts at 000 has no percent change.)

Finding the new value with a multiplier

Often you know the original amount and the percent change, and you want the new value. There are two ways to get it, and they always agree.

The two-step way matches how you would explain it in words. To increase 200200200 by 15%15\%15%, first find 15%15\%15% of the original, then add it on:

15% of 200=0.15×200=30,new value=200+30=230.15\% \text{ of } 200 = 0.15 \times 200 = 30, \qquad \text{new value} = 200 + 30 = 230.15% of 200=0.15×200=30,new value=200+30=230.

For a decrease you subtract instead. To decrease 200200200 by 15%15\%15%, find the same 303030 and take it away: 200−30=170200 - 30 = 170200−30=170.

The one-step way does it with a single multiplication.

Why a p%p\%p% increase multiplies the original by 1+p1001 + \frac{p}{100}1+100p​#

Take the 15%15\%15% increase on 200200200 from a moment ago. The two-step route found 15%15\%15% of 200200200, which is 303030, and added it on:

200+0.15×200=200+30=230.200 + 0.15 \times 200 = 200 + 30 = 230.200+0.15×200=200+30=230.

Both terms on the left include a copy of 200200200: the first term is the whole 200200200, which is 1×2001 \times 2001×200 (the original is 100%100\%100% of itself), and the second term is 15100×200\frac{15}{100} \times 20010015​×200. Write the shared 200200200 once, and add the two numbers that multiply it:

(1+15100)×200=1.15×200=230.\left(1 + \frac{15}{100}\right) \times 200 = 1.15 \times 200 = 230.(1+10015​)×200=1.15×200=230.

Nothing in that argument used anything special about 151515 or 200200200. Increasing any original by any p%p\%p% means keeping the whole original (the 111) and adding on p%p\%p% of it (the p100\frac{p}{100}100p​), so the multiplier is always 1+p1001 + \frac{p}{100}1+100p​. A decrease runs the identical argument with a subtraction: you keep 100%100\%100% and remove p%p\%p%, giving 1−p1001 - \frac{p}{100}1−100p​.

∎

So to grow an amount by p%p\%p%, multiply by 1+p1001 + \frac{p}{100}1+100p​; to shrink it by p%p\%p%, multiply by 1−p1001 - \frac{p}{100}1−100p​. This number is called the multiplier. A 15%15\%15% increase leaves you with 115%115\%115% of the original, which is why its multiplier is 1.151.151.15. A 15%15\%15% decrease leaves you with 85%85\%85%, so its multiplier is 0.850.850.85.

Worked example 3 Increase 250250250 by 8%8\%8% two ways

Two-step way. Find 8%8\%8% of the original, then add it on. Since 8%=0.088\% = 0.088%=0.08,

0.08×250=20,250+20=270.0.08 \times 250 = 20, \qquad 250 + 20 = 270.0.08×250=20,250+20=270.

One-step way. An 8%8\%8% increase keeps the original 100%100\%100% and adds 8%8\%8%, so the multiplier is 1+8100=1.081 + \frac{8}{100} = 1.081+1008​=1.08:

1.08×250=270.1.08 \times 250 = 270.1.08×250=270.

Both routes give 270270270, because the multiplier is just the two-step calculation packed into one number.

1−30100=1−0.30=0.701 - \frac{30}{100} = 1 - 0.30 = 0.701−10030​=1−0.30=0.70

So the multiplier is 0.700.700.70. Multiplying by 0.300.300.30 would give only the part removed, not the part that remains.

Discounts, tax, tip, and markup

These multipliers are exactly the arithmetic behind everyday money problems. A discount (a sale) is a percent decrease: ”25%25\%25% off” means you pay 100%−25%=75%100\% - 25\% = 75\%100%−25%=75% of the original price, a multiplier of 0.750.750.75. Sales tax, a tip, and a markup are percent increases added on top of a price: a 7%7\%7% tax means you pay 107%107\%107% of the listed price, a multiplier of 1.071.071.07, and a 40%40\%40% markup on cost means the selling price is 1.401.401.40 times the cost. Decide whether the percent is added or removed, build the matching multiplier, and multiply.

Worked example 4 A 606060 dollar backpack is 35%35\%35% off. Find the sale price.

A discount is a percent decrease, so you pay the part of the price that is left after the discount. A 35%35\%35% discount leaves 100%−35%=65%100\% - 35\% = 65\%100%−35%=65% of the original, so the multiplier is 1−0.35=0.651 - 0.35 = 0.651−0.35=0.65:

0.65×60=39.0.65 \times 60 = 39.0.65×60=39.

The sale price is 393939 dollars. As a check by the two-step route, 35%35\%35% of 606060 is 0.35×60=210.35 \times 60 = 210.35×60=21 off, and 60−21=3960 - 21 = 3960−21=39, the same price.

The grid below is the original price, split into 100100100 squares. Shade only the squares a shopper still pays for. Shade 656565 and the readout gives 0.650.650.65, the multiplier from the backpack above; try 707070 for a 30%30\%30% discount and 909090 for a 10%10\%10% discount. The number named in a sale is the part that leaves; the number you multiply by is the part that stays, so a 30%30\%30% discount multiplies by 0.700.700.70, never by 0.300.300.30.

Check your understanding

A restaurant adds an 8%8\%8% tip to a bill. Is this a percent increase or a percent decrease, and what is the multiplier?

Answer choices

A tip adds money on top of the bill, so it is a percent increase, the same direction as tax and a markup. You keep the whole bill (100%) and add 8%8\%8% more:

1+8100=1.081 + \frac{8}{100} = 1.081+1008​=1.08

So the multiplier is 1.081.081.08. A discount, by contrast, is a decrease with a multiplier below 111, like 0.920.920.92 for an 8%8\%8% discount.

Worked example 5 A 454545 dollar meal has 6%6\%6% tax and a 20%20\%20% tip, both on the menu price.

When the tax and the tip are each figured on the original menu price, you can add their percents because they share the same base. The bill is the menu price plus 6%6\%6% of it plus 20%20\%20% of it, which is 100%+6%+20%=126%100\% + 6\% + 20\% = 126\%100%+6%+20%=126% of the menu price. The multiplier is 1.261.261.26:

1.26×45=56.70.1.26 \times 45 = 56.70.1.26×45=56.70.

The total is 56.7056.7056.70 dollars. Step by step this is 6%6\%6% of 45=2.7045 = 2.7045=2.70 in tax and 20%20\%20% of 45=9.0045 = 9.0045=9.00 in tip, and 45+2.70+9.00=56.7045 + 2.70 + 9.00 = 56.7045+2.70+9.00=56.70, matching the multiplier. The percents could be combined only because both were taken of the same menu price. If the tip were figured on the after-tax total instead, you could not just add 666 and 202020.

Two changes in a row

Worked Example 5 could add the tax and tip together only because both were figured on the same menu price. Often a second percent is figured on the result of the first change instead, and then the percents cannot simply be added.

Worked example 6 The same 454545 dollar meal, but the tip is figured on the total after tax

Now suppose the 20%20\%20% tip is taken of the bill after the 6%6\%6% tax has already been added, not of the original menu price. The two percents no longer share a base, so apply each multiplier in turn, to whatever amount you have at that point.

First add the tax to the menu price:

1.06×45=47.70.1.06 \times 45 = 47.70.1.06×45=47.70.

That 47.7047.7047.70 is the amount the tip is taken of, so apply the tip’s multiplier to it:

1.20×47.70=57.24.1.20 \times 47.70 = 57.24.1.20×47.70=57.24.

The total is 57.2457.2457.24 dollars, a little more than the 56.7056.7056.70 from Worked Example 5, where both percents shared the menu price as their base. One multiplication can do both steps at once, since multiplying by 1.061.061.06 and then by 1.201.201.20 is the same as multiplying once by their product:

45×(1.06×1.20)=45×1.272=57.24.45 \times (1.06 \times 1.20) = 45 \times 1.272 = 57.24.45×(1.06×1.20)=45×1.272=57.24.

Whenever a second percent applies to a moving amount rather than a fixed one, apply each multiplier to the value that came before it, not to the original both times. Applying multiplier m1m_1m1​ and then m2m_2m2​ is the same as multiplying once by their product m1×m2m_1 \times m_2m1​×m2​, and multiplication can be done in either order, so it makes no difference which of the two changes you apply first, only which amount each one is based on.

Worked example 7 Increase 100100100 by 20%20\%20%, then decrease the result by 20%20\%20%

A 20%20\%20% increase multiplies by 1.201.201.20, and a 20%20\%20% decrease multiplies by 0.800.800.80. Apply them in turn:

100×1.20=120,120×0.80=96.100 \times 1.20 = 120, \qquad 120 \times 0.80 = 96.100×1.20=120,120×0.80=96.

The rise added 202020 to reach 120120120; the fall then removed 242424 to reach 969696. Equal percents, unequal dollar amounts, because the fall’s 20%20\%20% is measured against the bigger 120120120, not the original 100100100. One multiplication shows why the changes never cancel, since applying both multipliers in a row is the same as multiplying by their product:

1.20×0.80=0.96.1.20 \times 0.80 = 0.96.1.20×0.80=0.96.

A rise and a fall of the same percent always combine to a multiplier less than 111, so you never land back where you started.

Check your understanding

A price of 505050 dollars is increased by 10%10\%10%, and then the new price is decreased by 10%10\%10%. What is the final price?

Answer choices

A 10%10\%10% increase multiplies by 1.101.101.10 and a 10%10\%10% decrease multiplies by 0.900.900.90. Apply both, each to the amount at that point:

50×1.10=55,55×0.90=49.5050 \times 1.10 = 55, \qquad 55 \times 0.90 = 49.5050×1.10=55,55×0.90=49.50

The combined multiplier is 1.10×0.90=0.991.10 \times 0.90 = 0.991.10×0.90=0.99, less than 111, so the final price is 49.5049.5049.50, not the original 505050. Stopping after only the increase gives 555555; stopping after only the decrease gives 454545.

An 808080 dollar jacket is discounted 25%25\%25%, and then a coupon takes another 10%10\%10% off the sale price. What is the final price?

First take 25%25\%25% off 808080:

Then take 10%10\%10% off the new price of 606060:

Finding the original from the new value

Say a 20%20\%20% discount leaves an item priced at 404040 dollars. The discount left 100%−20%=80%100\% - 20\% = 80\%100%−20%=80% of the original, so the 404040 you paid is 80%80\%80% of the original price:

40=0.80×original.40 = 0.80 \times \text{original}.40=0.80×original.

You know a part (80%80\%80% of the original) and want the whole, so divide the part by that decimal to recover it:

original=400.80=50.\text{original} = \frac{40}{0.80} = 50.original=0.8040​=50.

The original price was 505050 dollars. This is the general pattern whenever you know a new value and the percent that produced it: read off what percent of the original the new value is, then undo the multiplication with division, instead of multiplying to find a part. The reason you divide instead of adding the percent back is the trap in Worked Example 8 below: 20%20\%20% of the sale price is a smaller amount than 20%20\%20% of the original, so adding 20%20\%20% of the sale price back on cannot recover it. The same logic handles an increase: a value that is the result of a 25%25\%25% increase is 125%125\%125% of the original, so you divide by 1.251.251.25 to get back to it.

Worked example 8 After a 30%30\%30% discount, a coat costs 636363 dollars. Find the original price.

A 30%30\%30% discount leaves 100%−30%=70%100\% - 30\% = 70\%100%−30%=70% of the original, so the sale price is 70%70\%70% of the original price:

63=0.70×original.63 = 0.70 \times \text{original}.63=0.70×original.

The 636363 is a known part (70%70\%70%) of the whole you want, so divide the part by 0.700.700.70 to recover the whole:

original=630.70=90.\text{original} = \frac{63}{0.70} = 90.original=0.7063​=90.

The original price was 909090 dollars. Check it forward: 30%30\%30% of 909090 is 272727 off, and 90−27=6390 - 27 = 6390−27=63, the sale price. A tempting wrong move is to take 30%30\%30% of 636363 and add it back, but 30%30\%30% of 636363 is only 18.9018.9018.90, and 63+18.90=81.9063 + 18.90 = 81.9063+18.90=81.90, not 909090. That fails because the discount was 30%30\%30% of the larger original, not 30%30\%30% of the smaller sale price.

(A 100%100\%100% decrease has a multiplier of 000, and dividing by 000 is undefined, so no division can recover an original from a value that was reduced all the way to 000.)

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Dividing the change by the new value. Percent change always compares the change to the original: changeoriginal×100%\frac{\text{change}}{\text{original}} \times 100\%originalchange​×100%. For a rise from 404040 to 505050, the increase is 1040=25%\frac{10}{40} = 25\%4010​=25%, not 1050=20%\frac{10}{50} = 20\%5010​=20%.
  • Thinking a p%p\%p% rise then a p%p\%p% fall returns to the start. It does not: the two multipliers are 1+p1001 + \frac{p}{100}1+100p​ and 1−p1001 - \frac{p}{100}1−100p​, and their product is always less than 111. A 20%20\%20% rise then a 20%20\%20% fall leaves you at 96%96\%96% of where you started, not 100%100\%100%.
  • Adding percents that sit on different bases. Percents only add when they are taken of the same whole. A 10%10\%10% raise followed by another 10%10\%10% raise is not a 20%20\%20% raise, because the second 10%10\%10% is figured on the already-raised salary. On 100100100 it gives 1.10×1.10×100=1211.10 \times 1.10 \times 100 = 1211.10×1.10×100=121, a 21%21\%21% rise, not 20%20\%20%.
  • Recovering the original by adding the percent back to the new value. After 20%20\%20% off, the original is the sale price divided by 0.800.800.80, not the sale price plus 20%20\%20% of itself. Those differ because 20%20\%20% of the sale price is smaller than 20%20\%20% of the original.
  • Mixing up the percent removed with the multiplier. A 30%30\%30% decrease multiplies by 0.700.700.70, the part you keep, not by 0.300.300.30, the part you lose. Always build the multiplier from what remains.

Key takeaways

  • Percent change =changeoriginal×100%= \dfrac{\text{change}}{\text{original}} \times 100\%=originalchange​×100%, where change=new−original\text{change} = \text{new} - \text{original}change=new−original. A positive result is an increase, a negative one a decrease, and the original is always the baseline on the bottom.
  • To find the new value after a p%p\%p% change, multiply the original by the multiplier: 1+p1001 + \frac{p}{100}1+100p​ for an increase, 1−p1001 - \frac{p}{100}1−100p​ for a decrease.
  • Discounts are decreases (pay 100%−p%100\% - p\%100%−p%), while tax, tip, and markup are increases (pay 100%+p%100\% + p\%100%+p%); build the matching multiplier and multiply.
  • When two percents are taken of the same amount, you can add them. When the second is taken of the result of the first, apply the multipliers one after another; the combined multiplier is their product, in either order. That is why a p%p\%p% rise then a p%p\%p% fall does not return to the start.
  • To find the original from a new value, divide by the multiplier that produced it (after 20%20\%20% off, divide the sale price by 0.800.800.80). The multiplier must not be zero, which rules out a 100%100\%100% decrease.

A bit of history (optional)

Some of the oldest homework we have asks about a loan. Clay tablets from Babylon, written nearly four thousand years ago, work out how a debt grows month after month. A common loan charged one part in sixty of the amount borrowed, every month, for as long as the loan ran. Twelve months of that rate comes to twelve parts in sixty, which we would now call twenty percent a year.

Notice what that rate was measured against: the amount borrowed at the start, not the larger amount owed later once months of interest had built up. That is the same rule this whole lesson turns on. A percent means nothing until you say what it is a percent of.

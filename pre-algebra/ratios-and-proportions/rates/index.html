Rates and Unit Rates

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A car covers 120120120 miles in 222 hours. A store sells 333 pounds of apples for 666 dollars. A printer runs off 404040 pages in 555 minutes. Each of these compares two amounts that are measured in different units: miles against hours, dollars against pounds, pages against minutes. That kind of comparison is called a rate. Asking “how much per one” gives a unit rate: miles for one hour or dollars for one pound. A unit rate makes deals comparable and lets you scale amounts.

Learning goals

  • Tell a rate from a ratio by the units it compares
  • Divide to find a unit rate, the amount for one unit, and explain why dividing does that
  • Compare two deals by reducing both to the same unit rate
  • Scale a unit rate up to find a total
  • Restate a rate over a longer or shorter stretch by scaling both quantities

What makes a rate a rate

A rate is a ratio that compares two quantities measured in different units. The ratio of 222 cups of flour to 333 cups of sugar from the last lesson compares two amounts measured in the very same unit: both are cups. The common unit cancels in the comparison, so we can write 2:32:32:3, while keeping the flour-to-sugar order clear. A rate compares different units, so those units remain:

120 miles2 hours,6 dollars3 pounds,40 pages5 minutes.\begin{gathered} \frac{120\text{ miles}}{2\text{ hours}},\qquad\frac{6\text{ dollars}}{3\text{ pounds}},\\ \frac{40\text{ pages}}{5\text{ minutes}}. \end{gathered}2 hours120 miles​,3 pounds6 dollars​,5 minutes40 pages​.​

Keep the units attached, in the requested order. “Miles per hour” is miles on top and hours on the bottom; “dollars per pound” is dollars on top and pounds on the bottom. The little word per is the signpost: it means “for each” and marks the quantity that goes in the denominator. Read the bar as “per,” so 120 miles2 hours\frac{120 \text{ miles}}{2 \text{ hours}}2 hours120 miles​ is ”120120120 miles per 222 hours.”

Answer choices

A rate compares quantities measured in different units. Laps and minutes are different units, so 333 laps in 999 minutes is a rate. Each other comparison uses the same unit on both sides: marbles, liters, or meters.

The unit rate

A rate written with a denominator of 111 is called a unit rate: the amount for a single unit of the second quantity. ”606060 miles per hour” is a unit rate because the hour count is 111. ”222 dollars per pound” is a unit rate because the pound count is 111. You will see unit rates everywhere, on speedometers, price tags, and nutrition labels. Unit rates appear there because one number that means “per one” is the easiest kind of rate to compare and to scale.

One division turns a rate into its unit rate. The car above traveled 120120120 miles in 222 hours, so

120 miles2 hours=(120÷2) miles per hour,=60 miles per hour.\begin{aligned} \frac{120\text{ miles}}{2\text{ hours}}&=(120\div2)\text{ miles per hour},\\ &=60\text{ miles per hour}. \end{aligned}2 hours120 miles​​=(120÷2) miles per hour,=60 miles per hour.​

Suppose the car holds a steady speed. Those 222 hours then share the 120120120 miles equally, and each hour’s share is 606060 miles. To turn any rate into a unit rate, divide the first quantity by the second.

Why dividing gives the amount per one

For a steady rate with a whole-number second quantity, dividing does exactly what the words “amount per one” ask: it splits a total into equal shares, one share for each unit. That picture is not the whole reason division works, though, since the second quantity is not always a whole number of shares. The real reason is scaling.

Why dividing the two quantities gives the amount per single unit#

Suppose a machine makes 848484 labels in 777 minutes at a steady rate. Divide both quantities by 777:

84 labels7 minutes=12 labels1 minute.\frac{84\text{ labels}}{7\text{ minutes}} =\frac{12\text{ labels}}{1\text{ minute}}.7 minutes84 labels​=1 minute12 labels​.

The comparison is unchanged because dividing both parts of a ratio by the same positive number gives an equivalent ratio. The second quantity is now one minute. Nothing depended on 848484 or 777: for numerical amounts aaa and bbb in their stated units, with b>0b>0b>0, divide both by bbb. The second amount becomes b÷b=1b\div b=1b÷b=1, while the first becomes a÷ba\div ba÷b. That is why dividing the first amount by the second gives the amount per single unit.

The scale factor can also enlarge an amount. A distance of 444 miles in 0.50.50.5 hour becomes

4 miles0.5 hour=8 miles1 hour,\frac{4\text{ miles}}{0.5\text{ hour}} =\frac{8\text{ miles}}{1\text{ hour}},0.5 hour4 miles​=1 hour8 miles​,

because dividing both numerical amounts by 0.50.50.5 doubles them. The same scaling argument works even when the second amount is not a whole number.

∎

If a car speeds up and slows down, total distance divided by total time gives its average rate. The car can cover different distances in individual hours. A steady rate describes every equal interval; an average describes the trip as a whole.

Worked example 1 Find the unit rate: 404040 pages in 555 minutes

The rate is 404040 pages for every 555 minutes, and “pages per minute” means pages for a single minute. Divide the pages by the minutes:

40 pages5 minutes=(40÷5) pages per minute,=8 pages per minute.\begin{aligned} \frac{40\text{ pages}}{5\text{ minutes}}&=(40\div5)\text{ pages per minute},\\ &=8\text{ pages per minute}. \end{aligned}5 minutes40 pages​​=(40÷5) pages per minute,=8 pages per minute.​

So the printer runs at 888 pages per minute. Keep the units attached to the answer. The number 888 standing on its own does not say whether it means pages per minute or minutes per page.

15÷2.5=6,2.5÷2.5=1.15\div2.5=6,\qquad2.5\div2.5=1.15÷2.5=6,2.5÷2.5=1.

Unit rates can use decimals

The two quantities will not always divide evenly, and that is fine. The quotient is then just a decimal, exactly the kind you practiced in the Decimals chapter. If 333 pounds of grapes cost 777 dollars, the price per pound is

7 dollars3 pounds=(7÷3) dollars per pound,=2.333… dollars per pound.\begin{aligned} \frac{7\text{ dollars}}{3\text{ pounds}}&=(7\div3)\text{ dollars per pound},\\ &=2.333\ldots\text{ dollars per pound}. \end{aligned}3 pounds7 dollars​​=(7÷3) dollars per pound,=2.333… dollars per pound.​

The 333s keep repeating forever, so round to a sensible number of places for the situation. For money, two decimal places (the nearest cent) is standard, so about 2.332.332.33 dollars per pound.

Worked example 2 A unit rate that is a decimal

A cyclist rides 171717 miles in 222 hours. Find the speed in miles per hour.

Speed in miles per hour is miles for a single hour, so divide the miles by the hours:

17 miles2 hours=(17÷2) miles per hour,=8.5 miles per hour.\begin{aligned} \frac{17\text{ miles}}{2\text{ hours}}&=(17\div2)\text{ miles per hour},\\ &=8.5\text{ miles per hour}. \end{aligned}2 hours17 miles​​=(17÷2) miles per hour,=8.5 miles per hour.​

The division comes out to the exact decimal 8.58.58.5, so the cyclist averages 8.58.58.5 miles per hour. A unit rate does not have to be a whole number; it just has to be the amount for one unit.

Comparing rates: the better deal

Two deals are hard to compare when they use different amounts. But once you reduce each deal to “per one”, the two deals line up on the same scale and the comparison is immediate. This is exactly how unit pricing works in a grocery store.

Worked example 3 Which is the better buy?

Two boxes of the same cereal come in different sizes: a 121212-ounce box costs 444 dollars, and a 202020-ounce box costs 666 dollars. Which box is the better buy per ounce?

Find the price per ounce for each box by dividing dollars by ounces. The small box:

4 dollars12 ounces≈0.33 dollars per ounce.\frac{4 \text{ dollars}}{12 \text{ ounces}} \approx 0.33 \text{ dollars per ounce}.12 ounces4 dollars​≈0.33 dollars per ounce.

The large box:

6 dollars20 ounces=0.30 dollars per ounce.\frac{6 \text{ dollars}}{20 \text{ ounces}} = 0.30 \text{ dollars per ounce}.20 ounces6 dollars​=0.30 dollars per ounce.

Now both prices are on the same footing, dollars for a single ounce. Since 0.30<0.330.30 < 0.330.30<0.33, the large box costs less per ounce, so the 202020-ounce box is the better buy. Comparing the sticker prices (444 dollars against 666 dollars) would have been misleading, because the boxes hold different amounts.

To compare two options, find the unit rate for each using the same units in the same order, then read off which is better. For a price, the lower dollars-per-item is the better buy; for a speed, the higher miles-per-hour is faster. The direction depends on what you want more or less of.

Scaling a rate to a new amount

At a steady rate, the unit rate predicts totals. Multiply the amount per one unit by the number of units you want. An average alone does not promise the same rate in a new interval. This is the same scaling you used for ratios: a unit rate is the rate scaled to a denominator of 111. Multiplying both quantities by the same positive factor gives an equivalent rate for that many units.

Worked example 4 Use a unit rate to find a total

A car travels at a steady 555555 miles per hour. How far does it go in 444 hours?

The unit rate 555555 miles per hour means 555555 miles for each single hour. For 444 hours, scale that up by multiplying by 444:

55 miles per hour×4 hours=220 miles.55 \text{ miles per hour} \times 4 \text{ hours} = 220 \text{ miles}.55 miles per hour×4 hours=220 miles.

Four hours is four groups of 555555 miles, so the car covers 220220220 miles.

Reading a rate the other way round

If 181818 identical beads weigh 666 grams, then 18÷6=318\div6=318÷6=3 beads fit in each gram. Reading the comparison the other way gives 6÷18=136\div18=\frac136÷18=31​ gram per bead. The numbers 333 and 13\frac1331​ are reciprocals, and their units reverse too. Both descriptions are useful; choose the one the question asks for. Taking a reciprocal requires a nonzero rate.

At a steady 3.53.53.5 liters per minute, how long does pumping 424242 liters take? Each minute accounts for 3.53.53.5 liters, so count how many such amounts fit in 424242. Dividing by 3.53.53.5 is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal. Since 3.5=723.5=\frac723.5=27​, the reciprocal rate is 13.5=27\frac1{3.5}=\frac273.51​=72​ minute per liter:

42÷3.5=42×27=847=12.42\div3.5=42\times\frac27=\frac{84}{7}=12.42÷3.5=42×72​=784​=12.

The pump takes 121212 minutes. Check by multiplication: 12×3.5=4212\times3.5=4212×3.5=42 liters. In general, divide the total by the positive unit rate to find the missing number of units. A zero rate and zero total cannot tell you how much time passed.

Converting between rates

Sometimes a rate is measured over one stretch of time and you want it over a longer or shorter stretch. Turning gallons per second into gallons per minute is one example. You convert by scaling, using the same equivalent-ratio move from the last lesson. Multiply or divide both quantities by the same number so the rate stays equal. Choose the number that resizes the bottom quantity to the stretch you want. That stretch may then have a shorter name: 606060 seconds is 111 minute.

Worked example 5 Convert a rate to new units

A water tank drains 333 gallons every 303030 seconds. Express this rate in gallons per minute.

One minute is 606060 seconds, and 606060 is twice 303030, so scale the time from 303030 seconds up to 606060 seconds by multiplying by 222. To keep the rate equal, multiply both quantities by 222:

3 gallons30 seconds=3×2 gallons30×2 seconds=6 gallons60 seconds.\begin{aligned} \frac{3 \text{ gallons}}{30 \text{ seconds}} &= \frac{3 \times 2 \text{ gallons}}{30 \times 2 \text{ seconds}}\\ &= \frac{6 \text{ gallons}}{60 \text{ seconds}}. \end{aligned}30 seconds3 gallons​​=30×2 seconds3×2 gallons​=60 seconds6 gallons​.​

Since 606060 seconds is exactly 111 minute, the tank drains 666 gallons per minute.

Scaling can shrink an interval too, using the same move in reverse. If that same tank keeps draining at this steady rate, the 666 gallons every 606060 seconds can be restated over a 151515-second window. Since 606060 seconds holds 444 windows of 151515 seconds, divide both quantities by 444:

6 gallons60 seconds=6÷4 gallons60÷4 seconds=1.5 gallons15 seconds.\begin{aligned} \frac{6 \text{ gallons}}{60 \text{ seconds}} &= \frac{6 \div 4 \text{ gallons}}{60 \div 4 \text{ seconds}}\\ &= \frac{1.5 \text{ gallons}}{15 \text{ seconds}}. \end{aligned}60 seconds6 gallons​​=60÷4 seconds6÷4 gallons​=15 seconds1.5 gallons​.​

So at that steady rate, the tank drains 1.51.51.5 gallons every 151515 seconds, the same rate seen over a shorter stretch. If the drain instead sped up or slowed down along the way, 1.51.51.5 gallons per 151515 seconds would still be the correct average, just not the exact amount in every single window.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Dividing the wrong way. “Miles per hour” puts miles on top and divides by hours, giving 120÷2=60120 \div 2 = 60120÷2=60. Dividing hours by miles (2÷1202 \div 1202÷120) answers a different question, hours per mile, not miles per hour. Let the word after “per” tell you the denominator.
  • Dropping the units. A rate without its units is ambiguous. "888" could be 888 pages per minute or 888 minutes per page. Always write the units, especially on the final answer.
  • Comparing totals instead of unit rates. A 666-dollar box is not automatically a worse deal than a 444-dollar box; it may hold far more. Reduce each option to a unit rate (price per ounce) before deciding.
  • Scaling only one quantity when converting. To restate a rate over a different stretch you multiply or divide both the top and bottom by the same number. Turning 333 gallons per 303030 seconds into per-minute means doubling both to 666 gallons per 606060 seconds, not just one of them.
  • Comparing in different units. Before you compare two rates, make sure both use the same units in the same order. Dollars per pound cannot be compared with dollars per ounce until one is converted.

Key takeaways

  • A rate is a ratio comparing two quantities in different units, like miles per hour or dollars per pound. In a rate the units stay attached, and the word per marks the denominator.
  • A unit rate has a denominator of 111 unit. Find it by dividing the first quantity by the second, because dividing both quantities by that same number scales the second quantity to 111.
  • A unit rate may be a decimal (for example 17÷2=8.517 \div 2 = 8.517÷2=8.5 miles per hour); round sensibly for the situation.
  • Compare deals by reducing each to a unit rate in the same units, then pick the better one (lower price per item, higher speed).
  • At a steady rate, multiply the unit rate by the number of units to find a total. Divide a total by a positive unit rate to recover the number of units. Reversing a nonzero rate takes its reciprocal and reverses its units. To restate a rate over a longer or shorter stretch of time, multiply or divide both of its quantities by the same positive number.

A bit of history (optional)

Sailors needed a way to measure a ship’s speed relative to the water. A log and line supplied a portable reference: a weighted wooden board went over the stern, and its drag kept it nearly in place while the ship sailed away. A knotted rope ran out behind the vessel.

One sailor counted the knots passing while another timed the interval with a sandglass. The knot spacing and the glass’s duration were chosen together, so the count translated into nautical miles per hour. The instrument had built the unit conversion into its design.

William Bourne described this method in 1574. A log, line and reel made in 1961, now in the Royal Museums Greenwich collection, is described with a 28-second glass; other versions used different timing. The method left a word behind: a ship’s speed is still measured in knots, meaning nautical miles per hour.

Sources: Royal Museums Greenwich’s log, line and reel; Knots: measuring speed at sea.

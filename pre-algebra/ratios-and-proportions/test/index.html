Where you stand

  • Read and simplify a ratio, scale both parts by one factor, tell a part-to-part comparison from a part-to-whole fraction, and split a total in a given ratio. Review: Ratios
  • Build a unit rate by dividing the first quantity by the second, keep its units, compare two offers with it, and scale it to a total or to new units. Review: Rates and Unit Rates
  • Decide whether two ratios are in proportion, and find a missing fourth term, keeping both sides in the same order and dividing by the number diagonally opposite the blank. Review: Proportions
  • Carry out the three percent tasks on a fixed whole: the part from a percent, the percent from a part and a whole, and the whole from a part and its percent. Review: Percent
  • Measure a change against the original, build the multiplier for an increase or a decrease, apply it, and divide by it to recover the amount that came before. Review: Percent Increase and Decrease
  • Move a value between percent, fraction and decimal form, treating the percent sign as an instruction to divide by one hundred. Review: Percent
  • Track which amount each percent is taken of: percents add into one figure only on a shared base, and a rise and a fall of the same percent do not cancel. Review: Percent Increase and Decrease

    A 110110110 cm plank is cut into two pieces, and the ratio of the shorter piece's length to the longer piece's length is 3:83:83:8. The longer piece is fixed to a wall with 333 screws for every 202020 cm of its length. How many screws does the longer piece need?

      Find the length of the longer piece before counting its screws.

    2. Hint 2

      Keep screws and length in the same order in both comparisons.

    Answer

    121212 screws.

    Full solution

    The cut divides the plank into eleven ratio parts.

    Each part measures

    110÷11=10110\div11=10110÷11=10

    cm, so the longer piece measures

    8×10=808\times10=808×10=80

    cm.

    Screws over length in cm is the same for the stated rule and for this piece.

    320=s80\frac{3}{20}=\frac{s}{80}203​=80s​

    The length 808080 is four times 202020, so the screw count is four times 333.

    s=3×4s=3\times4s=3×4 s=12s=12s=12

  7. Problem 7 Plumbing bill

    A plumber charges 150 dollars for 2.52.52.5 hours of work, at a steady hourly rate. A job takes 444 hours. The bill then adds 9%9\%9% tax, with no other charges. What is the total bill?

    1. Hint 1

      The charge for the job depends on how much one hour of work costs.

    2. Hint 2

      Price the four hours of work first, then apply the multiplier for the tax.

    Answer

    261.60 dollars.

    Full solution

    In dollars per hour, the plumber's rate is

    150÷2.5=60150\div2.5=60150÷2.5=60

    Four hours of work then cost, in dollars,

    60×4=24060\times4=24060×4=240

    Tax adds nine percent of that charge, so the multiplier is 1.091.091.09.

    1.09×240=261.601.09\times240=261.601.09×240=261.60

    The total bill is 261.60 dollars.

    As a check, nine percent of 240 dollars is 21.60 dollars, and

    240+21.60=261.60.240+21.60=261.60.240+21.60=261.60.

    Answer

    261.60 dollars.

    Key idea

    Scale the unit rate to the whole job first, then apply the tax multiplier to that charge.

  8. Problem 8 Marked rope

    A rope is marked in two colors with no overlap: 999 meters are blue and 151515 meters are red. The remaining 363636 meters are unmarked. Len says the marked parts make up about 66.7%66.7\%66.7% of the rope. Is Len right? Justify your answer.

    1. Hint 1

      Decide which length Len has used as the whole.

    2. Hint 2

      Compare the marked length with the whole length and write the result over one hundred.

    Answer

    No; the marked parts are 40%40\%40% of the rope.

    Full solution

    The marked length, in meters, is the blue and red parts together.

    9+15=249+15=249+15=24

    The whole rope, in meters, is the marked and unmarked lengths together.

    24+36=6024+36=6024+36=60

    The marked share of the whole rope is

    24÷60=0.4024\div60=0.4024÷60=0.40

    which is 40%40\%40%, so Len is not right.

    As a check, 40%40\%40% of 606060 meters is 0.40×60=240.40\times60=240.40×60=24 meters.

    Len's figure comes from dividing by the unmarked length.

    24÷36≈0.66724\div36\approx0.66724÷36≈0.667

    The unmarked 363636 meters is only one part of the rope, not the whole rope, so his figure compares one part with another part.

    No; the marked parts are 40%40\%40% of the rope.

    Key idea

    A percent of a whole is found by dividing by that whole, not by another part.

  9. Problem 9 Budget revision

    A council sets aside 12%12\%12% of its budget for a project and 8%8\%8% for emergencies. It then increases the project's money by 25%25\%25%, taking the extra from the rest of the budget. The emergency money and the total budget stay fixed. A member says the two now use 12%+8%+25%=45%12\%+8\%+25\%=45\%12%+8%+25%=45% of the budget. Is the member right? What percent do they now use together?

    1. Hint 1

      Ask what amount the 25%25\%25% is taken of, and compare it with the amount the other two percents are taken of.

    2. Hint 2

      Update the project share before adding the unchanged emergency share.

    Answer

    No; together they use 23%23\%23% of the budget.

    Full solution

    The 25%25\%25% is taken of the project's money, not of the whole budget.

    The project's money grows by a factor of 1.251.251.25, so its share of the fixed budget becomes

    1.25×12%=15%1.25\times12\%=15\%1.25×12%=15%

    The emergency share stays at eight percent.

    Both shares are now percents of the same total budget, so they add to

    15%+8%=23%15\%+8\%=23\%15%+8%=23%

    The member is wrong: that sum added a percent of the project's money to percents of the whole budget.

    As a check, on a budget of 1000 dollars the project's 120 dollars grows to 150 dollars, and with the 80 dollars for emergencies that makes 230 dollars, which is 23%23\%23% of the budget.

    Answer

    No; together they use 23%23\%23% of the budget.

    Key idea

    Update a changing amount on its own base before combining its share with other shares of a fixed total.

  10. Problem 10 Two orchards

    Orchard B has more trees than Orchard A. In Orchard A, 40%40\%40% of the trees are in bloom, and in Orchard B, 30%30\%30% are. Ravi says that exactly 35%35\%35% of all the trees in the two orchards are in bloom, halfway between 40%40\%40% and 30%30\%30%. Is Ravi right? Explain.

    1. Hint 1

      The two percents are taken of orchards of different sizes.

    2. Hint 2

      Compare each orchard's percent with 35%35\%35%: which orchard is above that mark, which is below, and by how much of its own trees?

    3. Hint 3

      Five percent of which orchard is the larger number of trees?

    Answer

    No; fewer than 35%35\%35% of all the trees are in bloom.

    Full solution

    Compared with 35%35\%35% of its trees, Orchard A has an extra amount equal to 5%5\%5% of its trees in bloom, and Orchard B is short by an amount equal to 5%5\%5% of its trees.

    Thirty-five percent of all the trees is 353535 of every 100100100 trees in Orchard A together with 353535 of every 100100100 trees in Orchard B.

    So the number in bloom is that amount, plus Orchard A's extra, minus Orchard B's shortfall.

    Five percent of the larger Orchard B is a bigger amount than five percent of Orchard A, so the shortfall is bigger than the extra, and fewer than 35%35\%35% of all the trees are in bloom, whatever the two orchards' sizes are, as long as B is larger.

    Ravi is not right.

    For one example, with 404040 trees in Orchard A and 606060 in Orchard B, the trees in bloom number

    0.40×40=160.40\times40=160.40×40=16

    and

    0.30×60=180.30\times60=180.30×60=18

    which is 343434 of the 100100100 trees, or 34%34\%34%.

    Answer

    No; fewer than 35%35\%35% of all the trees are in bloom.

    Key idea

    When two wholes of different sizes carry different percents, the percent of both together lies closer to the larger whole's percent, not halfway between them.

Ratios

Intuition

A recipe that mixes 222 cups of flour with 333 cups of sugar sets up a ratio, a way of comparing two amounts. So does a paint that blends 111 part blue to 444 parts white, and a class with 222 boys for every 333 girls. What makes a ratio so useful is that it captures a relationship you can scale. Double the recipe and you still want the same flavor, so you keep the same ratio. A ratio is built straight out of two ideas you already know, division and equivalent fractions, so this lesson is mostly about putting those tools to a new job: reading ratios, keeping them equal while you scale, and using them to split a total.

Learning goals

  • Write a comparison as a ratio, and say why order matters
  • Scale both parts by the same number to get an equivalent ratio
  • Simplify a ratio by dividing both parts by their GCF
  • Distinguish part-to-part from part-to-whole, where the whole sums the parts
  • Split a total by counting parts, valuing one, then scaling
  • Compare two ratios by writing each as a fraction

What a ratio is

A ratio compares two quantities by division: how much of one there is for each amount of the other. The ratio of 222 cups of flour to 333 cups of sugar is written

2:3,2 : 3,2:3,

read “two to three.” The same comparison can also be written as the fraction 23\frac{2}{3}32​. That link to fractions is what makes ratios easy: scaling, simplifying and comparing work here exactly as they do for fractions.

Two cautions come with the definition. First, order matters. The ratio of flour to sugar is 2:32 : 32:3, but the ratio of sugar to flour is 3:23 : 23:2. Whatever quantity you name first is written first. Second, a ratio is not a total. Saying flour to sugar is 2:32 : 32:3 does not claim there are exactly 222 and 333 cups. It says that for every 222 cups of flour there are 333 cups of sugar. You might really have 444 and 666 cups, or 202020 and 303030, and the comparison is still 2:32 : 32:3.

Check your understanding

A recipe uses 333 cups of water for every 555 cups of rice. What is the ratio of rice to water?

Answer choices

Rice is named first this time, so it is written first.

rice:water=5:3\text{rice} : \text{water} = 5 : 3rice:water=5:3

Writing it the other way, 3:53 : 53:5, would be the ratio of water to rice instead.

Equivalent ratios

Doubling a recipe keeps the flavor, and doubling both parts of a ratio keeps the comparison. Use twice as much of the 2:32 : 32:3 mixture: the flour goes from 222 cups to 2×2=42 \times 2 = 42×2=4, and the sugar from 333 cups to 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6. Those 444 cups of flour form two groups of 222, and the 666 cups of sugar form two groups of 333. Each group of flour still faces a group of sugar, so 4:64 : 64:6 makes the same comparison as 2:32 : 32:3.

Because a ratio is a comparison and not a count, multiplying or dividing both quantities by the same nonzero number leaves it unchanged. Ratios related this way are called equivalent ratios:

2:3  =  4:6  =  6:9  =  20:30.2 : 3 \;=\; 4 : 6 \;=\; 6 : 9 \;=\; 20 : 30.2:3=4:6=6:9=20:30.

Each one describes the same relationship, just measured in larger or smaller batches. The picture below shows the first two. For every 222 flour squares there are 333 sugar squares; doubling every square to 444 and 666 keeps the two colors in step.

This is exactly the rule for equivalent fractions. Writing the ratio 2:32 : 32:3 as 23\frac{2}{3}32​, scaling both parts is the same step that turns 23\frac{2}{3}32​ into 46\frac{4}{6}64​.

Take the ratio 7:117 : 117:11 and scale both parts by 333, which gives 21:3321 : 3321:33. As a fraction that is 2133\frac{21}{33}3321​, and the 333 that scaled both parts is still sitting in both the top and the bottom: 21=3×721 = 3 \times 721=3×7 and 33=3×1133 = 3 \times 1133=3×11. Cancel that shared 333 and the fraction becomes 711\frac{7}{11}117​ again, so 21:3321 : 3321:33 is the comparison 7:117 : 117:11 over again, which is why scaling both parts by the same number never changes what a ratio compares.

The full proof runs this same argument on any ratio, using a letter for the two parts and a letter for the scale factor in place of numbers. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the general proof

You can scale the recipe ratio yourself. The top bar below is one batch of the 2:32 : 32:3 mixture, cut into 555 equal parts, one for each part of that ratio. In that bar, 222 parts are shaded for flour and the other 333 are left for sugar. The bar under it is the same batch, and you choose how finely it is cut.

Cut each part into three and count what you get: 666 shaded parts against 999 unshaded, which is the ratio 6:96 : 96:9 from the list above. Cut into four instead and it is 8:128 : 128:12. The edge of the shaded region never moves while you do this, because the batch itself never changes size, only how finely it is sliced. The steady edge is the claim in the proof made visible: 2:32 : 32:3, 6:96 : 96:9 and 8:128 : 128:12 are one relationship counted in smaller and smaller pieces, not different mixtures. The readout below states this two ways: first as a fraction of the whole batch, flour and sugar together (you will meet that comparison properly later in this lesson), and then as the flour-to-sugar ratio itself, the part-to-part comparison this section is about. Watch that second number: it is the one that keeps matching 2:32 : 32:3 no matter how finely the batch is cut.

Simplifying a ratio

A ratio is in lowest terms (or simplest form) when its two parts share no common factor larger than 111. To get there, divide both parts by their greatest common factor, the same move you use to reduce a fraction. Simplest form is handy because it is the smallest whole-number version of the comparison. So two ratios of whole numbers are equivalent exactly when they reduce to the same lowest terms.

Worked example 1 Simplify the ratio 12:1812 : 1812:18

Find the greatest common factor of the two parts. The factors of 121212 are 1,2,3,4,6,121, 2, 3, 4, 6, 121,2,3,4,6,12 and the factors of 181818 are 1,2,3,6,9,181, 2, 3, 6, 9, 181,2,3,6,9,18, so the largest they share is 666.

Divide both parts by 666:

12:18=(12÷6):(18÷6)=2:3.12 : 18 = (12 \div 6) : (18 \div 6) = 2 : 3.12:18=(12÷6):(18÷6)=2:3.

Since 222 and 333 share no common factor beyond 111, the ratio 12:1812 : 1812:18 in lowest terms is 2:32 : 32:3.

Check your understanding

Simplify the ratio 10:1510 : 1510:15.

Answer choices

The greatest common factor of 101010 and 151515 is 555, so divide both parts by 555.

10:15=(10÷5):(15÷5)=2:310 : 15 = (10 \div 5) : (15 \div 5) = 2 : 310:15=(10÷5):(15÷5)=2:3

No common factor beyond 111 remains, so 2:32 : 32:3 is lowest terms.

Part-to-part and part-to-whole

When a whole is split into groups, there are two different comparisons you can make, and confusing them is the single most common ratio mistake. A part-to-part ratio compares one group to another group. A part-to-whole ratio compares one group to the total.

Suppose a class has only boys and girls, no other kind of student, with 222 boys for every 333 girls. The part-to-part ratio of boys to girls is 2:32 : 32:3. Adding the parts only gives the whole class because there is nothing left out: 2+3=52 + 3 = 52+3=5 students in every batch of 222 boys and 333 girls. So the part-to-whole ratio of boys to all students is 2:52 : 52:5, and as a fraction boys make up 25\frac{2}{5}52​ of the class, not 23\frac{2}{3}32​. The denominator of a part-to-whole fraction is the sum of the parts being compared, and that sum is the true whole only when those parts are the only ones there are.

Check your understanding

A bag contains only red marbles and blue marbles, in the ratio 3:53 : 53:5. What fraction of the marbles are red?

Answer choices

Red and blue are the only two kinds of marble, so the whole has 3+5=83 + 5 = 83+5=8 parts.

fraction red=33+5=38\text{fraction red} = \frac{3}{3 + 5} = \frac{3}{8}fraction red=3+53​=83​

The 35\frac{3}{5}53​ is the part-to-part ratio of red to blue, not the part-to-whole fraction.

Using a ratio to find an amount

A ratio lets you find one quantity from the other once you know a single actual amount. The key is the scale factor: the number you multiply a part of the ratio by to reach the real amount. Because both parts scale by the same factor, finding it for one part hands you the other.

Worked example 2 Scale a recipe

A recipe mixes flour to sugar in the ratio 3:23 : 23:2. If you use 999 cups of flour, how much sugar do you need?

Compare the flour you have to the flour in the ratio. It went from 333 to 999, so the scale factor is

9÷3=3.9 \div 3 = 3.9÷3=3.

Multiply both parts of the ratio by that same factor of 333:

3:2=(3×3):(2×3)=9:6.3 : 2 = (3 \times 3) : (2 \times 3) = 9 : 6.3:2=(3×3):(2×3)=9:6.

So you need 666 cups of sugar.

Check your understanding

A ratio of cats to dogs is 4:34 : 34:3. If there are 121212 cats, how many dogs are there?

Answer choices

The cats went from 444 to 121212, so the scale factor is 12÷4=312 \div 4 = 312÷4=3. Multiply both parts by 333.

4:3=(4×3):(3×3)=12:94 : 3 = (4 \times 3) : (3 \times 3) = 12 : 94:3=(4×3):(3×3)=12:9

So there are 999 dogs.

Worked example 3 Split a total in a ratio

Two friends share $20 in the ratio 2:32 : 32:3. How much does each friend get?

First count the total parts: 2+3=52 + 3 = 52+3=5. The $20 is divided into 555 equal parts, so one part is worth

20÷5=4 dollars.20 \div 5 = 4 \text{ dollars}.20÷5=4 dollars.

Now give each friend their number of parts. The first friend gets 222 parts and the second gets 333 parts:

2×4=8and3×4=12.2 \times 4 = 8 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 3 \times 4 = 12.2×4=8and3×4=12.

So they receive $8 and $12. Check the split by adding the shares: 8+12=208 + 12 = 208+12=20, which matches the total, so the work is right.

Check your understanding

Split 282828 dollars between two friends in the ratio 3:43 : 43:4. How much does each friend receive?

Answer choices

The ratio makes 3+4=73 + 4 = 73+4=7 parts, so one part is worth 28÷7=428 \div 7 = 428÷7=4 dollars.

3×4=12and4×4=163 \times 4 = 12 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 4 \times 4 = 163×4=12and4×4=16

So the shares are 121212 dollars and 161616 dollars, which add back to 282828 dollars.

Comparing two ratios

Two paint mixes can use the same two colors and still not match. One mix is 333 parts blue to 444 parts white, and another is 555 parts blue to 777 parts white. Which one packs in more blue? Call one ratio stronger than another when it has more of the first quantity for the same amount of the second. To decide which of two ratios is stronger, write each ratio as a fraction and compare the fractions, using a common denominator exactly as you would to compare any two fractions.

Worked example 4 Which is the stronger ratio, 3:43 : 43:4 or 5:75 : 75:7?

Write each ratio as a fraction. A common denominator for 444 and 777 is 282828, so rewrite both with that denominator:

34=2128,57=2028.\frac{3}{4} = \frac{21}{28}, \qquad \frac{5}{7} = \frac{20}{28}.43​=2821​,75​=2820​.

Now the comparison is just two like fractions. Since 21>2021 > 2021>20, we have 2128>2028\frac{21}{28} > \frac{20}{28}2821​>2820​, so the ratio 3:43 : 43:4 is stronger than 5:75 : 75:7: the first paint mix packs in more blue for the same white.

Check your understanding

Which is the stronger ratio, 2:52 : 52:5 or 3:73 : 73:7?

Answer choices

Write each as a fraction over the common denominator 353535.

25=1435,37=1535\frac{2}{5} = \frac{14}{35}, \qquad \frac{3}{7} = \frac{15}{35}52​=3514​,73​=3515​

Since 15>1415 > 1415>14, the ratio 3:73 : 73:7 is the stronger comparison.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reversing the order. 2:32 : 32:3 is not the same as 3:23 : 23:2. The quantity named first is always written first, so “boys to girls is 2:32 : 32:3” is different from “girls to boys.”
  • Mixing up part-to-part and part-to-whole. If a class has only boys and girls, in the ratio 2:32 : 32:3, boys are 25\frac{2}{5}52​ of the class (out of 555 total parts), not 23\frac{2}{3}32​. The whole is the sum of the parts, but only when those parts are all there are.
  • Changing only one part, or by different amounts. To keep a ratio equal you multiply or divide both parts by the same number: if one quantity triples, the other must triple too. Scaling 2:32 : 32:3 up gives 4:64 : 64:6, never 3:43 : 43:4.
  • Simplifying only one part. Divide both parts by the same common factor. 12:1812 : 1812:18 becomes 2:32 : 32:3, not 2:182 : 182:18 or 12:312 : 312:3.

Key takeaways

  • A ratio compares two quantities by division; write ”aaa to bbb” as a:ba : ba:b or ab\frac{a}{b}ba​, and remember that order matters.
  • Equivalent ratios come from multiplying or dividing both parts by the same nonzero number: 2:3=4:6=20:302 : 3 = 4 : 6 = 20 : 302:3=4:6=20:30.
  • Simplify a ratio by dividing both parts by their greatest common factor, just like a fraction: 12:18=2:312 : 18 = 2 : 312:18=2:3.
  • Part-to-whole: when two groups aaa and bbb together make up a whole, that whole is a+ba + ba+b, so the first group is aa+b\frac{a}{a + b}a+ba​ of it.
  • Split a total in a ratio by counting the parts, finding the value of one part, then scaling.
  • Compare two ratios by writing each as a fraction and using a common denominator.

Why scaling both parts keeps the ratio

The lesson shows the numeric case: scaling 7:117 : 117:11 by 333 leaves the comparison unchanged, because the 333 cancels back out once the ratio is written as a fraction. This runs the same argument with letters, so it covers every ratio and every scale factor at once.

Why scaling both parts keeps the ratio#

Write the ratio a:ba : ba:b as the fraction ab\frac{a}{b}ba​. For that fraction to make sense, bbb must not be zero. Scaling both parts by the same nonzero number kkk gives the ratio ka:kbka : kbka:kb, which as a fraction is kakb\frac{ka}{kb}kbka​. The factor kkk now appears in both the top and the bottom, and a common factor on the top and bottom of a fraction cancels:

kakb=ab.\frac{ka}{kb} = \frac{a}{b}.kbka​=ba​.

So ka:kbka : kbka:kb and a:ba : ba:b are the same comparison. The same reasoning runs in reverse for division: if both parts share a common factor, dividing it out lands on an equivalent ratio.

A bit of history (optional)

Ask a Greek geometer for the ratio of two lengths. Sometimes you would get two whole numbers back: if one shared unit measures the first length three times and the second length two times, the ratio is 3:23 : 23:2. But not every pair of lengths shares a measuring unit like that. The side of a square and its diagonal never do, however finely you try to slice one to fit the other, and Greek mathematicians proved it. So a ratio could not always be pinned to two whole numbers, and it stayed what this lesson says it is: a comparison, and not a total.

That left a hard question. If neither ratio is a number, how can you claim that two of them are equal? Eudoxus, a Greek thinker of about 350 BCE, answered it with copies. Lay ten of the first quantity against three of the second. See which pile wins. Then run the same contest on the other ratio. If every contest comes out the same way, the two ratios are one ratio.

The test never measures anything, and it never needs to. It only scales both parts and compares. Two thousand years later, the builders of the real numbers reached for that idea again. You used it yourself here: 2:32 : 32:3 and 4:64 : 64:6 are one ratio, because scaling both parts changed nothing.

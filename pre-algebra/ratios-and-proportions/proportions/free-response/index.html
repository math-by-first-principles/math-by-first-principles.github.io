    1. Two buckets of glaze . Foundational, 12 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    A pottery studio mixes glaze by weighing powder and measuring water, and two buckets count as the same glaze only when their ratios of powder to water are equal. This morning's buckets were mixed with different scoops: one holds 161616 grams of powder to 444444 milliliters of water, the other 282828 grams to 777777 milliliters.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Reduce each bucket's ratio of powder to water to lowest terms, showing the greatest common factor you divided by in each case, and say what the two results settle about the buckets.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      The studio now needs a bucket built on 242424 grams of powder. A helper reasons that 242424 grams is 888 more than the first bucket's 161616, so 888 more milliliters of water will do, giving 525252 milliliters. Test the helper's pair against the first bucket with the diagonal products, judge the reasoning behind it, and give the amount of water the studio's mix calls for at 242424 grams.

      Find and correct the error Say which line first goes wrong, why it is wrong, and then do it correctly. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why comparing the two diagonal products decides whether two ratios are equal. Build the reason from writing both ratios over one denominator rather than by quoting the rule, and then say what that same reasoning shows about changing a ratio by adding the same amount to both of its parts.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing here needs an opinion about the glaze. You hold two tests that return a verdict on any pair of ratios: reduce each of them to lowest terms, or multiply along the diagonals. Pick one and let the numbers speak first.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Take the helper's bucket at face value and stand it beside the first bucket as a pair of ratios, powder over water on both sides. Once the diagonals have spoken, ask what the studio's own ratio demands at that weight of powder.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Two fractions with the same number underneath are equal exactly when the numbers on top match, and that is something you already trust. Rewrite both ratios so they share a denominator, then look at what has landed on top of each.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Both reduce to 411\frac{4}{11}114​, after dividing by 444 and by 777, so the two buckets carry the same glaze.

    Part B

    The diagonal products are 832832832 and 105610561056, so the helper's bucket is a different glaze: a ratio is held by multiplying both parts, not by adding to them. The mix calls for 666666 milliliters of water.

    Part C

    Over one shared denominator, two ratios carry their diagonal products as numerators, and fractions with the same denominator are equal exactly when their numerators are, so equal ratios and equal diagonal products always arrive together. Adding the same amount to both parts moves the comparison unless the parts were equal already.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Take each bucket on its own and divide both parts of its ratio by everything the two numbers share.

    For the first bucket, 16=2×2×2×216 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 216=2×2×2×2 and 44=2×2×1144 = 2 \times 2 \times 1144=2×2×11, so they share two copies of 222 and GCF⁡(16,44)=4\operatorname{GCF}(16, 44) = 4GCF(16,44)=4:

    1644=16÷444÷4=411.\frac{16}{44} = \frac{16 \div 4}{44 \div 4} = \frac{4}{11}.4416​=44÷416÷4​=114​.

    For the second, 28=2×2×728 = 2 \times 2 \times 728=2×2×7 and 77=7×1177 = 7 \times 1177=7×11, so the only prime they share is 777 and GCF⁡(28,77)=7\operatorname{GCF}(28, 77) = 7GCF(28,77)=7:

    2877=28÷777÷7=411.\frac{28}{77} = \frac{28 \div 7}{77 \div 7} = \frac{4}{11}.7728​=77÷728÷7​=114​.

    Both ratios reduce to 411\frac{4}{11}114​, which reads as 444 grams of powder for every 111111 milliliters of water. Reducing a ratio never changes the comparison it makes, so each bucket makes that same comparison and

    1644=2877\frac{16}{44} = \frac{28}{77}4416​=7728​

    is a true proportion. The two buckets hold different amounts of glaze; what they share is the mix.

    Part B

    Test the helper's bucket exactly as any pair is tested. Set the first bucket's ratio beside it, powder over water on both sides, and multiply along each diagonal:

    16×52=832,44×24=1056.16 \times 52 = 832, \qquad 44 \times 24 = 1056.16×52=832,44×24=1056.

    The two products are different, so the two ratios are not equal and the helper's bucket is not the studio's glaze.

    The fault is in the move itself. A ratio is held by scaling, that is by multiplying both parts by one and the same number, and the helper added the same amount to both parts instead. Adding treats the two numbers as though the gap between them were what mattered, when what matters is how many times one goes into the other.

    Now find the water that does keep the mix. Write the studio's ratio against a bucket with 242424 grams of powder, letting www stand for the milliliters of water:

    16 g44 mL=24 gw mL.\frac{16 \text{ g}}{44 \text{ mL}} = \frac{24 \text{ g}}{w \text{ mL}}.44 mL16 g​=w mL24 g​.

    The diagonal that is fully known is 444444 and 242424, so multiply those two:

    44×24=1056.44 \times 24 = 1056.44×24=1056.

    That is the value of the other diagonal product, 16×w16 \times w16×w, so divide by the 161616 facing the blank:

    w=1056÷16=66.w = 1056 \div 16 = 66.w=1056÷16=66.

    The bucket needs 666666 milliliters of water. Check it by reducing: 2466\frac{24}{66}6624​ divides by 666 to give 411\frac{4}{11}114​, the studio's mix. The helper's 525252 milliliters was 141414 short of it, so the glaze would have come out thick.

    Part C

    The test has to be earned from something already trusted, and the trustworthy fact is about fractions with a shared denominator: two of them are equal exactly when the numbers on top are equal.

    So give the two ratios a shared denominator. Any pair of ratios, written ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ and cd\frac{c}{d}dc​, can both be written over b×db \times db×d, by multiplying the first above and below by ddd and the second above and below by bbb:

    ab=a×db×d,cd=c×bd×b.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times d}, \qquad \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c \times b}{d \times b}.ba​=b×da×d​,dc​=d×bc×b​.

    Neither rewriting moved a value, since each multiplied a top and a bottom by the same number, and d×bd \times bd×b is the same product as b×db \times db×d. The two now sit over one denominator, so they are equal exactly when a×da \times da×d and b×cb \times cb×c are equal, and those are precisely the diagonal products.

    On this question's numbers, the shared denominator is 44×5244 \times 5244×52 and the two numerators are

    16×52=832,24×44=1056,16 \times 52 = 832, \qquad 24 \times 44 = 1056,16×52=832,24×44=1056,

    so the studio's ratio and the helper's become 8322288\frac{832}{2288}2288832​ and 10562288\frac{1056}{2288}22881056​, which count different numbers of the same size of piece and therefore cannot be equal.

    The same reasoning judges the helper's move. Add some amount kkk to both parts of ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ and compare the result with the original: the diagonal products are a×(b+k)a \times (b + k)a×(b+k) and b×(a+k)b \times (a + k)b×(a+k), which the distributive property opens out as

    a×b+a×kanda×b+b×k.a \times b + a \times k \qquad \text{and} \qquad a \times b + b \times k.a×b+a×kanda×b+b×k.

    They agree only when a×ka \times ka×k and b×kb \times kb×k agree, that is only when nothing was added or when the two parts were equal to begin with. A one-to-one mix survives the addition; the studio's 161616 to 444444 does not, which is why the helper's bucket came out wrong.

    In one line

    The two buckets carry the same glaze: 1644\frac{16}{44}4416​ divides by 444 and 2877\frac{28}{77}7728​ divides by 777, and both land on 411\frac{4}{11}114​. The helper's bucket does not match, since 16×52=83216 \times 52 = 83216×52=832 while 44×24=105644 \times 24 = 105644×24=1056; a ratio is held by multiplying both parts by one number, not by adding one to both. The diagonal-product test itself comes from the common denominator: written over b×db \times db×d, two ratios carry their diagonal products as numerators, so equal ratios and equal diagonal products always arrive together.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    There are two kinds of quantity here, a count of coins and a weight in grams, and two situations, the reference tray and the sack. A proportion says the two situations compare those quantities in the same way.

    Name the unknown first, so the line means something: let nnn be the number of coins in the sack. Then write coins over grams on the left for the tray and coins over grams on the right for the sack:

    24 coins90 grams=n coins525 grams.\frac{24 \text{ coins}}{90 \text{ grams}} = \frac{n \text{ coins}}{525 \text{ grams}}.90 grams24 coins​=525 gramsn coins​.

    The order is the whole of the work. Counts sit on top on both sides and weights sit underneath on both sides, so each diagonal pairs a count with a weight. Writing coins over grams on one side and grams over coins on the other would set down a comparison nobody intended, and the number that came out would answer a different question.

    Writing it the other way up throughout, 9024=525n\frac{90}{24} = \frac{525}{n}2490​=n525​, is just as good, because both sides still list the same kind of quantity in the same place. What is not allowed is mixing the two orders within one line, coins over grams on one side and grams over coins on the other.

    There is a third true line hiding in these numbers, 24n=90525\frac{24}{n} = \frac{90}{525}n24​=52590​, which compares the two situations instead of the two quantities: the tray's coins against the sack's coins, and the tray's grams against the sack's grams. It is a genuine proportion and it leads to the same count. It is not what this part asks for, though, because its sides no longer hold matching kinds of quantity in matching positions.

    Part B

    For 505050 meters the same two steps give

    4×50=200,200÷9=22.2…,4 \times 50 = 200, \qquad 200 \div 9 = 22.2\ldots,4×50=200,200÷9=22.2…,

    which is not a whole number of liters, so that trade cannot be settled.

    In general the oil is the cloth multiplied by 444 and divided by 999. Since 9=3×39 = 3 \times 39=3×3 and 4=2×24 = 2 \times 24=2×2 share no factor above 111, both factors of 333 must come from the cloth itself, so the trade settles exactly when the cloth is a multiple of 999 meters: 999, 181818, 272727, 363636, 454545, and so on. Each of those returns 444 liters for every 999 meters.

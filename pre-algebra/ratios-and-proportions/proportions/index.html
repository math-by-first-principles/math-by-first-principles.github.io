Proportions

Intuition

You mixed a drink with 111 scoop of powder for every 222 cups of water, and it tasted right. To make a bigger batch you use 333 scoops and 666 cups, and it tastes exactly the same. The reason is that 1:21 : 21:2 and 3:63 : 63:6 are the same ratio, just measured in larger amounts. A statement that two ratios are equal, like 1:2=3:61 : 2 = 3 : 61:2=3:6, is called a proportion. Once you know that two ratios match, a single known amount tells you every other amount, such as how much powder for 101010 cups of water.

Learning goals

  • State a proportion as two equal ratios
  • Test two ratios by reducing both to lowest terms
  • Cross-multiply, and show why the diagonal products must match
  • Find a missing amount by scaling or by cross-multiplying
  • Set both ratios up in the same order, units included

What a proportion is

A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal. From the last two lessons you know that 3:63 : 63:6 is the same comparison as 1:21 : 21:2. That is because dividing both parts of 3:63 : 63:6 by 333 lands on 1:21 : 21:2. Writing that equality down is a proportion:

1:2=3:6.1 : 2 = 3 : 6.1:2=3:6.

Because a ratio can be written as a fraction whenever its second number is not zero, and every ratio in this lesson has one, the same proportion is usually written with two equal fractions. That fraction form is the one we will work in:

12=36.\frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{6}.21​=63​.

Read it as ”111 is to 222 as 333 is to 666.” A proportion is true only when the two ratios really are equivalent. If they are not equivalent, the statement is simply false, the same way 4=54 = 54=5 is false.

Telling whether two ratios are in proportion

Since the two sides are just fractions, deciding whether a proportion is true is the same job as deciding whether two fractions are equal. You already have two ways to do that from the Fractions chapter, and both work here.

Start with 6:86 : 86:8 and 9:129 : 129:12. The ratio 6:86 : 86:8 reduces to 3:43 : 43:4 (divide both parts by 222), and 9:129 : 129:12 also reduces to 3:43 : 43:4 (divide both parts by 333). They share the same simplest form, so

68=912\frac{6}{8} = \frac{9}{12}86​=129​

is a true proportion. That is the first test: simplify each ratio, then see whether the two land on the same lowest terms.

The second test scales one ratio to match the other. Multiplying both parts of 6:86 : 86:8 by 1.51.51.5 gives 6×1.5=96 \times 1.5 = 96×1.5=9 and 8×1.5=128 \times 1.5 = 128×1.5=12, which is the second ratio exactly. Whenever multiplying both parts of one ratio by the same factor gives the other ratio, the two are equivalent.

Worked example 1 Is 410=615\frac{4}{10} = \frac{6}{15}104​=156​ a true proportion?

Test it by simplifying each ratio to lowest terms.

The greatest common factor of 444 and 101010 is 222, so

410=4÷210÷2=25.\frac{4}{10} = \frac{4 \div 2}{10 \div 2} = \frac{2}{5}.104​=10÷24÷2​=52​.

The greatest common factor of 666 and 151515 is 333, so

615=6÷315÷3=25.\frac{6}{15} = \frac{6 \div 3}{15 \div 3} = \frac{2}{5}.156​=15÷36÷3​=52​.

Both ratios reduce to 25\frac{2}{5}52​, so they are equal. The proportion 410=615\frac{4}{10} = \frac{6}{15}104​=156​ is true.

Check your understanding

Are 1620\frac{16}{20}2016​ and 2025\frac{20}{25}2520​ in proportion? Test by reducing both to lowest terms.

Answer choices

Reduce each ratio to lowest terms. The greatest common factor of 161616 and 202020 is 444, so 1620=45\frac{16}{20} = \frac{4}{5}2016​=54​. The greatest common factor of 202020 and 252525 is 555, so 2025=45\frac{20}{25} = \frac{4}{5}2520​=54​. Both ratios reduce to the same lowest terms, so they are equal and the proportion is true.

2025\frac{20}{25}2520​ does reduce further, to 45\frac{4}{5}54​; and adding the same difference to both parts of a ratio does not keep it equivalent.

Cross-multiplication, and why it works

Simplifying is easy when the numbers are friendly, but it gets fiddly when they are not. There is a test that works on any two ratios and needs only multiplication.

Simplifying already showed that 68=912\frac{6}{8} = \frac{9}{12}86​=129​ is true. Multiply along each diagonal instead, the top of one side times the bottom of the other:

6×12=72and8×9=72.6 \times 12 = 72 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 8 \times 9 = 72.6×12=72and8×9=72.

The two diagonal products are equal. They are called the cross-products. The picture shows where that name comes from. It draws the proportion 12=36\frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{6}21​=63​, with the two products running along its diagonals and crossing in the middle.

The match is not luck. Over the shared denominator 8×12=968 \times 12 = 968×12=96, the ratio 68\frac{6}{8}86​ becomes 7296\frac{72}{96}9672​ and 912\frac{9}{12}129​ becomes 7296\frac{72}{96}9672​. Those two numerators are the diagonal products, and two equal ratios over one denominator must have equal numerators.

The same argument works for any proportion, not just this one pair of numbers. Writing it once for every possible proportion needs a letter in each of the four spots, aaa, bbb, ccc, and ddd, each one standing for whatever number happens to sit there. For any proportion, the same two diagonals give the same test:

ab=cd⟶a×d  and  b×c.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad a \times d \ \text{ and } \ b \times c.ba​=dc​⟶a×d  and  b×c.

The proportion is true exactly when these two cross-products are equal, a×d=b×ca \times d = b \times ca×d=b×c. The test carries a condition you have to check: bbb and ddd must not be zero. Their product b×db \times db×d is then not zero either. That nonzero product is the common denominator each ratio gets rewritten over, which is where the rule comes from.

Why equal ratios have equal cross-products#

The example above already did the whole job on one pair of numbers, and nothing in that step depended on the numbers chosen. Start with any true proportion ab=cd\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}ba​=dc​, where bbb and ddd must not be zero. Their product b×db \times db×d is then not zero either, so it can serve as a denominator. To turn ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ into a fraction over b×db \times db×d, multiply its top and bottom by ddd. To turn cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ into a fraction over b×db \times db×d, multiply its top and bottom by bbb:

ab=a×db×d,cd=c×bd×b.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times d}, \qquad \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c \times b}{d \times b}.ba​=b×da×d​,dc​=d×bc×b​.

The order of the factors does not change the product, so d×bd \times bd×b is the same as b×db \times db×d. Two fractions with the same denominator are equal exactly when their numerators are equal. Since the fractions were equal to begin with, the numerators must match:

a×d=c×b.a \times d = c \times b.a×d=c×b.

The argument also runs in reverse. If the cross-products are equal, the two rewritten fractions have equal numerators over the same denominator. That makes the two fractions equal, so the proportion is true.

∎

Check your understanding

Why must the cross-products of a true proportion be equal?

Answer choices

Rewriting ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ and cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ over the shared denominator b×db \times db×d gives numerators a×da \times da×d and c×bc \times bc×b, which are exactly the two cross-products. Since the original fractions were equal, and equal fractions over the same denominator must have equal numerators, the cross-products have to match. This works for any bbb and ddd that are not zero, not only ratios that already share a denominator.

Worked example 2 Use cross-products to test 37=1228\frac{3}{7} = \frac{12}{28}73​=2812​

Multiply along each diagonal and compare the two products.

The first cross-product is the top-left times the bottom-right:

3×28=84.3 \times 28 = 84.3×28=84.

The second cross-product is the bottom-left times the top-right:

7×12=84.7 \times 12 = 84.7×12=84.

The two cross-products are both 848484, so they are equal. That means 37=1228\frac{3}{7} = \frac{12}{28}73​=2812​ is a true proportion. (You can double-check by simplifying: 1228\frac{12}{28}2812​ divides by 444 to give 37\frac{3}{7}73​.)

Check your understanding

Are the ratios 58\frac{5}{8}85​ and 1224\frac{12}{24}2412​ in proportion?

Answer choices

Compare the two diagonal products. The top-left times the bottom-right is 5×245 \times 245×24, and the bottom-left times the top-right is 8×128 \times 128×12.

5×24=1208×12=965 \times 24 = 120 \qquad 8 \times 12 = 965×24=1208×12=96

The cross-products do not match, 120≠96120 \neq 96120=96, so the ratios are not equal and the proportion is false. (1224\frac{12}{24}2412​ reduces to 12\frac{1}{2}21​, which is not 58\frac{5}{8}85​.)

Finding a missing amount

The real power of a proportion is that you can know three of the four numbers and find the fourth. That holds for the positive amounts this lesson works with, such as cups, miles and dollars. This is the everyday use: you know one ratio and one matching amount, and you want the other. Set the two ratios equal, keeping the same kind of quantity in matching positions, then find the missing number with arithmetic you already have.

Both ratios must list their quantities in the same order. If the left ratio is cups of water over scoops of powder, the right ratio must also be cups of water over scoops of powder. Mixing the order (water over scoops on one side, scoops over water on the other) builds a false proportion and gives a wrong answer.

Method 1: scale the ratio

If one ratio’s known part is a whole-number multiple of the other’s, the fastest route is the equivalent-ratio scaling from the Ratios lesson. Find the scale factor from the pair you know, then apply that same factor to the other part.

Worked example 3 Scale to fill in the blank: 25=?20\frac{2}{5} = \frac{?}{20}52​=20?​

Look at the denominators, the pair you fully know: the bottom went from 555 to 202020. Find the scale factor by dividing:

20÷5=4.20 \div 5 = 4.20÷5=4.

A proportion stays true when you scale both parts of a ratio by the same number. So multiply the top by that same factor of 444:

25=2×45×4=820.\frac{2}{5} = \frac{2 \times 4}{5 \times 4} = \frac{8}{20}.52​=5×42×4​=208​.

The missing number is 888. This is the equivalent-fractions move from earlier chapters: 25\frac{2}{5}52​ and 820\frac{8}{20}208​ are the same ratio written in larger terms.

Method 2: cross-multiply, then divide

Scaling is neat, but it only stays simple when the known parts divide evenly. When they do not, cross-multiplication handles any proportion. The idea uses the rule you just proved: in a true proportion the cross-products are equal. So multiply the two numbers on the diagonal you fully know, which gives the value of the other cross-product. Then divide that value by the remaining known number to recover the missing one.

Take a missing numerator, and call the unknown amount nnn. That nnn is a variable standing for the amount you are finding, and here you solve for it by multiplying and dividing:

n6=104.\frac{n}{6} = \frac{10}{4}.6n​=410​.

The cross-products of a true proportion are equal, so n×4n \times 4n×4 must equal 6×106 \times 106×10. The right side is a product of two known numbers:

6×10=60.6 \times 10 = 60.6×10=60.

So nnn multiplied by 444 gives 606060:

4×n=60.4 \times n = 60.4×n=60.

The number that does that is found by dividing 606060 by 444, since division undoes multiplication:

n=60÷4=15.n = 60 \div 4 = 15.n=60÷4=15.

The missing amount is 151515. Every proportion with one blank yields to the same two steps. Multiply the diagonal you know, then divide by the number diagonally opposite the blank.

Check your understanding

Find the missing number: 912=n8\frac{9}{12} = \frac{n}{8}129​=8n​.

Answer choices

The two known denominators, 121212 and 888, are not a whole-number multiple of each other, so scaling does not give a clean factor here; cross-multiply instead. Multiply the diagonal you know, 999 and 888:

9×8=729 \times 8 = 729×8=72

Then divide by the number diagonally opposite the blank, 121212:

n=72÷12=6n = 72 \div 12 = 6n=72÷12=6

Dividing by 999 instead of 121212 gives the wrong answer 888; multiplying 12×8=9612 \times 8 = 9612×8=96 skips the division that isolates nnn.

Proportions in the real world

Most proportion problems arrive as a word problem, and the work is in the setup. The reliable habit is to write a sentence ratio first, with its units. Then build the second ratio in the same order and put the blank where the unknown belongs.

Worked example 4 A map scale

On a map, 222 centimeters represents 505050 real kilometers. Two towns are 777 centimeters apart on the map. How far apart are they in real life?

Write the scale as a ratio of map distance to real distance, and keep that same order on both sides. Let ddd be the real distance in kilometers:

2 cm50 km=7 cmd km.\frac{2 \text{ cm}}{50 \text{ km}} = \frac{7 \text{ cm}}{d \text{ km}}.50 km2 cm​=d km7 cm​.

Multiply the diagonal you know, 505050 and 777:

50×7=350.50 \times 7 = 350.50×7=350.

That equals the other cross-product, 2×d2 \times d2×d, so 2×d2 \times d2×d is 350350350. Divide by the 222 next to the blank:

d=350÷2=175.d = 350 \div 2 = 175.d=350÷2=175.

The towns are 175175175 kilometers apart. Keeping map centimeters over real kilometers on both sides is what made the setup correct; flipping one side would have given a nonsense distance.

Worked example 5 Predict from a steady speed

A car travels at a steady speed and covers 150150150 miles in 333 hours. At the same speed, how far does it go in 777 hours?

Steady speed means the ratio of miles to hours stays the same, so it is a proportion. Write miles over hours on both sides, with mmm for the unknown miles, then multiply the known diagonal and divide by the number facing the blank:

150 mi3 hr=m mi7 hr⟶m=150×73=350.\frac{150 \text{ mi}}{3 \text{ hr}} = \frac{m \text{ mi}}{7 \text{ hr}} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad m = \frac{150 \times 7}{3} = 350.3 hr150 mi​=7 hrm mi​⟶m=3150×7​=350.

The car covers 350350350 miles in 777 hours. You could also find the unit rate first, 150÷3=50150 \div 3 = 50150÷3=50 miles per hour, then multiply by 777 to get the same 350350350. A proportion is the unit-rate idea written as two equal ratios.

Check your understanding

If 444 notebooks cost 101010 dollars, which proportion correctly finds the cost ccc of 101010 notebooks at the same price?

Answer choices

Keep the same quantity on top on both sides. The first ratio is notebooks over dollars, 410\frac{4}{10}104​, so the second ratio must also put notebooks on top: 10c\frac{10}{c}c10​.

4×c=10×10c=100÷4=254 \times c = 10 \times 10 \qquad c = 100 \div 4 = 254×c=10×10c=100÷4=25

So 101010 notebooks cost 252525 dollars. The other options put dollars over notebooks on one side and notebooks over dollars on the other, or pair the two different 101010s together instead of matching notebooks with notebooks; mixing the order gives a wrong cost.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Setting up the ratios in different orders. Both sides must list quantities in the same order, for example miles over hours on the left and miles over hours on the right. Flipping one side (hours over miles) builds a false proportion and a wrong answer.
  • Cross-multiplying inside a single ratio. Cross-multiplication compares two ratios across an equals sign. There is nothing to cross-multiply in a lone fraction like 34\frac{3}{4}43​; the method needs the full proportion ab=cd\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}ba​=dc​.
  • Multiplying straight across instead of on the diagonals. The cross-products are the diagonal products, a×da \times da×d and b×cb \times cb×c, not a×ca \times ca×c and b×db \times db×d. Follow the crossing lines.
  • Dividing by the wrong number. After multiplying the known diagonal, divide by the number diagonally opposite the blank, not by either number you just used. In n6=104\frac{n}{6} = \frac{10}{4}6n​=410​ you multiply 6×10=606 \times 10 = 606×10=60, then divide by the 444 facing nnn across the diagonal.
  • Adding to compare ratios. Two ratios are equal when they scale by multiplication; a common difference between their parts is not what makes them equal. 23\frac{2}{3}32​ and 45\frac{4}{5}54​ are not in proportion even though each part of the second is 222 more; check with cross-products (2×5=102 \times 5 = 102×5=10 but 3×4=123 \times 4 = 123×4=12).

Key takeaways

  • A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal, written ab=cd\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}ba​=dc​ and read ”aaa is to bbb as ccc is to ddd.”
  • Two ratios are in proportion when they reduce to the same lowest terms or when one scales to the other by a single factor.
  • Cross-multiplication: the proportion is true exactly when the diagonal products are equal, a×d=b×ca \times d = b \times ca×d=b×c. It requires that bbb and ddd are not zero, so their product b×db \times db×d is not zero either. Both ratios can then be rewritten over that common denominator, which forces their numerators to match.
  • To find a missing amount, set both ratios up in the same order with their units, then scale the ratio you know by its factor, or cross-multiply the known diagonal and divide by the number diagonally opposite the blank; check by simplifying the finished proportion.

A bit of history (optional)

How do you measure a pyramid? You cannot climb it, and no rope will run up a slope of stone to the point.

The story goes that a Greek trader named Thales did it in Egypt, around 600 BCE. His tools were a stick and the sun. He pushed the stick upright into the sand and waited. When the stick’s own shadow grew as long as the stick, he knew the sun stood at the one angle where every upright height matches its own shadow. At that moment he measured the pyramid’s shadow, out to the point on the sand directly below the peak, and that length was the pyramid’s height.

Later tellings have him doing it at any hour of the day, which needs the full idea. The stick is to its shadow as the pyramid is to its shadow. Three of those four lengths can be paced out on the sand. The fourth is the one nobody could reach.

That is exactly this lesson, twenty-six centuries early. Write the two ratios in the same order, multiply the diagonal you know, and divide by the number facing the blank.

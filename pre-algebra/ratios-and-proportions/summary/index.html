Ratios, Rates, and Percents: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Ratio a:ba : ba:b
A comparison of two quantities by division, also written ab\frac{a}{b}ba​. Order matters, and it gives a relationship, not a count: 2:32 : 32:3 can describe 444 and 666 just as well as 202020 and 303030.
Scale factor
The number a ratio's part is multiplied by to reach a real amount. Both parts share ONE factor, so finding it from the known pair hands you the other.
Rate
A ratio of quantities in DIFFERENT units, so both units must be named rather than dropped. The word "per" marks the denominator; multiplying a rate by a matching quantity of that denominator's unit produces the numerator's unit.
Proportion
A statement that two ratios are equal, ab=cd\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}ba​=dc​, read "aaa is to bbb as ccc is to ddd". Like any equation it is true or false.
Percent %\%%
"Out of 100100100", an instruction to divide by 100100100: 25%=25100=0.2525\% = \frac{25}{100} = 0.2525%=10025​=0.25, never the plain 252525. 100%100\%100% is the whole, so above 100%100\%100% is more than the whole.
Whole, or base
The amount a percent is taken OF, called the whole. 20%20\%20% of 505050 is 101010 but 20%20\%20% of 200200200 is 404040, so a percent means nothing until its whole is named. In a percent change, that whole is called the base and is the value BEFORE the change.

Formulas and theorems

  • Scaling and simplifying a ratio

    a:b=ka:kb,a:b=aba : b = ka : kb, \qquad a : b = \frac{a}{b}a:b=ka:kb,a:b=ba​

    Use when b≠0b \neq 0b=0 and k≠0k \neq 0k=0. The factor hits BOTH parts, and it multiplies or divides, never adds. Divide by the greatest common factor for lowest terms; two ratios are equivalent exactly when their lowest terms match.

    e.g. 18:2418 : 2418:24 divides by their greatest common factor 666 to 3:43 : 43:4.

  • Part-to-whole fraction

    first part of the whole=aa+b\text{first part of the whole} = \frac{a}{a + b}first part of the whole=a+ba​

    Use when The two listed parts must be ALL of the whole and are positive, so the sum is nonzero. The part-to-part ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ is a different number.

    e.g. In 3:53 : 53:5 the first quantity is 38\frac{3}{8}83​ of the total, not 35\frac{3}{5}53​.

  • Splitting a total in a ratio

    one share=Ta+b\text{one share} = \frac{T}{a + b}one share=a+bT​
    Text description

    A bar of 400 cut into eight equal cells grouped 3 and 5, so one cell is 50 and the blocks are 150 and 250.

    Use when The two ratio numbers must count every share. Each amount is its own ratio number times one share.

  • Unit rate, and scaling it

    unit rate=A÷Btotal=unit rate×(units of B)\begin{gathered} \text{unit rate} = A \div B \\ \text{total} = \text{unit rate} \times (\text{units of } B) \end{gathered}unit rate=A÷Btotal=unit rate×(units of B)​
    Text description

    Two aligned number lines, dollars above and pounds below, stepping together by 1.75 dollars for every one pound.

    Use when B≠0B \neq 0B=0, and the quantity named after "per" is the one you divide BY. Carry the units, since a bare number does not say which way round it is; the quotient may be a decimal.

    e.g. 777 dollars for 444 pounds is 1.751.751.75 per pound, so 333 pounds cost 5.255.255.25.

  • Cross-product test

    ab=cdexactly whena×d=b×c\begin{gathered} \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \\ \text{exactly when} \quad a \times d = b \times c \end{gathered}ba​=dc​exactly whena×d=b×c​

    Use when b≠0b \neq 0b=0 and d≠0d \neq 0d=0. It runs both ways, so unequal products PROVE the ratios differ.

    e.g. 58\frac{5}{8}85​ and 1524\frac{15}{24}2415​: 5×24=120=8×155 \times 24 = 120 = 8 \times 155×24=120=8×15, so the proportion is true.

  • Missing term of a proportion

    blank=known diagonal productnumber diagonallyopposite the blank\text{blank} = \frac{\text{known diagonal product}}{\begin{array}{c} \text{number diagonally} \\ \text{opposite the blank} \end{array}}blank=number diagonallyopposite the blank​known diagonal product​

    Use when Needs matching quantities in matching positions and a nonzero number diagonally opposite the blank. It works with the blank on top or underneath, and never moves a term across the equals sign.

    e.g. 9n=1540\frac{9}{n} = \frac{15}{40}n9​=4015​: the known diagonal is 9×40=3609 \times 40 = 3609×40=360, so n=360÷15=24n = 360 \div 15 = 24n=360÷15=24.

  • Percent, fraction, and decimal

    p%=p100=p÷100p\% = \frac{p}{100} = p \div 100p%=100p​=p÷100

    Use when The %\%% sign is the division, so it must be cleared before the number enters any arithmetic. Dividing by 100100100 shifts the point two places. ppp may exceed 100100100 or itself be a decimal.

    e.g. 0.045=4.5%0.045 = 4.5\%0.045=4.5%, and 720=35100=35%\frac{7}{20} = \frac{35}{100} = 35\%207​=10035​=35%.

  • The percent relationship

    partwhole=p100\frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \frac{p}{100}wholepart​=100p​

    Use when The whole follows "of". Part =p100×= \frac{p}{100} \times=100p​× whole; whole === part ÷p100\div \frac{p}{100}÷100p​, which needs p≠0p \neq 0p=0; the percent needs a nonzero whole.

    e.g. What percent of 160160160 is 363636? 36÷160=0.225=22.5%36 \div 160 = 0.225 = 22.5\%36÷160=0.225=22.5%.

  • Percent change

    percent change=new−originaloriginal×100%\begin{gathered} \text{percent change} \\ = \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100\% \end{gathered}percent change=originalnew−original​×100%​

    Use when The ORIGINAL sits underneath and must be nonzero. Positive is an increase, negative a decrease, normally reported as a positive percent with the word "decrease".

    e.g. 757575 falling to 666666: −975=−0.12\frac{-9}{75} = -0.1275−9​=−0.12, a 12%12\%12% decrease.

  • The multiplier, forwards and backwards

    new=(1±p100)originaloriginal=new1±p100\begin{gathered} \text{new} = \left(1 \pm \frac{p}{100}\right)\text{original} \\ \text{original} = \frac{\text{new}}{1 \pm \frac{p}{100}} \end{gathered}new=(1±100p​)originaloriginal=1±100p​new​​

    Use when +++ for tax, tip, or markup and −-− for a discount, where a decrease needs p≤100p \le 100p≤100; reversing needs a nonzero multiplier. Build it from what REMAINS: p%p\%p% off leaves 100−p100\frac{100 - p}{100}100100−p​.

    e.g. 8%8\%8% tax on 250250250 gives 1.08×250=2701.08 \times 250 = 2701.08×250=270.

  • Stacked percent changes

    final=f1f2×originalf=1±p100\begin{gathered} \text{final} = f_1 f_2 \times \text{original} \\ f = 1 \pm \frac{p}{100} \end{gathered}final=f1​f2​×originalf=1±100p​​
    Text description

    Bars of 100, then 120 after a 20 percent rise, then 96 after a 20 percent fall, ending below the dashed line at the starting height.

    Use when Each factor acts on what the previous change produced, so the percents MULTIPLY rather than add, and the order does not matter. They add only when both are taken of the same base.

    e.g. Two 10%10\%10% raises give 1.10×1.10=1.211.10 \times 1.10 = 1.211.10×1.10=1.21, a 21%21\%21% rise, not 20%20\%20%.

Problem types, step by step

Simplify a ratio, or compare two ratios

  1. Divide both parts by their greatest common factor to reach lowest terms.
  2. For equivalence, reduce both ratios and compare the lowest terms, or cross-multiply.
  3. To rank two ratios by size, write each as a fraction over a common denominator.

e.g. 12:1812 : 1812:18 and 10:1510 : 1510:15 both reduce to 2:32 : 32:3, so they are equivalent.

Find the other quantity from a ratio and one amount

  1. Match the known amount to its own part of the ratio and divide to get the scale factor.
  2. Multiply the OTHER part by that same factor.
  3. Check that the finished pair reduces back to the original ratio.

e.g. Cats to dogs is 5:25 : 25:2 with 353535 cats: the factor is 35÷5=735 \div 5 = 735÷5=7, so there are 141414 dogs.

Split a total in a given ratio

  1. Add the two ratio numbers to count the shares, then divide the total by that count.
  2. Multiply each ratio number by the value of one share.
  3. Check the two amounts add back to the total.

e.g. 400400400 grams in 3:53 : 53:5: one share is 400÷8=50400 \div 8 = 50400÷8=50, giving 150150150 and 250250250 grams.

Find a unit rate and use it

  1. Divide the first quantity by the second, keeping the unit named after "per" on the bottom.
  2. To compare options, put both in the same units in the same order, then take the lower price per item or the higher speed.
  3. To predict a total, multiply the unit rate by the number of units.
  4. To change units, multiply or divide BOTH quantities by the same number.

e.g. 333 dollars for 666 ounces is 0.500.500.50 per ounce against 444 dollars for 101010 ounces at 0.400.400.40, so the larger box wins.

Set up and solve a proportion

  1. Write the known ratio as a fraction with its units, build the second in the SAME order, and put the blank where the unknown belongs.
  2. If one known pair scales to the other by a whole number, apply that factor to the remaining part.
  3. Otherwise multiply the diagonal you know and divide by the number diagonally opposite the blank.
  4. Check by simplifying the finished proportion.

e.g. A 777-foot post casts a 333-foot shadow and a tree casts 212121 feet: 73=h21\frac{7}{3} = \frac{h}{21}37​=21h​, so h=147÷3=49h = 147 \div 3 = 49h=147÷3=49.

Convert among a percent, a decimal, and a fraction

  1. Percent to fraction: write the number over 100100100 and simplify. Percent to decimal: shift two places left.
  2. Decimal to percent: shift two places right and attach the sign.
  3. Fraction to percent: rebuild over 100100100 when the denominator divides it, otherwise divide top by bottom and shift.

e.g. 58=5÷8=0.625=62.5%\frac{5}{8} = 5 \div 8 = 0.625 = 62.5\%85​=5÷8=0.625=62.5%.

Find the part, the percent, or the whole

  1. Label which two of the three you have; the whole follows the word "of".
  2. Part: multiply the whole by the percent as a decimal. Percent: divide the part by the whole and shift two places right.
  3. Whole: divide the part by the percent as a decimal.
  4. Check the size: under 100%100\%100% the recovered whole is the larger number.

e.g. 545454 is 72%72\%72% of what number? 54÷0.72=7554 \div 0.72 = 7554÷0.72=75.

Apply a percent change, or stack two

  1. Decide whether each percent is added (tax, tip, markup) or removed (a discount).
  2. Build the multiplier 1+p1001 + \frac{p}{100}1+100p​ or 1−p1001 - \frac{p}{100}1−100p​ and multiply the original by it.
  3. For a second change, multiply that result by the second multiplier.
  4. Multiply the factors together to read off the single net change.

e.g. A cost of 505050 marked up 60%60\%60% then reduced 25%25\%25%: 50×1.60×0.75=6050 \times 1.60 \times 0.75 = 6050×1.60×0.75=60.

Find the percent change, or recover the original

  1. Subtract for the amount of change, new minus original.
  2. Divide it by the ORIGINAL and shift two places right.
  3. Backwards, name what percent of the original the new value is, then divide the new value by that multiplier.
  4. Check by running the change forward again.

e.g. 121212 to 151515 is 312=25%\frac{3}{12} = 25\%123​=25% up, and 150150150 after a 25%25\%25% rise came from 150÷1.25=120150 \div 1.25 = 120150÷1.25=120.

Exam traps

  • Trap Reading a part-to-part ratio as a share of the whole, so "boys to girls is 2:32 : 32:3" makes boys 23\frac{2}{3}32​ of the class.

    Fix The whole is the SUM of the parts, so boys are 22+3=25\frac{2}{2 + 3} = \frac{2}{5}2+32​=52​. The 23\frac{2}{3}32​ compares boys to girls and is nobody's share of a total.

  • Trap Scaling or comparing ratios by adding, so 2:32 : 32:3 becomes 3:43 : 43:4, or 23\frac{2}{3}32​ and 45\frac{4}{5}54​ look equal because each part grew by 222.

    Fix Equivalence is multiplicative, so 2:32 : 32:3 scales to 4:64 : 64:6. Cross-multiplying settles the second case: 2×5=102 \times 5 = 102×5=10 but 3×4=123 \times 4 = 123×4=12.

  • Trap Multiplying straight across a proportion instead of along the diagonals, or dividing by the number you just used.

    Fix Multiply the diagonal you know, then divide by the number DIAGONALLY OPPOSITE the blank. In 49=14n\frac{4}{9} = \frac{14}{n}94​=n14​ that is 9×14=1269 \times 14 = 1269×14=126, then n=126÷4=31.5n = 126 \div 4 = 31.5n=126÷4=31.5.

  • Trap Building the two sides of a proportion in opposite orders, such as miles over hours on one side and hours over miles on the other.

    Fix Matching quantities belong in matching positions on both sides. A flipped side is false, yet it still cross-multiplies to a confident wrong answer.

  • Trap Dividing the change by the NEW value, or reusing one percent for the reverse move.

    Fix The base is the value BEFORE the change. 404040 up to 505050 is 1040=25%\frac{10}{40} = 25\%4010​=25%, but 505050 down to 404040 is 1050=20%\frac{10}{50} = 20\%5010​=20%, not 25%25\%25%.

  • Trap Multiplying by the bare percent, so "increase 200200200 by 15%15\%15%" gives 303030 and "30%30\%30% off 606060 dollars" gives 181818 dollars.

    Fix Those are only the part that changes. Use the multiplier: 1.15×200=2301.15 \times 200 = 2301.15×200=230, and 0.70×60=420.70 \times 60 = 420.70×60=42 dollars to pay. "p%p\%p% more than" means 1+p1001 + \frac{p}{100}1+100p​ times the base, not p100\frac{p}{100}100p​ times it.

  • Trap Expecting a p%p\%p% rise and a p%p\%p% fall to cancel out.

    Fix The two multipliers apply one after the other, so they multiply together rather than canceling. Up 20%20\%20% then down 20%20\%20% gives 1.2×0.8=0.961.2 \times 0.8 = 0.961.2×0.8=0.96, a 4%4\%4% net loss, in either order.

  • Trap Undoing a change by adding the percent back onto the new value.

    Fix The percent was figured on the larger original, so divide by the multiplier: after 20%20\%20% off, a price of 484848 came from 48÷0.80=6048 \div 0.80 = 6048÷0.80=60, not 48+0.20×48=57.6048 + 0.20 \times 48 = 57.6048+0.20×48=57.60.

    1. The club's raffle drum . Foundational, 11 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    A club's raffle uses identical paper slips, each carrying one member's name. The slips are folded the same way, tipped into a drum and stirred, and the organiser draws one out without looking. Twelve members entered and the drum holds 404040 slips in all: Maya put in 999, Owen put in 777, Priya put in 444, and the other nine members put in the remaining 202020 between them. The organiser did not enter.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find the probability that the slip drawn is one of Maya's. Give it as a fraction in lowest terms, as a decimal and as a percent, and say what in the setup lets you find it by counting at all.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Two more events for the same draw: that the slip carries the name of a member who entered, and that the slip carries the organiser's name. Find each probability, and say where each sits on the 000 to 111 scale and what that position means.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Another member reasons like this: twelve members have names in the drum, so each member has probability 112\frac{1}{12}121​ of winning. Explain what is wrong with counting that way, say what the equally likely outcomes actually are, and state what would have to be true of the entries for 112\frac{1}{12}121​ to be right.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Everything here rests on one question asked first: which outcomes are equally likely? The number you divide by has to count those outcomes and nothing else.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      An event can collect every outcome in the sample space, or none of them at all. Work out the favorable count in each of those two cases and let the fraction tell you where the value lands.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Maya and Priya did not put in the same number of slips. Ask whether a stirred drum can tell one member's slips from another's, and what would have to be equal before a count of members could work.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    P(Maya)=940=0.225=22.5%P(\text{Maya}) = \frac{9}{40} = 0.225 = 22.5\%P(Maya)=409​=0.225=22.5%, already in lowest terms. The 404040 slips are equally likely because they are identical, stirred, and drawn without looking.

    Part B

    Every slip carries an entered member's name, so that event has probability 4040=1\frac{40}{40} = 14040​=1, the top of the scale, and it is certain. No slip carries the organiser's name, so that event has probability 040=0\frac{0}{40} = 0400​=0, the bottom of the scale, and it is impossible.

    Part C

    The twelve members hold different numbers of slips, so they are not equally likely. The equally likely outcomes are the 404040 slips. Each member's probability would be 112\frac{1}{12}121​ only if all twelve held the same number of slips, which 404040 slips cannot be shared into anyway.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Start with the outcomes, because everything else is divided by their count. The drum holds 404040 slips, and the setup promises they are alike in every way that matters here: identical slips, folded the same way, stirred, and drawn without looking. That is what drawing at random means, and it is what makes the 404040 slips equally likely. Without that promise there would be nothing to count.

    Maya's slips are the favorable ones, and there are 999 of them, so her probability is the share of the 404040 that her slips take:

    P(Maya)=940.P(\text{Maya}) = \frac{9}{40}.P(Maya)=409​.

    That is already in lowest terms, since 9=3×39 = 3 \times 39=3×3 and 40=2×2×2×540 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 540=2×2×2×5 have no factor in common. For the decimal, divide:

    9÷40=0.225,9 \div 40 = 0.225,9÷40=0.225,

    and a percent is that decimal multiplied by 100100100, so 0.225=22.5%0.225 = 22.5\%0.225=22.5%. The three forms are one number written three ways, sitting a little under a quarter of the way up the scale from 000 to 111.

    Notice what the answer did not depend on: whose names are on the other slips, or how many people entered. Only two numbers matter, the favorable count and the total number of equally likely outcomes.

    Part B

    Both events use the same formula as part A. Only the favorable count is extreme.

    Every slip in the drum carries the name of a member who entered, so this event collects all 404040 outcomes:

    P(an entered member)=4040=1.P(\text{an entered member}) = \frac{40}{40} = 1.P(an entered member)=4040​=1.

    A probability of 111 is the top of the scale, and it says the event is certain: there is no way for the draw to miss it.

    The organiser did not enter, so not one slip carries the organiser's name and the event collects none of the outcomes:

    P(the organiser)=040=0.P(\text{the organiser}) = \frac{0}{40} = 0.P(the organiser)=400​=0.

    A probability of 000 is the bottom of the scale, and it says the event is impossible.

    These two are the ends that every other probability sits between, and the counting shows why nothing can escape them. A favorable count is never smaller than 000 and never larger than the total, so the fraction is never negative and never above 111. A raffle answer of 1.21.21.2 or −0.3-0.3−0.3 would be a signal that the counting had gone wrong, not a discovery about the drum.

    Part C

    The member has counted the wrong things. Counting gives a probability only when the things counted are equally likely, and the twelve members are not: Maya has 999 slips in the drum and Priya has 444, while a stirred drum knows nothing about names. What is equally likely here is a slip, not a member.

    So the sample space is the 404040 slips, and each member's probability is the share of the slips that member holds:

    P(Maya)=940,P(Owen)=740,P(Priya)=440.P(\text{Maya}) = \frac{9}{40}, \qquad P(\text{Owen}) = \frac{7}{40}, \qquad P(\text{Priya}) = \frac{4}{40}.P(Maya)=409​,P(Owen)=407​,P(Priya)=404​.

    Those are three different numbers, so no single fraction can serve for every member. As a check that nothing has gone missing, the three named members and the nine others hold 9+7+4+20=409 + 7 + 4 + 20 = 409+7+4+20=40 slips between them, so their probabilities add to 4040=1\frac{40}{40} = 14040​=1, the whole draw.

    Now the condition. For 112\frac{1}{12}121​ to be right, every member would have to hold the same number of slips, because only then are the twelve members equally likely and only then can they be counted like the faces of a fair die. This raffle cannot be repaired that way, since 404040 slips do not share equally among twelve members, but a drum of 363636 or 484848 would.

    The habit to carry away is that how many possibilities are there is never the whole question. The question is how many equally likely possibilities there are, and the two counts can be very different.

    In one line

    Maya holds 999 of the 404040 equally likely slips, so P(Maya)=940=0.225=22.5%P(\text{Maya}) = \frac{9}{40} = 0.225 = 22.5\%P(Maya)=409​=0.225=22.5%; the slips are equally likely because they are identical, stirred and drawn without looking. Every slip carries an entered member's name, so that event has probability 4040=1\frac{40}{40} = 14040​=1 and is certain, while the organiser entered no slip, so that event has probability 040=0\frac{0}{40} = 0400​=0 and is impossible. Counting members instead of slips fails because the twelve members hold different numbers of slips and so are not equally likely: Maya's is 940\frac{9}{40}409​, Owen's 740\frac{7}{40}407​ and Priya's 440\frac{4}{40}404​, while the nine other members hold 2040\frac{20}{40}4020​ between them, and those four shares add to 111. Only if all twelve held the same number of slips would each have probability 112\frac{1}{12}121​, and 404040 slips cannot be shared equally among twelve members.

    2. The prize wheel at the school fair . Application, 12 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Application 12 pts

    The school fair's prize wheel is a circle cut into 202020 wedges of the same size, so the pointer is as likely to stop on any one wedge as on any other. Two wedges are marked 'gift card', five are marked 'pencil', and the remaining thirteen are marked 'try again'.

    2. B
      Part B.

      Find the probability that a spin gives no prize at all, in two ways: by counting the 'try again' wedges directly, and by taking the complement of your part A probability. Then say why the two routes cannot disagree.

    3. C
      Part C.

      The operator adds five more 'try again' wedges, keeping every wedge the same size, and tells the queue that this 'changes nobody's chance of a prize, because the same prize wedges are still there'. Decide whether that is right, and argue it.

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

    The answer

    Part A

    P(prize)=2+520=720=0.35=35%P(\text{prize}) = \frac{2 + 5}{20} = \frac{7}{20} = 0.35 = 35\%P(prize)=202+5​=207​=0.35=35%.

    Counting gives 1320=0.65=65%\frac{13}{20} = 0.65 = 65\%2013​=0.65=65%, and the complement gives 1−720=13201 - \frac{7}{20} = \frac{13}{20}1−207​=2013​. The two events split the same 202020 wedges, so they cannot disagree.

    Part C

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The wedges are all the same size, so the 202020 wedges are the equally likely outcomes and the total is 202020. A prize of some kind means a gift card or a pencil, so the favorable wedges are the two marked 'gift card' together with the five marked 'pencil':

    2+5=7 wedges.2 + 5 = 7 \text{ wedges}.2+5=7 wedges.

    That addition is the addition principle from the counting lesson: the two groups do not overlap, since no wedge carries two markings, so their sizes add.

    Divide the favorable count by the total:

    P(prize)=720=0.35=35%.P(\text{prize}) = \frac{7}{20} = 0.35 = 35\%.P(prize)=207​=0.35=35%.

    The fraction is already in lowest terms, since 777 is prime and does not divide 202020. The decimal comes from 7÷20=0.357 \div 20 = 0.357÷20=0.35, or from scaling the fraction to hundredths, since 720=35100\frac{7}{20} = \frac{35}{100}207​=10035​, which reads off the percent at the same time. Read it back: a little over a third of the wheel pays something.

    Part B

    Count directly first. The wedges marked 'try again' are the thirteen that are left, so

    P(no prize)=1320=0.65=65%.P(\text{no prize}) = \frac{13}{20} = 0.65 = 65\%.P(no prize)=2013​=0.65=65%.

    Now the complement route. 'No prize' is exactly 'not a prize', so subtract from 111:

    P(no prize)=1−720=2020−720=1320.P(\text{no prize}) = 1 - \frac{7}{20} = \frac{20}{20} - \frac{7}{20} = \frac{13}{20}.P(no prize)=1−207​=2020​−207​=2013​.

    The two agree, and they were never free to do otherwise. Every wedge on the wheel either pays something or does not, never both and never neither, so the two events split the 202020 wedges between them with nothing left over:

    7+13=20.7 + 13 = 20.7+13=20.

    Divide that by 202020 and it says the two probabilities add to 111, which is the complement rule written the other way round. The direct count and the subtraction are the same counting done from opposite ends.

    That is also why the rule subtracts rather than flips. Turning 720\frac{7}{20}207​ upside down would give 207\frac{20}{7}720​, which is bigger than 111 and off the scale, so it cannot be anybody's probability.

    Part C

    The operator is looking at one of the two numbers in a probability and ignoring the other.

    Nothing has happened to the prize wedges: there are still seven of them. But a probability is not a count of favorable outcomes, it is their share of all the equally likely outcomes, and the wheel now has more of those. Five extra wedges make 20+5=2520 + 5 = 2520+5=25 in all, so

    P(prize)=725=0.28=28%.P(\text{prize}) = \frac{7}{25} = 0.28 = 28\%.P(prize)=257​=0.28=28%.

    Compare that with the wheel as it was by putting both over a common denominator:

    720=35100,725=28100.\frac{7}{20} = \frac{35}{100}, \qquad \frac{7}{25} = \frac{28}{100}.207​=10035​,257​=10028​.

    So the chance of a prize has dropped by seven parts in every hundred spins. The operator's sentence would be true only if a probability were the numerator alone.

    A quick way to feel it: the same seven prize wedges now have more company, so every spin has more ways to miss them. Push the idea and it becomes obvious. A wheel of a hundred equal wedges carrying the same seven prizes would pay on only 7100\frac{7}{100}1007​ of its spins, and nobody would call that the same chance.

    The general statement is worth keeping. Adding outcomes that are not favorable leaves the numerator alone and raises the denominator, which can never make the probability larger, and makes it strictly smaller whenever anything was favorable to begin with.

    In one line

    A prize comes from 2+5=72 + 5 = 72+5=7 of the 202020 equal wedges, so P(prize)=720=0.35=35%P(\text{prize}) = \frac{7}{20} = 0.35 = 35\%P(prize)=207​=0.35=35%. No prize is the other thirteen wedges, 1320=0.65=65%\frac{13}{20} = 0.65 = 65\%2013​=0.65=65%, and the complement route gives the same, since 1−720=13201 - \frac{7}{20} = \frac{13}{20}1−207​=2013​; they agree because every wedge falls in exactly one of the two events and 7+13=207 + 13 = 207+13=20. A probability is a share of the whole, and the whole grew.

    Another way: Work in percents from the start

    Because 202020 divides 100100100 evenly, each wedge is worth a whole number of percent: 120=5100=5%\frac{1}{20} = \frac{5}{100} = 5\%201​=1005​=5%. So the two gift-card wedges are 10%10\%10%, the five pencil wedges are 25%25\%25%, and

    10%+25%=35%.10\% + 25\% = 35\%.10%+25%=35%.

    The complement is then a subtraction from the whole written as a percent,

    100%−35%=65%,100\% - 35\% = 65\%,100%−35%=65%,

    with no fractions anywhere. The enlarged wheel does not spoil the trick, since 252525 also divides 100100100 evenly and each wedge is now worth 4%4\%4%, giving 7×4%=28%7 \times 4\% = 28\%7×4%=28%. What has changed is the value of one wedge, which is itself a sign that the whole has moved.

    When it is worth it When the number of equally likely outcomes divides 100100100 evenly, so one outcome is worth a whole number of percent and the counting can be done in percents. It is no help when it does not, and then the fraction is the honest starting point.

    Part B

    Counting outcomes gives a probability only when those outcomes are equally likely, and that is a promise about the object, not about how many things the object can do. A fair coin makes the promise because its two faces are alike; a wheel makes it because its wedges are the same size; a fair die makes it because its six faces are the same. In those cases the object's own symmetry does the work, and the words fair and equal are how a problem hands you that promise. Symmetry is not the only route to it: stirring a drum of identical slips, or drawing without looking from a bag of identical tiles, makes the outcomes equally likely by the way the choice is made rather than by the shape of the thing chosen.

    A drawing pin has neither route open to it.     12=0.5against1750=0.34.\frac{1}{2} = 0.5 \qquad \text{against} \qquad \frac{17}{50} = 0.34.21​=0.5against5017​=0.34.

'At least one dried' is the opposite event, so the complement rule gives 1−27=571 - \frac{2}{7} = \frac{5}{7}1−72​=75​, matching the direct count 42−12=3042 - 12 = 3042−12=30 and 3042=57\frac{30}{42} = \frac{5}{7}4230​=75​. With the first marker replaced, the second stage offers all 777 markers again, so the counts become 7×7=497 \times 7 = 497×7=49 and 4×4=164 \times 4 = 164×4=16, giving 1649\frac{16}{49}4916​ against 27=1449\frac{2}{7} = \frac{14}{49}72​=4914​. Replacing raises the probability because keeping a marker that writes leaves a box that is poorer in markers that write, 333 of 666 rather than 444 of 777.

    A drawer holds 888 batteries that look alike: 555 are charged and 333 are flat. Two are taken one after the other, without looking and without replacing the first. Find the probability that both are charged, and the probability that at least one is flat.

    The answer

    P(both charged)=5×48×7=2056=514P(\text{both charged}) = \frac{5 \times 4}{8 \times 7} = \frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14}P(both charged)=8×75×4​=5620​=145​, and P(at least one flat)=1−514=914P(\text{at least one flat}) = 1 - \frac{5}{14} = \frac{9}{14}P(at least one flat)=1−145​=149​, which the count 3656\frac{36}{56}5636​ confirms.

    Count the ordered pairs. The first battery is one of 888 and the second is one of the 777 left, so

    8×7=56 ordered pairs,8 \times 7 = 56 \text{ ordered pairs},8×7=56 ordered pairs,

    all equally likely, since the batteries look alike and are taken without looking. Both are charged when the first is one of the 555 charged and the second is one of the 444 charged still in the drawer:

    5×4=20,P(both charged)=2056=514.5 \times 4 = 20, \qquad P(\text{both charged}) = \frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14}.5×4=20,P(both charged)=5620​=145​.

    P(at least one flat)=1−514=914,P(\text{at least one flat}) = 1 - \frac{5}{14} = \frac{9}{14},P(at least one flat)=1−145​=149​,

    and the direct count agrees, since 56−20=3656 - 20 = 3656−20=36 ordered pairs contain a flat battery and 3656=914\frac{36}{56} = \frac{9}{14}5636​=149​.

Numerical Data Displays and Spread

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Two classes take the same ten-point quiz, and both classes have a mean score of 777. In one class the scores bunch close together, between 555 and 999. In the other they are scattered all the way from 222 to 101010. The mean cannot tell the two classes apart. Pictures of the scores can show what it misses: where the values gather and how widely they spread. A dot plot shows every value, a histogram groups values into intervals, and a box plot summarizes the data using five numbers.

Learning goals

  • Build a dot plot and a histogram, and tell a histogram from a bar graph
  • Find the five-number summary, with quartiles as the medians of the two halves
  • Compute the interquartile range Q3−Q1Q_3 - Q_1Q3​−Q1​ and explain what it measures
  • Draw a box plot from a five-number summary, and read one back
  • Compare two data sets by center and spread, and describe clusters, skew, and outliers

Dot plots: every value on a number line

The last lesson used bar graphs for categories, such as pets or seasons. Quiz scores are numbers on a scale, so a number line suits them. A dot plot puts one dot above the number line for each value. Repeated values stack up in a column. Some books call this a line plot; it is different from the line graph of the last lesson.

Here are the two classes, 111111 students each:

Class A:5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9Class B:2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 9, 10\begin{aligned} \text{Class A:}\quad & 5,\ 6,\ 6,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 8,\ 8,\ 9 \\ \text{Class B:}\quad & 2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7,\ 8,\ 8,\ 9,\ 9,\ 9,\ 10 \end{aligned}Class A:Class B:​5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8, 92, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 9, 10​

Each list adds up to 777777, and 77÷11=777 \div 11 = 777÷11=7, so both means are 777.

Count all the dots to find the number of values: 111111 in each class. The value below the tallest stack is the mode. Class A’s mode is 777, with five dots, and Class B’s is 999, with three. As in the lesson on averages, stacks tied above the rest give several modes. If several values occur and all their stacks have the same height, we say there is no mode.

The dots also show the shape of the data. Class A’s scores form a tight group around 777. Class B’s crowd together at the high end and trail off toward the low scores on the left.

To draw one by hand, mark a number line from the smallest value to the largest, with evenly spaced marks. Then place one dot per value, stacking repeats with equal gaps so that the stacks can be compared fairly.

Check your understanding

Nine students say how many pets they own: 0,2,1,1,3,1,0,2,10, 2, 1, 1, 3, 1, 0, 2, 10,2,1,1,3,1,0,2,1. In a dot plot of these answers, which value has the tallest stack, and how many dots are in it?

Answer choices

Count the dots each value gets: 000 appears twice, 111 four times, 222 twice, and 333 once.

2+4+2+1=92 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 92+4+2+1=9

The counts add to nine, a useful check on the total. The tallest stack is the 444 dots above the value 111.

Histograms: bars over number intervals

A dot plot can get crowded when many values are close together. We can group them into intervals, stretches of the number line, and count how many fall in each. A histogram draws a bar over each interval. We will use equal-width intervals, so each bar’s height shows the number of values it holds.

Worked example 1 Sort twenty reading times into intervals

Twenty students record how many whole minutes they read last night:

25, 12, 30, 4, 21, 46, 15, 27, 19, 35,20, 8, 25, 38, 15, 23, 41, 29, 18, 32.\begin{aligned} &25,\ 12,\ 30,\ 4,\ 21,\ 46,\ 15,\ 27,\ 19,\ 35, \\ &20,\ 8,\ 25,\ 38,\ 15,\ 23,\ 41,\ 29,\ 18,\ 32. \end{aligned}​25, 12, 30, 4, 21, 46, 15, 27, 19, 35,20, 8, 25, 38, 15, 23, 41, 29, 18, 32.​

Use intervals 101010 minutes wide: 000 to 999, 101010 to 191919, and so on up to 404040 to 494949, which covers the longest time, 464646. Go through the list once and make a tally mark for each time in its interval. Then count them:

MinutesFrequency
000 to 999222
101010 to 191919555
202020 to 292929777
303030 to 393939444
404040 to 494949222

Check the count: the frequencies must add to the twenty students.

2+5+7+4+2=20.2 + 5 + 7 + 4 + 2 = 20.2+5+7+4+2=20.

Each frequency becomes the height of one bar.

A bar graph’s bars stand for categories, such as Dog and Cat. They sit apart, and these categories could be put in any order. A histogram’s intervals have a fixed order on the number line, from smallest to largest. Its bars touch because the intervals are side by side.

Every whole number of minutes belongs to exactly one interval. That is why the labels read 000 to 999 and 101010 to 191919, and not 000 to 101010 and 101010 to 202020: with those labels, a time of exactly 101010 minutes would belong to two bars. Equal widths let us compare bar heights fairly; this course always uses them.

Grouping loses detail. This histogram shows that 777 students read for 202020 to 292929 minutes, but not their exact times. We can locate the median’s interval: the first two bars hold 777 values, and the first three hold 141414, so the 101010th and 111111th values are both in 202020 to 292929. Their mean, the median, is in that interval too. The longest time is in 404040 to 494949. Neither exact value can be read from the histogram alone.

Check your understanding

In the reading-time histogram above, which bar counts a student who read for exactly 202020 minutes, and how many students read for fewer than 202020 minutes?

Answer choices

A time of 202020 minutes is in the interval 202020 to 292929, since the interval 101010 to 191919 stops at 191919.

Fewer than 202020 minutes means the first two bars together:

2+5=72 + 5 = 72+5=7

So 777 students read for fewer than 202020 minutes.

Quartiles and the five-number summary

The median cuts sorted data into two halves. Cutting each half at its own middle splits the sorted list into four parts of nearly equal size. The three cut points are called the quartiles.

Start with Class B, already sorted. Its 111111 values have a single middle value, the 666th, so the median is 888. Leave that middle value out, and five values are left on each side of it. The lower half is the five values before it, 2,4,5,6,72, 4, 5, 6, 72,4,5,6,7, and the upper half is the five values after it, 8,9,9,9,108, 9, 9, 9, 108,9,9,9,10. The first quartile, Q1Q_1Q1​, is the median of the lower half, and the third quartile, Q3Q_3Q3​, is the median of the upper half:

2, 4, 5Q1, 6, 7, 8median, 8, 9, 9Q3, 9, 102,\ 4,\ \underset{Q_1}{5},\ 6,\ 7,\ \underset{\text{median}}{8},\ 8,\ 9,\ \underset{Q_3}{9},\ 9,\ 102, 4, Q1​5​, 6, 7, median8​, 8, 9, Q3​9​, 9, 10

So Q1=5Q_1 = 5Q1​=5 and Q3=9Q_3 = 9Q3​=9. Leaving aside the three marked places, there are two values in each of four groups: 222 and 444, then 666 and 777, then 888 and 999, then 999 and 101010. Each group holds about a quarter of the places in the list, which gives quartiles their name. The median halves the list; Q1Q_1Q1​ and Q3Q_3Q3​ halve those halves.

With an even count, the median is the mean of the two middle values. Nothing is left out when we split the list. The method is:

  1. Sort the values from least to greatest.
  2. Find the median.
  3. Split the list into halves. With an even count, take the first half and the second half. With an odd count, leave the middle value out of both halves.
  4. Q1Q_1Q1​ is the median of the lower half, and Q3Q_3Q3​ is the median of the upper half. A half with an even count takes the mean of its two middle values, just as the median does.

The list needs at least two values so that both halves have a value. Some books and calculators keep the middle value in both halves when the count is odd. That can give different quartiles; this course always leaves it out.

The five-number summary lists the minimum, Q1Q_1Q1​, the median, Q3Q_3Q3​, and the maximum, in that order. For Class B it is 2,5,8,9,102, 5, 8, 9, 102,5,8,9,10. The same steps give 5,6,7,8,95, 6, 7, 8, 95,6,7,8,9 for Class A.

A rider times the wait for a bus on ten mornings, in minutes: 12,5,20,9,3,14,8,15,6,1112, 5, 20, 9, 3, 14, 8, 15, 6, 1112,5,20,9,3,14,8,15,6,11.

median=9+112=10.\text{median} = \frac{9 + 11}{2} = 10.median=29+11​=10.

The lower half is 3,5,6,8,93, 5, 6, 8, 93,5,6,8,9, with middle value 666, and the upper half is 11,12,14,15,2011, 12, 14, 15, 2011,12,14,15,20, with middle value 141414. So Q1=6Q_1 = 6Q1​=6 and Q3=14Q_3 = 14Q3​=14. The smallest value is 333 and the largest is 202020, so the five-number summary is

3, 6, 10, 14, 20.3,\ 6,\ 10,\ 14,\ 20.3, 6, 10, 14, 20.

The interquartile range

The range measures spread using the two most extreme values. The interquartile range, or IQR, measures the spread of the middle of the data instead. Class A’s middle scores run from Q1=6Q_1 = 6Q1​=6 to Q3=8Q_3 = 8Q3​=8, a stretch 222 points wide. Class B’s run from 555 to 999, a stretch 444 points wide, twice as wide. That width is the IQR:

IQR=Q3−Q1.\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1.IQR=Q3​−Q1​.

Like the range, the IQR measures spread, not center, and it is never negative, because Q3Q_3Q3​ is never below Q1Q_1Q1​. An IQR of 000 is possible. For 1,5,5,5,5,5,5,5,121, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 121,5,5,5,5,5,5,5,12, both quartiles are 555, so the IQR is 000, yet the range is 111111. The middle of that data is piled on one value, though the values outside the middle still differ.

Moving the smallest value lower or the largest value higher can change the range without changing the IQR. With our quartile method, this holds for lists of at least six values.

Why moving an end value leaves the quartiles alone#

Suppose the student in Class A who scored 555 was marked wrongly and really scored 000. The sorted list becomes

0, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9.0,\ 6,\ 6,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 8,\ 8,\ 9.0, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9.

The 000 is still first, and every other score keeps its place. With 111111 values, Q1Q_1Q1​ is the 333rd value, the median is the 666th, and Q3Q_3Q3​ is the 999th. Those places still hold 666, 777, and 888, so the IQR stays 8−6=28 - 6 = 28−6=2. The range uses the smallest value itself, so it jumps from 9−5=49 - 5 = 49−5=4 to 9−0=99 - 0 = 99−0=9.

The same reasoning works for any list of six or more values. Each half has at least three values, so its middle value, or pair of middle values, excludes its two ends. Neither quartile uses the minimum or maximum. Changing an end value while keeping it at that end leaves the other values in place, so both quartiles and the IQR stay unchanged.

Both conditions matter. If Class A’s 555 becomes 101010, it changes place. The sorted list becomes 6,6,7,7,7,7,7,8,8,9,106, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 106,6,7,7,7,7,7,8,8,9,10, and Q1Q_1Q1​ rises to 777, so the IQR falls to 111.

A shorter list can also behave differently. In 2,4,6,8,102, 4, 6, 8, 102,4,6,8,10, the upper half is just 888 and 101010. Changing 101010 to 202020 keeps it last, but moves Q3Q_3Q3​ from 999 to 141414.

∎

A value far from the rest, like that 000, is an outlier. The range includes that far-off value; the IQR measures the width between the quartiles. When an extreme value stretches the range, the IQR can give a better picture of how spread out the central values are. It can still change if we add a new value, move a value past others, or change an end value in a short list.

Box plots: five numbers as a picture

A box plot, also called a box-and-whisker plot, draws the five-number summary above a number line. To draw one:

  1. Draw a number line that covers the minimum and the maximum, with evenly spaced marks.
  2. Draw a box from Q1Q_1Q1​ to Q3Q_3Q3​, and a line across the box at the median.
  3. Draw a whisker, a line from each side of the box out to the minimum and to the maximum.

In this lesson the whiskers always reach the minimum and the maximum. Some books stop a whisker early and mark far-off values as separate dots, using a rule for outliers that later courses teach.

The five marked numbers divide the sorted list into four parts of about equal size, counted by place. A longer section does not mean more values. Class B’s left whisker, from 222 to 555, is three times as long as its right whisker, from 999 to 101010. Yet each extends over two places beyond its quartile: 222 and 444 on the left, 999 and 101010 on the right. The left section is longer because those scores are more spread out.

With many distinct values, each section holds about a quarter of the values and the box about half. Very short lists can differ greatly: for 2,4,62, 4, 62,4,6, the quartiles are 222 and 666, so all three values lie on or inside the box. Both whiskers have zero length. Repeated values at a quartile can put extra scores on a boundary. Class A has 999 of its 111111 scores from Q1=6Q_1 = 6Q1​=6 to Q3=8Q_3 = 8Q3​=8, counting both edges, because both 666 and 888 appear twice. So a box need not contain exactly half the scores. Repeats can even make two of the five numbers equal, giving a section zero length: its values are all the same.

The box plot gives up detail for that summary. The dot plot shows that three students scored 999, but the box plot shows neither that nor how many students there are.

The values run from 333 to 202020, so draw a number line from 000 to 202020 with a mark every 222 minutes. Draw the box from Q1=6Q_1 = 6Q1​=6 to Q3=14Q_3 = 14Q3​=14, with a line across it at the median, 101010. Then draw the left whisker out to the minimum, 333, and the right whisker out to the maximum, 202020.

The right whisker, 141414 to 202020, covers 666 minutes; the left, 333 to 666, covers 333 minutes. Each extends over two mornings beyond its quartile. The longest waits are more spread out than the shortest.

Comparing data sets: center, spread, and shape

To compare two data sets, look at their centers, then their spreads, then their shapes. Box plots drawn on one shared number line make this quick.

Worked example 4 Compare the two classes

Use the five-number summaries, 5,6,7,8,95, 6, 7, 8, 95,6,7,8,9 for Class A and 2,5,8,9,102, 5, 8, 9, 102,5,8,9,10 for Class B.

The medians are 777 and 888, so Class B’s middle score is higher, even though both means are 777.

The IQRs are 222 and 444, and the ranges are 444 and 888. By either measure, Class B’s scores are twice as spread out.

Class A’s box and whiskers are symmetric around 777, matching the balanced shape of its dot plot. Class B’s median is near the right end of its box, and its left whisker is long. Its dot plot confirms that scores crowd together near the top and trail off toward the low end. Those low scores pull its mean down to 777, below its median of 888.

A cluster is a group of values close together, like Class B’s six scores from 888 to 101010. A gap is a stretch with no values. When the data has a long tail on one side, it is skewed toward that side. Class B’s tail extends left, so it is skewed left. When the two sides are roughly mirror images, the data is roughly symmetric; Class A’s two sides match exactly.

Here we describe these shapes by eye, including outliers: say which value looks far from the rest and why. Later courses give numerical rules for identifying them. Use a dot plot when you need this detail; a box plot alone cannot show every cluster or gap.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Finding quartiles without sorting. Quartiles, like the median, are found by place in the sorted list. The middle of an unsorted list means nothing.
  • Keeping the middle value in both halves. With an odd count, this course leaves the median out of both halves. Eleven values split into two halves of five, not six.
  • Reading a long section as more values. The four sections split the sorted list into about equal parts by place, so a longer section means its values are more spread out, not more numerous.
  • Treating a histogram like a bar graph. Its bars stand for number intervals in a fixed order, so they touch, and a value on a boundary such as 202020 belongs to exactly one interval.
  • Trusting the range when there is an outlier. One far-off value can stretch the range a long way while the IQR, built from the middle of the list, barely moves.

Key takeaways

  • Dot plots and histograms. A dot plot shows every value as a dot above a number line. A histogram counts values in equal intervals, and its bars touch in a fixed order, unlike a bar graph’s categories.
  • Quartiles. Sort, find the median, and split the list into halves, leaving the middle value out when the count is odd. Q1Q_1Q1​ and Q3Q_3Q3​ are the medians of the two halves.
  • Five-number summary and IQR. The summary is the minimum, Q1Q_1Q1​, the median, Q3Q_3Q3​ and the maximum. The IQR, Q3−Q1Q_3 - Q_1Q3​−Q1​, measures the spread of the middle of the data; in a list of six or more values, moving an end value further out changes the range but not the IQR.
  • Box plots. The box runs from Q1Q_1Q1​ to Q3Q_3Q3​ with a line at the median, and the whiskers reach the minimum and maximum. The sections roughly divide sorted positions into quarters. Short lists and repeated boundary values can make the counts differ greatly. A longer section means more spread, not more values.
  • Comparing. Compare center with the medians, spread with the IQR and range, and shape in words: clusters, gaps, skew and outliers.

A bit of history (optional)

John Tukey, an American mathematician and statistician, treated a new set of data like a detective’s case. Before settling on an explanation, look for clues. A quick picture might reveal a pattern or an odd value hidden in a long table of numbers.

His tools did not require a computer. The methods in his book could be worked with pencil and paper, with a calculator if available. One was a box-and-whisker plot: a compact picture of the middle and the spread, easy to sketch beside another group for comparison. Tukey presented it in a preliminary book edition in 1970, and it reached a wider audience with the 1977 edition of Exploratory Data Analysis.

The book opened by calling data exploration “detective work”. The sketches were a way to notice clues and ask new questions before deciding what the evidence meant.

Basic Counting Principles

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

The last lesson studied recorded data. Now we ask how many things could happen. A diner offers 444 sandwiches and 333 drinks, and a lunch includes one of each. You could list every lunch: ham with tea, ham with milk, and so on. But a bigger menu would make that list hard to manage.

Counting principles find the number of possibilities without listing them all. Multiplication counts equal groups of choices; addition combines separate groups. The key is deciding what one complete outcome contains. These counts will help us measure chance in the next lesson.

Learning goals

  • Explain multiplication of stage counts using a tree or grid
  • Add group sizes when one choice comes from separate, non-overlapping groups
  • Decide whether a count multiplies or adds by checking what one complete outcome contains
  • Count arrangements and codes, accounting for restricted choices and whether items may repeat

The multiplication principle

Start with the lunch, but shrink it so we can see everything. Say there are 222 sandwiches, ham and veg, and 333 drinks, tea, milk, and juice. A tree diagram lays out the choices in stages. Draw one branch for each sandwich, then from the end of every sandwich branch draw one branch for each drink. Each path from left to right makes one complete lunch, and the tips on the far right are all the lunches there are.

The 222 sandwich branches each split into 333 drink branches. The tips form 222 equal groups of 333, so 3+3=2×3=63 + 3 = 2 \times 3 = 63+3=2×3=6 lunches. This is the multiplication principle, also called the fundamental counting principle: multiply the stage counts when each stage offers the same number of choices regardless of the earlier choices. The number may differ from stage to stage, as 222 and 333 do here.

Why mmm choices then nnn choices give m×nm \times nm×n outcomes#

Suppose you choose one of 333 shirts and one of 222 hats. A tree has 333 shirt branches, each ending in 222 hat branches. Every outfit appears at exactly one tip: its shirt determines the first branch and its hat the second. The 333 groups of 222 tips give 2+2+2=3×2=62 + 2 + 2 = 3 \times 2 = 62+2+2=3×2=6 outfits.

Nothing in this reasoning depends on having 333 shirts or 222 hats. If the first stage has mmm choices and every first choice allows nnn second choices, the tree has mmm groups of nnn tips. Each tip is one complete outcome, and each outcome appears once. For positive whole-number counts mmm and nnn, adding those equal groups gives

n+n+⋯+n⏟m equal groups=m×n.\underbrace{n + n + \cdots + n}_{m\text{ equal groups}} = m \times n.m equal groupsn+n+⋯+n​​=m×n.

The second-stage options may differ between branches; only their number must stay the same. If either stage has no choices, there are no complete outcomes, and the product is 000.

∎

Trees, lists, and grids

A tree also gives an organized list: read its tips from top to bottom. Keep one sandwich fixed while running through all the drinks, then move to the next sandwich. This fixed order helps you check that no lunch was skipped or counted twice.

A grid shows the same count as a rectangle. Put the sandwiches down the side and the drinks across the top, and each cell is one lunch. A rectangle with 222 rows of 333 cells holds 2×3=62 \times 3 = 62×3=6 cells. That is the multiplication principle in picture form, since the area of a rectangle is rows times columns.

Worked example 1 Count lunches at the full diner

The diner from the opening offers 444 sandwiches and 333 drinks, and a lunch is one sandwich and one drink. How many different lunches are possible?

First choose a sandwich, then a drink. Every sandwich can pair with every drink, so each of the 444 sandwich choices has 333 drink choices:

4×3=12.4 \times 3 = 12.4×3=12.

There are 121212 possible lunches. As a check, a tree would draw 444 sandwich branches, each splitting into 333 drinks. That gives 444 groups of 333 tips, which is 121212 tips in all.

Three or more stages

The tree can keep growing. If a third stage gives every existing outcome 444 new branches, there are 444 times as many tips. Multiply by that stage’s count too. The same reasoning works for any number of stages, as long as the number of choices at each stage does not depend on which earlier choices were made.

Worked example 2 Build a sundae in three stages

A sundae is built in three stages: choose 111 of 333 ice-cream flavors, then 111 of 222 sauces, then 111 of 444 toppings. How many different sundaes are possible?

At every stage the full set of options is still there, whatever was chosen before, so multiply the three stage counts together:

3×2×4=24.3 \times 2 \times 4 = 24.3×2×4=24.

There are 242424 sundaes. You can see the multiplying happen step by step. The flavor and sauce alone give 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6 combinations, and then each of those 666 splits into 444 topping choices, 6×4=246 \times 4 = 246×4=24.

Check your understanding

A smoothie shop lets you pick one of 333 sizes, one of 555 flavors, and one of 222 add-ins. How many different smoothies (one size, one flavor, and one add-in) are possible?

Answer choices

A smoothie is a sequence of three choices, a size, then a flavor, then an add-in. Every stage offers the same options no matter what came before, so use the multiplication principle on all three stage counts.

3×5×2=303 \times 5 \times 2 = 303×5×2=30

Stopping at 151515 multiplies only the size and the flavor and forgets the add-in. Getting 666 multiplies only the size and the add-in and forgets the flavor. Adding the counts to get 101010 would answer a different question, picking just one item from the three groups instead of one of each.

The addition principle

Sometimes you choose just one item from several separate groups. If the groups do not overlap, adding their sizes counts the available items.

Suppose a prize shelf holds 555 books and 333 board games, and you take exactly one item home. Your prize is either a book or a game, never both at once. Because no item belongs to both groups, you can slide the two groups together into one pile of 5+3=85 + 3 = 85+3=8 items. Choosing one prize is then just choosing one item from that pile. So there are 888 possible prizes. This is the addition principle. If you choose one item from groups that share no members, the number of choices is the sum of the group sizes.

The words “and” and “or” can help you remember the difference. One book and one game gives 5×3=155 \times 3 = 155×3=15 pairs, because each book can go with any game. One prize, a book or a game, gives 5+3=85 + 3 = 85+3=8 choices. Check what a complete outcome contains rather than deciding from a word alone.

Addition also handles a tree with unequal branches. Suppose the diner allows 222 drinks with ham but 333 with veg. The ham lunches and veg lunches are separate cases, so there are 2+3=52 + 3 = 52+3=5 lunches. Multiplying 2×32 \times 32×3 would incorrectly give ham all 333 drink choices.

Worked example 3 Add or multiply? Reading the problem

A club must send one representative to a meeting. The representative may be any of the 777 seniors or any of the 666 juniors. How many choices does the club have?

The club picks one person, and that person comes from one of two separate groups (a senior or a junior, not both). No one is counted in both groups, so this is a single choice from separate groups: add.

7+6=13.7 + 6 = 13.7+6=13.

There are 131313 choices. Now compare a different task: send one senior and one junior as a pair of representatives. That is a sequence of two choices, so it multiplies, 7×6=427 \times 6 = 427×6=42. Do not decide from a single word alone. Ask what one complete answer contains. One representative contains a single name from one group, so it adds. A pair contains one name from each group, so it multiplies.

Check your understanding

A lunch special lets you choose your single main course from 444 hot dishes or 333 cold salads, and you choose exactly one dish. How many choices do you have for your one dish?

Answer choices

You choose exactly one dish, and it comes from one of two separate groups: a hot dish or a cold salad. So this is the addition principle.

4+3=74 + 3 = 74+3=7

Multiplying to get 121212 would count pairs of one hot dish and one cold salad. But you are choosing only one dish, not one of each.

Check your understanding

A cafe's combo is either a sandwich or a salad. A sandwich is one of 444 breads with one of 333 fillings, and a salad is one of 555 dressings with one of 222 toppings. How many different combos are possible?

Answer choices

A combo is one whole case or the other, so work out each case on its own first. A sandwich is a sequence of two choices, 4×3=124 \times 3 = 124×3=12 combos. A salad is also a sequence of two choices, 5×2=105 \times 2 = 105×2=10 combos. The two cases share no member, so add the case totals.

12+10=2212 + 10 = 2212+10=22

Getting 120120120 multiplies all four numbers together, as if one combo needed a bread, a filling, a dressing, and a topping all at once. Getting 141414 adds all four numbers instead of multiplying within each case first. Getting 171717 forgets to multiply the salad's two stages, using 555 instead of 5×2=105 \times 2 = 105×2=10.

Counting arrangements stage by stage

A special kind of counting asks in how many orders a set of items can be placed. Take 333 different books, A, B, and C, that you want to line up on a shelf. Treat each shelf position as a stage. For the first position you may use any of the 333 books. Once that book is placed it is used up, so only 222 books remain for the second position. After that, just 111 book remains for the third. The choices shrink at each stage, and by the multiplication principle you multiply them:

3×2×1=6.3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6.3×2×1=6.

Written out, those six orders are ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, and CBA: every way to line up three different books.

The multiplication principle still applies: whichever book goes first, exactly 222 remain for second place. The available books change, but their number does not. If you fill only the first few places from a larger set, multiply the counts for those places and stop.

Worked example 4 Hand out gold, silver, and bronze

Six runners finish a race, and the top three earn gold, silver, and bronze medals. In how many ways can the three medals be awarded?

Award the medals as a sequence of three stages, watching the choices shrink. Any of the 666 runners can win gold. Once gold is decided, 555 runners remain for silver, and then 444 remain for bronze:

6×5×4=120.6 \times 5 \times 4 = 120.6×5×4=120.

There are 120120120 ways. The choices drop from 666 to 555 to 444 because a runner who already has a medal cannot receive another. That is exactly what happens whenever an item cannot be reused. Notice we stopped after three stages, since only three medals are given out.

Check your understanding

In how many different orders can 444 different books be arranged in a row on a shelf?

Answer choices

Treat each of the four positions as a stage. The first has 444 choices, then the choices shrink as each book is placed.

4×3×2×1=244 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 244×3×2×1=24

Using 4×4×4×4=2564 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 2564×4×4×4=256 would wrongly let a book repeat; once a book is placed it is gone, so the counts must shrink. Getting 121212 stops after multiplying just two positions (4×34 \times 34×3), forgetting the last two spots. Getting 888 does not come from multiplying the shrinking counts at all.

Repeated choices and exponents

When the same number of choices repeats at every stage, the product becomes a power, which connects counting straight back to exponents. Flip a coin and there are 222 outcomes, heads or tails. Flip it again and each of those splits into 222, giving 2×2=42 \times 2 = 42×2=4. Flip it a third time and you reach 2×2×2=82 \times 2 \times 2 = 82×2×2=8. For nnn flips you multiply nnn twos:

2×2×⋯×2⏟n flips=2n.\underbrace{2 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 2}_{n \text{ flips}} = 2^n.n flips2×2×⋯×2​​=2n.

The exponent counts how many stages there are, and the base counts the choices at each stage. The same shape appears whenever choices repeat. A 444-digit code, where each digit can be any of the 101010 digits 000 through 999 and a digit may repeat, has

10×10×10×10=104=10,00010 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10^4 = 10{,}00010×10×10×10=104=10,000

possible values, including codes that start with 000. The base counts choices per stage; the exponent counts stages. For example, 25=322^5 = 3225=32 counts five coin flips, while 52=255^2 = 2552=25 counts two choices with five options each.

Worked example 5 Count two-digit numbers with a restricted first digit

A number’s first digit has a restriction that a code may not have. To count two-digit numbers using digits 000 through 999, fill the tens place first: it has 999 choices because 000 is not allowed. If repetition is allowed, the ones place has all 101010 choices:

9×10=90.9 \times 10 = 90.9×10=90.

If the two digits must be different, the tens place still has 999 choices. The ones place has 999 remaining choices, including 000, giving

9×9=81.9 \times 9 = 81.9×9=81.

The two factors are both 999 for different reasons: first we exclude 000, then we exclude the digit already used. Filling the restricted place first keeps the later count the same. If we filled the ones place first, the tens place would have 999 choices after a 000, but only 888 after any other digit. Those unequal branches would need separate counts.

Check your understanding

How many two-digit numbers can you make from 0,3,7,80, 3, 7, 80,3,7,8 if a digit cannot repeat?

Answer choices

Fill the tens place first. It can be 333, 777, or 888, so there are 333 choices. After using one digit, the ones place has 333 choices left, including 000.

3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9

Counting 444 choices for the tens place would include codes beginning with 000, which are not two-digit numbers.

Worked example 6 How many 3-symbol flags?

A signal flag shows 333 colored bands, stacked top to bottom. Each band can be colored with any of 555 available colors, and a color may repeat, so two or three bands can match. How many different flags are possible?

Each band is a stage with 555 choices, and the same 555 colors are available at every band no matter what the earlier bands used. Multiply three fives, which is a power of five:

5×5×5=53=125.5 \times 5 \times 5 = 5^3 = 125.5×5×5=53=125.

There are 125125125 possible flags. A color may repeat, so every band keeps all 555 choices; the counts do not shrink as they did for books.

Check your understanding

A padlock has 333 dials, each set to one of 666 symbols, and a symbol may repeat across dials. How many dial settings are possible?

Answer choices

Each dial is a stage with 666 choices, and the same 666 symbols are available at every dial, so multiply three sixes.

6×6×6=63=2166 \times 6 \times 6 = 6^3 = 2166×6×6=63=216

Getting 181818 adds the choices instead of multiplying. Getting 729729729 swaps the base and the exponent, 363^636, which counts a lock with 666 dials and only 333 symbols. Getting 120120120 wrongly shrinks the choices (6×5×46 \times 5 \times 46×5×4), as if a symbol could not be reused.

Counting the equally likely possibilities

These rules matter beyond menus and medals: they answer how many results an experiment like rolling dice or drawing cards has, without listing every one. When those results are all equally likely, that total is exactly what the next lesson, on probability, needs.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding when you should multiply, or the reverse. One book and one game makes a pair, so multiply the choices. One prize, a book or a game, needs addition. Check that the groups do not overlap before adding; an item in both groups would be counted twice.
  • Forgetting that arrangement choices shrink. When you place items in order, each item used is gone, so the counts fall 4,3,2,14, 3, 2, 14,3,2,1. Writing 4×4×4×44 \times 4 \times 4 \times 44×4×4×4 wrongly lets an item repeat.
  • Letting choices shrink when repetition is allowed. A 444-digit code can repeat digits, so every position keeps all 101010 choices, giving 10410^4104, not 10×9×8×710 \times 9 \times 8 \times 710×9×8×7. Shrink the counts only when an item cannot be reused.
  • Swapping the base and the exponent. With repeated choices, the base is the number of choices per stage and the exponent is the number of stages. Flipping a coin 555 times is 25=322^5 = 3225=32, not 52=255^2 = 2552=25.
  • Multiplying unequal branch counts. The number of choices at a stage must stay the same for every earlier choice, though the options may differ. If one branch has 222 endings and another has 333, count them separately and add: 2+3=52 + 3 = 52+3=5.
  • Double counting or skipping when listing. When you verify by listing, move through the choices in a fixed order so every outcome appears exactly once. A scattered list is how outcomes get repeated or lost.

Key takeaways

  • Multiply stage counts when each stage offers the same number of choices regardless of earlier picks. Two stages with mmm and nnn choices form mmm equal groups of nnn outcomes.
  • Add separate, non-overlapping cases. Ask what one complete outcome contains to decide whether to add or multiply.
  • In arrangements without reuse, the choices shrink as items are placed. Account for restrictions, such as a first digit that cannot be 000, before multiplying.
  • Repeated choices give a power: ccc choices at each of nnn stages give cnc^ncn outcomes. For example, nnn coin flips give 2n2^n2n sequences.
  • These counts can become probability denominators when the outcomes are also equally likely.

A bit of history (optional)

An ancient Chinese reader seeking guidance might turn to the I Ching, or Book of Changes. Its figures, called hexagrams, each contain six stacked lines. A line is either solid or broken in the middle. Readers used the figures and their accompanying words to think about questions and decisions in their lives.

These simple marks allow a surprising variety. Each of the six positions has two possibilities, so there are 26=642^6 = 6426=64 different figures. The book contains all sixty-four. Its traditions reach back more than two thousand years, and later readers studied it as philosophy as well as a guide to fortune telling.

The figures stayed the same while generations of readers found new meanings in them. A few solid and broken lines became the starting point for centuries of discussion about change.

Reading Data and Graphs

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

The last lesson used numbers to summarize a list. A picture can reveal patterns too. Suppose a teacher asks 303030 students to name their favorite kind of book. A long list of answers is hard to compare, but a graph, a picture of the data, can show which kind is most popular at a glance. Different questions need different pictures: comparing categories, following change over time, or showing parts of a whole. Reading them well means finding values and patterns while checking what the picture can actually tell you.

Learning goals

  • Build a frequency table from raw data, and check the frequencies sum to the total
  • Read an exact value from a pictograph, bar graph, line graph, or circle graph
  • Judge when a bar-length comparison is fair, and when to read the scale instead
  • Choose the display that fits a question, and trace a line graph's change over time
  • Refuse a conclusion the data does not support, and check the scale first

From raw data to a frequency table

The observations you collect are the raw data, whether or not they are numbers. Imagine asking 222222 students which pet they like best. You record each answer: dog, cat, dog, bird, fish, dog, and so on. Each kind of pet is a category, and its frequency is the number of times it is chosen. A frequency table pairs each category with its frequency. To build one, make a short stroke, called a tally mark, for each answer, then count the marks in each category.

PetFrequency
Dog888
Cat666
Bird555
Fish333

Keep the category and its count separate: Dog names the pet; 888 tells how many students chose it. The frequencies add to the total number of students,

8+6+5+3=22,8 + 6 + 5 + 3 = 22,8+6+5+3=22,

because every student was counted in exactly one category. This is also why circle-graph shares add to the whole. Not every graph shows frequencies. A line graph can track a measurement, such as temperature, over time.

Worked example 1 Turn a raw list into a frequency table

Twelve students name their favorite season: spring, summer, spring, fall, summer, winter, spring, summer, fall, spring, summer, winter.

Go through the list once and make a tally mark for each answer, adding to the right category as it comes up. After all twelve answers, count the marks in each row:

SeasonTallyFrequency
Spring| | | |444
Summer| | | |444
Fall| |222
Winter| |222

Add the frequencies as a check on your count:

4+4+2+2=12,4 + 4 + 2 + 2 = 12,4+4+2+2=12,

which matches the twelve students asked. A matching total is a useful check, but still tally each response once: a missed answer and a double-counted answer could cancel out.

Check your understanding

Ten students answer a favorite-color question in this order: red, blue, red, green, blue, red, green, blue, blue, red. Tally each answer as it arrives to build a frequency table for red, blue, and green. What are the three frequencies, and do they add up to the ten students asked?

Answer choices

Tallying the list finds red at the 1st, 3rd, 6th, and 10th spots (four marks), blue at the 2nd, 5th, 8th, and 9th spots (four marks), and green at the 4th and 7th spots (two marks).

Adding the frequencies, 4+4+2=104 + 4 + 2 = 104+4+2=10, matches the ten students asked. That match is a useful check that no response was lost, though it is not a guarantee, since one missed response and one double-counted response could cancel out.

Pictographs: a key gives each symbol a value

A pictograph (or picture graph) shows amounts with repeated symbols. Its key tells how much each symbol represents. In the pet survey below, one circle stands for 222 students. The Dog row has 444 circles, so it represents 4×2=84 \times 2 = 84×2=8 students. A partial symbol represents the same fraction of the key: half a circle here stands for 111 student.

The Bird row has two full circles and one half circle: 2.52.52.5 symbols. Its frequency is 2.5×2=52.5 \times 2 = 52.5×2=5, matching the table. Rounding the half circle up would wrongly give 666 students. To read any row, multiply its symbol count, including partial symbols, by the key’s value.

Worked example 2 Read a value from a pictograph

A pictograph of apples sold uses a key where each picture stands for 101010 apples. The Tuesday row shows 444 whole pictures and one half picture. How many apples were sold on Tuesday?

Count the symbols first. Four whole pictures and a half picture make

4+12=4.5 symbols.4 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 4.5 \text{ symbols}.4+21​=4.5 symbols.

Each symbol is worth 101010 apples, so multiply the symbol count by the key:

4.5×10=45.4.5 \times 10 = 45.4.5×10=45.

So 454545 apples were sold on Tuesday. The half picture added 555, not 111, because half a symbol is half of the key’s value.

Check your understanding

On a pictograph of books read, each book symbol stands for 444 books. A student's row shows 666 whole book symbols. How many books did that student read?

Answer choices

Each symbol is worth 444 books, so multiply the number of symbols by the key.

6×4=246 \times 4 = 246×4=24

Counting the symbols as 666 books ignores the key, which is the most common slip.

Bar graphs: comparing categories at a glance

A bar graph shows each category’s amount with a bar. Bars have equal widths and gaps between them; their lengths show the amounts. In this pet survey, those amounts are frequencies. Follow a bar’s end across to the number scale to read its value. For example, the Cat bar reaches 666 students.

The scale starts at 000 and uses equal spacing for equal increases. A bar reaching 888 is therefore twice as tall as one reaching 444. On this scale, bar heights are proportional to the values. If the scale starts above 000, that comparison breaks: twice the height need not mean twice the value. You can still read the labeled values and subtract to find a difference. We will see a cut-off scale near the end of the lesson.

A double bar graph, also called a grouped bar graph, compares two groups across the same categories. It puts two bars side by side in each category. A legend identifies the group represented by each color or pattern. Choose the category, use the legend to find the right bar, then read its value.

Check your understanding

Using the double bar graph above, how many books did Mia read in February?

Answer choices

Use the legend to find Mia's bar in the February group, then follow its top across to the scale. It reaches 666, so Mia read 666 books.

Worked example 3 Compare two groups on a double bar graph

Using the double bar graph above, in which month did Leo read more than Mia, and by how many books?

Go month by month and compare the paired bars. In January, Mia’s bar reaches 444 and Leo’s reaches 666, so Leo read more. In February both reach 666, a tie. In March, Mia reaches 888 and Leo reaches 444, so Mia read more. The only month Leo came out ahead is January, where the gap is

6−4=2.6 - 4 = 2.6−4=2.

Leo read 222 more books than Mia in January. The comparison uses the two bars for the same month.

Check your understanding

On a bar graph with a scale that starts at 000, the bar for September reaches 141414 and the bar for October reaches 202020. How many more does October represent?

Answer choices

Read each bar's value off the scale, then subtract to compare.

20−14=620 - 14 = 620−14=6

Reading labeled values and subtracting them works on any scale. A zero baseline matters for a different comparison: it is what lets you say one bar looks 'twice as tall' as a fair stand-in for 'twice the value.'

Line graphs: showing change over time

A line graph joins plotted readings with straight segments, often to show change over time. In the museum graph below, time runs across the horizontal axis and visitor counts up the vertical axis. A rising segment means the next count is higher, a falling segment means it is lower, and a flat segment means the two counts are equal. On the same graph, a steeper segment shows a larger change per unit of time.

A segment compares its two readings; it does not supply another measurement between them. The museum counts are daily totals, so there is no daily total halfway between Monday and Tuesday.

The horizontal axis needs a meaningful order. Days have one; favorite pets do not. Swapping Dog and Cat would change a line’s shape without changing any data, so a line graph would suggest a meaningless trend.

Worked example 4 Read a value and a trend from a line graph

Using the museum line graph above, how many visitors came on Thursday, and between which two days did the number of visitors fall?

Read a single day by finding its point and following across to the scale. Thursday’s point sits at 454545, so 454545 visitors came on Thursday.

Read the trend by following the line from left to right. From Monday to Tuesday it rises (202020 to 353535), and from Tuesday to Wednesday it falls (353535 to 303030). Then it rises again to Thursday and Friday. The single downward segment is between Tuesday and Wednesday, a drop of

35−30=5 visitors.35 - 30 = 5 \text{ visitors}.35−30=5 visitors.

So Thursday had 454545 visitors, and the only fall was from Tuesday to Wednesday. Overall the week trends upward, which the rising shape of the line shows at a glance.

Check your understanding

Using the museum line graph above, how many visitors came on Friday, and what happens between Thursday and Friday?

Answer choices

Friday's point is at 505050 visitors. Thursday's is at 454545, so the segment rises from Thursday to Friday.

Circle graphs: parts of a whole

A circle graph (or pie graph) shows how a whole splits into parts. The whole circle represents 100%100\%100% of the data. Each category gets a slice proportional to its share: half the data gets half the circle, and a quarter gets a quarter-circle slice.

In the travel graph below, each of 240240240 students belongs to exactly one group. The shares are 50%50\%50%, 25%25\%25%, 15%15\%15%, and 10%10\%10%, which add to 100%100\%100%. No student is left out or counted twice.

To read a circle graph, find the category and read its labeled share, usually a percent. To turn that share into a count, multiply by the total. For the 240240240 students below, the Walk slice is 25%25\%25%, so 25100×240=60\frac{25}{100} \times 240 = 6010025​×240=60 students walk. The percent alone is not a count: you need the total too.

Why a circle graph's slices add to the whole, and how big each slice is#

Suppose a different survey has 202020 responses, split into groups of 555, 666, and 999. Each group’s share is its count divided by 202020. Adding the shares gives

520+620+920=2020=1.\frac{5}{20} + \frac{6}{20} + \frac{9}{20} = \frac{20}{20} = 1.205​+206​+209​=2020​=1.

For any survey with at least one response, let TTT be the total and a,b,…a, b, \ldotsa,b,… the category counts. The dots mean to continue through every category. If every response belongs to exactly one category, then a+b+⋯=Ta + b + \cdots = Ta+b+⋯=T. So the shares add to

aT+bT+⋯=a+b+⋯T=TT=1.\begin{aligned} \frac{a}{T} + \frac{b}{T} + \cdots &= \frac{a + b + \cdots}{T} \\ &= \frac{T}{T} = 1. \end{aligned}Ta​+Tb​+⋯​=Ta+b+⋯​=TT​=1.​

Thus the shares fill one whole, or 100%100\%100%.

A slice gets the same fraction of a full turn as its share of the data. The group of 555 is a quarter of the survey, so its angle is 520×360∘=90∘\frac{5}{20} \times 360^\circ = 90^\circ205​×360∘=90∘. For any category,

slice angle=category counttotal count×360∘.\text{slice angle} = \frac{\text{category count}}{\text{total count}} \times 360^\circ.slice angle=total countcategory count​×360∘.

Since the shares add to 111, these angles add to one full turn, 360∘360^\circ360∘.

Choosing the right display

Choose a display to fit the question:

  • A bar graph or pictograph compares amounts across categories, such as favorite pets or books per student.
  • A line graph shows change over time, such as temperature through a day. Its horizontal axis needs a meaningful order.
  • A circle graph emphasizes shares of one whole, such as the parts of a budget.
  • A table lists the values directly. Use it when you need exact numbers rather than a quick visual comparison.

A display used outside its purpose misleads even when every number is correct. A line graph of unrelated categories invents a trend that is not there. A circle graph only makes sense when the slices really are parts of one whole.

Check your understanding

A gym asks each member to name their single favorite workout class, one of five choices, and wants to show how many members prefer each class. Which display fits best?

Answer choices

Each member names one class, so this is a fair comparison of separate categories, exactly what a bar graph or pictograph shows, and it displays the actual member counts directly.

A circle graph could technically be drawn here too, since every member falls into exactly one class and the shares would add to the whole, but it is the better tool when you care about each class's share of the whole rather than its exact count. A line graph needs an ordered axis such as time, which preference does not have.

Reading a graph honestly

Two habits help you avoid misleading conclusions.

First, draw only conclusions the data supports. If ice cream sales and sunburns both rise over the summer, their rising together does not show that ice cream causes sunburns. The graph alone cannot establish that cause. Similarly, a value beyond the last plotted point is a prediction, not a measurement you can read from the graph.

Check your understanding

A graph shows that ice cream sales and pool visits both rose in June, July, and August. Which conclusion does the graph support?

Answer choices

A graph reports what was measured, not why it happened. Two quantities rising together does not show that one caused the other, no matter how plausible a shared cause might seem.

The other three options all claim a specific cause that this graph never measured.

Second, check the scale. On an evenly spaced bar-graph scale, lengths are proportional to values only when the bars start at 000. Cutting off the bottom can make small differences look huge.

Brand A’s drawn bar spans 52−44=852 - 44 = 852−44=8 units; Brand B’s spans 48−44=448 - 44 = 448−44=4 units. That explains the two-to-one height ratio. The actual values are 525252 and 484848, only 444 apart. A cut-off scale need not be dishonest, but reading its labels keeps you from mistaking bar lengths for values.

Check your understanding

In the Brand A and Brand B graph above, Brand A's bar looks about twice as tall as Brand B's. Is it fair to say Brand A's value is about twice Brand B's value?

Answer choices

The axis here starts at 444444, not 000, so the bar heights are not a fair stand-in for the values. Reading the labels directly, 525252 and 484848 are close, a difference of just 444, even though the bar looks about twice as tall.

Saying “52>4852 > 4852>48” is true but does not justify “about twice.” Bar comparisons are perfectly valid when you read the labels instead of the heights.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the category with the value. Dog is a category; 888 students is its frequency. Read the amount from the scale, not from the category label.
  • Ignoring the pictograph key, or mishandling a partial symbol. A symbol is worth whatever the key says, not 111, so count the symbols and multiply by the key. A half symbol counts as half the key’s value, not one unit. With a key of 101010, a half symbol adds 555.
  • Trusting bar heights on a cut-off scale. On the evenly spaced scales here, “twice as tall” means “twice the value” only when the bars start at 000. Otherwise, read the labeled values.
  • Using a line graph for separate categories. Time has a meaningful order; favorite pets do not. Connecting pet categories creates a trend that changes if you rearrange the labels.
  • Reading a pie slice’s percent as a count. A 40%40\%40% slice is a share, not 404040 people. Multiply the percent by the total to get a count, unless the total happens to be 100100100.
  • Reading in conclusions the data does not show. Two quantities rising together does not establish a cause. A value beyond the plotted data is a prediction, not a reading.

Key takeaways

  • Frequency tables: pair each category with its count. If each observation is counted exactly once, the frequencies add to the total.
  • Pictographs: a value is the number of symbols times the key, and a partial symbol is that fraction of the key.
  • Bar graphs: read a bar’s value from the scale and use the legend in a double bar graph. On an evenly spaced scale starting at 000, bar lengths are proportional to values.
  • Line graphs: show changes between readings on an ordered axis. Rising means an increase, falling a decrease, and a flat segment means equal values at the two endpoints.
  • Circle graphs: the shares add to 100%100\%100%. Multiply a share by the total to get its count, or by 360∘360^\circ360∘ to get its angle.
  • Read only what the data supports, and check the scale before trusting a comparison.

A bit of history (optional)

To follow thirty years of trade in a table, a reader has to compare one number after another. William Playfair, a Scottish engineer and writer, wanted readers to see the pattern instead.

His Commercial and Political Atlas, first published in 1786, turned trade figures into lines rising and falling across the page. Imports and exports could be compared by following their shapes. It also contained a bar chart comparing Scotland’s trade with different countries. The Library of Congress’s account of Playfair’s work describes these pioneering statistical pictures.

Fifteen years later, in his Statistical Breviary, Playfair divided a circle to show how much of the Turkish Empire lay in Asia, Europe, and Africa. The familiar pie chart began as a picture of an empire’s land. Along with his bars and lines, it helped establish forms of data display that are now ordinary.

For a sense of how recent this is, the piano was already around by 1700, as the Metropolitan Museum’s history explains. Playfair’s statistical charts are younger than the piano.

Rounding and Estimation

Intuition

A cash register may show that your groceries cost 14.738214.738214.7382 dollars, but you hand over fifteen dollars and think of the bill as “about fifteen.” You just rounded. Real measurements and prices are often messier than you need them to be. So you trade a little exactness for a number that is simpler to read, remember, and compute with. Rounding replaces a number with the nearest tidy value, and estimation rounds the numbers in a calculation before you work it out. That way you can find an approximate answer in your head and, just as usefully, check that an exact answer is not wildly wrong. Neither idea needs new arithmetic. Both rest on the place value you already know.

Learning goals

  • Round to a place by snapping to the nearer multiple of it
  • Justify why, for a nonnegative decimal that ends, only the digit right of the rounding place decides
  • Carry a nine that rounds up, so 2.962.962.96 becomes 3.03.03.0
  • Name the exactly-five tie as a convention, not a forced result
  • Estimate by rounding before computing, and say what accuracy it costs
  • Check an exact answer for size, catching a misplaced decimal point

Rounding means snapping to the nearest

A number that is awkward to work with can be swapped for the nearest easy one.

Take 3.73.73.7 and round it to the nearest whole number. The two whole numbers on either side are 333 and 444. Since 3.73.73.7 is past the midpoint 3.53.53.5, it is closer to 444, so 3.73.73.7 rounds to 444. Round 3.23.23.2 the same way and it lands on 333, because 3.23.23.2 has not yet reached the halfway mark. Rounding is written with the symbol ≈\approx≈, read as “is approximately equal to,” so these two results are 3.7≈43.7 \approx 43.7≈4 and 3.2≈33.2 \approx 33.2≈3.

The midpoint 3.53.53.5 splits the gap between 333 and 444 into two equal halves. From 3.73.73.7, past that midpoint, the shorter trip is forward to 444. From 3.23.23.2, short of it, the shorter trip is back to 333.

To round a number to a given place means to replace it with the nearest multiple of that place’s value. The same goes at every place: the nearest multiple of one tenth, or of one hundredth. Every rounding question is this same question in disguise: of the two nearest multiples, which one is the number closer to?

Why you only look at one digit, for a nonnegative decimal that ends

You never have to measure the two distances. One digit settles it.

Round 8.49178.49178.4917 to the nearest tenth. The neighbors are 8.48.48.4 and 8.58.58.5, and the midpoint between them is 8.458.458.45. The hundredths digit is 999, which puts the number past 8.458.458.45, so it rounds to 8.58.58.5. The 171717 trailing that 999 never came into it.

For a nonnegative decimal that ends, look at the single digit just to the right of the rounding place. If it is 555 or more, round up; if it is less than 555, round down (leave the rounding digit as it is). The surprising part is that one digit is enough.

Here is why, on that same 8.49178.49178.4917. The deciding digit, the hundredths digit 999, was already more than the 555 hundredths needed to reach the midpoint 8.458.458.45. Everything after that 999, the digits 171717, adds only 0.00170.00170.0017 to the number, less than one whole hundredth, so those digits could never have pulled it back below the midpoint the 999 already put it past. The deciding digit had already settled the direction before the 171717 was even read.

The reasoning above works for any nonnegative decimal that ends, rounded to any place, not only 8.49178.49178.4917 rounded to the nearest tenth: whatever digits follow the deciding one, they can never add up to a whole unit of its place. You can read that argument written for every case in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full argument

Rounding to the nearest tenth is something you can see rather than compute. That is because on the grid below, a complete row of ten squares is one tenth. Shade a count that does not finish a row and ask which of the two nearest whole-row counts the shading is closer to.

Hunt for a count whose unfinished row is exactly half full. Set 454545: the midpoint 0.450.450.45 sits the same distance from 0.40.40.4 and 0.50.50.5, so the picture refuses to decide. A half-full row is a half-full row wherever it falls, so every count ending in 555 does the same thing: 555, 151515, 252525, and so on up to 959595. That tie is settled by a convention this lesson comes to shortly. Move one square either side of any of them and the picture picks a winner on its own.

Now try 969696: nine full rows and most of a tenth, near enough to a full grid. That count rounded to the nearest tenth therefore fills the grid completely, giving 1.01.01.0, which is the carry a 999 makes when it rounds up.

The squares left over past the last complete row are the hundredths digit. So “is the leftover 555 or more” and “is the unfinished row past half full” are the same question asked twice.

Worked example 1 Round 46.8346.8346.83 to the nearest whole number

Rounding to the nearest whole number, so the rounding place is the ones place, holding the 666. The digit just to the right of it is the tenths digit, which is 888.

Since 888 is 555 or more, round up: raise the ones digit from 666 to 777 and drop everything after the point.

46.83≈47.46.83 \approx 47.46.83≈47.

The hundredths digit 333 never mattered. Once the tenths digit is 888, the number is already past the midpoint 46.546.546.5, so it is closer to 474747 than to 464646.

Worked example 2 Round 5.71425.71425.7142 to the nearest hundredth

The hundredths place holds the second digit after the point. In 5.714‾25.7\mathbf{1}\underline{4}25.714​2, the bold 111 marks that rounding place, and the underlined 444 marks the deciding digit, the one that decides which way to round.

Since 444 is less than 555, round down: keep the hundredths digit as it is and drop everything after it.

5.7142≈5.71.5.7142 \approx 5.71.5.7142≈5.71.

The trailing 424242 is worth 0.00420.00420.0042, and reaching the midpoint 5.7155.7155.715 would need at least 0.0050.0050.005, half a hundredth. Since 0.00420.00420.0042 falls short of 0.0050.0050.005, it cannot pull the number up. The answer keeps exactly two decimal places, because that is what “to the nearest hundredth” asks for.

When a nine rounds up

Rounding a 999 up takes one extra move.

Worked example 3 Round 2.962.962.96 to the nearest tenth

The tenths place holds the 999, and the next digit, the hundredths 666, is 555 or more, so round the tenths up. Raising 999 tenths by one gives ten tenths, which is one whole:

2.96≈3.0.2.96 \approx 3.0.2.96≈3.0.

The ten tenths roll over into the ones place: the 999 turns into 000 and the 222 ones become 333. The answer is 3.03.03.0, not 2.102.102.10, and the trailing zero shows you rounded to the tenths place.

Raising any 999 by one makes ten, and ten does not fit in a single place. So it carries into the place on its left, exactly like carrying in addition. The 999 becomes a 000 and the digit beside it goes up by one. When that place is also a 999, it carries again: rounding 99.9699.9699.96 to the nearest tenth carries the tenths 999 into the ones, and that 999 carries once more into the tens, giving 100.0100.0100.0. A carry keeps traveling left for as long as it keeps meeting another 999.

The exactly-five case is a convention

There is one case the “5 or more rounds up” rule sweeps past without comment. What happens when the number is exactly at the midpoint, like 2.52.52.5 rounded to the nearest whole number? Here 2.52.52.5 is the same distance from 222 as it is from 333, so “nearest” does not pick a winner. No single neighbor is nearest; it is a genuine tie.

A deciding digit of 555 is not automatically this tie. It only lands exactly on the midpoint when every digit after that 555 is a zero, or there is nothing after it at all. The checkpoint number 12.35912.35912.359 had a deciding digit of 555, but the 999 right after it pushed the value past the midpoint, so that was never a tie: it was an ordinary case the ”555 or more” rule settled by distance, the same way it settles every other case.

A tie has to be broken by a rule we simply agree on. The most common such rule is round half up: when the number lands exactly on the midpoint, round it up. Under this convention 2.52.52.5 rounds to 333, 0.50.50.5 rounds to 111, and 7.857.857.85 to the nearest tenth rounds to 7.97.97.9. This is the convention used throughout these lessons, and it is the one that makes the clean “5 or more rounds up” wording correct.

This is a choice, not a logical necessity. Half could just as reasonably be rounded down, or rounded to whichever neighbor is even. In fact statisticians and many calculators use “round half to even” to avoid a slight upward bias when rounding many numbers. Remember, though, that the exactly-five rule is settled by agreement, while every other case is forced by which multiple is actually nearer.

Worked example 4 Round 2.52.52.5 and 3.53.53.5 to the nearest whole number

Both numbers sit exactly halfway between two whole numbers, so neither has a strictly nearer neighbor. This is the tie case, and the round-half-up convention settles it by sending the number to the upper neighbor.

For 2.52.52.5, the neighbors are 222 and 333, and round half up sends it up:

2.5≈3.2.5 \approx 3.2.5≈3.

For 3.53.53.5, the neighbors are 333 and 444, and again it goes up:

3.5≈4.3.5 \approx 4.3.5≈4.

Had we agreed on round-half-down instead, these two would have landed on 222 and 333. That is why this case is a convention rather than something forced by distance.

6.5 is exactly halfway  ⇒  round half up⇒76.5 \text{ is exactly halfway} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{round half up} \Rightarrow 76.5 is exactly halfway⇒round half up⇒7

Compare this with 12.35912.35912.359 from earlier: there the deciding digit was also 555, but the 999 right after it meant that case was never a tie. Here there is nothing after the 555 at all, so it genuinely is one.

Working backward: which numbers round to a given value

Sometimes the question runs the other way. Instead of rounding a number, you are told the result and asked which numbers could have produced it.

Take rounding to the nearest ten, with a result of 404040. A number rounds to 404040 when it is strictly closer to 404040 than to either neighboring multiple of ten, 303030 or 505050, which is every number within 555 of 404040 except the two exact midpoints, and those two midpoints are then settled by the round-half-up convention rather than by distance. The lower edge is 40−5=3540 - 5 = 3540−5=35, and the upper edge is 40+5=4540 + 5 = 4540+5=45.

The two edges are not treated the same way. 353535 is itself a tie between 303030 and 404040, and round half up sends every tie to the upper neighbor, so 353535 rounds to 404040 and belongs in the range. But 454545 is the tie between 404040 and 505050, and round half up sends it up to 505050, not back down to 404040, so 454545 is excluded. The numbers that round to 404040 run from 353535 up to, but not including, 454545. In symbols, that range is written 35≤n<4535 \le n < 4535≤n<45, where nnn stands for the number in question, the familiar symbol <<< still means “is strictly less than,” and the new symbol ≤\le≤ means “is less than or equal to,” so 35≤n35 \le n35≤n allows nnn to equal 353535 itself.

Worked example 5 Which whole numbers round to 707070, to the nearest ten?

Half of the rounding unit, ten, is 555. Subtract and add that 555 to 707070 to find the two edges:

70−5=65,70+5=75.70 - 5 = 65, \qquad 70 + 5 = 75.70−5=65,70+5=75.

The lower edge, 656565, is a tie between 606060 and 707070, and round half up sends every tie up, so 656565 rounds to 707070 and is included. The upper edge, 757575, is a tie between 707070 and 808080; round half up sends it to 808080, not back to 707070, so 757575 is excluded:

65≤n<75.65 \le n < 75.65≤n<75.

Counting the whole numbers in that range: the last one, 747474, minus the first, 656565, plus one, gives 101010 whole numbers.

Estimation: round first, then compute

Rounding can make a calculation easier before you even start it.

Suppose you want 312+489312 + 489312+489 in a hurry. Round each number to the nearest hundred first, then add the easy numbers:

312+489≈300+500=800.312 + 489 \approx 300 + 500 = 800.312+489≈300+500=800.

The exact sum is 801801801, so the estimate of 800800800 is excellent, and it took no paper. Rounding moved 312312312 down by 121212 and 489489489 up by 111111, so the two changes very nearly cancel each other out.

Estimation turns rounding into a calculating tool. Instead of working with the exact numbers, you round each one to something easy, then do the now-simpler arithmetic. The answer is approximate, but you get it quickly, often in your head, and that speed is the whole point. The same idea works with decimals. To estimate 6.83×4.16.83 \times 4.16.83×4.1, round to 7×4=287 \times 4 = 287×4=28, which is close to the exact product 28.00328.00328.003. You decide how rough to be by choosing the place you round to. Rounding to the nearest ten is faster but cruder than rounding to the nearest one.

A quick variation is front-end estimation, where you keep only the leading (front) digit of each number and treat the rest as zeros. For 312+489312 + 489312+489, the front digits give 300+400=700300 + 400 = 700300+400=700. Front-end estimation is even faster because you never stop to decide which way to round. Front-end estimation tends to run low, though, since chopping the trailing digits only ever throws value away. When you are adding, this makes it a quick lower estimate of the total, useful when you mostly want the size.

Worked example 6 Estimate 48.7+23.4+31.948.7 + 23.4 + 31.948.7+23.4+31.9 by rounding each to the nearest ten

Round every number to the nearest ten before adding anything. Look at the ones digit of each to decide which way it rounds.

The number 48.748.748.7 has ones digit 888, so it rounds up to 505050. The number 23.423.423.4 has ones digit 333, so it rounds down to 202020. The number 31.931.931.9 has ones digit 111, so it rounds down to 303030. Now add the easy numbers:

48.7+23.4+31.9≈50+20+30=100.48.7 + 23.4 + 31.9 \approx 50 + 20 + 30 = 100.48.7+23.4+31.9≈50+20+30=100.

So the sum is about 100100100. The exact total is 104.0104.0104.0, so rounding to the nearest ten landed within a few units. That rounding also replaced three messy decimals with round numbers you can add in your head.

Round the same three numbers to the nearest one instead, and the digits stay closer to the originals: 48.7→4948.7 \to 4948.7→49, 23.4→2323.4 \to 2323.4→23, and 31.9→3231.9 \to 3231.9→32, for a total of 104104104. That happens to match the exact total exactly here; it will not always be that close, but rounding to a finer place generally lands nearer the truth than rounding to a coarser one. The finer estimate cost more effort to add in your head, which is the trade estimation always asks you to make: a place rounded coarser is faster but rougher, and a place rounded finer is closer but slower.

Estimation as a reasonableness check

After you compute an exact answer, by hand or on a calculator, a quick estimate tells you whether that answer is even the right size.

Suppose you multiply 19.6×5.219.6 \times 5.219.6×5.2 and your calculator reads 10.19210.19210.192. Estimate: 20×5=10020 \times 5 = 10020×5=100. The exact answer should be about 100100100, but 10.19210.19210.192 is about 101010, ten times too small. So a digit or the decimal point went in the wrong place. (The correct product is 101.92101.92101.92.)

That catches a common and costly decimal mistake: a misplaced decimal point. A misplaced point throws the answer off by a factor of ten, a hundred, or more. The estimate did not give you the exact answer, but it flagged that something was wrong, which is often more valuable.

Worked example 7 A bill of 6.456.456.45 dollars for each of 888 tickets rings up as 5.165.165.16 dollars. Is that reasonable?

Each ticket is about 666 dollars and there are 888 tickets, so round and multiply:

6.45×8≈6×8=48.6.45 \times 8 \approx 6 \times 8 = 48.6.45×8≈6×8=48.

The total should be about 484848 dollars. The rung-up figure of 5.165.165.16 dollars is roughly 555 dollars, nearly ten times too small, so it cannot be right. The decimal point landed one place too far left.

Working it out exactly confirms the estimate caught a real error:

6.45×8=51.60.6.45 \times 8 = 51.60.6.45×8=51.60.

The true total is 51.6051.6051.60 dollars, close to the estimate of 484848, while the original 5.165.165.16 was off by a factor of ten.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Rounding digit by digit from the right. To round 4.464.464.46 to the nearest tenth, look only at the hundredths digit (666), which sends it to 4.54.54.5. Do not first round 4.464.464.46 to 4.54.54.5 and then claim the 555 pushes the tenths up again. For a nonnegative decimal that ends, one digit, the one immediately to the right of the rounding place, decides it.
  • Looking at the wrong digit. For a nonnegative decimal that ends, round to the place named, then check the single next digit. For “nearest tenth” the rounding digit is the tenths digit and the deciding digit is the hundredths, not the ones.
  • Dropping the place you rounded to. Rounding 5.71425.71425.7142 to the nearest hundredth gives 5.715.715.71, which keeps two decimal places. Writing 5.75.75.7 rounds to the wrong place, and writing 555 throws away the decimal entirely.
  • Forgetting the carry, or the trailing zero. A 999 that rounds up carries, so 2.962.962.96 to the nearest tenth is 3.03.03.0, not 2.102.102.10. Keep the trailing zero, since rounding to the nearest cent means two decimal places: 3.83.83.8 dollars to the nearest cent is written 3.803.803.80.
  • Rounding too early inside a calculation. When a problem calls for an exact answer, work with the exact numbers the whole way through and leave the result exact; do not round any number before you are done. If a problem instead asks for a rounded answer (money to the nearest cent, say), still compute with the exact numbers and round only once, at the very end. Rounding each number before you start is for estimates, where the small errors are the price you knowingly pay for speed.
  • Trusting an answer that fails the size check. If your exact result is ten times bigger or smaller than a quick estimate, recheck it. A misplaced decimal point is the usual culprit, and the estimate is what catches it.

Key takeaways

  • Rounding to a place means choosing the nearest multiple of that place’s value (nearest whole, nearest tenth, nearest hundredth). So choosing that nearest multiple snaps the number to the closer of its two neighbors.
  • For a nonnegative decimal that ends, you only need the single digit just to the right of the rounding place: 555 or more rounds up, less than 555 rounds down. The shortcut works because everything farther right adds less than one unit of that next place, so it cannot reverse the direction that digit already decided. The one exception is a deciding digit of exactly 555: the digits after it decide whether that is a genuine tie or a value strictly past the midpoint, though the round-up direction is the same either way.
  • A 999 in the rounding place carries when it rounds up: 2.962.962.96 to the nearest tenth is 3.03.03.0.
  • The exactly-five tie is broken by a convention. These lessons use round half up, which is what makes “5 or more rounds up” exactly correct; the choice is an agreement, not a forced result.
  • Estimation rounds the numbers in a calculation before computing, trading a little accuracy for a fast, in-your-head answer. Front-end estimation keeps only the leading digits and tends to run low.
  • An estimate is also a reasonableness check: it confirms the size of an exact answer. The estimate also catches a misplaced decimal point, which is off by a factor of ten or more.

Why the next digit alone decides, for any nonnegative decimal that ends

The lesson showed the reasoning on 8.49178.49178.4917: the digits after the deciding one add up to less than one whole unit of its place, so they can never undo what the deciding digit already decided. Here is that same argument run again on 4.03624.03624.0362, and then written so it covers every nonnegative decimal that ends, at once.

Why the next digit alone decides which way to round, for a nonnegative decimal that ends#

Round 4.03624.03624.0362 to the nearest hundredth. The two nearest hundredths are 4.034.034.03 and 4.044.044.04, and the midpoint between them is 4.0354.0354.035. The deciding digit is the thousandths digit 666, more than the 555 thousandths that reach the midpoint, so 4.03624.03624.0362 is already past 4.0354.0354.035 before the final 222 is even read. That 222 ten-thousandths is worth 0.00020.00020.0002, far less than one whole thousandth, so it could never have pulled the number back below the midpoint.

Nothing there depended on the digits chosen, only on the number being zero or positive, which every number in this lesson is: for a negative decimal, “up” and “down” trade places, and this argument does not cover that case. Fix the place you are rounding to and look at the two nearest multiples of that place. One is the number with the rounding digit left as it is, which is the round-down result. The other is the next multiple of that place going up, which is the round-up result. The exact midpoint between those two multiples is the lower one plus half a unit of the rounding place. Half a unit is a 555 in the next place down with nothing after it.

The digit right after the rounding place reaches the value of that midpoint 555 precisely when it becomes 555 itself. For a nonnegative decimal that ends, everything to the right of that digit adds up to less than one full unit of its place. It can reach a hair under that unit, and never the whole of it. So a digit of 555 or more puts the number at or past the midpoint, no matter what follows. A digit of 444 or less leaves it short of the midpoint, even at the most the tail can add. So the single digit immediately to the right is enough to settle the rounding direction in every such case: for a digit of 666 or more, or of 444 or less, it also settles which multiple is strictly nearer. Only when that digit is exactly 555 does the question of nearness stay open, because the tail digits still decide whether the number is a genuine tie or has slipped strictly past the midpoint. A genuine tie, reached only when a 555 has nothing but zeros after it, is the one case distance cannot settle, and a convention decides it instead.

A bit of history (optional)

A small error that always leans one way does not stay small.

In 1982 the stock exchange in Vancouver, a city in Canada, set up a new index at 100010001000. The index was worked out afresh after every trade, thousands of times a day. Each new value was cut short at three decimal places. Cut short, not rounded: the last digits were just dropped.

One drop cost close to nothing. Every drop fell the same way. Twenty-two months later the index read about 524524524. The true figure was 109910991099. Half of it had leaked out past the third decimal place, and the exchange had to restate the lot.

This lesson has told you why. Rounding sends a number up or down, to whichever mark is nearer, so the errors mostly cancel out. Chopping only ever throws value away, in the same direction every time. That is exactly what front-end estimation does on purpose, in small doses, and what the Vancouver exchange did by accident, thousands of times a day.

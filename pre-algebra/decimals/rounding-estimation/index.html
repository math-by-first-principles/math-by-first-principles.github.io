Rounding and Estimation

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A measured length of 14.738214.738214.7382 meters might be described as “about fifteen meters.” Rounding replaces a value with a nearby, simpler one. Estimation uses simpler numbers to work out an approximate result. Both help us judge size, but we need to know when an approximation is enough and when the exact value matters.

Learning goals

  • Round to a place by snapping to the nearer multiple of it
  • Justify why, for a nonnegative decimal that ends, only the digit right of the rounding place decides
  • Carry a nine that rounds up, so 2.962.962.96 becomes 3.03.03.0
  • Name the exactly-five tie as a convention, not a forced result
  • Estimate by rounding before computing, and say what accuracy it costs
  • Check an exact answer for size, catching a misplaced decimal point

Rounding means snapping to the nearest

To round to a place, choose the nearest multiple of that place’s value: a whole number, a tenth, a hundredth, and so on.

For 3.73.73.7, the neighboring whole numbers are 333 and 444. Their midpoint is 3.53.53.5. Since 3.73.73.7 is past the midpoint, it is closer to 444. But 3.23.23.2 is closer to 333.

3.7≈4,3.2≈3.3.7\approx4,\qquad3.2\approx3.3.7≈4,3.2≈3.

The symbol ≈\approx≈ means is approximately equal to. At the exact midpoint, both neighbors are equally near. These lessons use round half up: choose the larger neighbor in a tie. We will distinguish that agreement from nearness below.

Why you only look at one digit, for a nonnegative decimal that ends

Nonnegative means positive or zero. Round 8.49178.49178.4917 to the nearest tenth. Its hundredths digit is 999, so it lies past the midpoint 8.458.458.45 between 8.48.48.4 and 8.58.58.5. It rounds to 8.58.58.5.

For a nonnegative decimal that ends, inspect the digit immediately right of the requested place. Under round half up:

  • Below 555: keep the rounding digit.
  • 555 or above: increase the rounding place by one, carrying if needed.

Why the next digit alone decides which way to round, for a nonnegative decimal that ends#

Round 4.03494.03494.0349 to the nearest hundredth. The neighboring hundredths are 4.034.034.03 and 4.044.044.04, with midpoint 4.0354.0354.035. The thousandths digit, 444, gives four thousandths beyond 4.034.034.03. The final 999 adds 0.00090.00090.0009, less than one more thousandth, so the total is still below five thousandths beyond 4.034.034.03:

4.0349<4.035,4.0349≈4.03.4.0349<4.035,\qquad4.0349\approx4.03.4.0349<4.035,4.0349≈4.03.

Nothing here depended on 4.03494.03494.0349. For any requested place, the midpoint is the lower neighbor plus half a unit of that place. That half-unit is exactly 555 in the next place, with zeros in every later place.

The finite tail after that next digit adds less than one unit of its place. For example, even 0.000990.000990.00099 is less than 0.0010.0010.001. The same holds for any finite tail: filling all its remaining places with nines still leaves it one smallest unit short of the next larger place.

Therefore a next digit of 444 or less stays below the midpoint, and a digit of 666 or more is past it. A next digit of 555 reaches the midpoint. If all following digits are zero, it is an exact tie; otherwise it is past the midpoint. Round half up sends both of those cases to the upper neighbor.

This proves the shortcut for nonnegative decimals that end. For a repeating decimal, compare its value with the midpoint, as in the example below.

Answer choices

The next digit is 4 hundredths. Any finite tail adds less than one more hundredth, so the value stays below the midpoint 7.657.657.65.

Even 7.649997.649997.64999 is below 7.657.657.65. Thus the nearest tenth is 7.67.67.6. The tail has value, but not enough to cross the midpoint.

On the grid, a full row is one tenth. Try 454545 shaded squares: 0.450.450.45 is halfway between 0.40.40.4 and 0.50.50.5. Round half up chooses 0.50.50.5. Moving one square either way makes one neighbor strictly nearer.

Then try 969696 squares. The shading is closer to a full grid, so rounding to tenths gives 1.01.01.0. This is what happens when a 999 carries.

The leftover squares count hundredths. Five leftovers make an exact half-row; more than five pass the midpoint.

Fractions with repeating decimals can also be rounded by comparing with a midpoint. For 23=0.666…\tfrac{2}{3}=0.666\ldots32​=0.666…, the neighboring hundredths are 0.660.660.66 and 0.670.670.67, with midpoint 0.6650.6650.665. The value is above that midpoint, so its nearest hundredth is 0.670.670.67. The exact value remains 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​; 0.670.670.67 is an approximation.

Worked example 1 Round 46.8346.8346.83 to the nearest whole number

Rounding to the nearest whole number, so the rounding place is the ones place, holding the 666. The digit just to the right of it is the tenths digit, which is 888.

Since 888 is 555 or more, round up: raise the ones digit from 666 to 777 and drop everything after the point.

46.83≈47.46.83 \approx 47.46.83≈47.

The hundredths digit 333 never mattered. Once the tenths digit is 888, the number is already past the midpoint 46.546.546.5, so it is closer to 474747 than to 464646.

Worked example 2 Round 5.71425.71425.7142 to the nearest hundredth

The hundredths place holds the second digit after the point. In 5.714‾25.7\mathbf{1}\underline{4}25.714​2, the bold 111 marks that rounding place, and the underlined 444 marks the deciding digit, the one that decides which way to round.

Since 444 is less than 555, round down: keep the hundredths digit as it is and drop everything after it.

5.7142≈5.71.5.7142 \approx 5.71.5.7142≈5.71.

The trailing 424242 is worth 0.00420.00420.0042, and reaching the midpoint 5.7155.7155.715 would need at least 0.0050.0050.005, half a hundredth. Since 0.00420.00420.0042 falls short of 0.0050.0050.005, it cannot pull the number up. The answer keeps exactly two decimal places, because that is what “to the nearest hundredth” asks for.

When a nine rounds up

Rounding a 999 up takes one extra move.

Worked example 3 Round 2.962.962.96 to the nearest tenth

The tenths place holds the 999, and the next digit, the hundredths 666, is 555 or more, so round the tenths up. Raising 999 tenths by one gives ten tenths, which is one whole:

2.96≈3.0.2.96 \approx 3.0.2.96≈3.0.

The ten tenths roll over into the ones place: the 999 turns into 000 and the 222 ones become 333. The answer is 3.03.03.0, not 2.102.102.10, and the trailing zero shows you rounded to the tenths place.

Raising any 999 by one makes ten, and ten does not fit in a single place. So it carries into the place on its left, exactly like carrying in addition. The 999 becomes a 000 and the digit beside it goes up by one. When that place is also a 999, it carries again: rounding 99.9699.9699.96 to the nearest tenth carries the tenths 999 into the ones, and that 999 carries once more into the tens, giving 100.0100.0100.0. A carry keeps traveling left for as long as it keeps meeting another 999.

The exactly-five case is a convention

At 2.52.52.5, the distances to 222 and 333 are both 0.50.50.5. Neither neighbor is closer. Round half up chooses 333 by agreement.

A deciding digit of 555 does not always mean a tie. For rounding to tenths, 12.35012.35012.350 is exactly halfway between 12.312.312.3 and 12.412.412.4, but 12.35912.35912.359 is beyond halfway. Only a 555 followed entirely by zeros, or by no more digits, is the exact tie.

Other conventions exist, such as round half down or round half to even. They change which neighbor wins an exact tie; they do not change which neighbor is strictly closer. Round half to even sends ties to the neighbor whose rounding digit is even. It avoids always pushing ties upward, which can cause an upward drift over many roundings.

Worked example 4 Round 2.52.52.5 and 3.53.53.5 to the nearest whole number

Both numbers sit exactly halfway between two whole numbers, so neither has a strictly nearer neighbor. This is the tie case, and the round-half-up convention settles it by sending the number to the upper neighbor.

For 2.52.52.5, the neighbors are 222 and 333, and round half up sends it up:

2.5≈3.2.5 \approx 3.2.5≈3.

For 3.53.53.5, the neighbors are 333 and 444, and again it goes up:

3.5≈4.3.5 \approx 4.3.5≈4.

Had we agreed on round-half-down instead, these two would have landed on 222 and 333. That is why this case is a convention rather than something forced by distance.

Check your understanding

Using round half up, how does 7.57.57.5 round to a whole number, and why is a convention needed?

Answer choices

The distances are equal:

7.5−7=0.5,8−7.5=0.5.7.5-7=0.5,\qquad8-7.5=0.5.7.5−7=0.5,8−7.5=0.5.

Nearness cannot choose between them. The stated round-half-up convention chooses 8. Round half down would choose 7.

Working backward: which numbers round to a given value

If rounding to the nearest ten gives 404040, the input must lie between the midpoints to its neighboring tens, 303030 and 505050.

The lower midpoint is 353535. It rounds up to 404040, so include it. The upper midpoint is 454545. It rounds up to 505050, so exclude it.

Thus the possible inputs run from 353535 up to, but not including, 454545:

35≤n<45.35\le n<45.35≤n<45.

Here nnn names the input. The symbol ≤\le≤ means less than or equal to; <<< means strictly less than.

Worked example 5 Which whole numbers round to 707070, to the nearest ten?

Half of the rounding unit, ten, is 555. Subtract and add that 555 to 707070 to find the two edges:

70−5=65,70+5=75.70 - 5 = 65, \qquad 70 + 5 = 75.70−5=65,70+5=75.

The lower edge, 656565, is a tie between 606060 and 707070, and round half up sends every tie up, so 656565 rounds to 707070 and is included. The upper edge, 757575, is a tie between 707070 and 808080; round half up sends it to 808080, not back to 707070, so 757575 is excluded:

65≤n<75.65 \le n < 75.65≤n<75.

Counting the whole numbers in that range: the last one, 747474, minus the first, 656565, plus one, gives 101010 whole numbers.

Estimation: round first, then compute

To estimate 312+489312+489312+489, round each number to the nearest hundred, then add:

312+489≈300+500=800.312+489\approx300+500=800.312+489≈300+500=800.

The exact sum is 801801801. Here the changes nearly cancel: one number went down by 121212, the other up by 111111. They need not cancel in another calculation.

Choose the rounding place to suit the decision. Coarser rounding makes the arithmetic simpler but can lose more information. For example, rounding to whole numbers changes each input by at most half a unit. Adding two such rounded inputs can change the sum by as much as one unit. An estimate near a capacity limit may therefore be inconclusive.

Front-end estimation keeps the leading digit and replaces the rest with zeros: 312+489312+489312+489 becomes 300+400=700300+400=700300+400=700. Replacing later digits with zeros never increases a nonnegative input, so the estimated sum is no greater than the exact sum. It can be equal, as when 300+200300+200300+200 stays 300+200300+200300+200.

Another method, sometimes called front-rounding, rounds to one nonzero digit rather than merely chopping: 489489489 becomes 500500500. Follow the method the question specifies.

Worked example 6 Estimate 48.7+23.4+31.948.7 + 23.4 + 31.948.7+23.4+31.9 by rounding each to the nearest ten

Round every number to the nearest ten before adding anything. Look at the ones digit of each to decide which way it rounds.

The number 48.748.748.7 has ones digit 888, so it rounds up to 505050. The number 23.423.423.4 has ones digit 333, so it rounds down to 202020. The number 31.931.931.9 has ones digit 111, so it rounds down to 303030. Now add the easy numbers:

48.7+23.4+31.9≈50+20+30=100.48.7 + 23.4 + 31.9 \approx 50 + 20 + 30 = 100.48.7+23.4+31.9≈50+20+30=100.

The estimate is 100100100, compared with the exact total 104.0104.0104.0.

Using whole numbers instead gives 49+23+32=10449+23+32=10449+23+32=104. That happens to match exactly here. A finer rounding place keeps each input closer to its original value, but does not guarantee the errors in a whole calculation will cancel.

Estimation as a reasonableness check

An estimate can reveal a wrong-sized answer even when it cannot supply the exact result.

Suppose 19.6×5.219.6\times5.219.6×5.2 is recorded as 10.19210.19210.192. Since 20×5=10020\times5=10020×5=100, a result near 101010 is suspicious. Rechecking gives 101.92101.92101.92.

A misplaced decimal point changes a value by a factor of ten, a hundred, or more. An estimate helps flag that error; exact recalculation identifies what went wrong.

Worked example 7 A bill of 6.456.456.45 dollars for each of 888 tickets rings up as 5.165.165.16 dollars. Is that reasonable?

Each ticket is about 666 dollars and there are 888 tickets, so round and multiply:

6.45×8≈6×8=48.6.45 \times 8 \approx 6 \times 8 = 48.6.45×8≈6×8=48.

The total should be about 484848 dollars. The rung-up figure of 5.165.165.16 dollars is roughly 555 dollars, nearly ten times too small, so it cannot be right. The decimal point landed one place too far left.

Working it out exactly confirms the estimate caught a real error:

6.45×8=51.60.6.45 \times 8 = 51.60.6.45×8=51.60.

The true total is 51.6051.6051.60 dollars, close to the estimate of 484848, while the original 5.165.165.16 was off by a factor of ten.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Rounding in stages. Directly to the nearest tenth, 4.4494.4494.449 rounds to 4.44.44.4. But first rounding it to 4.454.454.45 and then to tenths gives 4.54.54.5. Use the original number and the requested place unless the task explicitly asks for successive rounding.
  • Looking at the wrong digit. For a nonnegative decimal that ends, round to the place named, then check the single next digit. For “nearest tenth” the rounding digit is the tenths digit and the deciding digit is the hundredths, not the ones.
  • Dropping the place you rounded to. Rounding 5.71425.71425.7142 to the nearest hundredth gives 5.715.715.71, which keeps two decimal places. Writing 5.75.75.7 rounds to the wrong place, and writing 555 throws away the decimal entirely.
  • Forgetting the carry, or the trailing zero. A 999 that rounds up carries, so 2.962.962.96 to the nearest tenth is 3.03.03.0, not 2.102.102.10. Keep the trailing zero, since rounding to the nearest cent means two decimal places: 3.83.83.8 dollars to the nearest cent is written 3.803.803.80.
  • Rounding too early inside a calculation. When a problem calls for an exact answer, work with the exact numbers the whole way through and leave the result exact; do not round any number before you are done. If a problem instead asks for a rounded answer (money to the nearest cent, say), still compute with the exact numbers and round only once, at the very end. Rounding each number before you start is for estimates, where the small errors are the price you knowingly pay for speed.
  • Trusting an answer that fails the size check. If your exact result is ten times bigger or smaller than a quick estimate, recheck it. A misplaced decimal point is the usual culprit, and the estimate is what catches it.

Key takeaways

  • Rounding to a place means choosing the nearest multiple of that place’s value (nearest whole, nearest tenth, nearest hundredth). So choosing that nearest multiple snaps the number to the closer of its two neighbors.
  • For a nonnegative decimal that ends, you only need the single digit just to the right of the rounding place: 555 or more rounds up, less than 555 rounds down. The shortcut works because everything farther right adds less than one unit of that next place, so it cannot reverse the direction that digit already decided. The one exception is a deciding digit of exactly 555: the digits after it decide whether that is a genuine tie or a value strictly past the midpoint, though the round-up direction is the same either way.
  • A 999 in the rounding place carries when it rounds up: 2.962.962.96 to the nearest tenth is 3.03.03.0.
  • The exactly-five tie is broken by a convention. These lessons use round half up, which is what makes “5 or more rounds up” exactly correct; the choice is an agreement, not a forced result.
  • Estimation rounds the numbers in a calculation before computing, trading a little accuracy for a fast, in-your-head answer. For a sum of nonnegative numbers, front-end estimation keeps the leading digits and never gives more than the exact sum.
  • An estimate is also a reasonableness check: it checks the size of an exact answer. The estimate also catches a misplaced decimal point, which is off by a factor of ten or more.

A bit of history (optional)

In January 1982, the Vancouver Stock Exchange introduced an index starting at 100010001000. It was recalculated after trades, thousands of times a day. Each new result was truncated to three decimal places: later digits were discarded instead of rounded.

The index kept sinking even while trading activity was strong. An investigation found that each discarded tail had lowered the next starting value. Tiny losses had accumulated for twenty-two months.

After weeks of corrective work, the exchange opened on 28 November 1983 with an index of 1098.8921098.8921098.892, up from about 525525525 at the previous close. The stock prices had not suddenly jumped; the calculation had been repaired.

Contemporary newspaper accounts and a later scholarly discussion record the embarrassing correction. The exchange had to rebuild almost two years of calculations, all because the same small downward error had been repeated so often.

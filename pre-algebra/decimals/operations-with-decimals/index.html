Operations with Decimals

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Decimal arithmetic builds on whole-number methods. The extra job is keeping track of place value: tenths must combine with tenths, and a product of tenths is counted in hundredths. We will use what decimals mean to decide where each point belongs.

Learning goals

  • Add and subtract two decimals that end by lining up the points, padding with trailing zeros
  • Explain why lining up the points is what stacks like place values
  • For two decimals that end, multiply as whole numbers, then count the decimal places of both factors
  • Divide by a decimal that ends by sliding both points until the divisor is whole
  • Slide the point when multiplying or dividing by ten, a hundred, a thousand

Adding and subtracting: line up the points

Column addition combines equal units: ones with ones, tens with tens. Decimals add tenths, hundredths, and smaller places to the same system.

Line up the decimal points to match the columns. Pad shorter decimals with trailing zeros, which do not change their values: 3.4=3.403.4=3.403.4=3.40.

Stack 3.403.403.40 over 1.251.251.25 with their points in one column and add:

3.40+ 1.254.65\begin{array}{r} 3.40 \\ +\ 1.25 \\ \hline 4.65 \end{array}3.40+ 1.254.65​​

Aligning the points puts the 444 tenths over the 222 tenths, and the 000 hundredths over the 555 hundredths. So every column once again holds digits of a single place value. That is the whole rule: line up the decimal points, then add or subtract column by column exactly as you do with whole numbers. The point in the answer drops straight down beneath the points above it.

Why lining up the points is the same as lining up place values#

Write 5.35.35.3 and 2.162.162.16 in expanded form:

5.3=5+310,2.16=2+110+6100.5.3=5+\frac{3}{10},\qquad 2.16=2+\frac{1}{10}+\frac{6}{100}.5.3=5+103​,2.16=2+101​+1006​.

Addition lets us reorder and regroup terms. Collect the ones, then tenths, then hundredths:

5.3+2.16=(5+2)+3+110+6100=7.46.5.3+2.16=(5+2)+\frac{3+1}{10}+\frac{6}{100}=7.46.5.3+2.16=(5+2)+103+1​+1006​=7.46.

Lining up the points puts exactly these terms in common columns. This works for any pair of decimals because their points locate the same place values. Misaligning the points would treat tenths as if they were hundredths.

Different units can still be added, but first express them in a common unit: 110+1100=11100\tfrac{1}{10}+\tfrac{1}{100}=\tfrac{11}{100}101​+1001​=10011​. In column arithmetic, carrying and borrowing do that regrouping in bundles of ten.

∎

12.60+ 3.4516.05\begin{array}{r} 12.60 \\ +\ 3.45 \\ \hline 16.05 \end{array}12.60+ 3.4516.05​​

The ones are 2+3+1=62+3+1=62+3+1=6, with one ten left. Keep the point aligned in the answer.

Multiplying decimals that end: multiply, then count the places

For multiplication, count decimal places rather than lining up points. In 0.3×0.20.3\times0.20.3×0.2, first compute 3×2=63\times2=63×2=6. The factors have one decimal place each, so give the product two places: 0.060.060.06.

Multiply as whole numbers, then give the product as many decimal places as both factors together. This rule applies to decimals that end. After placing the point, you may remove trailing zeros: 0.60=0.60.60=0.60.60=0.6.

Why the product's decimal places add up#

For 2.1×0.052.1\times0.052.1×0.05, write the factors as fractions:

2.1×0.05=2110×5100.2.1\times0.05=\frac{21}{10}\times\frac{5}{100}.2.1×0.05=1021​×1005​.

Multiply the numerators and denominators:

21×510×100=1051000=0.105.\frac{21\times5}{10\times100}=\frac{105}{1000}=0.105.10×10021×5​=1000105​=0.105.

The denominator has three zeros, matching the factors’ 1+21+21+2 decimal places.

In general, write each terminating decimal over 111 followed by one zero per decimal place. Its numerator is the whole number obtained by ignoring the point. Multiplying the numerators gives the whole-number product. Multiplying the denominators combines their zeros: for instance, ten times a hundred is a thousand. Thus the number of places in the product is the sum of the places in the factors, before removing any trailing zeros.

∎

Worked example 3 Multiply 1.4×0.61.4 \times 0.61.4×0.6

Ignore the decimal points and multiply the numbers as whole numbers:

14×6=84.14 \times 6 = 84.14×6=84.

Now count the decimal places in the two factors. The factor 1.41.41.4 has one place and 0.60.60.6 has one place, so the product needs 1+1=21 + 1 = 21+1=2 decimal places. Put the point before 848484. Write a zero before the point for readability:

1.4×0.6=0.84.1.4 \times 0.6 = 0.84.1.4×0.6=0.84.

Check the size: 1.41.41.4 is between 111 and 222, so multiplying it by 0.60.60.6 gives a product between 0.60.60.6 and 1.21.21.2. The answer 0.840.840.84 has that size.

Worked example 4 Multiply 0.25×0.30.25 \times 0.30.25×0.3

Strip the points and multiply as whole numbers:

25×3=75.25 \times 3 = 75.25×3=75.

Count the places: 0.250.250.25 has two decimal places and 0.30.30.3 has one, so the product needs 2+1=32 + 1 = 32+1=3 decimal places. Placing the point three from the right of 757575 needs a zero in the tenths place:

0.25×0.3=0.075.0.25 \times 0.3 = 0.075.0.25×0.3=0.075.

The tenths-place zero is essential. Removing it gives 0.750.750.75, with only two decimal places, ten times too big. The separate zero before the point is a readability convention.

Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100, or 1000

Multiplying by 101010 makes each digit worth ten times as much, so each moves one place left in the place-value chart. On paper, we can show this by sliding the point right. Use two places for 100100100, three for 100010001000:

4.72×10=47.2,4.72×100=472.4.72\times10=47.2,\qquad4.72\times100=472.4.72×10=47.2,4.72×100=472. 0.6×1000=600.0.6\times1000=600.0.6×1000=600.

Dividing reverses the move: slide the point left one place for 101010, two for 100100100, or three for 100010001000.

4.72÷10=0.472,4.72÷100=0.0472.4.72\div10=0.472,\qquad4.72\div100=0.0472.4.72÷10=0.472,4.72÷100=0.0472. 6÷1000=0.006.6\div1000=0.006.6÷1000=0.006.

Add placeholder zeros where needed. In 0.0060.0060.006, both zeros after the point are needed to put the 666 in the thousandths place.

Dividing decimals: make the divisor a whole number

To divide 4.54.54.5 by 0.90.90.9, multiply both numbers by ten:

4.5÷0.9=45÷9=5.4.5\div0.9=45\div9=5.4.5÷0.9=45÷9=5.

The division becomes easier because the divisor, the number we divide by, is whole. Scaling both numbers equally preserves the quotient.

For a nonzero divisor that ends, slide its point right until it is whole. Slide the dividend, the number being divided, the same number of places. Then use long division, placing the quotient’s point directly above the dividend’s.

Why scaling both numbers by ten leaves the quotient unchanged#

Multiply the numerator and denominator of 1.20.5\tfrac{1.2}{0.5}0.51.2​ by ten:

1.20.5=1.2×100.5×10=125=2.4.\frac{1.2}{0.5}=\frac{1.2\times10}{0.5\times10}=\frac{12}{5}=2.4.0.51.2​=0.5×101.2×10​=512​=2.4.

The factor 1010\tfrac{10}{10}1010​ equals one, so the value is unchanged. The divisor is now whole. If the divisor needs more than one move, use the same number of moves in both numbers.

∎

The general slide argument#

Consider a divisor with two decimal places:

0.840.07=0.84×1000.07×100=847=12.\frac{0.84}{0.07}=\frac{0.84\times100}{0.07\times100}=\frac{84}{7}=12.0.070.84​=0.07×1000.84×100​=784​=12.

The scale factor 100100\tfrac{100}{100}100100​ is one. The numbers need not be these particular decimals. Let aaa stand for any dividend and bbb for any nonzero divisor. The equivalent-fractions rule gives

ab=a×10b×10.\frac{a}{b}=\frac{a\times10}{b\times10}.ba​=b×10a×10​.

Repeat this step as many times as needed. A terminating divisor needs only finitely many moves to become whole, and every step preserves the quotient.

∎

Dividing a decimal by a nonzero whole number is the simplest case of all. There is no sliding to do, because the divisor is already whole. You just do long division and place the point in the quotient directly above the point in the dividend.

Worked example 5 Divide 7.2÷0.87.2 \div 0.87.2÷0.8

Move both points one place right:

7.2÷0.8=72÷8=9.7.2\div0.8=72\div8=9.7.2÷0.8=72÷8=9.

Check: 0.8×9=7.20.8\times9=7.20.8×9=7.2.

Worked example 6 Divide 9.6÷0.049.6 \div 0.049.6÷0.04

The divisor needs two moves. Write 9.6=9.609.6=9.609.6=9.60 to make the matching move clear:

9.60÷0.04=960÷4=240.9.60\div0.04=960\div4=240.9.60÷0.04=960÷4=240.

Check: 240×0.04=9.6240\times0.04=9.6240×0.04=9.6. The large quotient makes sense because many small groups of size 0.040.040.04 fit into 9.69.69.6.

Sliding the points only clears the divisor. It does not promise a whole-number quotient, since the dividend can still fail to divide evenly once the divisor is whole.

Worked example 7 Divide 5.4÷1.25.4 \div 1.25.4÷1.2

Move both points one place right:

5.4÷1.2=54÷12.5.4\div1.2=54\div12.5.4÷1.2=54÷12.

Twelve fits into 545454 four times, leaving 54−48=654-48=654−48=6. Write 545454 as 54.054.054.0: the remaining six ones are sixty tenths. Twelve fits into sixty tenths five times, so the next quotient digit is five tenths.

54÷12=4.5.54\div12=4.5.54÷12=4.5.

The divisor became whole, but the answer did not have to.

Worked example 8 Divide 3.5÷43.5 \div 43.5÷4

The divisor is already whole. Write 3.5=3.5003.5=3.5003.5=3.500, and keep the quotient’s point above the dividend’s.

  • Four does not fit into three wholes, so write 000 in the ones place.
  • In 353535 tenths, four fits 888 times, leaving 333 tenths.
  • Those are 303030 hundredths. Four fits 777 times, leaving 222 hundredths.
  • Those are 202020 thousandths. Four fits 555 times, leaving zero.
3.5÷4=0.875.3.5\div4=0.875.3.5÷4=0.875.

Not every division ends with remainder zero. The next lesson explains repeating decimals.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Lining up the points to multiply. Aligning decimal points is for addition and subtraction only. For two decimals that end, multiply by ignoring the points, multiplying as whole numbers, then counting the total decimal places.
  • Forgetting to count places in a product. 0.3×0.20.3 \times 0.20.3×0.2 is 0.060.060.06, not 0.60.60.6. The two factors have one place each, so the product has two. Multiplying two positive numbers below one gives something smaller, not larger. (This place-counting shortcut is for two decimals that end.)
  • Sliding the point the wrong way when multiplying by ten. Multiplying by 101010, 100100100, or 100010001000 moves the point to the right (the number grows); dividing by them moves it left. Count the zeros to know how many places.
  • Sliding only one number when dividing. When you move the point in the divisor, you must move it in the dividend by the same number of places. Moving just one of them changes the quotient.
  • Dropping placeholder zeros in the answer. A product like 0.0750.0750.075 or a sum like 16.0516.0516.05 needs its placeholder zeros after the point. These hold later nonzero digits in the right places; deleting one changes the value. A zero before the point, as in 0.0750.0750.075, instead helps the reader see the point.

Key takeaways

  • For two decimals that end, add and subtract by lining up the decimal points (pad with trailing zeros so every column is filled). Then work column by column as with whole numbers, and the point drops straight down.
  • Lining up the points works because it stacks like place values, and you may only combine digits of the same place value.
  • For two decimals that end, multiply by ignoring the points, multiplying as whole numbers, then giving the product as many decimal places as the two factors have together.
  • Multiplying by 101010, 100100100, or 100010001000 slides the point right by one, two, or three places; dividing by them slides it left.
  • For a divisor that ends, divide by sliding its point right until it is a whole number. Slide the dividend the same number of places, then divide and bring the point straight up.
  • Sliding both points the same amount is multiplying top and bottom by the same number (ten, a hundred, or a thousand). So sliding both points the same amount never changes the quotient.

A bit of history (optional)

Before 1971, British shoppers counted money in pounds, shillings, and pennies: twenty shillings made a pound, and twelve pennies made a shilling. That meant 240240240 pennies to the pound, with two different carrying rules for adding prices.

On Decimal Day, 15 February 1971, the pound changed to 100100100 new pence. Preparing for it meant changing bank accounts, coins, bookkeeping, and machines. Banks closed for normal business from Wednesday afternoon until Monday morning, giving staff Thursday, Friday, and the weekend for the switch.

The NatWest Group’s bank archives describe Scottish clearing staff working through the night to keep old and new payments separate. The head-office canteen stayed open all night too. For the public, Decimal Day was a new way to pay; behind the scenes, it took years of planning and a very busy final weekend.

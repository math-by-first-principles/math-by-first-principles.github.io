Decimal Place Value

Intuition

Look at a price like 2.752.752.75 dollars. The 222 is two whole dollars, but the 777 and the 555 stand for parts of a dollar: seven dimes and five pennies. A dime is one tenth of a dollar, and a penny is one hundredth. That little dot, the decimal point, is what lets you write amounts smaller than one whole. The point does that using the very same ten digits 000 through 999 you already use for whole numbers. Place value keeps going to the right past the ones place, each new column a tenth the size of the one before it.

Learning goals

  • Read a decimal by its places, tenths, hundredths and thousandths
  • Explain why each place right of the point is a tenth of its neighbor
  • Write a decimal such as 3.423.423.42 as the sum of its place values
  • Convert a terminating decimal to a fraction over ten, a hundred, or a thousand
  • Distinguish a trailing zero, which changes nothing, from a placeholder zero, which does
  • Compare two decimals place by place from the left, not by counting digits

Place value does not stop at the ones place

You already know how whole-number places work. Reading a number from right to left, each place is worth ten times the place before it: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands. Read that same row the other way, from left to right, and each place is worth one tenth of the place before it. A thousand becomes a hundred, a hundred becomes ten, and ten becomes one. Nothing forces that dividing-by-ten pattern to stop once you reach the ones place. Keep dividing by ten and you get brand-new, smaller places:

…,100,10,1,110,1100,11000,…\ldots,\quad 100,\quad 10,\quad 1,\quad \tfrac{1}{10},\quad \tfrac{1}{100},\quad \tfrac{1}{1000},\quad \ldots…,100,10,1,101​,1001​,10001​,…

The decimal point marks the boundary between the two regions. Immediately to its left is the ones place; immediately to its right the places continue as tenths, hundredths, and thousandths. The point itself is not a place; it is just a marker. In the number 3.423.423.42, each digit sits in a place that fixes what it is worth:

3.42  =  3⏟ones . 4⏟tenths  2⏟hundredths3.42 \;=\; \underbrace{3}_{\text{ones}}\, . \,\underbrace{4}_{\text{tenths}}\;\underbrace{2}_{\text{hundredths}}3.42=ones3​​.tenths4​​hundredths2​​

Notice that the place names to the right mirror the place names to the left, with “-ths” tacked on. The pairs run tens and tenths, hundreds and hundredths, thousands and thousandths.

Why each place is one tenth of the place to its left

Ten tenths make one whole, the same way ten ones make one ten. So one tenth is one whole split into ten equal parts, and one hundredth is one tenth split into ten equal parts again. The “one tenth” rule to the right of the point is not a separate rule for decimals. It is the exact same bundling rule that builds the whole-number places, simply run in reverse.

Each decimal place is one tenth of the place to its left#

The whole-number places are built by bundling in tens: ten ones make one ten, ten tens make one hundred, ten hundreds make one thousand. Stepping one place to the left therefore multiplies the place value by 101010. Stepping one place to the right undoes that step, so it must divide the place value by 101010.

Now apply that same one-step-right rule starting from the ones place, and do not stop. One step right of the ones place divides 111 by 101010, giving 110\tfrac{1}{10}101​, the tenths place. One step further right divides 110\tfrac{1}{10}101​ by 101010 again, giving 1100\tfrac{1}{100}1001​, the hundredths place. One more step gives 11000\tfrac{1}{1000}10001​, the thousandths, and the pattern never breaks because nothing about the bundling rule changes when you cross the decimal point.

So every place, on either side of the point, is exactly ten times the place to its right. That relation reads equally well in the other direction. Every place is one tenth of the place to its left.

∎

This is why the names mirror. The hundredths place is to the right of the ones place by the same two steps that carry the hundreds place to its left. So one is 100100100 and the other is 1100\tfrac{1}{100}1001​, a matched pair built by the same rule pointing in opposite directions.

Check your understanding

Why is the hundredths place worth one tenth of the tenths place?

Answer choices

Every place-value relation, on both sides of the point, comes from the same bundle-by-ten rule. Ten hundredths make one tenth, exactly as ten ones make one ten. That is why stepping one place to the right always divides the place value by 101010.

A decimal is the sum of its place values

Reading 3.423.423.42 by its places, it is three ones, four tenths, and two hundredths. Writing that out as a sum is called expanded form, and it lays bare exactly what each digit contributes:

3.42=3+410+2100.3.42 = 3 + \frac{4}{10} + \frac{2}{100}.3.42=3+104​+1002​.

This is the same move you already make for whole numbers, where 342=300+40+2342 = 300 + 40 + 2342=300+40+2. The only new part is that some of the places are fractions instead of whole numbers. As with whole numbers, a zero digit contributes nothing to the sum. But when a zero sits before a later nonzero digit, it is still essential: it holds that digit’s place open so the digit lands where it belongs.

Worked example 1 Write 5.8065.8065.806 in expanded form

Read off the place of each digit, working left to right from the point. Here 555 is ones, 888 is tenths, 000 is hundredths, and 666 is thousandths.

Write each digit times its place value, then drop the term that is zero:

5.806=5+810+0100+61000=5+810+61000.5.806 = 5 + \frac{8}{10} + \frac{0}{100} + \frac{6}{1000} = 5 + \frac{8}{10} + \frac{6}{1000}.5.806=5+108​+1000​+10006​=5+108​+10006​.

The 000 in the hundredths place adds nothing to the sum, but it cannot be erased. That zero is the placeholder that keeps the 666 in the thousandths place. Remove it and the 666 would slide left into the hundredths place, turning the number into the much larger 5.865.865.86.

Check your understanding

In the decimal 7.367.367.36, what is the value of the digit 666?

Answer choices

Count places to the right of the point: the first is tenths, the second is hundredths. The 333 is in the tenths place and the 666 is the second digit after the point, so it sits in the hundredths place.

6100=0.06\frac{6}{100} = 0.061006​=0.06

Its place is the hundredths; its value is 6100\tfrac{6}{100}1006​.

The first place after the point is tenths, so a single decimal digit is simply that many tenths.

Decimals can also name points that sit between the whole numbers on the number line. The decimal 0.70.70.7, for instance, sits seven tenths of the way from 000 to 111.

Every terminating decimal is a fraction over ten, a hundred, a thousand, and so on

Because each decimal place is built by dividing by ten, the digits after the point are tenths, hundredths, thousandths, and so on. That means any terminating decimal can be rewritten as a single fraction. Its bottom number is 101010, or 100100100, or 100010001000, and the list keeps going one zero at a time.

Take 0.420.420.42. The 444 is four tenths and the 222 is two hundredths, so in expanded form 0.42=410+21000.42 = \tfrac{4}{10} + \tfrac{2}{100}0.42=104​+1002​. Rewrite the tenths over a hundred, since 410=40100\tfrac{4}{10} = \tfrac{40}{100}104​=10040​, and add:

0.42=40100+2100=42100.0.42 = \frac{40}{100} + \frac{2}{100} = \frac{42}{100}.0.42=10040​+1002​=10042​.

The two-digit string “42” lands directly on top of 100100100. Every place can be rewritten over the smallest place present, exactly as the tenths were rewritten over a hundred here. So the digits after the point always collect into a single count on top of that smallest place. The general rule is quick to apply: the number of digits after the point is the number of zeros in the denominator.

0.7=710,0.42=42100,0.305=3051000.0.7 = \frac{7}{10}, \qquad 0.42 = \frac{42}{100}, \qquad 0.305 = \frac{305}{1000}.0.7=107​,0.42=10042​,0.305=1000305​.

The same rule works when the whole-number part is not zero. Keep it in front and write the part after the point as a fraction over the matching power of ten, or fold everything into one fraction:

3.42=3+42100=342100.3.42 = 3 + \frac{42}{100} = \frac{342}{100}.3.42=3+10042​=100342​.

A fraction read straight off the digits this way is not always in lowest terms; the lesson on converting between fractions and decimals, later in this chapter, covers reducing it in full.

Which fraction equals 0.1250.1250.125?

Answer choices

There are three digits after the point, so the denominator has three zeros: 100010001000. The digit string 125125125 lands directly on top of it, so 0.125=12510000.125 = \tfrac{125}{1000}0.125=1000125​.

The grid below is one whole cut into 100100100 equal squares. So a single square is one hundredth, and a complete row of ten squares is one tenth. Change how many squares are shaded, and the readout names the shaded amount as a count of squares. The readout also names that amount as a fraction over 100100100 and as a decimal, all at the same time.

Shade exactly three complete rows and the same amount answers to two names: 30100=0.30\tfrac{30}{100} = 0.3010030​=0.30 counted in squares, and three tenths, 0.30.30.3, counted in whole rows. Then set the count to 555, and again to 505050. Five squares is half of a single row, while fifty squares is half the whole grid. So 0.050.050.05 and 0.50.50.5 are nowhere near the same size, and the zero that separates them is doing real work.

Whatever count from 000 to 999999 you stop on, the picture is some number of complete rows plus some number of leftover squares smaller than a row. Those two counts are exactly what the tenths digit and the hundredths digit of the decimal record. Shade all 100100100 squares and the ten rows regroup into the single whole 1.001.001.00, so that one count sits outside the pattern rather than breaking it.

Reading a decimal aloud

A decimal that ends is said aloud by its places, and its last place is what names the whole reading. Out loud, 3.423.423.42 is “three and forty-two hundredths.” The 333 is the whole-number part, “and” stands for the point, and “forty-two” reads the two digits after the point as a whole number. The final word names the place where the last digit, the 222, lands. The name of that last place behaves like the denominator for the whole string of digits after the point. That behavior fits what you just saw: 0.42=421000.42 = \tfrac{42}{100}0.42=10042​, “forty-two hundredths.” So for any terminating decimal: read the whole-number part, then say “and” for the point. Read the rest as a whole number, and finish with the name of its last place.

Worked example 2 Write "two and seven hundredths" as a decimal

The whole-number part is two, so start with 222 and a point. The fractional part is “seven hundredths,” and the word hundredths tells you the last digit must land in the hundredths place. The hundredths place is the second place after the point.

To put the 777 in the second place, the tenths place needs a 000 to hold its spot open:

two and seven hundredths=2.07.\text{two and seven hundredths} = 2.07.two and seven hundredths=2.07.

Writing 2.72.72.7 instead would put the 777 in the tenths place, naming seven tenths, which is ten times too big. The placeholder zero is doing real work here.

Trailing zeros change nothing, placeholder zeros change everything

A zero tacked onto the far right end of a decimal does not change its value: 0.30.30.3, 0.300.300.30, and 0.3000.3000.300 are all the same number. This is genuinely useful, because it lets you give two decimals the same number of places. The decimal columns then line up when you compare or add them.

Why 0.3=0.300.3 = 0.300.3=0.30#

Write each one as a fraction. The single place in 0.30.30.3 is tenths, so 0.3=3100.3 = \tfrac{3}{10}0.3=103​. The two places in 0.300.300.30 are hundredths, so 0.30=301000.30 = \tfrac{30}{100}0.30=10030​. Now reduce the second fraction by dividing the top and bottom by 101010:

30100=30÷10100÷10=310.\frac{30}{100} = \frac{30 \div 10}{100 \div 10} = \frac{3}{10}.10030​=100÷1030÷10​=103​.

That is the very same number as 0.30.30.3. Tacking on the extra zero only added 0100\tfrac{0}{100}1000​, which is nothing at all, so the value cannot change. The same reasoning works for any number of trailing zeros: each new zero opens one more place to the right and then leaves it empty.

∎

Be careful, though, because this is only true for zeros on the far right. A zero that sits between the point and a nonzero digit is not optional filler; it is a placeholder doing real work. In 0.050.050.05 the zero pushes the 555 out of the tenths place and into the hundredths place. That shift makes the 555 one tenth as large as the 555 in 0.50.50.5. Erase that zero and you change the value entirely.

Check your understanding

Which of these zeros can be erased without changing the number's value: the zero in 0.600.600.60, or the zero in 0.060.060.06?

Answer choices

The zero in 0.600.600.60 is a trailing zero: nothing sits to its right, so erasing it leaves 0.60.60.6, the same value. The zero in 0.060.060.06 is a placeholder: it sits between the point and the 666, holding the 666 in the hundredths place. Erase it and the 666 slides into the tenths place, turning 0.060.060.06 into 0.60.60.6, ten times as large.

Comparing decimals

To compare two decimals that end, like the ones in this lesson, line up their decimal points and compare digit by digit from the left, exactly as you compare whole numbers.

Worked example 3 Which is larger, 0.450.450.45 or 0.50.50.5?

Give them the same number of decimal places by padding the shorter one with a trailing zero. You already showed that this padding does not change the shorter number’s value:

0.45versus0.50.0.45 \quad \text{versus} \quad 0.50.0.45versus0.50.

Now compare from the left, starting at the tenths place: four tenths against five tenths. Since 4<54 < 54<5, the tenths place settles it:

0.45<0.50,so 0.5 is larger.0.45 < 0.50, \quad \text{so } 0.5 \text{ is larger.}0.45<0.50,so 0.5 is larger.

It does not matter that 0.450.450.45 shows more digits. A single higher place to the left outweighs everything to its right.

The first place where the digits differ decides the whole comparison, exactly as the tenths place settled 0.450.450.45 against 0.50.50.5. What does not work is counting how many digits each number has. More digits do not make a larger number, because the places to the right keep shrinking.

Padding the shorter decimal with trailing zeros is the reliable way to prepare a comparison between two decimals that end. Add zeros until both numbers have the same number of places, and the columns line up on their own.

Check your understanding

Which symbol makes a true statement: 0.6  □  0.580.6 \;\square\; 0.580.6□0.58?

Answer choices

Pad the shorter decimal with a trailing zero so both have two places, then compare from the left: 0.600.600.60 against 0.580.580.58. They agree until the tenths place, where 666 meets 555.

6>5  ⇒  0.60>0.586 > 5 \;\Rightarrow\; 0.60 > 0.586>5⇒0.60>0.58

So 0.6>0.580.6 > 0.580.6>0.58. Having more digits does not make 0.580.580.58 larger.

Worked example 4 Order 0.40.40.4, 0.390.390.39, and 0.4050.4050.405 from least to greatest

Pad every decimal to three places so the columns line up:

0.400,0.390,0.405.0.400, \qquad 0.390, \qquad 0.405.0.400,0.390,0.405.

Compare the tenths place first. Two of them have 444 tenths (0.4000.4000.400 and 0.4050.4050.405) and one has only 333 tenths (0.3900.3900.390), so 0.390.390.39 is the smallest of the three. Now settle the remaining pair by moving right to the hundredths place: 0.4000.4000.400 has 000 hundredths while 0.4050.4050.405 has 000 as well. So move once more to the thousandths place, where 000 meets 555. Since 0<50 < 50<5:

0.39<0.4<0.405.0.39 < 0.4 < 0.405.0.39<0.4<0.405.

The longest-looking number, 0.4050.4050.405, is the largest of the three only because its first differing place is larger, not because it has the most digits.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Thinking more digits means larger. 0.45<0.50.45 < 0.50.45<0.5, because the tenths place is compared first. Line decimals up by the point and compare from the left, never by counting digits.
  • Erasing or ignoring the wrong zeros. A trailing zero on the right is harmless (0.3=0.300.3 = 0.300.3=0.30), but a zero between the point and another digit is not: 0.050.050.05 is only one tenth of 0.50.50.5, because that placeholder zero shifts the 555 into the hundredths place.
  • Mixing up the last-place name. 0.30.30.3 is three tenths, but 0.030.030.03 is three hundredths, ten times smaller. The final digit’s place names the fraction.
  • Confusing place with value. In 7.367.367.36 the place of the 666 is the hundredths, but its value is 6100\tfrac{6}{100}1006​. The place is the column’s name; the value is what the digit contributes.

Key takeaways

  • Place value continues past the decimal point: tenths, hundredths, thousandths. Each of those is one tenth of the place to its left, because the same bundle-by-ten rule runs in reverse.
  • A decimal is the sum of its place values, for example 3.42=3+410+21003.42 = 3 + \tfrac{4}{10} + \tfrac{2}{100}3.42=3+104​+1002​.
  • Every terminating decimal is a fraction over 101010, 100100100, 100010001000, and so on (the digits after the point match the zeros in the denominator), for example 0.42=421000.42 = \tfrac{42}{100}0.42=10042​.
  • For a decimal that ends, read with “and” at the point, and the final place names the denominator, so 3.423.423.42 is “three and forty-two hundredths.”
  • Trailing zeros on the right do not change the value (0.3=0.300.3 = 0.300.3=0.30), but placeholder zeros that shift a digit’s place do.
  • Compare decimals that end by padding to equal length and reading place by place from the left, not by counting digits.

A bit of history (optional)

Two swimmers touch the wall together. The clock still has to name a winner.

That is what happened in the men’s 400400400 meter individual medley at the 1972 Olympic Games. Both swimmers were timed at four minutes and 31.9831.9831.98 seconds by the hundredth. The electronic timer had actually measured one place further right, into the thousandths, and that reading settled it: 31.98131.98131.981 against 31.98331.98331.983, two thousandths of a second apart.

The result stood, but it did not sit well. In a thousandth of a second a swimmer travels about two millimeters. No pool is built to that tolerance, so one lane may simply be a hair shorter than the next. Swimming rules were later changed to time races only to the hundredth, so two equal times now share the medal.

Those judges were arguing about place value. They lined the two times up and compared them from the left, exactly as you did in this lesson. The tenths matched, and so did the hundredths. The first place that differed settled the race, because each place to the right is one tenth of the one before it.

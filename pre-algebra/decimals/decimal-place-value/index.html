Decimal Place Value

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A price of 2.752.752.75 dollars means two whole dollars, seven dimes, and five pennies. Dimes are tenths of a dollar; pennies are hundredths. The decimal point separates the whole-number places from these smaller places. The same ten digits still work: each column to the right is one tenth the size of its neighbor.

Learning goals

  • Read a decimal by its places, tenths, hundredths and thousandths
  • Explain why each place right of the point is a tenth of its neighbor
  • Write a decimal such as 3.423.423.42 as the sum of its place values
  • Convert a terminating decimal to a fraction over ten, a hundred, or a thousand
  • Distinguish a trailing zero, which changes nothing, from a placeholder zero, which does
  • Compare two decimals place by place from the left, not by counting digits

Place value does not stop at the ones place

Whole-number places grow by ten as you move left: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands. Moving right reverses that pattern. Divide a thousand by ten to get a hundred, then ten, then one. Keep dividing by ten to get smaller places:

100,10,1,110,1100,11000,…100,\quad 10,\quad 1,\quad \frac{1}{10},\quad \frac{1}{100},\quad \frac{1}{1000},\quad\ldots100,10,1,101​,1001​,10001​,…

The ones place is just left of the point. The tenths, hundredths, and thousandths are to its right. The point marks a boundary; it is not a place.

In 3.423.423.42, the 333 means three ones, the 444 means four tenths, and the 222 means two hundredths. The place names come in pairs: tens and tenths, hundreds and hundredths. The ones place is the center of this pattern and has no partner; there is no “oneths” place.

Why each place is one tenth of the place to its left

Ten tenths make one whole, just as ten ones make one ten. Smaller places use the same bundling rule in reverse.

Each decimal place is one tenth of the place to its left#

Split one whole into ten equal parts. Each part is 110\tfrac{1}{10}101​. Split one of those tenths into ten equal parts again:

110÷10=1100.\frac{1}{10}\div 10=\frac{1}{100}.101​÷10=1001​.

Ten hundredths therefore make one tenth. Splitting a hundredth into ten equal parts gives a thousandth.

This works at every place: ten units of a smaller place bundle into one unit of its left neighbor. Reversing that bundle divides by ten, so each place is one tenth of the place to its left.

∎

A decimal is the sum of its place values

Writing a number as the sum of its place values gives its expanded form:

3.42=3+410+2100.3.42 = 3 + \frac{4}{10} + \frac{2}{100}.3.42=3+104​+1002​.

This is like 342=300+40+2342=300+40+2342=300+40+2, except some places are fractions. A zero adds nothing to the sum, but it may still hold a later digit in the correct column.

Worked example 1 Write 5.8065.8065.806 in expanded form

The digits name five ones, eight tenths, zero hundredths, and six thousandths:

5.806=5+810+0100+61000.5.806 = 5 + \frac{8}{10} + \frac{0}{100} + \frac{6}{1000}.5.806=5+108​+1000​+10006​.

The zero term can be left out of the sum:

5.806=5+810+61000.5.806 = 5 + \frac{8}{10} + \frac{6}{1000}.5.806=5+108​+10006​.

But the zero in the decimal must stay. Erasing it would move the 666 to hundredths and change the number to 5.865.865.86.

The first place after the point is tenths, so a single decimal digit is simply that many tenths.

Decimals can also name points that sit between the whole numbers on the number line. The decimal 0.70.70.7, for instance, sits seven tenths of the way from 000 to 111.

Every terminating decimal is a fraction over ten, a hundred, a thousand, and so on

A terminating decimal is one whose written digits end. Its last place lets us write it as one fraction.

For 0.420.420.42, rewrite four tenths as forty hundredths, then add two hundredths:

0.42=40100+2100=42100.0.42 = \frac{40}{100}+\frac{2}{100}=\frac{42}{100}.0.42=10040​+1002​=10042​.

The same method works for any terminating decimal: express every place in units of the last place. One, two, or three digits after the point give a denominator of 101010, 100100100, or 100010001000. Each further digit adds one more zero to the denominator:

0.7=710,0.305=3051000.0.7=\frac{7}{10},\qquad 0.305=\frac{305}{1000}.0.7=107​,0.305=1000305​.

Include the whole-number part too:

3.42=3+42100=342100.3.42=3+\frac{42}{100}=\frac{342}{100}.3.42=3+10042​=100342​.

These fractions may still need reducing. We will practice that in the conversion lesson.

Which fraction equals 0.2180.2180.218?

Answer choices

The final digit is in the thousandths place. The number is 218 thousandths:

0.218=21810000.218=\frac{218}{1000}0.218=1000218​

The grid is one whole cut into 100100100 equal squares. One square is a hundredth; a row of ten squares is a tenth.

Shade three complete rows. That is 303030 hundredths, or 333 tenths: 0.30=0.30.30=0.30.30=0.3. Then compare 555 shaded squares with 505050. Five squares cover half a row; fifty cover half the grid. Watch the decimal readout: fifty squares is ten times five, so 0.50.50.5 is ten times 0.050.050.05.

For counts from 000 to 999999, the tenths digit counts complete rows and the hundredths digit counts leftover squares. At 100100100 squares, ten rows regroup into the whole 1.001.001.00.

Reading a decimal aloud

Read 3.423.423.42 as three and forty-two hundredths. Say the whole-number part, “and” for the point, then the digits after it as a whole number. Finish with the name of the last place. Here 424242 counts hundredths, just as in 42100\tfrac{42}{100}10042​.

Worked example 2 Write "two and seven hundredths" as a decimal

Start with 222 for the whole-number part. Seven hundredths puts the 777 two places after the point, so a zero must hold the tenths place:

2+7100=2.07.2+\frac{7}{100}=2.07.2+1007​=2.07.

Writing 2.72.72.7 would name seven tenths instead of seven hundredths.

Trailing zeros change nothing, placeholder zeros change everything

A trailing zero at the right end after the point does not change a decimal: 0.3=0.30=0.3000.3=0.30=0.3000.3=0.30=0.300. This lets you line up the same number of places when comparing or adding.

Why 0.3=0.300.3 = 0.300.3=0.30#

Three tenths and thirty hundredths are equal fractions:

0.30=30100=30÷10100÷10=310=0.3.0.30=\frac{30}{100}=\frac{30\div10}{100\div10}=\frac{3}{10}=0.3.0.30=10030​=100÷1030÷10​=103​=0.3.

In place-value terms, the extra zero adds 0100\tfrac{0}{100}1000​. More generally, appending zeros after the last decimal place adds only zero-valued terms. It leaves every existing digit in its place, so the number stays the same.

∎

An internal placeholder zero is different. In 0.050.050.05, it keeps the 555 in the hundredths place. Erasing that zero gives 0.50.50.5, which is ten times as large.

Check your understanding

Delete the last zero in 2.802.802.80, or the zero after the point in 2.082.082.08. Which deletion keeps the value?

Answer choices

The trailing zero gives 2.80=2.82.80=2.82.80=2.8: no other digit changes place.

Deleting the internal zero gives 2.08≠2.82.08\ne2.82.08=2.8. It moves the 8 from hundredths to tenths, changing its contribution from 8100\tfrac{8}{100}1008​ to 810\tfrac{8}{10}108​.

Comparing decimals

To compare two decimals that end, like the ones in this lesson, line up their decimal points and compare digit by digit from the left, exactly as you compare whole numbers.

Worked example 3 Which is larger, 0.450.450.45 or 0.50.50.5?

Give them the same number of decimal places by padding the shorter one with a trailing zero. You already showed that this padding does not change the shorter number’s value:

0.45versus0.50.0.45 \quad \text{versus} \quad 0.50.0.45versus0.50.

Now compare from the left, starting at the tenths place: four tenths against five tenths. Since 4<54 < 54<5, the tenths place settles it:

0.45<0.50,so 0.5 is larger.0.45 < 0.50, \quad \text{so } 0.5 \text{ is larger.}0.45<0.50,so 0.5 is larger.

It does not matter that 0.450.450.45 shows more digits. A single higher place to the left outweighs everything to its right.

The first differing place decides the comparison. Padding the shorter decimal with trailing zeros makes the columns easy to match; counting digits does not tell you which value is larger.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Thinking more digits means larger. 0.45<0.50.45 < 0.50.45<0.5, because the tenths place is compared first. Line decimals up by the point and compare from the left, never by counting digits.
  • Erasing or ignoring the wrong zeros. A trailing zero on the right is harmless (0.3=0.300.3 = 0.300.3=0.30), but a zero between the point and another digit is not: 0.050.050.05 is only one tenth of 0.50.50.5, because that placeholder zero shifts the 555 into the hundredths place.
  • Mixing up the last-place name. 0.30.30.3 is three tenths, but 0.030.030.03 is three hundredths, ten times smaller. The final digit’s place names the fraction.
  • Confusing place with value. In 7.367.367.36 the place of the 666 is the hundredths, but its value is 6100\tfrac{6}{100}1006​. The place is the column’s name; the value is what the digit contributes.

Key takeaways

  • Place value continues past the decimal point: tenths, hundredths, thousandths. Each of those is one tenth of the place to its left, because the same bundle-by-ten rule runs in reverse.
  • A decimal is the sum of its place values, for example 3.42=3+410+21003.42 = 3 + \tfrac{4}{10} + \tfrac{2}{100}3.42=3+104​+1002​.
  • Every terminating decimal is a fraction over 101010, 100100100, 100010001000, and so on (the digits after the point match the zeros in the denominator), for example 0.42=421000.42 = \tfrac{42}{100}0.42=10042​.
  • For a decimal that ends, read with “and” at the point, and the final place names the denominator, so 3.423.423.42 is “three and forty-two hundredths.”
  • Trailing zeros on the right do not change the value (0.3=0.300.3 = 0.300.3=0.30), but placeholder zeros that shift a digit’s place do.
  • Compare decimals that end by padding to equal length and reading place by place from the left, not by counting digits.

A bit of history (optional)

At the 1972 Munich Olympics, Sweden’s Gunnar Larsson and the United States’ Tim McKee finished the men’s 400-meter individual medley with the same displayed time: four minutes and 31.9831.9831.98 seconds.

The electronic timer had recorded another digit. Larsson’s time was 4:31.9814{:}31.9814:31.981, McKee’s 4:31.9834{:}31.9834:31.983. Officials awarded Larsson gold by two thousandths of a second. The Swedish Olympic Committee’s account recalls how this tiny margin later changed swimming’s rules.

Such fine timing raised a practical question: could minute differences in lane length decide a medal? Thousandths were still used at the 1973 World Championships, but later international races used hundredths to decide ties. A clock could record an extra digit; the sport had to decide whether that digit was a fair way to separate two swimmers.

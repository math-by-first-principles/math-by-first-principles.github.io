Converting Between Fractions and Decimals

Intuition

A fraction like 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ and a decimal like 0.750.750.75 are two ways of writing the very same amount, three quarters of a whole. Neither is more correct than the other; which one you use depends on the job. A recipe is happy with 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ of a cup, but a calculator screen and a price tag prefer 0.750.750.75. A decimal that ends is already a fraction once you read its places, and a fraction turns into a decimal by the division its bar has always meant.

Learning goals

  • Convert a decimal that ends to a fraction by reading its last place as the denominator
  • Divide numerator by denominator to turn any fraction into a decimal
  • Scale to a denominator of ten or a hundred instead of dividing
  • Predict whether a fraction terminates from the prime factors of its denominator
  • Explain why a fraction's decimal, when it does not end, must repeat, since a remainder must come back

A decimal that ends is already a fraction

You saw in the place-value lesson that the digits after the decimal point are tenths, hundredths, and thousandths. That single fact is the whole method for turning a decimal that ends into a fraction.

Take 0.490.490.49. The 444 is four tenths and the 999 is nine hundredths, so written out it is 410+9100\tfrac{4}{10} + \tfrac{9}{100}104​+1009​. Put both pieces over a hundred and add:

0.49=40100+9100=49100.0.49 = \frac{40}{100} + \frac{9}{100} = \frac{49}{100}.0.49=10040​+1009​=10049​.

The two-digit string "494949" lands directly on top of 100100100, which is the shortcut. A decimal that ends always works this way, because its last place names a denominator every digit can be written over. The rule is short: the number of digits after the point is the number of zeros in the denominator.

0.7=710,0.49=49100,0.013=131000.0.7 = \frac{7}{10}, \qquad 0.49 = \frac{49}{100}, \qquad 0.013 = \frac{13}{1000}.0.7=107​,0.49=10049​,0.013=100013​.

A fraction read straight off the digits may not yet be in lowest terms, so the conversion has a second half. A fraction is in lowest terms when its top and bottom share no factor larger than 111. Reduce by dividing the top and bottom by their greatest common factor, the same simplifying you did in the fractions chapter.

Worked example 1 Write 0.350.350.35 as a fraction in lowest terms

There are two digits after the point, so the denominator is a hundred and the digits sit on top:

0.35=35100.0.35 = \frac{35}{100}.0.35=10035​.

Now reduce. The greatest common factor of 353535 and 100100100 is 555, so divide the top and bottom by 555:

35100=35÷5100÷5=720.\frac{35}{100} = \frac{35 \div 5}{100 \div 5} = \frac{7}{20}.10035​=100÷535÷5​=207​.

Since 777 and 202020 share no common factor larger than 111, the fraction 720\tfrac{7}{20}207​ is in lowest terms, and that is the answer.

A number with a whole part converts the same way: leave the whole part alone and convert only the decimal piece. The result is a mixed number you can read off directly. For instance 2.62.62.6 is 2610=2352\tfrac{6}{10} = 2\tfrac{3}{5}2106​=253​.

A fraction is a division waiting to happen

Going the other direction, from a fraction to a decimal, rests on a fact you have known since the fractions chapter. The fraction bar means divide.

Take 38\tfrac{3}{8}83​. The bar says divide 333 by 888. Since 888 does not go into 333, write 000 in the ones place, bring the point up, and keep dividing into 3.0003.0003.000, exactly as you did with whole-number long division, appending a zero each time there is still a remainder to carry:

38=3÷8=0.375.\frac{3}{8} = 3 \div 8 = 0.375.83​=3÷8=0.375.

Every fraction goes the same way: it is the numerator divided by the denominator, so carry out that division and keep the decimal point in the quotient lined up above the point in the dividend. The remainder does not always clear, and the last section of this lesson says which fractions it clears for.

Why dividing the numerator by the denominator gives the decimal#

Run the argument on 94\tfrac{9}{4}49​. Split 999 wholes equally among 444 groups. Collecting all four of those shares has to rebuild the 999 you started with. So the value of 94\tfrac{9}{4}49​ is the number that gives 999 when you take 444 of it, which is exactly what 9÷49 \div 49÷4 asks for. Long division answers that a place at a time. Four goes into 999 twice, leaving 111 over. That 111 becomes 101010 tenths, and four goes into 101010 twice, leaving 222 tenths. Those 222 tenths become 202020 hundredths, and four goes into 202020 exactly five times. The quotient is 2.252.252.25, and four copies of 2.252.252.25 do rebuild the 999.

Nothing in that depended on the digits 999 and 444: the same reasoning turns any fraction into the division that produces its decimal.

Worked example 2 Write 78\tfrac{7}{8}87​ as a decimal

The bar means divide, so compute 7÷87 \div 87÷8. Eight does not fit into 777, so the quotient starts with 000 and a point, and the division runs into 7.0007.0007.000.

Eight into 707070 tenths goes 888 times (8×8=648 \times 8 = 648×8=64), leaving 666 tenths, which become 606060 hundredths. Eight into 606060 goes 777 times (565656), leaving 444 hundredths, which become 404040 thousandths. Eight into 404040 goes exactly 555 times with no remainder:

78=7÷8=0.875.\frac{7}{8} = 7 \div 8 = 0.875.87​=7÷8=0.875.

The remainder reached 000, so the decimal terminates. A quick sanity check: 78\tfrac{7}{8}87​ is just under 111, and 0.8750.8750.875 is just under 111, so the size is right.

58=5÷8=0.625\frac{5}{8} = 5 \div 8 = 0.62585​=5÷8=0.625

1.61.61.6 comes from dividing the wrong way round, 8÷58 \div 58÷5. 0.580.580.58 and 0.850.850.85 just rearrange the two digits without dividing at all.

A shortcut when the denominator scales cleanly

Long division always works, but you can often skip it. The fraction 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ is the classic case. A hundred is 4×254 \times 254×25, so multiply the top and bottom by 252525:

34=3×254×25=75100=0.75.\frac{3}{4} = \frac{3 \times 25}{4 \times 25} = \frac{75}{100} = 0.75.43​=4×253×25​=10075​=0.75.

That step uses equivalent fractions: multiplying the top and bottom by the same number does not change the value. So 75100\tfrac{75}{100}10075​ is still three quarters, now written with a bottom you can read as hundredths. Once the bottom is a hundred, the top is simply the hundredths, and the decimal is read off directly. This is the same conversion as the long division 3÷4=0.753 \div 4 = 0.753÷4=0.75, just reached by scaling instead of dividing.

The general rule follows. If the denominator divides evenly into ten, a hundred, a thousand, or any longer string of zeros, then scaling the fraction to that denominator turns it into a decimal. You choose the multiplier that makes the denominator into that power of ten. You can then read the decimal straight off, with no division of the numerator at all. Ten, a hundred, and a thousand are simply the first three worth trying by hand; if none of them works, a larger one sometimes still does; the last section of this lesson gives the test that settles the question for good.

Choosing the multiplier is the whole skill here. Take 35\tfrac{3}{5}53​: multiplying top and bottom by 222 gives 610\tfrac{6}{10}106​, which reads straight off as 0.60.60.6. Multiplying by 333 or by 444 instead gives 915\tfrac{9}{15}159​ and 1220\tfrac{12}{20}2012​, equally true names for the same amount. But 151515 and 202020 are not ten, a hundred, or a thousand, so neither can be read off as a decimal without more scaling.

Worked example 3 Write 720\tfrac{7}{20}207​ as a decimal by scaling

Look at the denominator 202020. It does not divide 101010, but it does divide 100100100, because 100=20×5100 = 20 \times 5100=20×5. So multiply the top and bottom by 555 to make the denominator a hundred:

720=7×520×5=35100.\frac{7}{20} = \frac{7 \times 5}{20 \times 5} = \frac{35}{100}.207​=20×57×5​=10035​.

A denominator of a hundred means two decimal places, and the top "353535" gives those two digits:

720=35100=0.35.\frac{7}{20} = \frac{35}{100} = 0.35.207​=10035​=0.35.

This is the reverse of Worked Example 1, where 0.350.350.35 reduced to 720\tfrac{7}{20}207​, a useful check that the two directions agree.

The picture below shows why scaling does not change the amount: cutting each of the five fifths into two makes ten tenths. The shaded portion is unchanged, so 25\tfrac{2}{5}52​ and 410=0.4\tfrac{4}{10} = 0.4104​=0.4 are the same number.

Terminating or repeating: it depends on the denominator

Some fractions, like 38=0.375\tfrac{3}{8} = 0.37583​=0.375, give a decimal that ends. Others do not. Try 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ with long division: 1÷31 \div 31÷3 gives 0.333…0.333\ldots0.333…, where the remainder is 111 at every step, so the 333s never stop. We write a repeating decimal with a bar over the block of digits that repeats forever:

13=0.333…=0.3‾,16=0.1666…=0.16‾.\frac{1}{3} = 0.333\ldots = 0.\overline{3}, \qquad \frac{1}{6} = 0.1666\ldots = 0.1\overline{6}.31​=0.333…=0.3,61​=0.1666…=0.16.

You can predict which happens before doing any division by looking at the prime factors of the denominator. The dividing line is clean: a fraction in lowest terms terminates exactly when its denominator has no prime factor other than 222 and 555. If any other prime, such as 333 or 777, survives in the denominator, the decimal repeats.

Why only the primes 2 and 5 give a terminating decimal#

First see why 222 and 555 are the special primes. Start with the denominators a terminating decimal can have. A decimal with one place is a count of tenths. A decimal with two places is a count of hundredths. Three places count thousandths. So the denominators that can occur are ten, a hundred, a thousand, and so on. They are precisely the ”111 followed by zeros” numbers. A decimal therefore terminates exactly when the fraction can be rewritten with one of those denominators. Now factor those denominators: ten is 2×52 \times 52×5, a hundred is 2×2×5×52 \times 2 \times 5 \times 52×2×5×5, a thousand is three 222s times three 555s. Every one of them is built from nothing but 222s and 555s. Now think about a fraction in lowest terms. Such a fraction can be scaled up to one of these denominators only if its denominator already divides one of them. And a denominator divides one of them only when it is built from nothing but 222s and 555s. For example 720\tfrac{7}{20}207​ has denominator 20=2×2×520 = 2 \times 2 \times 520=2×2×5, all 222s and 555s, and indeed 720=35100\tfrac{7}{20} = \tfrac{35}{100}207​=10035​ terminates. A denominator does not need both primes, and 18\tfrac{1}{8}81​ shows why. Its denominator is 8=2×2×28 = 2 \times 2 \times 28=2×2×2, with no 555 at all, yet 1000÷8=1251000 \div 8 = 1251000÷8=125, so 888 divides a thousand and 18=1251000=0.125\tfrac{1}{8} = \tfrac{125}{1000} = 0.12581​=1000125​=0.125. But 16\tfrac{1}{6}61​ has denominator 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3. That stubborn factor of 333 is the obstacle. It can never be turned into a string of 222s and 555s. So no ”111 followed by zeros” number is a multiple of 666. The decimal for 16\tfrac{1}{6}61​ therefore cannot terminate.

∎

Why a fraction's decimal, when it does not end, must repeat#

When dividing one whole number by another does not stop, the digits do not wander on forever without a pattern; they settle into a repeating block. Watch that happen on 411\tfrac{4}{11}114​, where the starting remainder comes back after two steps. Dividing 404040 by 111111 gives the digit 333 and leaves a remainder of 777. Dividing 707070 by 111111 gives the digit 666 and leaves a remainder of 444. That 444 is the remainder the division started with, so the next two digits have to be 333 and 666 again. Only the remainders 444 and 777 ever appeared, and the return of the 444 forces that same block of digits again. The decimal is 0.36‾0.\overline{36}0.36, and it cycles because a remainder came back.

Nothing about 111111 was essential to that. In any long division, every remainder is a whole number smaller than the divisor, so there are only finitely many remainders the division can ever produce. If a remainder of 000 never turns up, the division runs forever while drawing from that same short, fixed list, so eventually some remainder has to repeat. The instant it does, the digits that follow it must match the digits that followed it the first time, because each digit is decided entirely by the current remainder. From there the same block of digits cycles forever. That is why dividing one whole number by another gives only one of two kinds of decimal: it ends, or it settles into a repeating block. Nothing else can happen.

Check your understanding

Dividing 555 by 666, the remainder 222 shows up twice in a row. What does that guarantee about the decimal?

Answer choices

Every digit in a long division is decided entirely by the current remainder: the same remainder always produces the same next digit and the same next remainder. So once a remainder repeats, the whole chain of digits after it has to repeat too, forever. That is the reason a fraction's decimal can only end or settle into a repeating block; a remainder returning does not stop the division on its own, and the exact repeating digits still have to come from working the division out.

To apply the test, reduce the fraction to lowest terms first, then prime factorize the denominator. Reducing first matters: 612\tfrac{6}{12}126​ looks like it has the bad factor 333. But it reduces to 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, whose denominator is just 222, so it terminates (0.50.50.5).

Worked example 4 Without dividing, decide whether 940\tfrac{9}{40}409​ and 512\tfrac{5}{12}125​ terminate

Apply the test to each: reduce to lowest terms, then check the prime factors of the denominator.

For 940\tfrac{9}{40}409​, the numerator 999 and denominator 404040 share no common factor, so it is already in lowest terms. Prime factorize the denominator:

40=2×2×2×5.40 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 5.40=2×2×2×5.

No prime other than 222 and 555 appears, so 940\tfrac{9}{40}409​ terminates. Scaling confirms it, since 40×25=100040 \times 25 = 100040×25=1000, giving 940=2251000=0.225\tfrac{9}{40} = \tfrac{225}{1000} = 0.225409​=1000225​=0.225.

For 512\tfrac{5}{12}125​, the fraction is already in lowest terms, and

12=2×2×3.12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3.12=2×2×3.

The factor of 333 is neither 222 nor 555, so 512\tfrac{5}{12}125​ repeats. Dividing confirms it: 5÷12=0.41666…=0.416‾5 \div 12 = 0.41666\ldots = 0.41\overline{6}5÷12=0.41666…=0.416.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Dividing the wrong way. The fraction ab\tfrac{a}{b}ba​ is a÷ba \div ba÷b, top divided by bottom, no matter which number is bigger. For 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ that is 3÷4=0.753 \div 4 = 0.753÷4=0.75, not 4÷34 \div 34÷3; for 94\tfrac{9}{4}49​ it is still numerator over denominator, 9÷49 \div 49÷4, even though 999 is the larger number. Reversing the order is the most common slip.
  • Forgetting to reduce a decimal-to-fraction answer. 0.35=351000.35 = \tfrac{35}{100}0.35=10035​ is correct but not finished. Divide by the greatest common factor to reach 720\tfrac{7}{20}207​.
  • Miscounting decimal places. 0.0130.0130.013 has three digits after the point, so the denominator is a thousand, giving 131000\tfrac{13}{1000}100013​, not 13100\tfrac{13}{100}10013​. The number of digits is the number of zeros.
  • Testing for termination before reducing. 612\tfrac{6}{12}126​ seems to have a factor of 333 in the bottom, but it reduces to 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ and terminates. Always reduce to lowest terms before prime factorizing the denominator.
  • Writing a repeating decimal as if it ends. Rounding 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ to 0.330.330.33 is an approximation, not an equality. The exact value is 0.3‾0.\overline{3}0.3, with the 333s continuing forever.

Key takeaways

  • A decimal that ends is already a fraction: the digits after the point sit over ten, a hundred, or a thousand (one zero per digit). So 0.49=491000.49 = \tfrac{49}{100}0.49=10049​; then reduce with the GCF, for example 0.35=7200.35 = \tfrac{7}{20}0.35=207​.
  • A fraction is a division: the bar means a÷ba \div ba÷b, so dividing the numerator by the denominator gives the decimal, for example 38=3÷8=0.375\tfrac{3}{8} = 3 \div 8 = 0.37583​=3÷8=0.375.
  • When the denominator divides ten, a hundred, a thousand, or any longer string of zeros, scale to that denominator and read the decimal off instead of dividing, for example 34=75100=0.75\tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{75}{100} = 0.7543​=10075​=0.75.
  • A fraction in lowest terms terminates exactly when its denominator has no prime factor other than 222 and 555. Any other prime in the reduced denominator makes the decimal repeat, written with an overline like 13=0.3‾\tfrac{1}{3} = 0.\overline{3}31​=0.3.
  • A fraction’s decimal, when it does not end, must repeat, because long division has only finitely many possible remainders. So one remainder is forced to recur, and the digits cycle from there.

Why the fraction bar always means divide

The lesson showed the reasoning on 94\tfrac{9}{4}49​: sharing 999 wholes into 444 equal groups has to rebuild the 999, which is exactly what 9÷49 \div 49÷4 means. Here is that same argument written with letters, so it covers every fraction at once, together with the reason long division actually builds the decimal one place at a time.

Why dividing the numerator by the denominator gives the decimal, for any fraction#

Call the decimal you are looking for ddd. By the meaning of the fraction bar, ab\tfrac{a}{b}ba​ is the number ddd for which b×d=ab \times d = ab×d=a: split aaa into bbb equal shares, and collecting all bbb of those shares must rebuild aaa. But “the number that gives aaa when multiplied by bbb” is the definition of a÷ba \div ba÷b. So the value of the fraction and the result of the division a÷ba \div ba÷b are the same number, and computing one computes the other.

Long division produces that number one decimal place at a time. How many times does the divisor fit into the current amount? You record that digit in the quotient. Then you carry the leftover remainder to the next place by attaching a zero, which makes the remainder ten times as many of the next smaller place. Every digit you write is the correct count for its place value. Digit by digit, that quotient is the decimal expansion of a÷ba \div ba÷b, so it is the decimal expansion of ab\tfrac{a}{b}ba​ as well.

∎

Counting exactly how soon a remainder must repeat

The lesson gives the reason a repeat is forced: only finitely many remainders exist, so one has to recur. Here that count is made exact, for any denominator bbb.

A guaranteed bound on how long a repeating block can be#

Take the long division of aaa by bbb. Every step produces a remainder, and each remainder is a whole number from 000 up to b−1b - 1b−1, so the division has only bbb possible remainders to offer. If a remainder of 000 ever appears, the division stops and the decimal terminates. Now suppose 000 never appears. That leaves only the b−1b - 1b−1 values from 111 up to b−1b - 1b−1, so within at most bbb steps some earlier remainder has to show up again: there are more steps than there are available remainders, so there is nowhere else for the remainders to go. The moment a remainder repeats, the digits that follow it must match the digits that followed it the first time, because each digit is decided entirely by the current remainder. So the repeating block can never be longer than b−1b - 1b−1 digits. For 411\tfrac{4}{11}114​, b−1=10b - 1 = 10b−1=10, and the actual block, 363636, is well inside that bound.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

Divide 111 by 777 and the digits keep coming. Nothing on the page promises that they will ever settle into a loop. You could grind out a hundred places and still be guessing.

The proof that they must comes from an idea small enough to hold in one hand, one that mathematicians eventually named the pigeonhole principle, or the drawer principle: put more socks into drawers than there are drawers, and some drawer ends up holding two. The name is often linked to the German mathematician Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, who used the idea in the early 1800s. It sounds too obvious to be worth naming, yet it decides questions that nothing else can reach.

Now look again at your long division. Dividing by 777, every remainder has to be one of 0,1,2,3,4,5,60, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 60,1,2,3,4,5,6. That is seven drawers, and the division keeps handing you socks. Some remainder must come back a second time. The moment it does, the same digits that followed it the first time must follow it again.

That is the whole reason a fraction’s decimal, when it does not end, must repeat. It is the argument you worked through in this lesson.

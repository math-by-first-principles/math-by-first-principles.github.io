Operations with Signed Rational Numbers

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A thermometer on a winter morning reads −2.5-2.5−2.5 degrees. A friend lends you three quarters of a dollar, and until you pay it back your balance with your friend is −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ of a dollar. Neither number is an integer, and neither is positive. The integers chapter settled how signs behave. The fractions chapter and this one settled how fractions and decimals behave. This lesson puts the two together. A calculation splits into two questions: its sign, decided by the integer rules, and its size, worked out with the fraction and decimal methods you already have. Zero keeps one old habit too: it still refuses to be a divisor.

Learning goals

  • Write a negative fraction or decimal as a fraction of two integers with a nonzero denominator
  • Compare and order signed fractions and decimals by their positions on the number line
  • Add and subtract signed rational numbers by deciding the sign, then working out the size
  • Multiply and divide signed rational numbers, and explain why zero can never be the divisor
  • Explain why −34\frac{-3}{4}4−3​, 3−4\frac{3}{-4}−43​ and −34-\frac{3}{4}−43​ all name the same number

Fractions and decimals on both sides of zero

The fraction 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ sits three quarters of a unit to the right of zero. Its opposite, −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​, sits the same distance to the left, between −1-1−1 and 000. In the same way −2.5-2.5−2.5 is the opposite of 2.52.52.5, halfway between −3-3−3 and −2-2−2. A negative fraction or decimal is the mirror image of a positive one across zero, exactly as −5-5−5 mirrors 555.

Every number in that picture can be written as one integer divided by another. The number 1.51.51.5 is 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​, and the integer −2-2−2 is −21\tfrac{-2}{1}1−2​. For −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​, ask what (−3)÷4(-3) \div 4(−3)÷4 is, which means asking what number times 444 gives −3-3−3. Four copies of −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ are four steps of three quarters to the left, and together they land 333 units left of zero. So 4×(−34)=−34 \times \left(-\tfrac{3}{4}\right) = -34×(−43​)=−3, and therefore

−34=−34.\frac{-3}{4} = -\frac{3}{4}.4−3​=−43​.

A rational number is a number that can be written as a fraction ab\frac{a}{b}ba​, where aaa and bbb are integers and bbb is not zero.

The denominator must not be zero because the bar means divide, and dividing by zero never gives one definite number. Many familiar numbers pass the test. Every integer is rational, since −7=−71-7 = \tfrac{-7}{1}−7=1−7​. Zero is rational too, as 0=010 = \tfrac{0}{1}0=10​, and it is still neither positive nor negative and still its own opposite. Every decimal that ends is rational, because the decimals chapter wrote each one as a fraction over a power of ten, such as 101010, 100100100 or 100010001000. For example −2.5=−2510=−52-2.5 = -\tfrac{25}{10} = \tfrac{-5}{2}−2.5=−1025​=2−5​.

A repeating decimal that came from dividing two integers is rational for the plainest reason: it is that fraction. The decimal −0.3‾-0.\overline{3}−0.3 is −13-\tfrac{1}{3}−31​. (Algebra I shows that every repeating decimal is a fraction, even one you did not get by dividing.) A repeating decimal has no last digit, though, so writing out its digits has to stop somewhere, and the stopped decimal is no longer exact. When one turns up in a calculation, work with its fraction instead.

Comparing and ordering

The integers chapter compared numbers by where they sit: of two numbers on the line, the one farther right is the greater. That rule talked about positions, never about whole numbers, so it still decides every comparison. Every negative is less than zero, and less than every positive, so −18<0<0.01-\tfrac{1}{8} < 0 < 0.01−81​<0<0.01.

Two negatives need more care. The absolute value of a number is still its distance from zero, its size without its sign, and now that distance can be a fraction of a unit: ∣−0.6∣=0.6\lvert -0.6 \rvert = 0.6∣−0.6∣=0.6 and ∣−0.45∣=0.45\lvert -0.45 \rvert = 0.45∣−0.45∣=0.45. Compare −0.6-0.6−0.6 and −0.45-0.45−0.45. The number −0.6-0.6−0.6 is farther from zero, and on the negative side farther from zero means farther left. So

−0.6<−0.45,-0.6 < -0.45,−0.6<−0.45,

even though 0.6>0.450.6 > 0.450.6>0.45. That reversal is for two negatives only. Of two positives, the one farther from zero is the greater, and any negative is less than any positive, whatever their absolute values.

When the two numbers are written in different forms, put them in one form first. Compare −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ and −0.6-0.6−0.6. The fraction 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ has a repeating decimal, so change the decimal instead: 0.6=350.6 = \tfrac{3}{5}0.6=53​. Over the common denominator 151515, the sizes are 1015\tfrac{10}{15}1510​ and 915\tfrac{9}{15}159​. The number −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ is farther from zero, so it is farther left: −23<−0.6-\tfrac{2}{3} < -0.6−32​<−0.6.

Adding and subtracting: the sign, then the size

The integers chapter added signed numbers by walking along the number line. A walk does not care whether its steps are whole units. Start at 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ and add −56-\tfrac{5}{6}−65​, which is a step of five sixths to the left. The first half unit of that step brings you to zero. The rest of it, 56−12=13\tfrac{5}{6} - \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{3}65​−21​=31​, carries you past zero, so you land at −13-\tfrac{1}{3}−31​.

That walk split into two separate questions. Which side of zero do you finish on? And how far from zero are you? The first is a question about signs, and the integer rules answer it. The second is a question about sizes, and the fraction methods answer it.

So the rules from the integers chapter carry straight over, with fraction and decimal sizes in place of whole-number ones.

Same signs: add the sizes and keep the shared sign. Different signs: subtract the smaller size from the larger, and take the sign of the number farther from zero. Subtraction: add the opposite, a−b=a+(−b)a - b = a + (-b)a−b=a+(−b).

When the two sizes are equal and the signs differ, nothing is left over, and the sum is zero: 25+(−25)=0\tfrac{2}{5} + \left(-\tfrac{2}{5}\right) = 052​+(−52​)=0. That is the zero pair from the integers chapter, with a fraction in each half. Adding zero changes nothing, so zero needs no rule of its own.

This settles a question the fractions chapter left open. Adding and Subtracting Fractions kept every difference zero or positive by putting the larger fraction first. Now 14−23\tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{2}{3}41​−32​ has an answer. Rewrite it as 14+(−23)\tfrac{1}{4} + \left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right)41​+(−32​). The sizes are 312\tfrac{3}{12}123​ and 812\tfrac{8}{12}128​, so the difference of the sizes is 512\tfrac{5}{12}125​. The number −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ is farther from zero, so

14−23=−512.\frac{1}{4} - \frac{2}{3} = -\frac{5}{12}.41​−32​=−125​.

2.50−0.75=1.75.2.50 - 0.75 = 1.75.2.50−0.75=1.75.

The number −2.5-2.5−2.5 is farther from zero, so the answer takes its sign:

The thermometer reads −1.75-1.75−1.75 degrees. That makes sense: the temperature went up, but by less than it needed to reach zero.

Owing 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ of a dollar is a balance of −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​. Borrowing more adds to what you owe, so your balance with your friend goes down, and the new balance is −34−25-\tfrac{3}{4} - \tfrac{2}{5}−43​−52​.

−34−25=−34+(−25).-\frac{3}{4} - \frac{2}{5} = -\frac{3}{4} + \left(-\frac{2}{5}\right).−43​−52​=−43​+(−52​).

Now both numbers are negative, so add the sizes and keep the minus sign. Over the common denominator 202020, the sizes are 1520\tfrac{15}{20}2015​ and 820\tfrac{8}{20}208​:

1520+820=2320.\frac{15}{20} + \frac{8}{20} = \frac{23}{20}.2015​+208​=2023​.

So the balance is −2320-\tfrac{23}{20}−2023​ of a dollar, which is −1320-1\tfrac{3}{20}−1203​, the opposite of 13201\tfrac{3}{20}1203​ (the minus sign covers the whole mixed number). The number 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ is farther from zero, so the answer is positive:

−0.4+23=415.-0.4 + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{15}.−0.4+32​=154​.

Rounding 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ to 0.670.670.67 first would give 0.270.270.27. That is close to 415\tfrac{4}{15}154​, which is about 0.2670.2670.267, but it is not equal to it. The fraction is the exact answer.

Multiplying and dividing

Multiplying two negatives was where the integers chapter needed its most careful argument.

The integer sign rules still hold for fractions and decimals#

Start with a positive times a negative, 35×(−23)\tfrac{3}{5} \times \left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right)53​×(−32​). From the fractions chapter, 35\tfrac{3}{5}53​ of an amount means three fifths of it. Here the amount is a step of two thirds to the left. Three fifths of a leftward step is a shorter leftward step, and its length is 35×23=25\tfrac{3}{5} \times \tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{2}{5}53​×32​=52​. So

35×(−23)=−25.\frac{3}{5} \times \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) = -\frac{2}{5}.53​×(−32​)=−52​.

Swapping the two factors cannot change a product, so (−23)×35\left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \times \tfrac{3}{5}(−32​)×53​ is also −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​.

Now take two negatives, (−23)×(−35)\left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \times \left(-\tfrac{3}{5}\right)(−32​)×(−53​). Use the same trick the integers chapter used, a sum that is worth nothing. Since 35+(−35)=0\tfrac{3}{5} + \left(-\tfrac{3}{5}\right) = 053​+(−53​)=0, multiplying it by −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ must give 000. The distributive property works it out the other way:

(−23)×35+(−23)×(−35)=0.\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) \times \frac{3}{5} + \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) \times \left(-\frac{3}{5}\right) = 0.(−32​)×53​+(−32​)×(−53​)=0.

The first product is −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​, from the step before. The only number that brings −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​ up to zero is 25\tfrac{2}{5}52​. So (−23)×(−35)=25\left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \times \left(-\tfrac{3}{5}\right) = \tfrac{2}{5}(−32​)×(−53​)=52​, and two negatives make a positive here too.

Division is still multiplication with a factor missing. To find (−25)÷(−23)\left(-\tfrac{2}{5}\right) \div \left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right)(−52​)÷(−32​), ask what number times −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ gives −25-\tfrac{2}{5}−52​. The first step showed that 35\tfrac{3}{5}53​ does, so the quotient is the positive number 35\tfrac{3}{5}53​.

Nothing in those steps depended on two thirds or three fifths. A positive amount of a leftward step, whether three fifths of it or one and a half times it, is still a leftward step. The zero-sum trick works for any pair of numbers, and division is always a missing factor. Decimals are covered as well, because every decimal that ends is a fraction. So the sign rules of the integers chapter hold for every nonzero signed fraction and decimal.

∎

For nonzero signed rational numbers, multiply or divide the sizes as usual. The answer is positive when the two signs match and negative when they differ. With more factors, count the negative ones: an even count gives a positive answer, and an odd count a negative one. Each pair of negatives multiplies to a positive, so only an unpaired negative can leave the answer negative; and dividing is multiplying by the divisor’s reciprocal, which has the same sign as the divisor (shown below), so the count works for a chain of divisions too.

Dividing by a nonzero fraction still means multiplying by its reciprocal, the fraction turned upside down. The reciprocal of a negative fraction is negative. The reciprocal of −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ is −32-\tfrac{3}{2}−23​, because two negatives make a positive and (−23)×(−32)=1\left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \times \left(-\tfrac{3}{2}\right) = 1(−32​)×(−23​)=1. Turning a fraction upside down does not change its sign.

Worked example 4 Compute −49÷(−23)-\tfrac{4}{9} \div \left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right)−94​÷(−32​)

Settle the sign first. Both numbers are negative, so the signs match and the quotient is positive.

Now the size. Dividing by 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ means multiplying by its reciprocal 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​:

49×32=1218=23.\frac{4}{9} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{12}{18} = \frac{2}{3}.94​×23​=1812​=32​.

So −49÷(−23)=23-\tfrac{4}{9} \div \left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right) = \tfrac{2}{3}−94​÷(−32​)=32​. Check by multiplying back, which is what division means:

23×(−23)=−49.✓\frac{2}{3} \times \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) = -\frac{4}{9}. \checkmark32​×(−32​)=−94​.✓

Worked example 5 Compute (−0.25)×83×(−1.5)(-0.25) \times \tfrac{8}{3} \times (-1.5)(−0.25)×38​×(−1.5)

No factor is zero, so count the negative factors. There are two, −0.25-0.25−0.25 and −1.5-1.5−1.5, an even count, so the product is positive.

For the size, write the decimals as fractions so that all three factors are in one form: 0.25=140.25 = \tfrac{1}{4}0.25=41​ and 1.5=321.5 = \tfrac{3}{2}1.5=23​. Then

14×83×32=2424=1.\frac{1}{4} \times \frac{8}{3} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{24}{24} = 1.41​×38​×23​=2424​=1.

So (−0.25)×83×(−1.5)=1(-0.25) \times \tfrac{8}{3} \times (-1.5) = 1(−0.25)×38​×(−1.5)=1. The decimal 0.250.250.25 ends, so you could keep it, but 83\tfrac{8}{3}38​ repeats as a decimal. That is why fractions are the better form here.

Zero follows the same rules it did for integers. If any factor is zero, the product is zero, as (−58)×0=0\left(-\tfrac{5}{8}\right) \times 0 = 0(−85​)×0=0 shows. Zero divided by a nonzero number is zero, so 0÷(−34)=00 \div \left(-\tfrac{3}{4}\right) = 00÷(−43​)=0. But zero can never be the divisor. The quotient (−2.5)÷0(-2.5) \div 0(−2.5)÷0 would be a number that times 000 gives −2.5-2.5−2.5. Every number times 000 is 000, so no such number exists, and (−2.5)÷0(-2.5) \div 0(−2.5)÷0 is undefined. And 0÷00 \div 00÷0 fails the other way: every number times 000 gives 000, so no single number is the answer. That is the integers chapter’s argument, and it never needed −2.5-2.5−2.5 to be an integer. Zero also has no reciprocal, since no number times 000 gives 111.

Where the minus sign goes

So far −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ has been written with its minus sign in front, and also as −34\tfrac{-3}{4}4−3​. A third spelling puts the sign in the denominator, 3−4\tfrac{3}{-4}−43​.

Why −34\frac{-3}{4}4−3​, 3−4\frac{3}{-4}−43​ and −34-\frac{3}{4}−43​ are one number#

Read each fraction bar as division. In −34\tfrac{-3}{4}4−3​ the numerator is negative and the denominator is positive. The signs differ, so the quotient is negative, and its size is 3÷4=343 \div 4 = \tfrac{3}{4}3÷4=43​. That makes it −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​.

In 3−4\tfrac{3}{-4}−43​ the signs differ the other way round. The quotient is again negative with size 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​, so it is −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ too. The third spelling, −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​, is the opposite of 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​ by definition. All three name the point three quarters of a unit left of zero:

−34=3−4=−34.\frac{-3}{4} = \frac{3}{-4} = -\frac{3}{4}.4−3​=−43​=−43​.

Nothing there depended on 333 and 444. For any integers aaa and bbb, with bbb not zero, −ab\tfrac{-a}{b}b−a​ and a−b\tfrac{a}{-b}−ba​ have the same size as ab\tfrac{a}{b}ba​ and the opposite sign (when aaa is 000, all three are 000). So −ab=a−b=−ab\tfrac{-a}{b} = \tfrac{a}{-b} = -\tfrac{a}{b}b−a​=−ba​=−ba​.

∎

A minus sign on both the top and the bottom is different. In −3−4\tfrac{-3}{-4}−4−3​ the signs match, so the quotient is positive: −3−4=34\tfrac{-3}{-4} = \tfrac{3}{4}−4−3​=43​. So a fraction is negative exactly when one of its numerator and denominator is negative and the other is positive. (A numerator of zero makes the fraction zero, which has no sign.) Writing the minus sign in front of the fraction, or in the numerator, with a positive denominator, is a convention for writing answers. It is not a rule of arithmetic, since all three spellings are equal.

Parentheses keep two signs from touching. A negative number that follows an operation sign is usually written in parentheses, as in 2.5−(−0.75)2.5 - (-0.75)2.5−(−0.75) or 3×(−12)3 \times \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)3×(−21​). A minus sign in front of parentheses means the opposite of everything inside, so −(−34)=34-\left(-\tfrac{3}{4}\right) = \tfrac{3}{4}−(−43​)=43​. Work out the inside first: −(0.5−2)=−(−1.5)=1.5-(0.5 - 2) = -(-1.5) = 1.5−(0.5−2)=−(−1.5)=1.5.

The same idea governs a negative mixed number. The sign belongs to the whole mixed number, so −212-2\tfrac{1}{2}−221​ is the opposite of 2122\tfrac{1}{2}221​:

−212=−(2+12)=−52.-2\tfrac{1}{2} = -\left(2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = -\frac{5}{2}.−221​=−(2+21​)=−25​.

It does not mean −2+12-2 + \tfrac{1}{2}−2+21​, which is −112-1\tfrac{1}{2}−121​. On the number line, −212-2\tfrac{1}{2}−221​ lies half a unit to the left of −2-2−2, not to its right.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading a negative mixed number as a sum. −212-2\tfrac{1}{2}−221​ means −(2+12)-\left(2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)−(2+21​), which is −52-\tfrac{5}{2}−25​. It is not −2+12-2 + \tfrac{1}{2}−2+21​, which is −112-1\tfrac{1}{2}−121​.
  • Comparing two negatives by their sizes. 0.6>0.450.6 > 0.450.6>0.45, but −0.6<−0.45-0.6 < -0.45−0.6<−0.45, because −0.6-0.6−0.6 is farther left. Between two negatives, the one with the larger absolute value is the smaller.
  • Changing the sign when taking a reciprocal. The reciprocal of −23-\tfrac{2}{3}−32​ is −32-\tfrac{3}{2}−23​, not 32\tfrac{3}{2}23​. Their product must be 111, which is positive.
  • Treating a minus sign on top and bottom as negative. −3−4\tfrac{-3}{-4}−4−3​ is 34\tfrac{3}{4}43​, because the signs match.
  • Rounding a repeating decimal and then writing ===. −0.4+0.67-0.4 + 0.67−0.4+0.67 is 0.270.270.27, but −0.4+23-0.4 + \tfrac{2}{3}−0.4+32​ is exactly 415\tfrac{4}{15}154​. Use the fraction to get the exact answer.

Key takeaways

  • A rational number can be written as a fraction of two integers with a nonzero denominator. Integers, decimals that end, and the repeating decimals of fractions are all rational.
  • On the number line, the number farther right is the greater. Between two negatives, the one with the larger absolute value is the smaller.
  • A signed calculation splits into a sign, decided by the integer rules, and a size, worked out with fraction or decimal methods. A size of zero gives the answer zero.
  • −34=3−4=−34\tfrac{-3}{4} = \tfrac{3}{-4} = -\tfrac{3}{4}4−3​=−43​=−43​, while −3−4=34\tfrac{-3}{-4} = \tfrac{3}{4}−4−3​=43​. A reciprocal keeps the sign of the fraction it came from.
  • Zero is rational and neither positive nor negative. A product with a zero factor is zero, and dividing by zero is still undefined.

A bit of history (optional)

Why is a fraction of two integers called rational? The word sounds as if it means sensible, and the real story is not far off.

Around 300 BCE Euclid, a Greek mathematician working in Alexandria, a city in Egypt, wrote about comparing one quantity with another. His word for that comparison was logos. The same word could also mean a word, a reason or an account.

When his books were later put into Latin, translators chose the word ratio for it. In Latin, ratio meant a reckoning, a calculation you could write down. English kept the word twice. It gave us ratio, and through French it also gave us reason.

The English word rational grows from that same Latin root. So a rational number is a reckonable one. It says how one integer measures against another, three against four, or five against two. Nothing in that idea mentions a sign.

That is why the negatives belong. In this lesson −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ is the integer −3-3−3 measured against 444, and it is as reckonable as its positive mirror image.

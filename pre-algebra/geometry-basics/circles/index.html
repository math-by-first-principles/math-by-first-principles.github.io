Circles

Intuition

Every wheel keeps the same promise: spin it through exactly one full turn, and the distance it rolls compares to its own width in exactly the same way every time, for a bicycle tire or a truck tire, a small wheel or a huge one. That link between how far a circle reaches around and how far it reaches across is what makes circles different from the straight-sided shapes in the last lesson, where perimeter was just a sum of side lengths. A circle has no straight sides to add up, so measuring the distance around it, and the space inside it, takes a new idea: a single number, called pi, that connects every circle’s size to its boundary. Once you have it, both the distance around a circle and the area inside it follow from formulas you can derive yourself. A circle itself is simply the set of all points that sit the same distance from one fixed point, the center, and holding that one distance constant is exactly what makes a wheel roll smoothly and a clock face round.

Learning goals

  • Name the radius, diameter and center, and use d=2rd = 2rd=2r
  • Define π\piπ as circumference over diameter, the same for every circle
  • Find a circumference with C=πdC = \pi dC=πd or C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr
  • Derive A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2 by rearranging thin sectors into a parallelogram
  • Separate a semicircle's area from its perimeter, which adds the diameter
  • Split a composite figure into circles and polygons, then add

The parts of a circle

Every measurement of a circle is built from a few named parts, so it is worth learning the vocabulary first. The picture below shows every part named in the list underneath it.

  • The center is the fixed point that every point on the circle is the same distance from. A circle is often named by its center, like circle OOO.
  • A radius is a straight segment from the center to any point on the circle. Because every point on the circle is the same distance from the center, every radius of a circle has the same length. We call that length rrr.
  • A diameter is a straight segment that passes through the center and joins two points on the circle. It is a radius extended straight across to the far side, so a diameter is made of two radii laid end to end. Its length is written ddd.
  • A chord is any straight segment joining two points on the circle. A diameter is the special, longest chord, the one that happens to pass through the center; a chord in general does not.
  • An arc is a piece of the circle itself, a curved section of the boundary between two points.
  • The circumference is the distance all the way around the circle. It is the circle’s version of a perimeter: the total length of its curved boundary.

The most useful relationship here is the simplest one. Since a diameter is exactly two radii in a straight line, the diameter is always twice the radius:

d=2rand equivalentlyr=d2.d = 2r \qquad \text{and equivalently} \qquad r = \frac{d}{2}.d=2rand equivalentlyr=2d​.

Keep this conversion ready, because some facts about circles are stated with the radius and others with the diameter. Slipping between the two is the source of most circle mistakes.

Check your understanding

A circle is drawn with a point OOO marked exactly in the middle, the same distance from every point on the circle. Segment PQPQPQ passes through OOO and joins two points on the circle. Segment OROROR joins OOO to one point on the circle. Segment STSTST joins two other points on the circle without passing through OOO. What is point OOO, and what are PQPQPQ, OROROR, and STSTST?

Answer choices

The point the same distance from every point on the circle is, by definition, the center, so OOO is the center. PQPQPQ passes through OOO and joins two points on the circle, so it is a diameter. OROROR joins the center to one point on the circle, so it is a radius. STSTST joins two points on the circle without passing through the center, so it is a chord that is not a diameter, and not a radius either, since neither of its endpoints is the center.

What pi really is

Here is the question that unlocks everything. Take any circle and walk all the way around it: that distance is the circumference CCC. Now measure straight across it through the center: that is the diameter ddd. How do those two lengths compare?

For every circle, no matter how big or small, the circumference is the same number of times the diameter. Roll a coin and a dinner plate each through one full turn. In both cases the distance covered is just over three times the width you rolled across. That fixed ratio of circumference to diameter is the number called pi, written with the Greek letter π\piπ:

π=Cd.\pi = \frac{C}{d}.π=dC​.

Both CCC and ddd are lengths measured in the same unit, so dividing one by the other cancels that unit away entirely: π\piπ is a bare number, not a distance.

The value of π\piπ is about 3.141593.141593.14159, a little more than 333. It is not a fraction of two whole numbers, and its decimals never end and never repeat. So in practice we use a rounded value: π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14, or sometimes the fraction 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​, which is close. The fraction is especially handy whenever the radius or diameter is a multiple of 777, since the 777 then cancels cleanly and leaves a whole number: for r=14r = 14r=14 cm,

C≈2×227×14=2×22×2=88 cm,C \approx 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 2 \times 22 \times 2 = 88 \text{ cm},C≈2×722​×14=2×22×2=88 cm,

with no decimal at all. Because we round π\piπ, any number we compute from it is approximate, even on the step where the arithmetic itself is exact once π\piπ has already been replaced by its rounded value. That is why a circle answer is reported as “about” a certain amount rather than as an exact one.

Why the ratio is the same for every circle

It would be a strange coincidence if a coin and a planet happened to share the same ratio by luck. They do not: the ratio is forced to be constant, because all circles have the same shape. A small circle is just a scaled-down copy of a large one, and scaling is the key.

Why C÷dC \div dC÷d is the same constant for every circle#

Take any circle and imagine enlarging it by some factor kkk, the way a photocopier blows up an image. Every length in the figure is multiplied by the same kkk. The radius becomes kkk times as long, and the diameter becomes kkk times as long. The whole boundary is scaled uniformly as well, so the circumference also becomes kkk times as long. Scaling stretches every single part of the picture by the same amount. So scaling cannot stretch the boundary by a different factor than it stretches the width across.

Now look at the ratio of circumference to diameter for the enlarged circle. The circumference is k×Ck \times Ck×C and the diameter is k×dk \times dk×d, so their ratio is

k×Ck×d=Cd.\frac{k \times C}{k \times d} = \frac{C}{d}.k×dk×C​=dC​.

The factor kkk cancels top and bottom. The enlarged circle has exactly the same circumference-to-diameter ratio as the one you started with. Since every circle is a scaled copy of every other circle, they must all share that one ratio. That single shared number is what we name π\piπ.

∎

So π\piπ is not a property of one particular circle; it is a property of circles as a shape. That is exactly why a formula written with π\piπ works for every circle at once.

Each ratio is computed within its own circle, where CCC and ddd share a unit that cancels, so both come out as the same bare number despite the different sizes and units. That is exactly why π\piπ can be defined once and reused for every circle: the ratio does not depend on size.

Circumference: the distance around

The definition of π\piπ does the work for us. Starting from π=Cd\pi = \dfrac{C}{d}π=dC​ and multiplying both sides by ddd gives the circumference directly:

C=π d.C = \pi \, d.C=πd.

The distance around a circle is π\piπ times its diameter. And since the diameter is twice the radius (d=2rd = 2rd=2r), we can write the same formula with the radius instead:

C=π d=π(2r)=2πr.C = \pi \, d = \pi (2r) = 2 \pi r.C=πd=π(2r)=2πr.

Both forms say the same thing; use whichever matches what you are given. If you know the diameter, use C=πdC = \pi dC=πd. If you know the radius, use C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr.

Worked example 1 Circumference from a radius

A circular pond has a radius of 777 m. How far is it around the pond? Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

The radius is given, so use C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr:

C≈2×3.14×7.C \approx 2 \times 3.14 \times 7.C≈2×3.14×7.

Multiply left to right. First 2×3.14=6.282 \times 3.14 = 6.282×3.14=6.28, then

C≈6.28×7=43.96 m.C \approx 6.28 \times 7 = 43.96 \text{ m}.C≈6.28×7=43.96 m.

So the distance around the pond is about 444444 m. The answer is approximate because we rounded π\piπ to 3.143.143.14; the true value is a little different.

Area: the space inside

Circumference measured the boundary. Area measures the flat space the disk covers, counted in square units just as it was for polygons. The formula is

A=πr2,A = \pi r^2,A=πr2,

read as “pi r squared.” Notice it uses the radius, and the radius is squared, so this is genuinely a measure of square units, not a length. The surprise is that the same constant π\piπ that governs the boundary also governs the area. We can see exactly why by cutting the disk, the flat region a circle encloses, into pieces and rebuilding it into a shape we already know how to measure.

Slice the disk into many thin equal sectors, like the slices of a pie. Each slice is almost a thin triangle: two straight edges of length rrr and a slightly curved base along the circle. Now pull the slices apart and lay them in a row, pointing up and down in turn, so they interlock into a long strip.

That strip is built entirely from pieces of the disk, and rearranging pieces never changes how much area they cover. So the strip has exactly the area of the disk, no matter how many sectors you use. The diagram simplifies each sector to straight edges throughout, to keep the picture easy to read; a real sector’s base is a gentle curve rather than a straight line, and using more, thinner sectors brings that curve closer and closer to straight, so the strip closes in on a true parallelogram. The proof below reasons about that limit directly, measuring the ever-more-parallelogram-like strip one dimension at a time, which pins down the disk’s area too.

Why the area of a circle is πr2\pi r^2πr2#

So all that is left is to measure that ever-more-parallelogram-like strip, one dimension at a time.

The height of the strip is the straight edge of a sector. As the sectors get thinner, those straight edges stand up straighter, so the perpendicular height of the strip gets closer and closer to the radius rrr.

Its base is built from the curved edges of the sectors. Half of the sectors point upward and lay their curved edges along the top. The other half point downward and lay their curved edges along the bottom instead. The full circumference is 2πr2\pi r2πr, and exactly half of it forms the bottom edge. As the sectors get thinner, each curved edge flattens toward a straight segment, so the bumpy base gets closer and closer to the straight length

base=12(2πr)=πr.\text{base} = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi r) = \pi r.base=21​(2πr)=πr.

The more sectors you use, the closer the strip comes to an actual parallelogram of base πr\pi rπr and perpendicular height rrr. A parallelogram’s area is base times height, so the area of the disk is

A=base×height=(πr)(r)=πr2.A = \text{base} \times \text{height} = (\pi r)(r) = \pi r^2.A=base×height=(πr)(r)=πr2.

The base contributes one factor of rrr through πr\pi rπr, and the height contributes another factor of rrr, which is why the radius ends up squared.

∎

Check your understanding

In the sector-to-parallelogram argument, the strip's base gets closer to πr\pi rπr and its height gets closer to rrr as the sectors get thinner. Why does the radius end up squared in A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2?

Answer choices

A parallelogram's area is base times height. The base is πr\pi rπr (one factor of rrr) and the height is rrr (a second factor of rrr), so the product is (πr)(r)=πr2(\pi r)(r) = \pi r^2(πr)(r)=πr2. The squaring comes from that second factor of rrr in the height, not from squaring π\piπ or the diameter.

So for a circle,

A=πr2(r is the radius).A = \pi r^2 \qquad (r \text{ is the radius}).A=πr2(r is the radius).

If a problem hands you the diameter instead, do not put it where the radius belongs. Halve it first to get the radius, then square. This is the single most common circle error, and the next section comes back to it.

Worked example 2 Area from a radius

A circular tabletop has a radius of 555 cm. Find its area. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

Use A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2. Square the radius first, then multiply by π\piπ:

r2=52=25.r^2 = 5^2 = 25.r2=52=25.

Now multiply by π\piπ:

A≈3.14×25=78.5 cm2.A \approx 3.14 \times 25 = 78.5 \text{ cm}^2.A≈3.14×25=78.5 cm2.

The tabletop covers about 78.578.578.5 square centimetres. The unit is square centimetres because area counts unit squares, and the radius was squared in the formula.

Worked example 3 Area from a diameter (halve first)

A circular plate has a diameter of 121212 cm. Find its area. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

The formula needs the radius, but we were given the diameter, so halve it first:

r=d2=122=6 cm.r = \frac{d}{2} = \frac{12}{2} = 6 \text{ cm}.r=2d​=212​=6 cm.

Now square the radius and multiply by π\piπ:

A=πr2≈3.14×62=3.14×36=113.04 cm2.A = \pi r^2 \approx 3.14 \times 6^2 = 3.14 \times 36 = 113.04 \text{ cm}^2.A=πr2≈3.14×62=3.14×36=113.04 cm2.

So the plate covers about 113 cm2113 \text{ cm}^2113 cm2. A frequent mistake is to square the diameter by accident, 3.14×122=452.163.14 \times 12^2 = 452.163.14×122=452.16. That answer is four times too large: the diameter is twice the radius, so squaring it squares that factor of 222 as well, turning it into a factor of 22=42^2 = 422=4.

Semicircles

A semicircle is half a circle, cut along a diameter. Its area is simply half the area of the full circle:

Asemicircle=12πr2.A_{\text{semicircle}} = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2.Asemicircle​=21​πr2.

Its boundary needs more care. The curved part is half the circumference, 12(2πr)=πr\tfrac{1}{2}(2\pi r) = \pi r21​(2πr)=πr, but the boundary of a semicircle is not only the curve. To close the shape you also travel back along the straight diameter, which has length d=2rd = 2rd=2r. So the full distance around a semicircle is the curved half plus the straight diameter:

Psemicircle=πr+2r.P_{\text{semicircle}} = \pi r + 2r.Psemicircle​=πr+2r.

Forgetting the straight edge is a classic slip: a semicircle’s perimeter is not just half the circle’s circumference. That is because cutting a circle in half adds a new straight side that was not there before.

Worked example 4 Perimeter of a semicircle

A semicircle has a radius of 444 cm. Find the distance around it. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

The boundary is the curved half plus the straight diameter. The curved half is πr\pi rπr:

curve≈3.14×4=12.56 cm.\text{curve} \approx 3.14 \times 4 = 12.56 \text{ cm}.curve≈3.14×4=12.56 cm.

The straight edge is the diameter d=2r=8d = 2r = 8d=2r=8 cm. Add the two parts:

P≈12.56+8=20.56 cm.P \approx 12.56 + 8 = 20.56 \text{ cm}.P≈12.56+8=20.56 cm.

So the distance around the semicircle is about 20.5620.5620.56 cm. Leaving out the straight diameter would give only 12.5612.5612.56 cm, which traces just the curved part and never closes the shape.

Composite figures

Real shapes often combine a circle or semicircle with the polygons from the previous lesson. The method is the same one you used for composite polygons. Break the figure into parts you know, find each part’s area, and add them (or subtract a part that has been cut away). Area is additive, so the whole is the sum of its non-overlapping pieces.

For example, take a running track shaped like a rectangle with a semicircle capping each end. That track’s area is equal to the rectangle plus two semicircular ends. Two semicircles of the same radius together make one full circle, so the total area is the rectangle’s area plus a single circle’s area. Keep the two kinds of measurement straight throughout. Lengths and circumferences are in linear units, while areas are in square units, exactly as in the perimeter and area lesson.

Worked example 5 A rectangle with a semicircle on top

A window is a 444 m wide rectangle standing 555 m tall, topped by a semicircle whose diameter is the 444 m width. Find the total area of the window. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

Split the window into the rectangle and the semicircle, find each area, then add.

The rectangle is 444 m wide and 555 m tall:

Arect=4×5=20 m2.A_{\text{rect}} = 4 \times 5 = 20 \text{ m}^2.Arect​=4×5=20 m2.

The semicircle sits on the 444 m width, so that width is its diameter, and the radius is half of it:

r=42=2 m.r = \frac{4}{2} = 2 \text{ m}.r=24​=2 m.

A semicircle is half a circle, so its area is

Asemi=12πr2≈12×3.14×22=12×3.14×4=6.28 m2.A_{\text{semi}} = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 \approx \frac{1}{2} \times 3.14 \times 2^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 3.14 \times 4 = 6.28 \text{ m}^2.Asemi​=21​πr2≈21​×3.14×22=21​×3.14×4=6.28 m2.

Add the two non-overlapping pieces:

A≈20+6.28=26.28 m2.A \approx 20 + 6.28 = 26.28 \text{ m}^2.A≈20+6.28=26.28 m2.

So the window covers about 26.2826.2826.28 square metres. The radius for the semicircle was the half-width 222 m, not the full 444 m width, which is the same halve-the-diameter step from before.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing radius and diameter. The diameter is twice the radius (d=2rd = 2rd=2r). Both area and the 2πr2\pi r2πr form of circumference use the radius, so when a problem gives the diameter, halve it before substituting.
  • Squaring the diameter in the area formula. A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2 squares the radius. Using the diameter by mistake makes the answer four times too big. That is because squaring turns the factor of 222 between ddd and rrr into a factor of 444.
  • Mixing up circumference and area. Circumference C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr is a length, in linear units like cm. Area A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2 is a count of unit squares, in square units like cm2\text{cm}^2cm2. One has rrr to the first power, the other has rrr squared.
  • Treating a rounded π\piπ as exact. Since π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14 is rounded, any answer built from it is approximate. Write it with "≈\approx≈", and do not round π\piπ partway and then claim an exact result.
  • Forgetting the straight edge of a semicircle. A semicircle’s perimeter is the curved half plus the diameter (πr+2r\pi r + 2rπr+2r), because slicing a circle in half exposes a new straight side.

Key takeaways

  • A circle is all points a fixed distance from a center. A radius rrr reaches from the center to the edge; a diameter ddd crosses through the center, and d=2rd = 2rd=2r.
  • Pi is the ratio of any circle’s circumference to its diameter, π=Cd≈3.14\pi = \dfrac{C}{d} \approx 3.14π=dC​≈3.14. It is the same constant for every circle because all circles are scaled copies of one another.
  • Circumference: C=πd=2πrC = \pi d = 2\pi rC=πd=2πr, straight from the definition of π\piπ. It is a length.
  • Area: A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2, derived by slicing the disk into thin sectors that rearrange into a parallelogram with base πr\pi rπr and height rrr. It is a count of square units.
  • A semicircle has area 12πr2\tfrac{1}{2}\pi r^221​πr2 and perimeter πr+2r\pi r + 2rπr+2r (the curve plus the diameter).
  • For a composite figure, split it into circles, semicircles, and polygons, find each area, and add (or subtract a removed piece).

A bit of history (optional)

A straight ruler cannot follow a curve. So how did anyone ever pin down π\piπ?

Archimedes, a Greek engineer, found a way around it in the third century BCE. He worked in Syracuse, a city on the island of Sicily. He could not trace the circle’s own curve, but polygons are built entirely from straight sides, and their side lengths can be worked out from geometry rather than read off a ruler.

So he trapped the circle between two polygons. One was drawn just inside the circle and one just outside, and he calculated both perimeters from their straight side lengths using geometric relationships, not measurement. The circle’s boundary has to be longer than the inner one and shorter than the outer one. That gives π\piπ a floor and a ceiling at the same time.

Then he sharpened the trap. Doubling the number of sides again and again, he reached polygons of ninety-six sides. The two bounds closed until π\piπ sat between 22371\tfrac{223}{71}71223​ and 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​.

That upper bound is the friendly 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​ you met earlier in this lesson. It is also the move you made with the sectors. A curve gives up its size to straight pieces, once the pieces get thin enough.

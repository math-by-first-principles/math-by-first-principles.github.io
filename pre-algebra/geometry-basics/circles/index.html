Circles

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Roll a circular wheel along the ground without slipping. In one full turn it travels a little more than three times its width. A larger wheel travels farther, but the comparison with its own width stays the same. That shared ratio is called pi.

For polygons, you found the perimeter by adding straight sides. A circle has no straight sides to add. Pi gives you a way to find the distance around it, and cutting its interior into pieces will help you find its area.

Learning goals

  • Name the radius, diameter and center, and use d=2rd = 2rd=2r
  • Define π\piπ as circumference over diameter, the same for every circle
  • Find circumference and area, or work backward to a radius or diameter
  • Explain A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2 by rearranging thin sectors into a near-parallelogram
  • Separate a semicircle's area from its perimeter, which adds the diameter
  • Split a composite figure into circles and polygons, then add

The parts of a circle

A circle is all the points in a plane a fixed positive distance from one point, its center. The circle is the curved boundary; the disk is the region it encloses.

  • The center is the fixed point that every point on the circle is the same distance from. A circle is often named by its center, like circle OOO.
  • A radius is a straight segment from the center to any point on the circle. Every radius of a circle has the same length, written rrr.
  • A diameter is a straight segment that passes through the center and joins two points on the circle. It is a radius extended straight across to the far side, so a diameter is made of two radii laid end to end. Its length is written ddd.
  • A chord is any straight segment joining two points on the circle. A diameter is a longest chord; other chords do not pass through the center.
  • An arc is a piece of the circle itself, a curved section of the boundary between two points.
  • The circumference is the distance all the way around the circle. It is the circle’s version of a perimeter: the total length of its curved boundary.

A radius of 333 cm gives a diameter of 3+3=63 + 3 = 63+3=6 cm. Any diameter contains two radii end to end, so

d=2rand equivalentlyr=d2.d = 2r \qquad \text{and equivalently} \qquad r = \frac{d}{2}.d=2rand equivalentlyr=2d​.

Check which measurement you are given: converting a diameter to a radius means halving it.

Answer choices

OOO is the center. PQPQPQ crosses through the center between two points on the circle, so it is a diameter. OROROR runs from the center to the circle, so it is a radius. STSTST joins two points on the circle but misses the center, so it is a chord that is not a diameter.

What pi really is

Measure the circumference CCC and diameter ddd of a round object in the same unit. If its diameter is 101010 cm, its circumference is about 31.431.431.4 cm. Dividing gives about 31.4÷10=3.1431.4 \div 10 = 3.1431.4÷10=3.14. Repeating this with other round objects gives nearly the same result, with small differences from measurement.

For an exact circle, the ratio of circumference to diameter is the same for every size. This number is pi, written with the Greek letter π\piπ:

π=Cd.\pi = \frac{C}{d}.π=dC​.

The length units cancel in C÷dC \div dC÷d, so π\piπ is a number, not a distance.

Pi is irrational: it cannot be written as a fraction of two whole numbers. Its decimal expansion never ends or repeats. Its value begins 3.14159…3.14159\ldots3.14159…, so 3.143.143.14 and 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​ are useful approximations, not exact values. Use ≈\approx≈ or the word “about” when an answer uses one of them.

Why the ratio is the same for every circle

Every circle is an enlarged or reduced copy of every other circle. Scaling changes the lengths, but not their ratio.

Why C÷dC \div dC÷d is the same constant for every circle#

Enlarge a circle with diameter 444 cm until every length is twice as large. Its diameter becomes 888 cm, and its circumference doubles too: every part of the boundary has been stretched by the same factor. In circumference divided by diameter, that factor of 222 cancels.

The same reasoning works for any positive scale factor kkk. A circle with circumference CCC and diameter ddd becomes one with circumference kCkCkC and diameter kdkdkd:

k×Ck×d=Cd.\frac{k \times C}{k \times d} = \frac{C}{d}.k×dk×C​=dC​.

The common factor cancels. Since any circle can be scaled to any other, all circles share this ratio.

∎

Circumference: the distance around

The distance around a circle is π\piπ times its diameter. Starting from π=Cd\pi = \dfrac{C}{d}π=dC​ and multiplying both sides by ddd gives

C=π d.C = \pi \, d.C=πd.

Since d=2rd = 2rd=2r, we can write the same formula with the radius:

C=π d=π(2r)=2πr.C = \pi \, d = \pi (2r) = 2 \pi r.C=πd=π(2r)=2πr.

Both forms say the same thing; use whichever matches what you are given. If you know the diameter, use C=πdC = \pi dC=πd. If you know the radius, use C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr.

For a diameter of 888 cm, the circumference is exactly 8π8\pi8π cm. Keeping π\piπ in the answer means no rounding has occurred. Replacing it with 3.143.143.14 gives C≈25.12C \approx 25.12C≈25.12 cm.

The approximation 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​ can simplify arithmetic when a radius or diameter is a multiple of 777. For a radius of 141414 cm,

C≈2×227×14=88 cm.C \approx 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 88 \text{ cm}.C≈2×722​×14=88 cm.

The 777 cancels, but the answer is still approximate because 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​ is not exactly π\piπ.

Area: the space inside

Circumference measures the boundary. Area measures the space inside, in square units. As with polygons, we can cut and rearrange pieces without changing their total area.

A sector is a slice of a disk bounded by two radii and the arc between them. Cut a disk into an even number of equal sectors, then arrange them with the curved edges facing up and down in turn.

Each sector looks nearly like a triangle, except that its outer edge is curved. The pieces fit together into a strip:

The drawing uses straight edges to suggest the shape; actual sectors leave curved bumps along the top and bottom.

For a radius of 333 cm, the full circumference is 6π6\pi6π cm. Half of that curved boundary forms the bottom of the strip and half forms the top. Its base is therefore close to 3π3\pi3π cm, while its perpendicular height is close to the radius, 333 cm. Base times height gives an area close to (3π)(3)=9π(3\pi)(3) = 9\pi(3π)(3)=9π square centimeters.

Why the area of a circle is πr2\pi r^2πr2#

Cutting and rearranging never changes the disk’s area. Making the sectors thinner only changes the strip’s shape: its bumps get smaller, its base gets closer to half the circumference, πr\pi rπr, and its end radii stand more nearly upright. That brings its perpendicular height closer to rrr.

Compare the strip with a parallelogram of base πr\pi rπr and height rrr. No finite number of sectors gives a perfect match. But thinner sectors make the regions where the two shapes differ smaller than any area we choose. The strip’s area never changes, so it must equal that parallelogram’s area:

A=(πr)(r)=πr2.A = (\pi r)(r) = \pi r^2.A=(πr)(r)=πr2.

The picture is an approximation, but the formula is exact.

∎

The two factors of rrr come from the base and the height, just as two length measurements produce square units for a rectangle.

Check your understanding

In the sector-to-parallelogram argument, the strip's base gets closer to πr\pi rπr and its height gets closer to rrr as the sectors get thinner. Why does the radius end up squared in A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2?

Answer choices

A parallelogram's area is base times height. The base is πr\pi rπr (one factor of rrr) and the height is rrr (a second factor of rrr), so the product is (πr)(r)=πr2(\pi r)(r) = \pi r^2(πr)(r)=πr2. The squaring comes from that second factor of rrr in the height, not from squaring π\piπ or the diameter.

The formula uses the radius. If you are given a diameter, halve it before squaring.

Worked example 2 Area from a radius

A circular tabletop has a radius of 555 cm. Find its area. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

Use A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2. Square the radius first, then multiply by π\piπ:

r2=52=25.r^2 = 5^2 = 25.r2=52=25.

Now multiply by π\piπ:

A≈3.14×25=78.5 cm2.A \approx 3.14 \times 25 = 78.5 \text{ cm}^2.A≈3.14×25=78.5 cm2.

The tabletop covers about 78.578.578.5 square centimeters. The unit is square centimeters because area counts unit squares, and the radius was squared in the formula.

A=πr2≈3.14×62=3.14×36=113.04 cm2.A = \pi r^2 \approx 3.14 \times 6^2 = 3.14 \times 36 = 113.04 \text{ cm}^2.A=πr2≈3.14×62=3.14×36=113.04 cm2.

So the plate covers about 113 cm2113 \text{ cm}^2113 cm2. A frequent mistake is to square the diameter by accident, 3.14×122=452.163.14 \times 12^2 = 452.163.14×122=452.16. Finding a missing radius or diameter

You can run a formula backward by undoing its operations, as you did with equations. Suppose a circle has circumference 26π26\pi26π cm. Since C=πdC = \pi dC=πd, divide by π\piπ to find its diameter:

d=Cπ=26ππ=26 cm.d = \frac{C}{\pi} = \frac{26\pi}{\pi} = 26 \text{ cm}.d=πC​=π26π​=26 cm.

Its radius is half the diameter, 131313 cm. You could also divide the circumference by 2π2\pi2π to find the radius directly.

Area needs one more step. If a circle has area 169π169\pi169π square centimeters, divide by π\piπ to undo the multiplication in A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2:

r2=169ππ=169 cm2.r^2 = \frac{169\pi}{\pi} = 169 \text{ cm}^2.r2=π169π​=169 cm2.

Then take the positive square root: r=169=13r = \sqrt{169} = 13r=169​=13 cm. A radius is a positive length, so −13-13−13 cm cannot be the answer. The diameter is 2×13=262 \times 13 = 262×13=26 cm. In general, these steps give

r=Aπ,d=2r.r = \sqrt{\frac{A}{\pi}}, \qquad d = 2r.r=πA​​,d=2r.

Keeping π\piπ exact lets it cancel in these examples. If the circumference or area is approximate, the length you find will be approximate too.

Semicircles

A semicircle is half a circle, cut along a diameter. Its area is simply half the area of the full circle:

Asemicircle=12πr2.A_{\text{semicircle}} = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2.Asemicircle​=21​πr2.

Its perimeter includes both the curved edge and the straight cut. The curved edge is half the circumference, 12(2πr)=πr\tfrac{1}{2}(2\pi r) = \pi r21​(2πr)=πr. The straight edge is the diameter, 2r2r2r, so

Psemicircle=πr+2r.P_{\text{semicircle}} = \pi r + 2r.Psemicircle​=πr+2r.

Cutting the disk in half exposes a new straight edge. That is why you halve the area but cannot simply halve the circumference to get the perimeter.

Worked example 4 Perimeter of a semicircle

A semicircle has a radius of 444 cm. Find the distance around it. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

The boundary is the curved half plus the straight diameter. The curved half is πr\pi rπr:

curve≈3.14×4=12.56 cm.\text{curve} \approx 3.14 \times 4 = 12.56 \text{ cm}.curve≈3.14×4=12.56 cm.

The straight edge is the diameter d=2r=8d = 2r = 8d=2r=8 cm. Add the two parts:

P≈12.56+8=20.56 cm.P \approx 12.56 + 8 = 20.56 \text{ cm}.P≈12.56+8=20.56 cm.

So the distance around the semicircle is about 20.5620.5620.56 cm. Leaving out the straight diameter would give only 12.5612.5612.56 cm, which traces just the curved part and never closes the shape.

Composite figures

Use the same method as for composite polygons: split the figure into pieces whose areas you can find, then add the non-overlapping pieces or subtract a cutout.

For example, a running track shaped like a rectangle with a semicircle at each end encloses the rectangle’s area plus one full circle’s area. Two semicircles of the same radius make one circle. For total perimeter, count every boundary edge, including edges around cutouts. Do not count a shared join between pieces. As with composite polygons, an outer perimeter counts only the outside boundary; a total perimeter also counts the boundaries of any holes.

Worked example 5 A rectangle with a semicircle on top

A window is a 444 m wide rectangle standing 555 m tall, topped by a semicircle whose diameter is the 444 m width. Find the total area and outer perimeter of the window. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing radius and diameter. The diameter is twice the radius (d=2rd = 2rd=2r). Both area and the 2πr2\pi r2πr form of circumference use the radius, so when a problem gives the diameter, halve it before substituting.
  • Squaring the diameter in the area formula. A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2 squares the radius. Using the diameter by mistake makes the answer four times too big. That is because squaring turns the factor of 222 between ddd and rrr into a factor of 444.
  • Mixing up circumference and area. Circumference C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr is a length, in linear units like cm. Area A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2 is a count of unit squares, in square units like cm2\text{cm}^2cm2. One has rrr to the first power, the other has rrr squared.
  • Treating a rounded π\piπ as exact. Since π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14 is rounded, any answer built from it is approximate. Write it with "≈\approx≈", and do not round π\piπ partway and then claim an exact result.
  • Forgetting the straight edge of a semicircle. A semicircle’s perimeter is the curved half plus the diameter (πr+2r\pi r + 2rπr+2r), because slicing a circle in half exposes a new straight side.

Key takeaways

  • A circle is all points in a plane a fixed positive distance from its center. Its diameter is twice its radius: d=2rd = 2rd=2r.
  • Pi: π=Cd≈3.14\pi = \dfrac{C}{d} \approx 3.14π=dC​≈3.14. It is the same constant for every circle because all circles are scaled copies of one another.
  • Circumference: C=πd=2πrC = \pi d = 2\pi rC=πd=2πr, straight from the definition of π\piπ. It is a length.
  • Area: A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2. Thin sectors form a near-parallelogram with base close to πr\pi rπr and perpendicular height close to rrr. Area uses square units; halve a given diameter first.
  • Parts of circles: a semicircle has area 12πr2\tfrac{1}{2}\pi r^221​πr2 and perimeter πr+2r\pi r + 2rπr+2r. For composites, add or subtract areas. Total perimeter includes cutout boundaries but excludes shared joins.

A bit of history (optional)

A straight ruler cannot follow a curve. So how did anyone ever pin down π\piπ?

Archimedes, a Greek mathematician and engineer, found a way around it in the third century BCE. He worked in Syracuse, on the island of Sicily. Instead of measuring the curve, he used polygons, whose straight sides he could calculate with geometry.

He trapped the circle between two regular polygons, one just inside and one just outside. The circle’s boundary was longer than the inner perimeter and shorter than the outer perimeter. Calculating those perimeters gave him a lower and an upper bound for π\piπ.

He then doubled the number of sides repeatedly, reaching polygons with ninety-six sides. The bounds narrowed until he could show that π\piπ lay between 22371\tfrac{223}{71}71223​ and 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​. His argument appears in Measurement of a Circle.

The familiar 227\tfrac{22}{7}722​ was his upper bound, not a claim that he had found π\piπ exactly. Straight-sided shapes had let him pin down a number belonging to a curve.

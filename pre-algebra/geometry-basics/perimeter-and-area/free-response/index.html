    1. Two measurements of one rectangle . Foundational, 11 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    A rectangular sheet of card measures 161616 cm along the bottom and 777 cm up the side. Two quite different questions can be asked about it: how far it is around the edge, and how much flat space it covers. This question measures both, then changes the card in two ways and watches what each measurement does.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find the perimeter and the area of the card. For the area, say how many unit squares sit in one row and how many rows there are, and give both answers with their units.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A second card is cut with both dimensions doubled: 323232 cm along the bottom and 141414 cm up the side. Find its perimeter and its area, say how many times larger each one is than the first card's, and account for the area result using the rows of unit squares.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Aiden says that knowing a rectangle's perimeter is enough to know its area. Test the claim in both directions: give the dimensions of a rectangle with the same perimeter as the first card but a different area, and the dimensions of one with the same area as the first card but a different perimeter. Say what each of the two shows.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing here needs a formula you cannot rebuild on the spot. Perimeter is the four sides added up, and area is the number of one centimetre squares that tile the card, which arrive in equal rows.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Ask what doubling the bottom edge does to a single row of squares, and separately what doubling the side does to the number of rows. Then put those two answers together.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      One of the two measurements pins down what the sides add to, and the other pins down what they multiply to. Look for two lengths with the right sum but a different product, then two with the right product but a different sum.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The perimeter is 464646 cm. Each row holds 161616 unit squares and there are 777 rows, so the area is 112 cm2112 \text{ cm}^2112 cm2.

    Part B

    Perimeter 929292 cm, twice the first card's. Area 448 cm2448 \text{ cm}^2448 cm2, four times the first card's, because each row now holds twice as many squares and there are twice as many rows.

    Part C

    Neither measurement fixes the other, and many rectangles show it. For example 191919 cm by 444 cm shares the perimeter 464646 cm but covers 76 cm276 \text{ cm}^276 cm2, and 282828 cm by 444 cm shares the area 112 cm2112 \text{ cm}^2112 cm2 but measures 646464 cm round. Any other pair with the right sum, or the right product, does the same job.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Perimeter is the distance all the way round the boundary, so add the four sides. Two of them measure 161616 cm and two measure 777 cm:

    P=16+7+16+7=2(16+7)=46 cm.P = 16 + 7 + 16 + 7 = 2(16 + 7) = 46 \text{ cm}.P=16+7+16+7=2(16+7)=46 cm.

    Area is a count of unit squares. Lay 111 cm squares along the bottom edge and 161616 of them fill one row, because that row is 161616 cm long. Stack rows up the 777 cm side and there are 777 of them, each one 111 cm tall. Every square belongs to exactly one row, with no gaps and no overlaps, so the count is a multiplication rather than a long tally:

    A=7×16=112 cm2.A = 7 \times 16 = 112 \text{ cm}^2.A=7×16=112 cm2.

    The two answers carry different units on purpose. The perimeter is a length, so it is reported in centimetres; the area is a count of squares, so it is reported in square centimetres.

    Part B

    Take the perimeter first:

    P=2(32+14)=2×46=92 cm,P = 2(32 + 14) = 2 \times 46 = 92 \text{ cm},P=2(32+14)=2×46=92 cm,

    which is exactly twice the 464646 cm of the first card. That is no accident: all four sides doubled, so their total doubled.

    Now the area:

    A=32×14=448 cm2.A = 32 \times 14 = 448 \text{ cm}^2.A=32×14=448 cm2.

    Set that beside 112 cm2112 \text{ cm}^2112 cm2. Since 448=4×112448 = 4 \times 112448=4×112, the area is four times as large, not twice.

    The rows of unit squares say why. Doubling the bottom edge puts twice as many squares in each row, 323232 instead of 161616. Doubling the side gives twice as many rows, 141414 instead of 777. The count is rows times squares per row, so it picks up a factor of two from each direction at once:

    2×2=4.2 \times 2 = 4.2×2=4.

    Perimeter runs along one direction at a time, so it collects a single factor of two. Area covers two directions together, so it collects two of them.

    Part C

    A perimeter of 464646 cm says only that the length and the width add to 232323 cm, since P=2(l+w)P = 2(l + w)P=2(l+w). Plenty of pairs of lengths add to 232323, and they do not have equal products. Alongside 161616 and 777, take 191919 and 444:

    P=2(19+4)=46 cm,A=19×4=76 cm2.P = 2(19 + 4) = 46 \text{ cm}, \qquad A = 19 \times 4 = 76 \text{ cm}^2.P=2(19+4)=46 cm,A=19×4=76 cm2.

    Same perimeter as the card, smaller area, so the perimeter did not fix the area.

    The other direction fails too. An area of 112 cm2112 \text{ cm}^2112 cm2 says only that the length and the width multiply to 112112112. Take 282828 and 444:

    A=28×4=112 cm2,P=2(28+4)=64 cm.A = 28 \times 4 = 112 \text{ cm}^2, \qquad P = 2(28 + 4) = 64 \text{ cm}.A=28×4=112 cm2,P=2(28+4)=64 cm.

    Same area as the card, and a boundary 181818 cm longer.

    So the claim fails both ways round. A perimeter pins down what the two sides add to; an area pins down what they multiply to. A sum does not determine a product and a product does not determine a sum, which is exactly why a shape needs both measurements reported and why one can never be read off the other.

    2. A parallelogram, its base, its height and its slant . Foundational, 12 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    A workshop cuts a panel in the shape of a parallelogram. Its base measures 171717 cm, its slanted side measures 141414 cm, and the perpendicular distance from the base straight across to the side opposite measures 666 cm. Three lengths are on the table, and part of the work is deciding which of them each measurement calls for.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find the perimeter and the area of the panel, name the given lengths each one uses, and attach the correct units.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Explain why the perpendicular height is the length the area rule uses, by describing the cut and the slide that turn the panel into a rectangle. Name the rectangle's two dimensions, and say why the slanted side cannot be one of them.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A second panel is cut with the same base of 171717 cm and the same perpendicular height of 666 cm, but leaning over further, so its slanted side measures 15.515.515.5 cm. Priya says the second panel must cover more space, since it is built from longer sides. Decide whether she is right, work out both panels' perimeters, and say what leaning further does to each of the two measurements.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Two of the three given lengths belong to the distance round the edge and two belong to the space inside, and they are not the same two. Settle which is which before multiplying anything.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Picture a vertical cut from the top corner straight down to the base, then slide the piece you cut off across to the far end. Ask what shape you are holding afterwards and what its two dimensions measure.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The perimeter is 626262 cm, which uses the base and the slanted side. The area is 102 cm2102 \text{ cm}^2102 cm2, which uses the base and the perpendicular height.

    Part B

    Cutting the right triangle off one end and sliding it to the other leaves a rectangle 171717 cm by 666 cm holding the same material, so the panel covers 17×617 \times 617×6. The slanted side is not a side of that rectangle at all, and being longer than the height it would overstate the space.

    Part C

    She is wrong. Both panels cover 102 cm2102 \text{ cm}^2102 cm2, since the base and the perpendicular height are unchanged. Their perimeters differ, 626262 cm against 656565 cm, so leaning further lengthens the boundary and leaves the space inside alone.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The perimeter is the distance round the edge, so it is the four sides added. A parallelogram has two sides of 171717 cm and two of 141414 cm:

    P=2(17+14)=2×31=62 cm.P = 2(17 + 14) = 2 \times 31 = 62 \text{ cm}.P=2(17+14)=2×31=62 cm.

    Every side gets walked, so the slanted side certainly counts here.

    The area asks a different question, and it does not use the slanted side at all. It is the base multiplied by the perpendicular height, the straight across distance from the base to the side opposite it:

    A=b×h=17×6=102 cm2.A = b \times h = 17 \times 6 = 102 \text{ cm}^2.A=b×h=17×6=102 cm2.

    Using the slanted side instead would give 17×14=23817 \times 14 = 23817×14=238, and part B says why that number is not the area of anything on this table.

    Note the units. The perimeter is a length, so it is in centimetres; the area counts unit squares, so it is in square centimetres.

    Part B

    Drop a vertical line from the top left corner of the panel straight down to the base. That line is the perpendicular height, 666 cm, and it cuts a right triangle off the left end.

    Now slide that triangle across to the right end. Opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal and parallel, so it fits against the slanted right side exactly, with no gap and no overlap. What is left is a rectangle: its bottom is still the base, 171717 cm, and its height is the vertical line you drew, 666 cm. So the area is

    A=17×6=102 cm2.A = 17 \times 6 = 102 \text{ cm}^2.A=17×6=102 cm2.

    Moving a piece of a shape to a new place never changes how much space the shape covers, so the panel and the rectangle cover the same amount.

    The slanted side cannot be one of the rectangle's dimensions because it is not a side of the rectangle at all: the cut and the slide consumed both slanted edges, turning them into the two upright ends. It is also longer than the height, since it travels sideways as well as upward, so multiplying by it counts space the panel does not cover:

    17×14=238,17 \times 14 = 238,17×14=238,

    more than twice the true area.

    Part C

    Work out the second panel's area from the same rule as the first. Its base is 171717 cm and its perpendicular height is 666 cm, so

    A=17×6=102 cm2,A = 17 \times 6 = 102 \text{ cm}^2,A=17×6=102 cm2,

    the same as the first panel. The slanted side never enters the rule, so changing it from 141414 cm to 15.515.515.5 cm changes nothing at all about the area.

    The perimeters are another matter. The first panel gives 2(17+14)=622(17 + 14) = 622(17+14)=62 cm, and the second gives

    P=2(17+15.5)=2×32.5=65 cm.P = 2(17 + 15.5) = 2 \times 32.5 = 65 \text{ cm}.P=2(17+15.5)=2×32.5=65 cm.

    Priya is right that the second panel is built from longer sides, and wrong about what that buys. Leaning a parallelogram further lengthens its boundary while the space inside stays put.

    The cut and slide picture explains it. Both panels stand on the same base and reach the same perpendicular height, so the same cut turns each of them into the same rectangle, 171717 cm by 666 cm. Leaning further only carries the material further sideways; it does not add any. If the area did depend on the slanted side, two panels that rearrange into the identical rectangle would have to cover different amounts of space, which cannot happen.

    In one line

    The panel measures P=2(17+14)=62P = 2(17 + 14) = 62P=2(17+14)=62 cm round, which uses the slanted side, and covers A=17×6=102 cm2A = 17 \times 6 = 102 \text{ cm}^2A=17×6=102 cm2, which uses the perpendicular height instead.     3. Halves, averages, and where the formulas come from . Reasoning, 14 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Reasoning 14 pts

    The triangle rule carries a factor of one half and the trapezoid rule carries an average, and neither is there by decree. This question rebuilds both, once by doubling a shape and once by cutting one up, and then sets the trapezoid's area beside two rectangles built on its parallel sides.

    1. A
      Part A.

      A triangle has a base of 141414 cm and a perpendicular height of 131313 cm to that base. A half turn copy of it is joined to the original along one of the two sides that is not the base, so that the two 141414 cm bases end up as the opposite sides of a parallelogram. Find that parallelogram's area, then give the triangle's own area, and say why the second follows from the first.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A trapezoid has parallel sides of 111111 m and 191919 m with a perpendicular height of 555 m between them. Cut it along a diagonal into two triangles, find each triangle's area, and add them. Find the area again from the trapezoid rule, and say what the two triangles share that makes the two routes agree.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Two rectangles can be built on that trapezoid's parallel sides, each 555 m tall: one 111111 m wide and one 191919 m wide. Find both areas, place the trapezoid's area against them, and explain why it lands where it does rather than merely somewhere in between.

      Carry your own answer forward The comparison uses the trapezoid area you found in part B, so carry your own value forward into it, whatever it came out to; the relation you find and the reason behind it are what is being marked.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing in this question is remembered. Each rule comes from rebuilding the shape out of shapes you already know, either by doubling it into something familiar or by cutting it into familiar pieces.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      A diagonal splits a trapezoid into two triangles whose bases are the two parallel sides. Ask what the perpendicular height of each one is before computing anything.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Add the two rectangle areas and halve the total, then multiply the trapezoid rule out and compare the two expressions term by term.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The parallelogram covers 14×13=182 cm214 \times 13 = 182 \text{ cm}^214×13=182 cm2, and the triangle is one of the two equal pieces it is built from, so the triangle covers 91 cm291 \text{ cm}^291 cm2.

    Part B

    The diagonal gives triangles of 47.5 m247.5 \text{ m}^247.5 m2 and 27.5 m227.5 \text{ m}^227.5 m2, adding to 75 m275 \text{ m}^275 m2, which is what the trapezoid rule gives. Both triangles have the same perpendicular height, 555 m, so their two halves collect into half of the sum of the parallel sides.

    Part C

    The rectangles cover 55 m255 \text{ m}^255 m2 and 95 m295 \text{ m}^295 m2, and the trapezoid's 75 m275 \text{ m}^275 m2 is exactly halfway between them, because averaging the two parallel sides is the same operation as averaging those two rectangles.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Rotate the copy by half a turn and set it against the original along one of the sides that is not the base. The two shapes fit with no gap and no overlap, and the two 141414 cm bases finish as the opposite pair of sides of the parallelogram. The perpendicular distance between that pair is the distance from the triangle's base to its far vertex, which is the triangle's own height, 131313 cm. So the parallelogram stands on a base of 141414 cm with a perpendicular height of 131313 cm, and its area is base times height:

    Aparallelogram=14×13=182 cm2.A_{\text{parallelogram}} = 14 \times 13 = 182 \text{ cm}^2.Aparallelogram​=14×13=182 cm2.

    That parallelogram is built from two triangles, and they are copies of one another, so they cover equal amounts of space. One triangle is therefore exactly half of the pair:

    A=12×14×13=12×182=91 cm2.A = \frac{1}{2} \times 14 \times 13 = \frac{1}{2} \times 182 = 91 \text{ cm}^2.A=21​×14×13=21​×182=91 cm2.

    This is where the factor of one half in the triangle rule comes from. It is not a decoration on the formula: it is the record of the triangle being one of two equal pieces of a parallelogram that shares its base and its height. The unit is square centimetres, because the answer counts unit squares.

    Part B

    Cut from one end of the short parallel side to the far end of the long one. The two triangles that result have the parallel sides as their bases, one of 191919 m and one of 111111 m, and the perpendicular distance between those parallel sides, 555 m, is the height of both.

    Take them one at a time:

    A1=12×19×5=47.5 m2,A2=12×11×5=27.5 m2.A_1 = \frac{1}{2} \times 19 \times 5 = 47.5 \text{ m}^2, \qquad A_2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 11 \times 5 = 27.5 \text{ m}^2.A1​=21​×19×5=47.5 m2,A2​=21​×11×5=27.5 m2.

    Area is additive, so the trapezoid is their sum:

    A=47.5+27.5=75 m2.A = 47.5 + 27.5 = 75 \text{ m}^2.A=47.5+27.5=75 m2.

    The trapezoid rule gives the same total:

    A=11+192×5=15×5=75 m2.A = \frac{11 + 19}{2} \times 5 = 15 \times 5 = 75 \text{ m}^2.A=211+19​×5=15×5=75 m2.

    The agreement is not luck. Because both triangles stand on the same height, their areas are 12×19×5\frac{1}{2} \times 19 \times 521​×19×5 and 12×11×5\frac{1}{2} \times 11 \times 521​×11×5, and the common factor 12×5\frac{1}{2} \times 521​×5 can be taken out of the sum:

    12×5×(19+11)=12(a+b)h,\frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times (19 + 11) = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h,21​×5×(19+11)=21​(a+b)h,

    writing aaa and bbb for the parallel sides and hhh for the height. So adding the two triangles is the trapezoid rule, written out the long way. The average of the parallel sides, 151515 m, is then the width of a rectangle 555 m tall that covers the same space.

    Part C

    The two rectangles are quick:

    11×5=55 m2,19×5=95 m2.11 \times 5 = 55 \text{ m}^2, \qquad 19 \times 5 = 95 \text{ m}^2.11×5=55 m2,19×5=95 m2.

    The trapezoid covers 75 m275 \text{ m}^275 m2, more than the narrow rectangle and less than the wide one, which it had to be: the trapezoid is wider than 111111 m everywhere except along its short parallel side, and narrower than 191919 m everywhere except along its long one.

    But it does better than merely landing in between. It lands exactly halfway:

    55+952=1502=75 m2.\frac{55 + 95}{2} = \frac{150}{2} = 75 \text{ m}^2.255+95​=2150​=75 m2.

    The reason is inside the formula. Multiplying the trapezoid rule out splits it into those very rectangles:

    12(a+b)h=12(ah+bh),\frac{1}{2}(a + b)h = \frac{1}{2}(ah + bh),21​(a+b)h=21​(ah+bh),

    and ahahah and bhbhbh are the areas of the rectangle on the short parallel side and the rectangle on the long one. Averaging the two parallel sides and averaging the two rectangles are therefore the same operation done in a different order. That is what the trapezoid rule is averaging.

    One warning falls out of the same line. Adding the parallel sides and multiplying by the height without halving gives 30×5=150 m230 \times 5 = 150 \text{ m}^230×5=150 m2, which is the two rectangles added rather than averaged. That number is a genuine area, but of the wrong figure: it is the parallelogram made by the trapezoid and a flipped copy of it, so it counts the trapezoid twice.

    In one line

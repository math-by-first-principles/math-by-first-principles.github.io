The Coordinate Plane

Intuition

So far, geometry has measured sizes: the space around a shape, inside it, or filling a solid. Now it turns to a different question: naming exactly where something is. On a single number line you can pin down any point with one number: walk left or right until you reach it. That works for a line, but a flat page is two-dimensional, and one number cannot say where a point sits on it. If a friend says “the treasure is 333 steps from the corner,” you still do not know which direction to walk. The fix is to use two numbers: how far across, and how far up. Two number lines crossing at right angles turn that idea into a precise map, the coordinate plane. On that map every point in the plane has its own pair of numbers, and no two points share the same pair.

Learning goals

  • Name, plot, and read a point as an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), across then up
  • Say why order matters, so (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) differs from (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3)
  • Number the four quadrants counterclockwise, and give each its sign pattern
  • Measure horizontal and vertical distance with absolute differences
  • Read a rectangle's perimeter and area off its four corners

Two number lines, one plane

You already know the number line from the integers chapter. It is a straight line with 000 in the middle, positive numbers to the right and negative numbers to the left. The coordinate plane is built from two of them.

Draw one number line horizontally across the page. Call it the x-axis; it measures position left and right. Draw a second number line vertically up the page, crossing the first at a right angle. Call it the y-axis; it measures position up and down. The point where the two axes cross, where both readings are 000, is the origin.

Together these two lines turn the whole flat page into a grid where any point can be located. To reach a point you read how far across it is along the x-axis, then how far up or down it is along the y-axis. Two readings, one for each direction, are exactly enough to name any point in the plane. That matches how one reading was enough on a single line.

Naming a point: the ordered pair (x, y)

A point’s location is written as an ordered pair of numbers inside parentheses, separated by a comma:

(x,y).(x, y).(x,y).

The first number, xxx, is the x-coordinate. It says how far the point is to the right (positive) or left (negative) of the origin, measured along the x-axis. The second number, yyy, is the y-coordinate: how far the point is up (positive) or down (negative) from the origin, measured along the y-axis. The word “ordered” is the heart of it: the order is fixed as across first, then up. Because that order is fixed, you always read and write the horizontal number before the vertical one.

That fixed order is exactly why the order matters. The pair (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) means go 333 right and then 222 up. The pair (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) means go 222 right and then 333 up. Those are two different places on the page, so (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) and (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) are different points even though they use the same two numbers. Swapping the coordinates moves the point, which is why (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) is an ordered pair and not just a pair of two numbers in any order.

Here is a short way to remember the order. You walk into a room before you climb a ladder, so go across the hall, then up the stairs. The x-coordinate (across) always comes first.

Plotting a point from its coordinates

To plot a point means to mark its location given the pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y). The recipe follows straight from the meaning of the two numbers:

  1. Start at the origin, (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0).
  2. Move along the x-axis by the first number: right if it is positive, left if it is negative.
  3. From there move parallel to the y-axis by the second number: up if it is positive, down if it is negative.
  4. Mark the point where you land.

To plot (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2), start at the origin, move 333 units right, then 222 units up, and mark the spot. To plot (−4,1)(-4, 1)(−4,1), move 444 units left (because the x-coordinate is negative), then 111 unit up. To plot (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3), do not move across at all (the x-coordinate is 000), then move 333 units down, which lands you right on the y-axis.

Answer choices

A negative x-coordinate means move left, and a positive y-coordinate means move up:

(−5,4):    5 left, then 4 up.(-5, 4): \;\; 5 \text{ left, then } 4 \text{ up}.(−5,4):5 left, then 4 up.

Across comes first, so the 555 is horizontal and the 444 is vertical, not the other way around.

Reading the coordinates of a plotted point

Reading runs the recipe in reverse. Given a point already marked on the grid, you find its coordinates by measuring back to the axes:

  1. Drop straight down (or up) from the point to the x-axis and read the number you land on. That is the x-coordinate.
  2. Go straight across (left or right) from the point to the y-axis and read the number there. That is the y-coordinate.
  3. Write them in order as (x,y)(x, y)(x,y).

Take point KKK in the figure above. KKK sits above the x-axis, so trace straight down to it and you land on 333: that is the x-coordinate. Slide across from KKK to the y-axis and you land on 333, so its y-coordinate is 333 too. Written as an ordered pair, KKK is at (3,3)(3, 3)(3,3).

Point LLL sits below the x-axis, so trace straight up to it instead: you land on −3-3−3, the x-coordinate. Slide across from LLL to the y-axis and you land on −2-2−2, the y-coordinate. So point LLL is at (−3,−2)(-3, -2)(−3,−2).

The four quadrants

The two axes cut the plane into four regions, called quadrants. They are numbered with Roman numerals I, II, III, IV, starting in the upper-right region and going counterclockwise:

  • Quadrant I: upper right.
  • Quadrant II: upper left.
  • Quadrant III: lower left.
  • Quadrant IV: lower right.

Each quadrant has its own pattern of signs. You do not have to memorize those patterns, because each one follows directly from which way you move along each axis.

A point’s x-coordinate is positive to the right of the y-axis and negative to the left. Its y-coordinate is positive above the x-axis and negative below. Combine those two facts and every quadrant’s sign pattern follows for free, matching the diagram above: Quadrant I is (+,+)(+, +)(+,+).

Moving counterclockwise to the next quadrant crosses exactly one axis, so exactly one sign flips. Crossing the y-axis flips the x-sign; crossing the x-axis flips the y-sign. That single rule produces all four patterns in order: (+,+)(+,+)(+,+), (−,+)(-,+)(−,+), (−,−)(-,-)(−,−), (+,−)(+,-)(+,−). It is one idea applied four times, not four separate rules to memorize.

A point that lands on an axis belongs to no quadrant, like (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0) in the diagram above. If a point sits on the x-axis its y-coordinate is 000, and if it sits on the y-axis its x-coordinate is 000. The axes are the boundaries of the quadrants, not part of any of them, and the origin sits on both.

Worked example 1 Plot a point and name its quadrant

Plot the point (−3,5)(-3, 5)(−3,5) and state which quadrant it lies in.

Start at the origin. The x-coordinate is −3-3−3, so move 333 units to the left (negative means left). The y-coordinate is 555, so from there move 555 units up (positive means up). Mark the point where you land.

Now read off the signs to find the quadrant. The x-coordinate is negative and the y-coordinate is positive, which is the upper-left region:

(−,+)  ⇒  Quadrant II.(-, +) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Quadrant II}.(−,+)⇒Quadrant II.

So (−3,5)(-3, 5)(−3,5) sits in Quadrant II, 333 units left of the y-axis and 555 units above the x-axis.

Check your understanding

In which quadrant does the point (−6,−1)(-6, -1)(−6,−1) lie?

Answer choices

Look at the signs of the two coordinates. Here x=−6x = -6x=−6 is negative and y=−1y = -1y=−1 is negative.

A negative x means the point is left of the y-axis, and a negative y means it is below the x-axis. Both together put the point in the lower-left region:

(−,−)  ⇒  Quadrant III.(-, -) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Quadrant III}.(−,−)⇒Quadrant III.

Both coordinates negative always lands in Quadrant III.

Horizontal and vertical distance on the grid

When two points share a coordinate, the distance between them is easy to read straight off the grid. That is because the segment joining them runs along a gridline.

If two points have the same y-coordinate, they sit at the same height, so the segment between them is horizontal. The distance is just how far apart their x-coordinates are. If two points have the same x-coordinate, they sit in the same column, so the segment between them is vertical. The distance is then how far apart their y-coordinates are. In both cases you find the gap by subtracting the smaller value from the larger one. That subtraction is the same as taking the absolute value of their difference, so the distance never comes out negative.

For example, the points (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) and (7,5)(7, 5)(7,5) share the height y=5y = 5y=5, so they are a horizontal distance

∣7−2∣=5|7 - 2| = 5∣7−2∣=5

apart. The points (−3,1)(-3, 1)(−3,1) and (−3,6)(-3, 6)(−3,6) share the column x=−3x = -3x=−3, so they are a vertical distance

∣6−1∣=5|6 - 1| = 5∣6−1∣=5

apart. You can also simply count the unit spaces between the two points and get the same number.

Why horizontal distance is the difference of the x-coordinates#

Take two points on the same horizontal line, say (x1,y)(x_1, y)(x1​,y) and (x2,y)(x_2, y)(x2​,y), with the same height yyy, matching the (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) and (7,5)(7, 5)(7,5) example above. The y-coordinate does not change between them, so moving from one to the other is pure left-right motion along that height, exactly like moving along a single number line.

On a number line the distance between two positions is how many units separate them, which you get by subtracting the smaller from the larger. So the distance here is the gap between x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​:

d=∣x2−x1∣.d = |x_2 - x_1|.d=∣x2​−x1​∣.

The absolute value bars just make sure the distance is never reported as negative. The reported length is the same no matter which point you list first. The identical argument with the roles of xxx and yyy swapped shows that two points sharing an x-coordinate are a vertical distance ∣y2−y1∣|y_2 - y_1|∣y2​−y1​∣ apart. In this lesson, a single coordinate difference gives the full distance only for a horizontal or vertical segment; a slanted segment needs a different method, which is not covered here.

Worked example 2 Read coordinates and measure a horizontal distance

A point PPP sits 444 units to the left of the origin and 222 units up. A point QQQ sits 333 units to the right of the origin and 222 units up. Find the coordinates of each point and the distance between them.

Translate each description into an ordered pair, across first and then up. Point PPP is 444 left and 222 up, so P=(−4,2)P = (-4, 2)P=(−4,2). Point QQQ is 333 right and 222 up, so Q=(3,2)Q = (3, 2)Q=(3,2).

Both points have the same y-coordinate, 222, so they lie on the same horizontal line and the distance between them is horizontal. Subtract the x-coordinates and take the absolute value:

d=∣3−(−4)∣=∣3+4∣=7.d = |3 - (-4)| = |3 + 4| = 7.d=∣3−(−4)∣=∣3+4∣=7.

So PPP and QQQ are 777 units apart. Counting unit spaces from x=−4x = -4x=−4 across to x=3x = 3x=3 gives the same 777 units, which is a good way to check the subtraction.

Perimeter and area of a rectangle on the grid

Once you can measure horizontal and vertical gaps, you can handle a rectangle whose corners are given as coordinates. That works as long as its sides run along the gridlines, which makes it an axis-aligned rectangle. Find the length of one horizontal side and one vertical side, then use the rectangle formulas you already know from the perimeter and area lesson:

P=2(l+w),A=l×w.P = 2(l + w), \qquad A = l \times w.P=2(l+w),A=l×w.

The two side lengths are just a horizontal distance and a vertical distance, each found by subtracting coordinates. Because the rectangle is axis-aligned, every side is either horizontal or vertical, so no slanted length is ever needed.

Worked example 3 Perimeter and area of a rectangle from its corners

A rectangle has corners at (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1), (6,1)(6, 1)(6,1), (6,4)(6, 4)(6,4), and (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4). Find its perimeter and area.

First find the length of a horizontal side. The bottom side runs from (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) to (6,1)(6, 1)(6,1), and these two corners share the height y=1y = 1y=1. So that side is horizontal, and its length is the gap in the x-coordinates:

l=∣6−1∣=5.l = |6 - 1| = 5.l=∣6−1∣=5.

Now find a vertical side. The left side runs from (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) to (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4), and these two corners share the column x=1x = 1x=1. So that side is vertical, and its length is the gap in the y-coordinates:

w=∣4−1∣=3.w = |4 - 1| = 3.w=∣4−1∣=3.

The rectangle is 555 units long and 333 units wide. Apply the perimeter and area formulas:

P=2(l+w)=2(5+3)=16,P = 2(l + w) = 2(5 + 3) = 16,P=2(l+w)=2(5+3)=16,A=l×w=5×3=15.A = l \times w = 5 \times 3 = 15.A=l×w=5×3=15.

So the rectangle has a perimeter of 161616 units and an area of 151515 square units.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Swapping x and y. The order is across first, then up: (x,y)(x, y)(x,y). Plotting (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) as ”333 right, 222 up” lands on the wrong point. Remember (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) and (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) are different places.
  • Mixing up the direction of negatives. A negative x-coordinate moves left, a negative y-coordinate moves down. Watch the sign of each coordinate separately.
  • Reading the wrong axis for a coordinate. To read a plotted point, move straight to the x-axis (down if the point is above it, up if the point is below it) for the first number, and straight to the y-axis for the second. Reading height off the x-axis is a common slip.
  • Putting axis points in a quadrant. A point on an axis has a 000 coordinate and belongs to no quadrant. For example (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4) is on the y-axis, not in Quadrant III or IV.
  • Subtracting coordinates for a diagonal distance. Subtracting coordinates gives the straight-line distance only when the two points share a coordinate (a horizontal or vertical segment). For points that are diagonal to each other, one coordinate’s difference is only part of the movement, not the full distance between them.
  • Forgetting the absolute value. A distance is never negative. Subtracting in the wrong order can give a negative number, for example −4−3=−7-4 - 3 = -7−4−3=−7, but the distance is ∣−4−3∣=7|-4 - 3| = 7∣−4−3∣=7, not −7-7−7.

Key takeaways

  • The coordinate plane is two perpendicular number lines: a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis crossing at the origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0).
  • A point is named by an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y): the x-coordinate is the horizontal position (right if positive, left if negative). The y-coordinate is the vertical position (up if positive, down if negative). Order matters, so (3,2)≠(2,3)(3, 2) \ne (2, 3)(3,2)=(2,3).
  • To plot (x,y)(x, y)(x,y): from the origin go across by xxx, then up or down by yyy. To read a point, move straight to the x-axis (down from above it, up from below it) and straight to the y-axis.
  • The axes make four quadrants, numbered I to IV counterclockwise from the upper right, with sign patterns (+,+)(+,+)(+,+), (−,+)(-,+)(−,+), (−,−)(-,-)(−,−), (+,−)(+,-)(+,−). A point on an axis is in no quadrant.
  • For points that share a coordinate, the horizontal distance is ∣x2−x1∣|x_2 - x_1|∣x2​−x1​∣ and the vertical distance is ∣y2−y1∣|y_2 - y_1|∣y2​−y1​∣.
  • From the four corners of an axis-aligned rectangle, read a horizontal side and a vertical side, then use P=2(l+w)P = 2(l + w)P=2(l+w) and A=l×wA = l \times wA=l×w.

Reflecting a point across an axis

Reflecting a point across an axis means flipping it to the mirror-image position on the other side of that axis, the same distance away.

Reflect a point across the x-axis and it keeps the same x-coordinate, so it stays in the same column. Its y-coordinate changes sign, so the point flips from above the axis to below, or below to above. So (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) reflected across the x-axis becomes (3,−2)(3, -2)(3,−2).

Reflect a point across the y-axis and the opposite happens: the y-coordinate stays the same while the x-coordinate changes sign. So (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) reflected across the y-axis becomes (−3,2)(-3, 2)(−3,2).

Crossing the x-axis always flips the y-sign, and crossing the y-axis always flips the x-sign; each reflection touches exactly one coordinate and leaves the other unchanged. Flipping a sign changes that coordinate’s value only when it started out positive or negative. A point already sitting on the mirror axis has 000 for the coordinate that would flip, and flipping the sign of 000 still gives 000, so the point does not move at all.

A bit of history (optional)

Before maps carried grids, how did you tell someone where a place was? You gave a route: sail past the headland, then follow the coast for six days, directions from somewhere else.

Around the year 150150150, an astronomer named Ptolemy tried something different. Working in Alexandria, a port city in Egypt, he listed thousands of places and gave each one two numbers: how far around the world it lay, and how far north or south of the equator. Two numbers, in a fixed order, and the place was pinned, no route or landmarks needed.

Many of his numbers were badly wrong; longitude was hard to pin down without a clock. But the scheme underneath them was right. It is the ordered pair you plotted in this lesson, fifteen centuries early.

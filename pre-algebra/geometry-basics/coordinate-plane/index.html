The Coordinate Plane

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

So far, geometry has measured sizes: the space around a shape, inside it, or filling a solid. Now we want to name exactly where something is. On a number line, one number gives a position. On a flat page, “the treasure is 333 steps from the corner” leaves you wondering which direction to walk. “Go 333 steps right, then 222 steps up” names a place.

The coordinate plane turns those two directions into a precise map. Each point gets two numbers: one for its horizontal position and one for its vertical position.

Learning goals

  • Name, plot, and read a point as an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), across then up
  • Say why order matters, so (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) differs from (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3)
  • Number the four quadrants counterclockwise, and give each its sign pattern
  • Measure horizontal and vertical distance with absolute differences
  • Read a rectangle's perimeter and area off its four corners
  • Reflect a point across either axis by changing the sign of one coordinate

Two number lines, one plane

You already know the number line from the integers chapter: 000 marks the starting position, positive numbers lie to its right, and negative numbers lie to its left. The coordinate plane uses two perpendicular number lines, which cross at a right angle.

The horizontal number line is the x-axis; it measures position left and right. The vertical number line is the y-axis; it measures position up and down, with positive numbers above 000 and negative numbers below. The axes cross at their zeros, a point called the origin.

The two readings locate any point: one fixes its column and the other fixes its height. Together they pick out exactly one place. The grid below uses the same unit length on both axes.

Naming a point: the ordered pair (x, y)

A point’s location is written as an ordered pair, two numbers in a fixed order inside parentheses:

(x,y).(x, y).(x,y).

The first number, xxx, is the x-coordinate. It gives the horizontal position: right if positive, left if negative. The second, yyy, is the y-coordinate. It gives the vertical position: up if positive, down if negative. Both are measured from the origin, whose coordinates are (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0).

For example, (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) means 333 right and 222 up, while (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) means 222 right and 333 up. These are different places, so order matters. Swapping coordinates changes the point unless the coordinates are equal, as in (3,3)(3, 3)(3,3).

To remember the order, think “across the hall, then up the stairs.” The x-coordinate comes first; the sign tells you whether “across” means left or right and “up” means up or down.

Plotting a point from its coordinates

To plot a point means to mark its location from its coordinates:

  1. Start at the origin, (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0).
  2. Move along the x-axis by the first number: right if it is positive, left if it is negative.
  3. From there move parallel to the y-axis by the second number: up if it is positive, down if it is negative.
  4. Mark the point where you land.

To plot (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2), start at the origin, move 333 units right, then 222 units up, and mark the spot. To plot (−4,1)(-4, 1)(−4,1), move 444 units left (because the x-coordinate is negative), then 111 unit up. To plot (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3), do not move across at all (the x-coordinate is 000), then move 333 units down, which lands you right on the y-axis.

Answer choices

The first number is the x-coordinate (across) and the second is the y-coordinate (up or down). Here x=−5x = -5x=−5 and y=4y = 4y=4.

A negative x-coordinate means move left, and a positive y-coordinate means move up:

(−5,4):    5 left, then 4 up.(-5, 4): \;\; 5 \text{ left, then } 4 \text{ up}.(−5,4):5 left, then 4 up.

Across comes first, so the 555 is horizontal and the 444 is vertical, not the other way around.

Reading the coordinates of a plotted point

To read a plotted point, work back to the axes:

  1. Trace straight down or up from the point to the x-axis and read the number you land on. That is the x-coordinate.
  2. Go straight across (left or right) from the point to the y-axis and read the number there. That is the y-coordinate.
  3. Write them in order as (x,y)(x, y)(x,y).

For KKK, trace straight down to the x-axis to read 333, then across to the y-axis to read 333 again. So KKK is at (3,3)(3, 3)(3,3).

Point LLL is below the x-axis, so trace straight up to that axis: it reads −3-3−3. Across at the y-axis, it reads −2-2−2. So LLL is at (−3,−2)(-3, -2)(−3,−2).

Answer choices

The x-coordinate always comes first, so it is (1,−4)(1, -4)(1,−4), not (−4,1)(-4, 1)(−4,1).

The four quadrants

The two axes cut the plane into four regions, called quadrants. They are numbered with Roman numerals I, II, III, IV, starting in the upper-right region and going counterclockwise:

  • Quadrant I: upper right.
  • Quadrant II: upper left.
  • Quadrant III: lower left.
  • Quadrant IV: lower right.

Each quadrant’s sign pattern follows from its position relative to the axes.

A point’s x-coordinate is positive to the right of the y-axis and negative to the left. Its y-coordinate is positive above the x-axis and negative below. Combine those two facts and every quadrant’s sign pattern follows. For example, Quadrant I is right and above, so its pattern is (+,+)(+, +)(+,+).

Moving counterclockwise to the next quadrant crosses exactly one axis, so exactly one sign flips. Crossing the y-axis flips the x-sign; crossing the x-axis flips the y-sign. That single rule produces all four patterns in order: (+,+)(+,+)(+,+), (−,+)(-,+)(−,+), (−,−)(-,-)(−,−), (+,−)(+,-)(+,−).

A point that lands on an axis belongs to no quadrant, like (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0) in the diagram above. If a point sits on the x-axis its y-coordinate is 000, and if it sits on the y-axis its x-coordinate is 000. The axes are the boundaries of the quadrants, not part of any of them, and the origin sits on both.

Worked example 1 Plot a point and name its quadrant

Plot the point (−3,5)(-3, 5)(−3,5) and state which quadrant it lies in.

Start at the origin. The x-coordinate is −3-3−3, so move 333 units left. The y-coordinate is 555, so from there move 555 units up. Mark the point where you land.

Now read off the signs to find the quadrant. The x-coordinate is negative and the y-coordinate is positive, which is the upper-left region:

(−,+)  ⇒  Quadrant II.(-, +) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Quadrant II}.(−,+)⇒Quadrant II.

So (−3,5)(-3, 5)(−3,5) sits in Quadrant II, 333 units left of the y-axis and 555 units above the x-axis.

Check your understanding

In which quadrant does the point (−6,−1)(-6, -1)(−6,−1) lie?

Answer choices

Look at the signs of the two coordinates. Here x=−6x = -6x=−6 is negative and y=−1y = -1y=−1 is negative.

A negative x means the point is left of the y-axis, and a negative y means it is below the x-axis. Both together put the point in the lower-left region:

(−,−)  ⇒  Quadrant III.(-, -) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Quadrant III}.(−,−)⇒Quadrant III.

Both coordinates negative always lands in Quadrant III.

Horizontal and vertical distance on the grid

Points at the same height are separated horizontally. For example, (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) and (7,5)(7, 5)(7,5) share the y-coordinate 555. Count the spaces from x=2x = 2x=2 to x=7x = 7x=7: the distance is

∣7−2∣=5|7 - 2| = 5∣7−2∣=5

units. Points in the same column are separated vertically. The points (−3,1)(-3, 1)(−3,1) and (−3,6)(-3, 6)(−3,6) share the x-coordinate −3-3−3, so their distance is

∣6−1∣=5|6 - 1| = 5∣6−1∣=5

units.

When one coordinate stays the same, use the difference of the other coordinates. Absolute value gives the size of that gap, whichever point you list first. This gives the full distance only for horizontal or vertical segments, not slanted ones.

Why horizontal distance is the difference of the x-coordinates#

Take (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2) and (4,−2)(4, -2)(4,−2). Both have height −2-2−2, so moving between them changes only the horizontal position. The gap is the same as from 111 to 444 on a number line: 4−1=34 - 1 = 34−1=3 units. Listing them in reverse gives 1−4=−31 - 4 = -31−4=−3, whose absolute value is still 333.

The height and the particular numbers do not affect this reasoning. For any two points (x1,y)(x_1, y)(x1​,y) and (x2,y)(x_2, y)(x2​,y), the shared yyy keeps them on one horizontal line. Their distance is the gap from x1x_1x1​ to x2x_2x2​ on a number line:

d=∣x2−x1∣.d = |x_2 - x_1|.d=∣x2​−x1​∣.

Absolute value makes this the larger coordinate minus the smaller. If the coordinates are equal, the two points coincide and the distance is 000.

For a vertical segment, the x-coordinate stays fixed and only the y-coordinate changes. The same number-line reasoning gives d=∣y2−y1∣d = |y_2 - y_1|d=∣y2​−y1​∣.

∎

Worked example 2 Read coordinates and measure a horizontal distance

A point PPP sits 444 units to the left of the origin and 222 units up. A point QQQ sits 333 units to the right of the origin and 222 units up. Find the coordinates of each point and the distance between them.

Translate each description into an ordered pair, across first and then up. Point PPP is 444 left and 222 up, so P=(−4,2)P = (-4, 2)P=(−4,2). Point QQQ is 333 right and 222 up, so Q=(3,2)Q = (3, 2)Q=(3,2).

Both points have y-coordinate 222, so subtract the x-coordinates and take the absolute value:

d=∣3−(−4)∣=∣3+4∣=7.d = |3 - (-4)| = |3 + 4| = 7.d=∣3−(−4)∣=∣3+4∣=7.

So PPP and QQQ are 777 units apart. You can check by counting 444 units from −4-4−4 to 000 and another 333 from 000 to 333.

Distance to an axis uses the same rule. From (−4,2)(-4, 2)(−4,2) to the y-axis, move horizontally to (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2): the distance is ∣0−(−4)∣=4|0 - (-4)| = 4∣0−(−4)∣=4. To reach the x-axis, move vertically to (−4,0)(-4, 0)(−4,0): the distance is ∣0−2∣=2|0 - 2| = 2∣0−2∣=2. In general, distance to the y-axis is ∣x∣|x|∣x∣, and distance to the x-axis is ∣y∣|y|∣y∣.

Perimeter and area of a rectangle on the grid

A rectangle is axis-aligned when its sides are parallel to the axes: two horizontal and two vertical. Find one horizontal side length and one vertical side length from its corners, then use the formulas from the perimeter and area lesson:

P=2(l+w),A=l×w.P = 2(l + w), \qquad A = l \times w.P=2(l+w),A=l×w.

Worked example 3 Perimeter and area of a rectangle from its corners

A rectangle has corners at (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1), (6,1)(6, 1)(6,1), (6,4)(6, 4)(6,4), and (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4). Find its perimeter and area.

The bottom corners, (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) and (6,1)(6, 1)(6,1), share the height y=1y = 1y=1. Their horizontal distance is

l=∣6−1∣=5.l = |6 - 1| = 5.l=∣6−1∣=5.

The left corners, (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) and (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4), share the column x=1x = 1x=1. Their vertical distance is

w=∣4−1∣=3.w = |4 - 1| = 3.w=∣4−1∣=3.

The rectangle is 555 units long and 333 units wide. Apply the perimeter and area formulas:

P=2(l+w)=2(5+3)=16,P = 2(l + w) = 2(5 + 3) = 16,P=2(l+w)=2(5+3)=16,A=l×w=5×3=15.A = l \times w = 5 \times 3 = 15.A=l×w=5×3=15.

So the rectangle has a perimeter of 161616 units and an area of 151515 square units.

Reflecting across an axis

Reflecting a point across an axis means finding its mirror-image position. Imagine folding the page along that axis: the point and its reflection would meet.

Across the x-axis, (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) reflects to (3,−2)(3, -2)(3,−2). Both points are in the same column, and each is 222 units from the x-axis, one above and one below. The x-coordinate stays the same; the y-coordinate changes to its opposite.

Across the y-axis, (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) reflects to (−3,2)(-3, 2)(−3,2). The points have the same height and lie 333 units from the y-axis on opposite sides. This time, the y-coordinate stays the same and the x-coordinate changes to its opposite.

So reflecting (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) across the x-axis gives (x,−y)(x, -y)(x,−y), and reflecting it across the y-axis gives (−x,y)(-x, y)(−x,y). A point on the mirror axis stays where it is: the coordinate that changes is 000, and −0=0-0 = 0−0=0. For example, reflecting (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3) across the y-axis leaves it at (0,−3)(0, -3)(0,−3).

Check your understanding

Reflect (−4,3)(-4, 3)(−4,3) across the x-axis. What are the coordinates of its reflection?

Answer choices

Reflecting across the x-axis keeps the x-coordinate and changes the y-coordinate to its opposite.

(−4,3)→(−4,−3).(-4, 3) \to (-4, -3).(−4,3)→(−4,−3).

The point stays in the same column, moving from 333 units above the x-axis to 333 units below it.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Swapping x and y. Write horizontal position first, then vertical position: (x,y)(x, y)(x,y). The points (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) and (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) are different places.
  • Mixing up negative directions. A negative x-coordinate means left; a negative y-coordinate means down.
  • Reading the wrong axis. Trace vertically to the x-axis for the first coordinate and horizontally to the y-axis for the second.
  • Putting axis points in a quadrant. A point on an axis has a 000 coordinate and belongs to no quadrant. For example, (0,−4)(0, -4)(0,−4) is on the y-axis, not in Quadrant III or IV.
  • Using a coordinate difference incorrectly. It gives the full distance only for a horizontal or vertical segment. Take its absolute value: −4−3=−7-4 - 3 = -7−4−3=−7, but the distance is 777 units.

Key takeaways

  • The horizontal x-axis and vertical y-axis cross at the origin, (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0). Plot (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) across first, then up or down. To read a point, trace back to those axes in that order.
  • Quadrants I to IV run counterclockwise from the upper right: (+,+)(+,+)(+,+), (−,+)(-,+)(−,+), (−,−)(-,-)(−,−), (+,−)(+,-)(+,−). Points on an axis belong to no quadrant.
  • For points sharing a y-coordinate, horizontal distance is ∣x2−x1∣|x_2 - x_1|∣x2​−x1​∣. For points sharing an x-coordinate, vertical distance is ∣y2−y1∣|y_2 - y_1|∣y2​−y1​∣.
  • For an axis-aligned rectangle, use coordinate differences to find its side lengths, then P=2(l+w)P = 2(l + w)P=2(l+w) and A=l×wA = l \times wA=l×w.
  • Reflecting across the x-axis changes the sign of yyy; reflecting across the y-axis changes the sign of xxx. A point on the mirror axis stays fixed.

A bit of history (optional)

Imagine describing a port by its route: sail past a headland, then follow the coast for six days. That tells a traveler how to get there, but it is harder to use when drawing a map of the world.

In the second century, the astronomer Ptolemy worked in Alexandria, Egypt. His Geography gathered information about thousands of places and assigned them longitude and latitude: an east-west position measured from a chosen starting line, and a north-south position measured from the equator. A mapmaker could place a city from those numbers without retracing a journey. The James Ford Bell Library describes his system.

Many of the positions were wrong. Ptolemy was assembling reports about a world he could not survey himself, and the figures often misplaced distant places. Yet his book remained useful for centuries: later mapmakers used its coordinates to draw their own maps. The Library of Congress holds many of those later editions.

Volume and Surface Area

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A cereal box poses two different measurement questions: how much space does it hold, and how much cardboard covers it? Volume answers the first; surface area answers the second. The same distinction applies to water inside a tank and paint on its outside.

Both measurements build on area. Stacking layers tells us how much space a solid fills. Unfolding its outside into flat pieces tells us how much material covers it.

Learning goals

  • Measure volume in cubic units and convert units using the length factor cubed
  • Find prism and cylinder volumes by multiplying base area by perpendicular height
  • Recover a missing dimension from volume using division or a square root
  • Unfold a net and add every outer surface's area in square units
  • Read a right cylinder's side as a rectangle whose width is the circumference

Volume: counting unit cubes

Area counts unit squares covering a flat region. Volume counts unit cubes filling a solid, with no gaps or overlaps. A unit cube has edges one unit long, so a cube with edges 111 cm long occupies one cubic centimeter, written 1 cm31 \text{ cm}^31 cm3.

A box with whole-number side lengths fills exactly with unit cubes. Fractional lengths, slanted faces, and curved surfaces may need parts of cubes too. Volume measures all the space enclosed, including those parts.

A length uses units such as cm, an area uses square units such as cm2\text{cm}^2cm2, and a volume uses cubic units such as cm3\text{cm}^3cm3. The exponents record the two directions of a square and the three directions of a cube.

Volume of a rectangular prism

A rectangular prism is a box with six rectangular faces. Its length lll, width www, and height hhh meet at right angles. To count the cubes inside, count one layer and then multiply by the number of layers.

The figure’s bottom layer is one cube thick. It covers a 444 by 333 rectangle, so it holds

4×3=12 cubes.4 \times 3 = 12 \text{ cubes}.4×3=12 cubes.

The base area is 121212 square units. Each unit-thick layer holds 121212 cubes, and two layers give

12×2=24 cubes.12 \times 2 = 24 \text{ cubes}.12×2=24 cubes.

Multiplying the base area by the height counts every cube exactly once.

Why the volume of a rectangular prism is length times width times height#

For a box 333 units long, 222 wide, and 555 high, each layer holds 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6 unit cubes. Five layers hold 6+6+6+6+6=6×5=306 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 6 \times 5 = 306+6+6+6+6=6×5=30 cubes. Each cube belongs to one layer and one position within that layer, so none is missed or counted twice.

The counts can be any positive whole numbers. With length lll, width www, and height hhh, one layer has l×wl \times wl×w cubes and there are hhh layers:

V=(l×w)+(l×w)+⋯+(l×w)⏟h layers=(l×w)×h.V = \underbrace{(l \times w) + (l \times w) + \cdots + (l \times w)}_{h \text{ layers}} = (l \times w) \times h.V=h layers(l×w)+(l×w)+⋯+(l×w)​​=(l×w)×h.

The same counting argument gives V=lwhV = lwhV=lwh.

∎

For a rectangular prism,

V=l×w×h.V = l \times w \times h.V=l×w×h.

The formula also applies to fractional lengths: smaller cubes measure the fractional parts. For example, a 1.51.51.5 by 111 by 111 box contains 3×2×2=123 \times 2 \times 2 = 123×2×2=12 cubes with half-unit edges. Since 2×2×2=82 \times 2 \times 2 = 82×2×2=8 of these smaller cubes fill one unit cube, each has volume one eighth of a cubic unit. The box’s volume is 12÷8=1.512 \div 8 = 1.512÷8=1.5 cubic units, the same as 1.5×1×11.5 \times 1 \times 11.5×1×1.

A cube is a rectangular prism whose edges all have the same length sss, so its volume is

V=s×s×s=s3.V = s \times s \times s = s^3.V=s×s×s=s3.

This is why s3s^3s3 is read ”sss cubed”, just as s2s^2s2 is ”sss squared”: the powers measure a cube’s volume and a square’s area.

Converting cubic units

A meter is 100100100 cm, so a cube with edges 111 m long has 100100100 centimeter cubes along each edge. Each layer holds 100×100100 \times 100100×100 cubes, and there are 100100100 layers:

1 m3=100×100×100 cm3=1,000,000 cm3.1 \text{ m}^3 = 100 \times 100 \times 100 \text{ cm}^3 = 1{,}000{,}000 \text{ cm}^3.1 m3=100×100×100 cm3=1,000,000 cm3.

In Perimeter and Area, square units used the length conversion factor twice. Here it appears three times. To convert cubic units, cube the length conversion factor. In the reverse direction, divide by that cubed factor.

It is often simpler to convert all lengths to the same unit before multiplying. For a box 0.60.60.6 m long, 202020 cm wide, and 101010 cm high, change 0.60.60.6 m to 606060 cm. Its volume is 60×20×10=12,000 cm360 \times 20 \times 10 = 12{,}000 \text{ cm}^360×20×10=12,000 cm3. In cubic meters this is 12,000÷1,000,000=0.012 m312{,}000 \div 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.012 \text{ m}^312,000÷1,000,000=0.012 m3.

Volume of any prism: base area times height

A prism has two matching polygon ends in parallel planes, joined by parallelogram faces. The matching ends are its bases. Slices parallel to the bases have the same shape and size. A box has rectangular bases; a triangular prism, such as a tent, has triangular bases.

The layer idea still works when the base is not rectangular. A prism with base area 777 square units has 777 cubic units in each unit-thick layer. A height of 333 units gives three layers, for a volume of 7×3=217 \times 3 = 217×3=21 cubic units. Parts of unit cubes fill the edges where needed. With base area BBB and height hhh, the rule is

V=B×h.V = B \times h.V=B×h.

Here hhh is the perpendicular height: the distance between the base planes, measured at right angles to them. It need not point up in the picture. For the tent below, it runs lengthwise. Even if a prism leans, V=BhV = BhV=Bh uses this perpendicular distance, not a slanted edge. Sliding layers sideways changes their positions without changing how much space they fill.

For a box, B=lwB = lwB=lw, giving V=lwhV = lwhV=lwh. For a triangular prism, B=12bh△B = \tfrac{1}{2} b h_\triangleB=21​bh△​, using the triangle formula from Perimeter and Area. The triangle’s own height helps find BBB; the distance between the triangular ends then gives the prism’s volume.

Worked example 1 Volume of a triangular prism

A tent is a triangular prism. Its triangular end has a base of 666 m and a perpendicular height of 444 m, and the tent runs 101010 m long. Find its volume.

First find the area of the triangular base, BBB, using the triangle area formula:

B=12×b×h△=12×6×4=12 m2.B = \frac{1}{2} \times b \times h_\triangle = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 4 = 12 \text{ m}^2.B=21​×b×h△​=21​×6×4=12 m2.

The 101010 m length is the distance between the triangular ends, so it plays the role of hhh in V=BhV = BhV=Bh:

V=B×h=12×10=120 m3.V = B \times h = 12 \times 10 = 120 \text{ m}^3.V=B×h=12×10=120 m3.

The tent encloses 120120120 cubic meters: square meters of base area times meters of length.

Volume of a cylinder

A cylinder here has two matching circular bases in parallel planes, joined by a curved side. A can is a familiar example. Slices parallel to the bases are matching circles, so the layers give the same volume rule, V=BhV = BhV=Bh.

For instance, a cylinder with radius 222 units has base area 4π4\pi4π square units. Stacking that base through a perpendicular height of 333 units gives 4π×3=12π4\pi \times 3 = 12\pi4π×3=12π cubic units. The curved edge needs parts of cubes, but the layer reasoning is unchanged.

In general, the base area is πr2\pi r^2πr2, so

V=πr2h.V = \pi r^2 h.V=πr2h.

Use the radius rrr, not the diameter. If the diameter is given, halve it first. As with a prism, hhh measures the perpendicular distance between the bases, even if the cylinder leans or lies on its side.

Worked example 2 Volume of a cylinder

A can of soup is a cylinder with radius 333 cm and height 101010 cm. Find its volume. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

The base is a circle of radius 333 cm, so find its area first:

A=πr2≈3.14×32=3.14×9=28.26 cm2.A = \pi r^2 \approx 3.14 \times 3^2 = 3.14 \times 9 = 28.26 \text{ cm}^2.A=πr2≈3.14×32=3.14×9=28.26 cm2.

Now stack that base through the height of 101010 cm:

V=A×h≈28.26×10=282.6 cm3.V = A \times h \approx 28.26 \times 10 = 282.6 \text{ cm}^3.V=A×h≈28.26×10=282.6 cm3.

The can holds about 283283283 cubic centimeters. This is approximate because we used 3.143.143.14 for π\piπ.

Finding a missing length from volume

In Perimeter and Area, dividing an area by a known length recovered a missing length. Run a volume formula backward in the same way, using the inverse operations from Solving One-Step Equations. For a box with volume 198 cm3198 \text{ cm}^3198 cm3, length 111111 cm, and width 333 cm, substitute the known values:

198=11×3×h=33h.198 = 11 \times 3 \times h = 33h.198=11×3×h=33h.

Divide both sides by 333333 to undo the multiplication: h=198÷33=6h = 198 \div 33 = 6h=198÷33=6 cm. Check by multiplying: 11×3×6=19811 \times 3 \times 6 = 19811×3×6=198. The same method finds any missing box dimension: divide the volume by the product of the other two lengths.

For any prism or cylinder with positive base area, V=BhV = BhV=Bh gives h=V÷Bh = V \div Bh=V÷B. For example, a cylinder of radius 333 cm and volume 63π cm363\pi \text{ cm}^363π cm3 has base area 9π cm29\pi \text{ cm}^29π cm2, so its height is

h=63π9π=7 cm.h = \frac{63\pi}{9\pi} = 7 \text{ cm}.h=9π63π​=7 cm.

A cylinder’s radius needs one more step because it is squared, just as finding a radius from area did in Circles. Suppose its volume is 96π cm396\pi \text{ cm}^396π cm3 and its height is 666 cm. Substituting gives

96π=πr2×6.96\pi = \pi r^2 \times 6.96π=πr2×6.

Divide both sides by 6π6\pi6π to get r2=16r^2 = 16r2=16. Then r=16=4r = \sqrt{16} = 4r=16​=4 cm. Choose the positive square root because a radius is a positive length. Checking, π×42×6=96π\pi \times 4^2 \times 6 = 96\piπ×42×6=96π, the given volume.

In general, divide the cylinder’s volume by πh\pi hπh to find r2r^2r2, then take its square root. Keep an exact multiple of π\piπ when given one; rounding first can make an exact length harder to recognize.

Surface area: unfolding the net

Surface area is the total area of every outer surface of a solid, flat or curved. It measures how much material would cover the outside. Because it adds areas, its units are square units. Volume uses cubic units to measure the space inside.

Cutting and unfolding the surface of a box, prism, or right circular cylinder gives a flat arrangement called a net. A box’s net shows all six faces without overlap, so adding their areas gives the box’s surface area.

Opposite faces of a box match, so its six rectangles come in three pairs.

Why the surface area of a box is 2(lw+lh+wh)2(lw + lh + wh)2(lw+lh+wh)#

Consider a box 666 cm long, 333 cm wide, and 222 cm high. Its top and bottom each have area 6×3=18 cm26 \times 3 = 18 \text{ cm}^26×3=18 cm2. Its front and back each have area 6×2=12 cm26 \times 2 = 12 \text{ cm}^26×2=12 cm2, and its two ends each have area 3×2=6 cm23 \times 2 = 6 \text{ cm}^23×2=6 cm2. All six faces together give

S=2(18)+2(12)+2(6)=72 cm2.S = 2(18) + 2(12) + 2(6) = 72 \text{ cm}^2.S=2(18)+2(12)+2(6)=72 cm2.

These pairs exist for any rectangular box: sliding one face straight across the box lands it on its opposite face. For length lll, width www, and height hhh, the top and bottom each have area lwlwlw, the front and back each have area lhlhlh, and the ends each have area whwhwh. Counting each face once gives

S=2(l×w)+2(l×h)+2(w×h).S = 2(l \times w) + 2(l \times h) + 2(w \times h).S=2(l×w)+2(l×h)+2(w×h).

Factoring out 222 gives the shorter form S=2(lw+lh+wh)S = 2(lw + lh + wh)S=2(lw+lh+wh).

∎

For a cube, l=w=h=sl = w = h = sl=w=h=s. All six faces are squares of area s2s^2s2, so

S=6s2.S = 6 s^2.S=6s2.

Worked example 4 Surface area of a box

A box is 555 cm long, 444 cm wide, and 333 cm high. Find its surface area.

Find the area of one face of each kind, then double each because opposite faces match:

lw=5×4=20,lh=5×3=15,wh=4×3=12.lw = 5 \times 4 = 20, \qquad lh = 5 \times 3 = 15, \qquad wh = 4 \times 3 = 12.lw=5×4=20,lh=5×3=15,wh=4×3=12.

Add the three and double the total:

S=2(20+15+12)=2×47=94 cm2.S = 2(20 + 15 + 12) = 2 \times 47 = 94 \text{ cm}^2.S=2(20+15+12)=2×47=94 cm2.

It takes 949494 square centimeters of material to cover all six faces.

Surface area of a cylinder

For surface area, we use a right circular cylinder: its side runs perpendicular to its circular bases, as on a straight can. Cylinders in problems here are right circular cylinders unless stated otherwise. This condition lets the curved side unroll into a rectangle. Cut off the two circular ends and slit the side along a straight line between the bases.

The unrolled rectangle must reach all the way around the rim. Its width is therefore the circumference, not the diameter.

Why the surface area of a cylinder is 2πr2+2πrh2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h2πr2+2πrh#

Take a closed right circular cylinder with radius 333 cm and height 555 cm. Each circular end has area π×32=9π cm2\pi \times 3^2 = 9\pi \text{ cm}^2π×32=9π cm2, giving 18π cm218\pi \text{ cm}^218π cm2 for both ends. The side unrolls into a rectangle 555 cm high and 6π6\pi6π cm wide, since the rim’s circumference is 2π×32\pi \times 32π×3. Its area is 6π×5=30π cm26\pi \times 5 = 30\pi \text{ cm}^26π×5=30π cm2. The total is 18π+30π=48π cm218\pi + 30\pi = 48\pi \text{ cm}^218π+30π=48π cm2.

For any radius rrr and height hhh, the two ends contribute 2πr22\pi r^22πr2. The slit along the side becomes the rectangle’s two edges of length hhh. The circular rims straighten into its other two edges, each as long as the circumference 2πr2\pi r2πr. So the side’s area is

(2πr)(h)=2πrh.(2\pi r)(h) = 2\pi r h.(2πr)(h)=2πrh.

Adding the two ends and the side gives the total surface area:

S=2πr2+2πrh.S = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h.S=2πr2+2πrh.

The two circle areas account for the r2r^2r2 term; the side’s width times height accounts for the rhrhrh term.

∎

This is the surface area of a closed right circular cylinder. If a container has an open end, leave out that circle’s area. The rectangular-side formula does not apply to a leaning cylinder.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up units. Volume uses cubic units; surface area uses square units. Convert lengths to the same unit first. Cubic units convert by the length factor cubed, not by the length factor.
  • Adding the dimensions instead of multiplying. Volume of a box is l×w×hl \times w \times hl×w×h, not l+w+hl + w + hl+w+h. A 555 by 333 by 444 box has volume 606060, not 121212.
  • Using the diameter for a cylinder. Both V=πr2hV = \pi r^2 hV=πr2h and the surface area use the radius. Given the diameter, halve it first. Forgetting makes the volume four times too big, because the radius is squared.
  • Counting a face once instead of twice. A box has three pairs of opposite faces. Surface area is 2(lw+lh+wh)2(lw + lh + wh)2(lw+lh+wh); leaving off the factor of 222 counts only half the box.
  • Forgetting the ends of a cylinder. The full surface area is the curved side plus both circular caps: 2πrh+2πr22\pi r h + 2\pi r^22πrh+2πr2. The side alone (2πrh2\pi r h2πrh) covers a tube open at both ends, not a closed can.

Key takeaways

  • Volume counts cubic units, including parts of cubes. Convert cubic units by the length factor cubed.
  • A prism or cylinder has V=BhV = BhV=Bh, using perpendicular height. For a box, V=lwhV = lwhV=lwh; for a cube, V=s3V = s^3V=s3; for a cylinder, V=πr2hV = \pi r^2hV=πr2h.
  • Find a missing box side or prism or cylinder height by dividing by the known factors. For a cylinder’s radius, divide by πh\pi hπh and take the positive square root.
  • Surface area adds every outer surface’s area in square units. A box gives S=2(lw+lh+wh)S = 2(lw + lh + wh)S=2(lw+lh+wh); a cube gives S=6s2S = 6s^2S=6s2.
  • A closed right circular cylinder has S=2πr2+2πrhS = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rhS=2πr2+2πrh: two ends plus a rectangle whose width is the circumference and whose height is hhh.

A bit of history (optional)

Take a neat stack of coins and push it sideways until it leans. Have you changed how much metal is in the stack?

The amount stays the same: no coins were added or removed. The Italian mathematician Bonaventura Cavalieri studied a similar idea in his method of indivisibles, published in 163516351635. He treated a solid as parallel slices, comparing their areas to investigate volume. MacTutor’s biography of Cavalieri describes this work.

His name is attached to this principle: if two solids lie between the same parallel planes and every slice parallel to those planes has the same area in both, their volumes are equal. An upright stack and the same stack with coins shifted sideways illustrate the idea. Their slices match, even though their outlines differ.

A stack of paper offers the same surprise. Its edges can lean or curve as the sheets slide, yet the amount of paper stays fixed. Cavalieri’s work turned such comparisons into a way to study solids far beyond an ordinary box.

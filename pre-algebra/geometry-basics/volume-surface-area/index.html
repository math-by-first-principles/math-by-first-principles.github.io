Volume and Surface Area

Intuition

The last two lessons stayed flat. Perimeter measured the distance around a shape and area measured the space inside it, both living on a single sheet of paper. Real objects are not flat: a cereal box, a soup can, and a brick all take up space in three directions. Two new questions come up the moment a shape has thickness. The first is “how much space does it fill?”, which is its volume. Volume is the right measure for how much cereal a box holds or how much water a tank takes. The second is “how much material covers the outside?”, which is its surface area. Surface area is the right measure for how much cardboard the box needs or how much paint coats the can. Volume counts unit cubes; surface area adds up the areas of every outer surface. Both follow from ideas you already have, stacking and unfolding, so you can rebuild every formula here yourself.

Learning goals

  • Count unit cubes to measure volume, always in cubic units
  • Stack layers to get V=lwhV = lwhV=lwh for a rectangular prism
  • Multiply base area by height for any prism or cylinder
  • Unfold a net and add the area of every outer surface for surface area, always in square units
  • Read a cylinder's side as a rectangle whose width is the circumference

Volume: counting unit cubes

To measure a flat region we tiled it with unit squares. To measure a solid we do the same thing one dimension up: we fill it with unit cubes. A unit cube is a cube one unit long on every edge. A cube one centimetre on each edge has a volume of one cubic centimetre, written 1 cm31 \text{ cm}^31 cm3. The volume of a solid is the number of unit cubes it takes to fill the solid, with no gaps and no overlaps. A box with whole-number sides fills exactly, cube by cube. A solid with a curved surface or a slanted face cannot be packed with only whole cubes, but the idea is the same: volume counts how many cubic units of space the solid encloses, whole cubes plus whatever fraction of a cube is needed to finish the count.

This is why volume is always measured in cubic units. A length needs one direction and uses plain units like cm; an area needs two directions and uses square units like cm2\text{cm}^2cm2. A volume needs three directions, and the little raised 333 in cm3\text{cm}^3cm3 records exactly that. The exponent is not decoration. It counts how many directions the unit spans.

Volume of a rectangular prism

A rectangular prism has a length lll, a width www, and a height hhh, all meeting at right angles. A box and a brick are both rectangular prisms. Filling the prism with unit cubes turns into a multiplication the same way tiling a rectangle did. The one difference is that now there is an extra factor for the stacked layers.

The figure shows a box 444 long, 333 wide, and 222 high. Look at just the bottom layer of cubes. It is one cube thick and tiles the floor of the box, which is a 444 by 333 rectangle, so that single layer holds

4×3=12 cubes.4 \times 3 = 12 \text{ cubes}.4×3=12 cubes.

That count is exactly the area of the base. Now stack layers to fill the height. Each layer is a copy of the bottom one with the same 121212 cubes, and the box is 222 layers tall, so the total is

12×2=24 cubes.12 \times 2 = 24 \text{ cubes}.12×2=24 cubes.

There is the whole idea: fill one layer (that is length times width), then stack height-many copies of it (that is the times height). Multiplying all three counts every cube exactly once.

Why the volume of a rectangular prism is length times width times height#

Take a box whose length is lll units, width is www units, and height is hhh units, with lll, www, and hhh whole numbers. Fill the bottom of the box with a single layer of unit cubes. The floor is a rectangle lll long and www wide, and one cube covers one unit square of it. Therefore this bottom layer contains exactly l×wl \times wl×w cubes, one cube for each unit square of the floor.

Now stack identical layers upward until the box is full. Each layer is one unit tall and is a copy of the bottom layer, so each holds the same l×wl \times wl×w cubes. The box is hhh units tall, so it takes exactly hhh layers to reach the top, with no gaps and no overlaps.

The total number of cubes is the cubes in one layer added up once per layer:

V=(l×w)+(l×w)+⋯+(l×w)⏟h layers=(l×w)×h.V = \underbrace{(l \times w) + (l \times w) + \cdots + (l \times w)}_{h \text{ layers}} = (l \times w) \times h.V=h layers(l×w)+(l×w)+⋯+(l×w)​​=(l×w)×h.

Every unit cube sits in exactly one layer and one spot within that layer, so the product counts each cube once. That is the volume.

∎

So for a rectangular prism,

V=l×w×h.V = l \times w \times h.V=l×w×h.

A cube is the special box whose length, width, and height are all the same value sss, so its volume is

V=s×s×s=s3.V = s \times s \times s = s^3.V=s×s×s=s3.

This is exactly why s3s^3s3 is read ”sss cubed”. Raising a length to the third power is the volume of the cube built on that length. That is just what s2s^2s2 did for the area of a square. The exponent notation from chapter 6 and the geometry of a cube are the same idea seen from two sides.

Volume of any prism: base area times height

A prism is a solid with two identical flat ends (the bases) joined by straight sides. Slice it anywhere between the two bases, always parallel to them, and that slice matches the base exactly in shape and size, which is what makes it a prism. A box is a prism whose base is a rectangle. A triangular prism, the shape of a tent or a wedge, is a prism whose base is a triangle. The layer-and-stack argument never used the fact that the base was a rectangle, so it carries over word for word to any prism.

Slice the prism into thin layers parallel to its base. Each layer is a flat copy of the base, and stacking enough of them to reach the height hhh rebuilds the solid. The number of unit cubes in one unit-thick layer is just the area of the base, BBB, and there are hhh layers, so

V=B×h.V = B \times h.V=B×h.

Here hhh is the perpendicular distance between the two bases: how far apart their planes are, measured straight across rather than along a slanted edge. That measurement does not have to point straight up in the picture: for the tent below, it runs lengthwise instead.

For a box the base is a rectangle, B=l×wB = l \times wB=l×w, and this collapses back to l×w×hl \times w \times hl×w×h. For a triangular prism the base is a triangle, so B=12 b h△B = \tfrac{1}{2} \, b \, h_\triangleB=21​bh△​ using the triangle area from the previous lesson. The volume of that prism is then that base area times the distance between its two triangular ends. The single rule volume equals base area times height covers every prism at once.

Worked example 1 Volume of a triangular prism

A tent is a triangular prism. Its triangular end has a base of 666 m and a perpendicular height of 444 m, and the tent runs 101010 m long. Find its volume.

First find the area of the triangular base, BBB, using the triangle area formula:

B=12×b×h△=12×6×4=12 m2.B = \frac{1}{2} \times b \times h_\triangle = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 4 = 12 \text{ m}^2.B=21​×b×h△​=21​×6×4=12 m2.

This 101010 m is a different length from the triangle’s own height above: it is the distance between the two triangular ends, which plays the role of hhh in V=B×hV = B \times hV=B×h:

V=B×h=12×10=120 m3.V = B \times h = 12 \times 10 = 120 \text{ m}^3.V=B×h=12×10=120 m3.

So the tent encloses 120120120 cubic metres. The base area carries the units of square metres, and the extra factor of metres from the length pushes the result up to cubic metres.

Volume of a cylinder

A cylinder, the shape of a can or a pipe, is like a prism but with a circle for its base instead of a polygon. The reasoning does not change. Picture the cylinder sliced into thin circular disks, each a copy of the base circle, stacked to reach the height hhh. A circle cannot be tiled by whole unit squares the way a rectangle can, but each unit-thick disk still takes up an amount of space equal to the area of the circle, and there are hhh of these disks. So once again the cylinder’s volume is base area times height:

V=(base area)×h.V = (\text{base area}) \times h.V=(base area)×h.

The base is a circle of radius rrr, and you found its area in the last lesson: A=πr2A = \pi r^2A=πr2. Substituting that base area gives the cylinder volume:

V=πr2h.V = \pi r^2 h.V=πr2h.

Notice this is the very same “base area times height” rule, with the circle’s πr2\pi r^2πr2 playing the part the rectangle’s l×wl \times wl×w played for a box. The only new ingredient is the circle area, which you already have. This formula needs the radius, since it is rrr that gets squared: if a problem gives the diameter, halve it first.

Worked example 2 Volume of a cylinder

A can of soup is a cylinder with radius 333 cm and height 101010 cm. Find its volume. Use π≈3.14\pi \approx 3.14π≈3.14.

The base is a circle of radius 333 cm, so find its area first:

A=πr2≈3.14×32=3.14×9=28.26 cm2.A = \pi r^2 \approx 3.14 \times 3^2 = 3.14 \times 9 = 28.26 \text{ cm}^2.A=πr2≈3.14×32=3.14×9=28.26 cm2.

Now stack that base through the height of 101010 cm:

V=A×h≈28.26×10=282.6 cm3.V = A \times h \approx 28.26 \times 10 = 282.6 \text{ cm}^3.V=A×h≈28.26×10=282.6 cm3.

So the can holds about 283283283 cubic centimetres. The answer is approximate because π\piπ was rounded to 3.143.143.14. The unit is cubic centimetres because squaring the radius gave square centimetres and the height added one more factor of centimetres.

Surface area: unfolding the net

Volume measured the inside. Surface area measures the outside: it is the total area of every outer surface of a solid, flat or curved. That total is the amount of material it would take to wrap or paint the solid. Because it is a sum of areas, surface area is measured in square units, just like ordinary area, not in cubic units. Keeping that straight is the single biggest trap of this lesson, so it is worth saying once more: volume is cubic, surface area is square.

For the boxes, prisms, and cylinders in this lesson, the clean way to find surface area is to unfold the solid flat. Cut along enough edges and lay every flat piece out on a table (a cylinder’s curved side unrolls flat too, since it is curved in only one direction), and you get a flat picture called a net. The net shows every outer surface of the solid, just spread out where you can see it. So the solid’s surface area is the plain area of that net: the sum of the areas of those outer surfaces, each one a shape you already know how to measure.

For a box the net is six rectangles that come in three matching pairs, because opposite faces of a box are identical. With length lll, width www, and height hhh:

  • The top and bottom are each lll by www, area l×wl \times wl×w.
  • The front and back are each lll by hhh, area l×hl \times hl×h.
  • The two ends are each www by hhh, area w×hw \times hw×h.

Add all six. Each kind appears twice, so

S=2(l×w)+2(l×h)+2(w×h)=2(lw+lh+wh).S = 2(l \times w) + 2(l \times h) + 2(w \times h) = 2(lw + lh + wh).S=2(l×w)+2(l×h)+2(w×h)=2(lw+lh+wh).

Why the surface area of a box is 2(lw+lh+wh)2(lw + lh + wh)2(lw+lh+wh)#

A rectangular box has exactly six flat faces, and the surface area is the sum of their six areas. The faces come in three pairs of opposites. Opposite faces of a box are congruent, meaning identical in size and shape. That congruence holds because each face sits directly across from an identical copy of itself: sliding one face straight through the box lands it exactly on the other.

The top and the bottom are both rectangles of size lll by www, so together they contribute 2(l×w)2(l \times w)2(l×w). The front and the back are both lll by hhh, contributing 2(l×h)2(l \times h)2(l×h). The left and right ends are both www by hhh, contributing 2(w×h)2(w \times h)2(w×h). Every face has now been counted exactly once.

Adding the three pairs gives the total surface area:

S=2(l×w)+2(l×h)+2(w×h).S = 2(l \times w) + 2(l \times h) + 2(w \times h).S=2(l×w)+2(l×h)+2(w×h).

Factoring the common 222 out front writes it more compactly as S=2(lw+lh+wh)S = 2(lw + lh + wh)S=2(lw+lh+wh). The same logic, unfold and add up every outer surface, works for every solid in this lesson, boxes, prisms, and cylinders; only the shapes of the pieces change. A box or prism unfolds into entirely flat pieces; a cylinder’s curved side is the one exception, and it too unrolls flat instead of lying flat already, because it is curved in only one direction.

∎

A cube is the box where l=w=h=sl = w = h = sl=w=h=s, so all six faces are identical squares of area s2s^2s2, and

S=6s2.S = 6 s^2.S=6s2.

Worked example 4 Surface area of a box

A box is 555 cm long, 444 cm wide, and 333 cm high. Find its surface area.

Find the area of one face of each kind, then double each because opposite faces match:

lw=5×4=20,lh=5×3=15,wh=4×3=12.lw = 5 \times 4 = 20, \qquad lh = 5 \times 3 = 15, \qquad wh = 4 \times 3 = 12.lw=5×4=20,lh=5×3=15,wh=4×3=12.

Add the three and double the total:

S=2(20+15+12)=2×47=94 cm2.S = 2(20 + 15 + 12) = 2 \times 47 = 94 \text{ cm}^2.S=2(20+15+12)=2×47=94 cm2.

So it takes 949494 square centimetres of material to cover the box. The unit is square centimetres because surface area adds up flat face areas, even though the box itself encloses a volume measured in cubic centimetres.

Surface area of a cylinder

A cylinder unfolds too, and its net reveals the formula. Cut off the two circular ends and slit the curved side from top to bottom. The two ends are circles. The curved side, once unrolled, flattens into a rectangle, and that is the only piece that needs a second look.

Here is the key step. The curved side wraps around the circular rim. So when you unroll the side, its width is exactly the distance around that rim, which is the circumference C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr. Its height is just the height of the cylinder, hhh. So the unrolled side is a rectangle of width 2πr2\pi r2πr and height hhh, with area

(2πr)×h=2πrh.(2\pi r) \times h = 2\pi r h.(2πr)×h=2πrh.

The two circular ends each have area πr2\pi r^2πr2, contributing 2πr22 \pi r^22πr2 together. Adding the two ends and the rolled-out side gives the full surface area:

S=2πr2+2πrh.S = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h.S=2πr2+2πrh.

Why the surface area of a cylinder is 2πr2+2πrh2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h2πr2+2πrh#

A cylinder has three pieces of surface: a top, a bottom, and the curved side that joins them. The surface area is the sum of all three.

The top and the bottom are congruent circles of radius rrr. The area of a circle is πr2\pi r^2πr2, so the two ends together contribute 2πr22\pi r^22πr2.

Now picture peeling the curved side off and unrolling it onto a table. It opens out into a flat rectangle, because the cut runs straight up the side. That vertical cut edge is a straight seam of length hhh, which is the height of the cylinder. The seam becomes the left and right edges of the rectangle, so the rectangle’s height is hhh. The top rim and the bottom rim, which were circles, straighten out into the top and bottom edges of the rectangle. Each rim had length equal to the circumference of the circular end, C=2πrC = 2\pi rC=2πr, so the rectangle’s width is 2πr2\pi r2πr. A rectangle’s area is width times height, so the curved side has area

(2πr)(h)=2πrh.(2\pi r)(h) = 2\pi r h.(2πr)(h)=2πrh.

Adding the two ends and the side gives the total surface area:

S=2πr2+2πrh.S = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h.S=2πr2+2πrh.

The first term accounts for the two circular caps, and the second accounts for the wrapped-around side. This split explains why one piece carries r2r^2r2 and the other carries rhr hrh.

∎

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up volume and surface area units. Volume counts unit cubes, so it is cubic (cm3\text{cm}^3cm3); surface area sums the areas of outer surfaces, so it is square (cm2\text{cm}^2cm2). If your volume answer ends in cm2\text{cm}^2cm2, or your surface-area answer ends in cm3\text{cm}^3cm3, something is wrong.
  • Adding the dimensions instead of multiplying. Volume of a box is l×w×hl \times w \times hl×w×h, not l+w+hl + w + hl+w+h. A 555 by 333 by 444 box has volume 606060, not 121212.
  • Using the diameter for a cylinder. Both V=πr2hV = \pi r^2 hV=πr2h and the surface area use the radius. Given the diameter, halve it first. Forgetting makes the volume four times too big, because the radius is squared.
  • Counting a face once instead of twice. A box has three pairs of opposite faces. Surface area is 2(lw+lh+wh)2(lw + lh + wh)2(lw+lh+wh); leaving off the factor of 222 counts only half the box.
  • Forgetting the ends of a cylinder. The full surface area is the curved side plus both circular caps: 2πrh+2πr22\pi r h + 2\pi r^22πrh+2πr2. The side alone (2πrh2\pi r h2πrh) covers a tube open at both ends, not a closed can.

Key takeaways

  • Volume is the number of unit cubes that fill a solid, measured in cubic units (cm3\text{cm}^3cm3). Surface area is the total area of every outer surface, measured in square units (cm2\text{cm}^2cm2).
  • Rectangular prism: V=l×w×hV = l \times w \times hV=l×w×h, by filling one l×wl \times wl×w layer and stacking hhh of them. A cube has V=s3V = s^3V=s3.
  • Any prism (and a cylinder): V=(base area)×hV = (\text{base area}) \times hV=(base area)×h. For a triangular prism the base is 12b h△\tfrac{1}{2} b\, h_\triangle21​bh△​; for a cylinder the base is πr2\pi r^2πr2, so V=πr2hV = \pi r^2 hV=πr2h (use the radius).
  • Surface area from a net: unfold a box, prism, or cylinder flat and add the area of every outer surface. A box gives S=2(lw+lh+wh)S = 2(lw + lh + wh)S=2(lw+lh+wh); a cube gives S=6s2S = 6 s^2S=6s2.
  • Cylinder surface area: two circular ends plus the unrolled side, whose width is the circumference, so S=2πr2+2πrhS = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r hS=2πr2+2πrh.

A bit of history (optional)

Take a neat stack of coins and push it sideways until it leans. Have you changed how much metal is in the stack?

Of course not, because you moved the coins without adding or removing one. Cavalieri, an Italian mathematician, turned that plain fact into a rule in 163516351635.

Slice two solids with the same set of level cuts. If every cut gives equal areas in both solids, the two solids hold equal volume. The leaning stack and the upright stack match slice for slice. So they must hold the same amount of metal.

That is why a leaning box still has volume equal to base area times its perpendicular height, the straight-across distance between the two ends, never the length of a slanted edge. A slanted pile of paper holds just as much paper as a tidy one. The lean never touches a single slice.

You already used this idea without naming it. You filled the bottom layer, counted its cubes, and stacked copies of it to the top. Cavalieri only added that the copies do not have to sit square.

Graphing Proportional Relationships

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

One pencil costs 222 dollars, two cost 444, and three cost 666: buy twice as many and you pay twice as much. Distance behaves the same way when you walk at a steady 333 miles per hour. Plot hours and miles as ordered pairs on the coordinate plane from the last lesson. The points line up through the origin, and the walking rate tells you how much the line climbs for each hour across.

Learning goals

  • Test whether a table or graph is proportional, using yx\frac{y}{x}xy​ and the origin
  • Write y=kxy = kxy=kx from a table, reading kkk as the unit rate with its units
  • Plot a proportional table as ordered pairs, and read new values off the line
  • Explain why the points of y=kxy = kxy=kx lie on a straight line through the origin
  • Interpret a negative kkk, and the inputs a real situation allows

Proportional tables

Here is the walk as a table. Let xxx be the number of hours walked and yyy the number of miles covered, at a steady 333 miles per hour.

Hours walked, xxx000111222333444
Miles covered, yyy000333666999121212

For each nonzero xxx, divide yyy by xxx. You get 31=3\frac{3}{1} = 313​=3, 62=3\frac{6}{2} = 326​=3, 93=3\frac{9}{3} = 339​=3 and 124=3\frac{12}{4} = 3412​=3: every ratio is the same number. In the language of the ratios chapter, the columns are equivalent ratios of miles to hours. The ratio 6:26 : 26:2 is 3:13 : 13:1 scaled by 222, and 12:412 : 412:4 is 3:13 : 13:1 scaled by 444.

A quantity yyy is proportional to xxx when it is always the same fixed number times xxx. That number is the constant of proportionality, written kkk. For the walk, k=3k = 3k=3. In this lesson we use nonzero constants; we leave out k=0k = 0k=0, where yyy is always 000.

Since y=kxy = kxy=kx, dividing both sides by a nonzero xxx gives y÷x=ky \div x = ky÷x=k. To test a table, divide yyy by xxx wherever xxx is not zero. The ratios must all equal the same nonzero number. Check any column with x=0x = 0x=0 separately: it must give y=0y = 0y=0, because k×0=0k \times 0 = 0k×0=0. There is no ratio to calculate in that column; division by zero is undefined. If the table contains only (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), it fits every value of kkk and does not identify a rate.

The walk’s ratios stay equal because the pace is steady. If the walker stops for lunch while the clock keeps running, time increases without any extra distance, so the ratio changes.

The equation y = kx

Start with one column of the walk. There 62=3\frac{6}{2} = 326​=3, and multiplying both sides by 222 gives 6=3×26 = 3 \times 26=3×2: the miles are the rate times the hours. Every other column works the same way, since 9=3×39 = 3 \times 39=3×3 and 12=3×412 = 3 \times 412=3×4.

In general, every column with nonzero xxx has yx=k\frac{y}{x} = kxy​=k. Multiply both sides by xxx, using the balance principle from one-step equations:

yx=k⟹y=kx.\frac{y}{x} = k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = kx.xy​=k⟹y=kx.

The equation also covers the zero column, because k×0=0k \times 0 = 0k×0=0. So one equation describes the whole table. For the walk it is y=3xy = 3xy=3x.

The number kkk is the unit rate from Chapter 7. Putting x=1x = 1x=1 into the equation gives y=ky = ky=k: the output for one unit of input. Here that is 333 miles in one hour, so report the rate with its units: 333 miles per hour. In general, kkk has units of yyy per unit of xxx.

The order matters. Dividing hours by miles instead gives 13\frac{1}{3}31​ hour per mile. This is the reciprocal rate, with the units reversed.

The equation answers questions in both directions. To find yyy, put in xxx and multiply by kkk. To find xxx, put in yyy and solve kx=ykx = ykx=y by dividing both sides by kkk, which works because kkk is not 000.

Worked example 1 From a table to y=kxy = kxy=kx, then forward and backward

A pump fills an initially empty pool at a steady rate. The table gives the liters yyy in the pool after xxx minutes.

Minutes, xxx222555888
Liters, yyy303030757575120120120

Divide each yyy by its xxx:

302=755=1208=15.\frac{30}{2} = \frac{75}{5} = \frac{120}{8} = 15.230​=575​=8120​=15.

The ratios match, so the table is proportional with k=15k = 15k=15 liters per minute, and y=15xy = 15xy=15x.

Forward: after 777 minutes the pool holds y=15×7=105y = 15 \times 7 = 105y=15×7=105 liters.

Backward: to reach 300300300 liters, solve 15x=30015x = 30015x=300. Divide both sides by 151515:

x=300÷15=20.x = 300 \div 15 = 20.x=300÷15=20.

The pool reaches 300300300 liters after 202020 minutes. Check it: 15×20=30015 \times 20 = 30015×20=300.

Plotting a proportional table

Each column of a table is an ordered pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y). The walk gives (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), (1,3)(1, 3)(1,3), (2,6)(2, 6)(2,6), (3,9)(3, 9)(3,9) and (4,12)(4, 12)(4,12). Plot them with hours across and miles up.

Each step between neighboring plotted points is 111 across and 333 up. That is the unit rate drawn on the grid: one hour across and the 333 miles walked in it up.

Once you have the line, you can read values that were never in the table. Halfway between 222 and 333 hours, at x=2.5x = 2.5x=2.5, the line sits at 7.57.57.5 miles, and the equation agrees: 3×2.5=7.53 \times 2.5 = 7.53×2.5=7.5. When a reading falls between gridlines, the equation gives the exact value and the picture gives a check.

Worked example 2 Find kkk from one point on the graph

A straight line through the origin shows the cost yyy, in dollars, of xxx kilograms of rice. The line passes through the point (4,6)(4, 6)(4,6). Find the equation, and the cost of 101010 kilograms.

The origin cannot give kkk, because 00\frac{0}{0}00​ has no value. Use the other point, which says 444 kilograms cost 666 dollars:

k=64=1.5.k = \frac{6}{4} = 1.5.k=46​=1.5.

So rice costs 1.51.51.5 dollars per kilogram, and y=1.5xy = 1.5xy=1.5x. As a check, the line should pass through (1,1.5)(1, 1.5)(1,1.5), because one kilogram costs the unit rate.

For 101010 kilograms, y=1.5×10=15y = 1.5 \times 10 = 15y=1.5×10=15, so 101010 kilograms cost 151515 dollars.

Why the points line up through the origin

The same straight-line pattern holds for any constant kkk. First consider the full equation, with every number allowed as an input. A situation may keep only some of those inputs, as we will see below.

Why the points of y=kxy = kxy=kx lie on a straight line through the origin#

Take y=4xy = 4xy=4x. At x=0x = 0x=0, y=4×0=0y = 4 \times 0 = 0y=4×0=0, so its graph contains the origin. Draw the straight trip from the origin to (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4): it moves 111 across and 444 up. Repeating that trip reaches (2,8)(2, 8)(2,8), then (3,12)(3, 12)(3,12). Each copy continues in the same direction, so the path does not bend.

Scaling the whole trip keeps that direction too. Half a trip reaches (0.5,2)(0.5, 2)(0.5,2), halfway to (1,4)(1, 4)(1,4). Reversing it reaches (−1,−4)(-1, -4)(−1,−4), on the same line on the other side of the origin. For any chosen horizontal coordinate xxx, scaling both parts of the trip by xxx reaches (x,4x)(x, 4x)(x,4x). Thus every point of y=4xy = 4xy=4x lies on this line.

Nothing depended on choosing 444. For any kkk, start with the trip to (1,k)(1, k)(1,k). Scaling it by xxx reaches (x,kx)(x, kx)(x,kx) on the same straight line. A positive xxx continues in the original direction; a negative xxx reverses it, and x=0x = 0x=0 stays at the origin. If kkk is negative, the original trip goes down instead of up.

Every point on this line is reached by scaling that trip. A point whose horizontal coordinate is ttt uses a scale factor of ttt, so its vertical coordinate is ktktkt. It therefore satisfies y=kxy = kxy=kx. With every input allowed, the graph is the whole line.

∎

This also lets us recognize a proportional graph. Consider a straight line through the origin and (2,6)(2, 6)(2,6). That point gives k=62=3k = \frac{6}{2} = 3k=26​=3. The graph of y=3xy = 3xy=3x passes through both points, and only one straight line passes through two distinct points. It must be the same line.

For any straight line through the origin other than an axis, choose a second point and set k=yxk = \frac{y}{x}k=xy​. The same two-point argument shows that the line is the graph of y=kxy = kxy=kx. The vertical axis is excluded because its points all have x=0x = 0x=0, with many different yyy values. The horizontal axis has k=0k = 0k=0, which this lesson leaves out.

Steepness and the sign of k

On the same grid, a walk at 333 miles per hour gives a steeper line than one at 222 miles per hour: it climbs 333 miles instead of 222 for each hour across. In general, a line y=kxy = kxy=kx passes through (1,k)(1, k)(1,k). For positive kkk, a larger unit rate puts that point higher and makes the line steeper. Compare graphs with the same axis units and scales; changing a scale changes how steep a line looks.

The rate can also be negative. Then yyy goes down as xxx goes up. The line y=−2xy = -2xy=−2x passes through (1,−2)(1, -2)(1,−2). From the origin, each step of 111 across goes 222 down, so the line falls from left to right. The ratio yx\frac{y}{x}xy​ is still the same at every point except the origin: −21\frac{-2}{1}1−2​, −42\frac{-4}{2}2−4​ and 6−3\frac{6}{-3}−36​ all equal −2-2−2.

So the sign of kkk gives the direction, rising for a positive kkk and falling for a negative one. The size of kkk, its absolute value, gives the steepness. The lines y=2xy = 2xy=2x and y=−2xy = -2xy=−2x are equally steep, tilted opposite ways.

Worked example 3 A negative rate: the temperature falls

From noon the temperature falls at a steady 333 degrees per hour. Let xxx be the hours after noon and yyy the change in temperature since noon, in degrees. Find the equation, the change by 555 p.m., and the temperature then if it was 101010 degrees at noon.

After 111 hour the change is −3-3−3, after 222 hours it is −6-6−6, and after 444 hours it is −12-12−12. Divide each change by its hours:

−31=−62=−124=−3.\frac{-3}{1} = \frac{-6}{2} = \frac{-12}{4} = -3.1−3​=2−6​=4−12​=−3.

So k=−3k = -3k=−3 degrees per hour and y=−3xy = -3xy=−3x. Its graph falls through the origin, and for times after noon the points sit below the x-axis, in Quadrant IV.

By 555 p.m., x=5x = 5x=5, so the change is y=−3×5=−15y = -3 \times 5 = -15y=−3×5=−15 degrees. The temperature is then 10+(−15)=−510 + (-15) = -510+(−15)=−5 degrees.

The change in temperature is proportional to time: it starts at 000 and changes at a constant rate. The temperature itself is not: it is 101010 at noon, where a proportional quantity would have to be 000.

Proportional or not?

A relationship can fail to be proportional in two different ways, and each shows up on the graph.

Take a bike rental that charges a 444-dollar fee plus 222 dollars per hour. One hour costs 666 dollars, two cost 888, three cost 101010 and four cost 121212. The cost goes up by the same 222 dollars every hour, but the ratios are 666, 444, 103\frac{10}{3}310​ and 333, which do not match. On the graph the points lie on a straight line, but the line meets the y-axis at (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4), the fee, instead of at the origin.

Now take the area of a square: side 111 gives area 111, side 222 gives 444, side 333 gives 999. The ratios are 111, 222 and 333, so they do not match. Extending the area rule to side length 000 gives area 000, but the other points bend upward instead of lying on a straight line.

The graph test needs both features: its points lie on a straight line through the origin, other than either axis. Being straight is not enough, as the rental shows. Passing through the origin is not enough either, as the square’s area shows.

Two phone plans charge by the gigabyte of data used in a month.

What the situation allows

The equation y=2xy = 2xy=2x makes sense for every xxx: negatives, fractions and decimals included. Its full graph is a line through the origin. A situation’s domain is its set of allowed inputs. Its graph keeps only the points for those inputs. If 000 is excluded, the situation’s graph need not contain the origin, but its points still lie on a line through it.

Pencils at a fixed 222 dollars each, with no bulk discount, follow y=2xy = 2xy=2x. You can buy 000, 111, 222 or 333 pencils, but not 2.52.52.5 of one or −1-1−1. So the graph of the pencil situation is a row of separate dots at whole-number values of xxx. Every one of them sits on the line y=2xy = 2xy=2x.

A walk at a steady 222 miles per hour also follows y=2xy = 2xy=2x. Every elapsed time from the start to the end is allowed, including 1.51.51.5 hours if the walk lasts that long. Its graph is a solid piece of the line starting at the origin. Negative elapsed times are excluded. A 333-hour walk stops at (3,6)(3, 6)(3,6), so its domain is all times from 000 to 333 hours.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Taking equal steps for proportional. A table can rise by the same amount each time and still fail. The rental costs 6,8,10,126, 8, 10, 126,8,10,12 go up by 222, but the ratios 6,4,103,36, 4, \frac{10}{3}, 36,4,310​,3 do not match.
  • Calling every straight line proportional. The line must also pass through the origin. A line that meets the y-axis anywhere else has a starting amount, like a fee.
  • Flipping the ratio. yx\frac{y}{x}xy​ gives yyy‘s unit per unit of xxx, which is kkk. The ratio xy\frac{x}{y}yx​ is the rate read the other way: the reciprocal rate, with the units reversed.
  • Using the origin to find kkk. At (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) the ratio is 00\frac{0}{0}00​, which has no value. Use any other point on the graph.
  • Ignoring what the situation allows. Pencil dots joined into a solid line suggest 2.52.52.5 pencils can be bought, and a walk extended left suggests negative hours.

Key takeaways

  • A proportional relationship follows y=kxy = kxy=kx. Here kkk is nonzero, and equals yx\frac{y}{x}xy​ wherever xxx is not 000. If 000 is an allowed input, its output must be 000.
  • kkk is the unit rate: the value of yyy when x=1x = 1x=1, in units of yyy per unit of xxx.
  • Scaling the trip from (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) to (1,k)(1, k)(1,k) shows why the full graph of y=kxy = kxy=kx is a straight line through the origin.
  • The sign of kkk sets the direction, rising or falling from left to right, and its size sets the steepness when axis units and scales stay the same.
  • Test a table by its ratios, and a graph by whether its points lie on a straight line through the origin, excluding the axes here. The domain decides which points belong to a situation.

A bit of history (optional)

How do you draw something that keeps changing, like a speed?

In the fourteenth century, Nicole Oresme, a scholar working in Paris, explored that question with pictures. He drew a horizontal line to stand for time. At each moment along it he drew an upright line whose height showed the speed. A steady speed gave uprights of equal height, so their tops made a flat edge.

Then he pictured a speed that starts at nothing and grows by the same amount in every equal stretch of time. The first upright had no height at all. Each one after it rose above the last by the same step. Their tops formed a straight, slanted edge, rising from the very start of the time line.

Oresme called these dimensions longitude and latitude. His pictures were not yet the modern coordinate plane with algebraic equations, but they let him study changing quantities through geometric shapes. This was centuries before Descartes developed analytic geometry, connecting algebra and geometry. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes Oresme’s diagrams and their role in this history.

Points, Lines, and Angles

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Look at the corner of this page, a wheelchair ramp, or a pair of clock hands. Each has an opening between two directions. Geometry gives us a way to name and measure that opening. We start with points and straight lines, then use them to describe angles and find missing angle measures.

Learning goals

  • Name a point, line, segment and ray, tell their endpoints, and identify collinear points
  • Read the middle letter of a three-letter angle name as the vertex
  • Classify an angle as acute, right, obtuse, straight or reflex
  • Find a complement from 90∘90^\circ90∘ and a supplement from 180∘180^\circ180∘
  • Show why vertical angles are equal, and why a linear pair is supplementary
  • Distinguish parallel from perpendicular lines, and apply the three transversal-angle relationships when the crossed lines are parallel

Points, lines, segments, and rays

The drawings below distinguish four objects by where they start and stop.

  • A point marks a single location and has no size. We draw it as a dot and name it with a capital letter, like point AAA.
  • A line is perfectly straight and runs forever in both directions. The line through two points AAA and BBB is written AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}AB. A line has no endpoints.
  • A line segment is the straight piece between two points, including both ends. The segment from AAA to BBB is written AB‾\overline{AB}AB. A segment has exactly two endpoints.
  • A ray starts at one point and runs forever in one direction, like a beam of light from a flashlight. The ray starting at AAA and passing through BBB is written AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB. A ray has exactly one endpoint, and we always name that endpoint first.

The arrowheads in a drawing carry the meaning: an arrow says “keeps going this way,” while a plain endpoint says “stops here.”

A segment has a measurable length: the distance between its endpoints. A line and a ray extend forever, so neither has a finite length.

Which notation represents the ray that starts at point AAA and passes through point BBB?

Answer choices

The ray starting at AAA and passing through BBB is written AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB. (AB‾\overline{AB}AB is the segment between them, AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}AB is the full line, and BA→\overrightarrow{BA}BA would start at BBB instead.)

Planes, collinear points, intersecting lines

A plane is a flat surface extending forever in every direction, like an endless sheet of paper. We draw our points and lines on a plane.

  • Points that all lie on one straight line are collinear. Any two distinct points lie on a line. Three points may lie on one line, or one may be off the line through the other two.
  • Two different lines that cross are intersecting lines. They share exactly one point, because two straight lines cannot cross twice.

Check your understanding

Points DDD, EEE, and FFF all lie on the same straight line. What are they called?

Answer choices

Collinear means lying on one straight line. The points need not share a location, and a segment has only two endpoints.

Angles: two rays from a common vertex

When two rays start from the same endpoint, the opening between them is an angle. The shared endpoint is the vertex, and the two rays are the sides (or arms) of the angle.

We name an angle with the ∠\angle∠ symbol. Using three letters, the middle letter is always the vertex. The angle below can be written ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC or ∠CBA\angle CBA∠CBA, and either way the vertex is BBB. When only one angle sits at a vertex, we may name it by that single vertex letter, like ∠B\angle B∠B.

Check your understanding

In ∠PQR\angle PQR∠PQR, which point is the vertex?

Answer choices

In a three-letter angle name, the middle letter is always the vertex.

In ∠PQR\angle PQR∠PQR, the middle letter is QQQ, so QQQ is the shared endpoint of the two rays.

An angle’s size measures the turn from one side to the other. Drawing longer sides does not change that turn. We measure angles in degrees, written with a small raised circle, as in 90∘90^\circ90∘.

Measuring angles in degrees

Imagine standing at the vertex and rotating one ray until it lands on the other. A full turn, all the way around back to the start, is defined as 360∘360^\circ360∘. Everything else is a fraction of that full turn:

full turn=360∘,half turn=180∘,quarter turn=90∘.\text{full turn} = 360^\circ, \qquad \text{half turn} = 180^\circ, \qquad \text{quarter turn} = 90^\circ.full turn=360∘,half turn=180∘,quarter turn=90∘.

A half turn (180∘180^\circ180∘) leaves the ray pointing in exactly the opposite direction, forming a straight line. A quarter turn (90∘90^\circ90∘) makes a square corner. These two landmarks help us classify angles.

Types of angles

Compare an angle with a quarter turn (90∘90^\circ90∘) and a half turn (180∘180^\circ180∘):

  • An acute angle is greater than 0∘0^\circ0∘ and less than 90∘90^\circ90∘ (a narrow opening).
  • A right angle is exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘ (a square corner). We mark it with a small square instead of an arc.
  • An obtuse angle is greater than 90∘90^\circ90∘ and less than 180∘180^\circ180∘ (a wide opening).
  • A straight angle is exactly 180∘180^\circ180∘: the two sides point in opposite directions and form a straight line.

A reflex angle is greater than 180∘180^\circ180∘ and less than 360∘360^\circ360∘: more than half a turn but less than a full turn. For an opening between 0∘0^\circ0∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘, the reflex angle on the other side completes the full turn. For example, a 110∘110^\circ110∘ opening leaves a reflex angle of 360∘−110∘=250∘360^\circ - 110^\circ = 250^\circ360∘−110∘=250∘.

Check your understanding

An angle measures 105∘105^\circ105∘. Which type is it?

Answer choices

An obtuse angle measures between 90∘90^\circ90∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Since 90∘<105∘<180∘90^\circ < 105^\circ < 180^\circ90∘<105∘<180∘, the angle is obtuse. (Acute is greater than 0∘0^\circ0∘ and less than 90∘90^\circ90∘, right is exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘, straight is exactly 180∘180^\circ180∘.)

Check your understanding

An angle measures 225∘225^\circ225∘. Which type is it?

Answer choices

A reflex angle is greater than 180∘180^\circ180∘ and less than 360∘360^\circ360∘. Since 180∘<225∘<360∘180^\circ < 225^\circ < 360^\circ180∘<225∘<360∘, this angle is reflex. An obtuse angle is less than 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Complementary and supplementary angles

The angles 30∘30^\circ30∘ and 60∘60^\circ60∘ add to a right angle, since 30∘+60∘=90∘30^\circ + 60^\circ = 90^\circ30∘+60∘=90∘. Two angles with this total are complementary. Each is the other’s complement. If one angle is xxx, subtract it from the total to find the other:

90∘−x.90^\circ - x.90∘−x.

We use angles with positive measures here. A complement therefore exists only when xxx is less than 90∘90^\circ90∘; otherwise 90∘−x90^\circ - x90∘−x is zero or negative.

The angles 110∘110^\circ110∘ and 70∘70^\circ70∘ add to a straight angle: 110∘+70∘=180∘110^\circ + 70^\circ = 180^\circ110∘+70∘=180∘. Two angles with this total are supplementary, and each is the other’s supplement. For an angle xxx, the supplement is

180∘−x.180^\circ - x.180∘−x.

A supplement therefore exists only when xxx is less than 180∘180^\circ180∘; otherwise 180∘−x180^\circ - x180∘−x is zero or negative.

These pairs do not have to touch. The words describe the sum of their measures, wherever the angles are drawn.

Memory tip

C in complementary comes before S in supplementary, just as 909090 comes before 180180180. Complementary goes with the smaller total, 90∘90^\circ90∘.

Worked example 1 Find the complement and the supplement of 37∘37^\circ37∘

For the complement, subtract from 90∘90^\circ90∘:

90∘−37∘=53∘.90^\circ - 37^\circ = 53^\circ.90∘−37∘=53∘.

Check: 37∘+53∘=90∘37^\circ + 53^\circ = 90^\circ37∘+53∘=90∘.

For the supplement, subtract from 180∘180^\circ180∘:

180∘−37∘=143∘.180^\circ - 37^\circ = 143^\circ.180∘−37∘=143∘.

Check: 37∘+143∘=180∘37^\circ + 143^\circ = 180^\circ37∘+143∘=180∘.

The supplement is larger because we subtract the same angle from a larger total.

90∘−65∘=25∘90^\circ - 65^\circ = 25^\circ90∘−65∘=25∘

180∘−40∘=140∘180^\circ - 40^\circ = 140^\circ180∘−40∘=140∘

(The complement, which adds to 90∘90^\circ90∘, would be 50∘50^\circ50∘ instead.)

Adjacent angles and linear pairs

Two angles are adjacent when they share a vertex and a side but do not overlap. The 30∘30^\circ30∘ and 60∘60^\circ60∘ angles shown above are adjacent, as are the 110∘110^\circ110∘ and 70∘70^\circ70∘ angles in the supplementary pair.

A special case is the linear pair: two adjacent angles whose outer sides point in opposite directions, together forming a straight line. Because a straight line is a straight angle of 180∘180^\circ180∘, the two angles of a linear pair must add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. Thus a linear pair is always supplementary.

Check your understanding

Why must the two angles in a linear pair always add to 180∘180^\circ180∘?

Answer choices

A linear pair's outer sides point in opposite directions, so the two angles fill a straight angle of 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Adjacent angles need not add to 180∘180^\circ180∘ unless their outer sides form a straight line.

Worked example 2 Find xxx from a linear pair

Two angles form a linear pair (their outer sides make a straight line). One measures xxx and the other measures 124∘124^\circ124∘. Find xxx.

A linear pair is supplementary, so the two angles add to 180∘180^\circ180∘:

x+124∘=180∘.x + 124^\circ = 180^\circ.x+124∘=180∘.

Subtract 124∘124^\circ124∘ from both sides to isolate xxx:

x=180∘−124∘=56∘.x = 180^\circ - 124^\circ = 56^\circ.x=180∘−124∘=56∘.

Check: 56∘+124∘=180∘56^\circ + 124^\circ = 180^\circ56∘+124∘=180∘, as required for a linear pair.

Vertical angles are equal

When two straight lines cross, they make four angles. The angles directly across from each other, sharing only the vertex, are vertical angles. Vertical angles are always equal.

In the picture, the two angles labeled aaa form one vertical pair, and the two labeled bbb form the other.

Why vertical angles are equal#

Number the angles around the crossing point as shown. Angles 111 and 333 are opposite, as are angles 222 and 444.

Suppose angle 222 is 47∘47^\circ47∘. Angles 111 and 333 each form a linear pair with it, so each is 180∘−47∘=133∘180^\circ - 47^\circ = 133^\circ180∘−47∘=133∘. They are equal because we subtracted the same angle from 180∘180^\circ180∘ twice.

That reasoning does not depend on choosing 47∘47^\circ47∘. For any crossing, angles 111 and 222 form a linear pair:

∠1+∠2=180∘.\angle 1 + \angle 2 = 180^\circ.∠1+∠2=180∘.

Angles 222 and 333 form a linear pair on the other line:

∠2+∠3=180∘.\angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^\circ.∠2+∠3=180∘.

Both sums equal 180∘180^\circ180∘, so they equal each other:

∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3.\angle 1 + \angle 2 = \angle 2 + \angle 3.∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3.

Subtract the shared angle ∠2\angle 2∠2 from both sides:

∠1=∠3.\angle 1 = \angle 3.∠1=∠3.

Angles 222 and 444 each form a linear pair with angle 111. Subtracting angle 111 from 180∘180^\circ180∘ gives both their measures, so ∠2=∠4\angle 2 = \angle 4∠2=∠4 as well.

∎

Drag point AAA or point BBB to turn either line, and watch angles 111 and 333: however you turn the lines, they stay equal, and so do angles 222 and 444.

Check your understanding

In the proof above, ∠1+∠2=180∘\angle 1 + \angle 2 = 180^\circ∠1+∠2=180∘ and ∠2+∠3=180∘\angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^\circ∠2+∠3=180∘ because each pair is a linear pair. What step turns these two equations into ∠1=∠3\angle 1 = \angle 3∠1=∠3?

Answer choices

Both sums equal 180∘180^\circ180∘, so ∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3\angle 1 + \angle 2 = \angle 2 + \angle 3∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3.

Subtracting the shared angle ∠2\angle 2∠2 from both sides leaves ∠1=∠3\angle 1 = \angle 3∠1=∠3.

Worked example 3 Use vertical angles to find xxx

Two straight lines cross. One of the four angles measures 3x+103x + 103x+10 degrees, and the angle directly across from it (its vertical angle) measures 70∘70^\circ70∘. Find xxx.

Vertical angles are equal, so set the two expressions equal:

3x+10=70.3x + 10 = 70.3x+10=70.

Subtract 101010 from both sides:

3x=60.3x = 60.3x=60.

Divide both sides by 333:

x=20.x = 20.x=20.

Check: 3(20)+10=703(20) + 10 = 703(20)+10=70, which matches the vertical angle.

Answer choices

So it is also 65∘65^\circ65∘. (The angle next to it, a linear pair, would be 115∘115^\circ115∘.)

Angles around a point

If several angles share a vertex and together sweep all the way around it with no gaps or overlaps, they make one full turn. So angles around a point add to 360∘360^\circ360∘. For instance, three angles filling a full turn without overlap, measuring 120∘120^\circ120∘, 150∘150^\circ150∘, and an unknown xxx, must satisfy

120∘+150∘+x=360∘,120^\circ + 150^\circ + x = 360^\circ,120∘+150∘+x=360∘,

which gives x=90∘x = 90^\circx=90∘. Splitting the full turn into pieces does not change its total.

Check your understanding

Three angles fill a full turn with no gaps or overlaps. They measure 100∘100^\circ100∘, 140∘140^\circ140∘, and x∘x^\circx∘. What is xxx?

Answer choices

The three angles fill a full turn, so 100∘+140∘+x=360∘100^\circ + 140^\circ + x = 360^\circ100∘+140∘+x=360∘. Thus x=360∘−240∘=120∘x = 360^\circ - 240^\circ = 120^\circx=360∘−240∘=120∘.

220∘220^\circ220∘ subtracts only the 140∘140^\circ140∘ angle, while 240∘240^\circ240∘ is the known angles' total rather than the missing angle.

Parallel and perpendicular lines

Two lines in the same plane that never meet, staying the same distance apart forever, are parallel. Think of the two rails of a straight train track. We write ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m to say line ℓ\ellℓ is parallel to line mmm.

Two lines that cross to form a right angle are perpendicular, written ℓ⊥m\ell \perp mℓ⊥m. The opposite angle is also 90∘90^\circ90∘ because vertical angles are equal. Each neighbor is 180∘−90∘=90∘180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ180∘−90∘=90∘ because it forms a linear pair with the right angle. All four angles are therefore right angles; marking one with a small square is enough. The lines in a plus sign are an example.

Check your understanding

Line ℓ\ellℓ crosses line mmm and forms a 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle at the crossing. What must be true of ℓ\ellℓ and mmm?

Answer choices

Two lines that cross to form a right angle are perpendicular, written ℓ⊥m\ell \perp mℓ⊥m.

Parallel lines never cross at all, so ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m cannot be true here.

Parallel lines and a transversal

A transversal is a line that crosses two or more other lines at distinct points. When it crosses two parallel lines, we can relate angles at one crossing to angles at the other. Each of the next three rules requires parallel lines.

Corresponding angles occupy matching positions at the two crossings, such as the upper-right angles labeled xxx below. Suppose the lower one measures 72∘72^\circ72∘. Slide that crossing along the transversal to the upper crossing, without turning it. The parallel lines run in the same direction, so the lower line lands on the upper one. The transversal slides along itself. The 72∘72^\circ72∘ angle fits exactly onto its matching angle, so the upper one also measures 72∘72^\circ72∘.

The slide works for any angle size. It carries each angle onto the angle in the same position, so corresponding angles are equal.

Check your understanding

Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. A corresponding angle at the upper crossing measures 58∘58^\circ58∘. What is the matching corresponding angle at the lower crossing?

Answer choices

Corresponding angles formed when a transversal crosses two parallel lines are equal.

So the matching angle at the lower crossing is also 58∘58^\circ58∘. (122∘122^\circ122∘ is its supplement, not its corresponding angle.)

Alternate interior angles lie between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal. Start with a 72∘72^\circ72∘ interior angle. Its corresponding angle at the other crossing is 72∘72^\circ72∘. The angle vertically opposite that corresponding angle is the alternate interior partner, so it is also 72∘72^\circ72∘.

This uses two rules that hold for any angle size: corresponding angles are equal, and vertical angles are equal. Both interior angles equal the same angle, so alternate interior angles are equal to each other.

Check your understanding

Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. An alternate interior angle at one crossing measures 118∘118^\circ118∘. What is its alternate interior partner at the other crossing?

Answer choices

Alternate interior angles formed when a transversal crosses two parallel lines are equal, so the partner is also 118∘118^\circ118∘.

62∘62^\circ62∘ is the supplement of 118∘118^\circ118∘, but this pair is equal rather than supplementary.

Co-interior angles, also called same-side interior angles, lie between the parallel lines on the same side of the transversal. Suppose one is 72∘72^\circ72∘. Its neighboring interior angle forms a linear pair with it, so the neighbor is 180∘−72∘=108∘180^\circ - 72^\circ = 108^\circ180∘−72∘=108∘. That neighbor’s alternate interior partner is the co-interior angle at the other crossing. It is also 108∘108^\circ108∘, giving a co-interior total of 72∘+108∘=180∘72^\circ + 108^\circ = 180^\circ72∘+108∘=180∘.

The same reasoning works for any starting angle. A co-interior angle and its neighboring interior angle form a linear pair. Replacing the neighbor with its equal alternate interior partner keeps the total at 180∘180^\circ180∘. Co-interior angles are therefore supplementary.

Check your understanding

Two lines are marked parallel and cut by a transversal. A co-interior angle between them measures 105∘105^\circ105∘. What is its co-interior partner, also between the lines?

Answer choices

Co-interior angles lie between two parallel lines on the same side of the transversal and are supplementary, adding to 180∘180^\circ180∘. So the partner is 180∘−105∘=75∘180^\circ - 105^\circ = 75^\circ180∘−105∘=75∘.

105∘105^\circ105∘ would be right only if the pair were corresponding or alternate interior instead, since those pairs are equal, not supplementary. The rule needs the two crossed lines to be marked parallel; without that, none of these relationships is guaranteed.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Swapping complementary and supplementary. Complementary adds to 90∘90^\circ90∘; supplementary adds to 180∘180^\circ180∘. The complement of 40∘40^\circ40∘ is 50∘50^\circ50∘, but its supplement is 140∘140^\circ140∘.
  • Thinking a straight angle is 360∘360^\circ360∘. A straight angle is a half turn, 180∘180^\circ180∘. A full turn (all the way around) is 360∘360^\circ360∘.
  • Looking for the complement of a large angle. Only angles less than 90∘90^\circ90∘ have a complement. There is no complement of 120∘120^\circ120∘, since 90∘−120∘90^\circ - 120^\circ90∘−120∘ is negative.
  • Assuming any two equal-looking angles are vertical. Vertical angles must be the opposite pair formed by two crossing straight lines. Two angles that merely look the same size, but are not across a single crossing point, are not a vertical pair. Their appearance alone does not establish equality.
  • Confusing adjacent with vertical. Adjacent angles touch (share a side); vertical angles are across from each other and share only the vertex.

Key takeaways

  • A point marks a location. A line has no endpoints, a ray has one, and a segment has two. Name a ray from its endpoint.
  • An angle measures a turn between two rays. In a three-letter name, the middle letter is the vertex. A right angle is 90∘90^\circ90∘, a straight angle is 180∘180^\circ180∘, and a full turn is 360∘360^\circ360∘.
  • Complementary angles total 90∘90^\circ90∘; supplementary angles total 180∘180^\circ180∘. A linear pair is supplementary, vertical angles are equal, and angles filling a full turn without gaps or overlaps total 360∘360^\circ360∘.
  • Parallel lines lie in the same plane and never meet. Perpendicular lines meet at four right angles.
  • When a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding and alternate interior angles are equal, while co-interior angles are supplementary. These three rules require the parallel condition.

A bit of history (optional)

How much do you have to take on trust?

Around 300300300 BCE, Euclid taught in Alexandria, Egypt. His Elements offered a way to organize mathematical knowledge: begin with a few assumptions, then build proofs that use those assumptions and earlier results. He drew on work by earlier mathematicians, arranging it into a connected account.

Some starting assumptions sound simple: you can draw a straight line between two points, and all right angles are equal. The assumption about parallel lines was more troublesome. For centuries, mathematicians tried to prove it from the others. That effort eventually led to geometries that use different assumptions about parallel lines.

The Elements filled thirteen books and remained central to mathematics teaching for about two thousand years. Its lasting achievement was the way it organized the reasoning, allowing readers to follow how one result supported the next. MacTutor’s biography describes this history.

One early result is the equality of vertical angles. Euclid’s argument uses the same idea as ours: remove a shared angle from two equal totals, each a straight angle. He described those totals as two right angles. Read Proposition 15.

Points, Lines, and Angles

Intuition

Geometry is built from the simplest possible ingredients. Start with a single point, a dot marking a position. Let points line up and you get a line. Two rays meeting at a corner open up an angle, the amount of turn between them. That is nearly the whole alphabet of shapes. Look at the corner of this page, the slope of a wheelchair ramp, or the spread of a pair of clock hands. Once you can name and measure these pieces, the rest of geometry is just putting them together. This lesson gives you that vocabulary and the handful of angle facts you will reuse in every geometry lesson that follows.

Learning goals

  • Name a point, line, segment and ray, tell their endpoints, and identify collinear points
  • Read the middle letter of a three-letter angle name as the vertex
  • Classify an angle as acute, right, obtuse, straight or reflex
  • Find a complement from 90∘90^\circ90∘ and a supplement from 180∘180^\circ180∘
  • Show why vertical angles are equal, and why a linear pair is supplementary
  • Distinguish parallel from perpendicular lines, and apply the three transversal-angle relationships when the crossed lines are parallel

Points, lines, segments, and rays

Four objects do almost all the work in basic geometry. They differ by one simple question: how far do they extend?

  • A point marks a single location and has no size at all. We draw it as a dot and name it with a capital letter, like point AAA.
  • A line is perfectly straight and runs forever in both directions. The line through two points AAA and BBB is written AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}AB. A line has no endpoints.
  • A line segment is the straight piece between two points, including both ends. The segment from AAA to BBB is written AB‾\overline{AB}AB. A segment has exactly two endpoints.
  • A ray starts at one point and runs forever in one direction, like a beam of light from a flashlight. The ray starting at AAA and passing through BBB is written AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB. A ray has exactly one endpoint, and we always name that endpoint first.

The arrowheads in a drawing carry the meaning: an arrow says “keeps going this way,” while a plain endpoint says “stops here.”

So the count of endpoints tells the three straight objects apart: a line has 000, a ray has 111, and a segment has 222. The length of a segment is a real number we can measure (the distance between its two ends). A line and a ray, by contrast, run forever, so neither has a length.

So it has exactly 222 endpoints. (A line has 000, and a ray has 111.)

Answer choices

The ray starting at AAA and passing through BBB is written AB→\overrightarrow{AB}AB. (AB‾\overline{AB}AB is the segment between them, AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}AB is the full line, and BA→\overrightarrow{BA}BA would start at BBB instead.)

Planes, collinear points, intersecting lines

Points and lines live on a flat surface called a plane. You can picture a plane as an endless sheet of paper that extends forever in every direction. A few more words name common situations on a plane:

  • Points that all lie on one straight line are collinear. Any two points are always collinear, since a single line passes through them. Three points, however, may or may not be collinear: pick three dots at random and they usually are not.
  • Two different lines that cross share exactly one point; they are intersecting lines. The shared point is the only place they meet, because two straight lines cannot cross twice.

We will meet two more relationships between lines, parallel and perpendicular, after we have angles to describe them with.

Check your understanding

Points DDD, EEE, and FFF all lie on the same straight line. What are they called?

Answer choices

Points that all lie on one straight line are collinear.

(Intersecting describes two lines that cross, and perpendicular and adjacent describe angles or lines meeting at a corner, not a set of points.)

Angles: two rays from a common vertex

When two rays start from the same endpoint, the opening between them is an angle. The shared endpoint is the vertex, and the two rays are the sides (or arms) of the angle.

We name an angle with the ∠\angle∠ symbol. Using three letters, the middle letter is always the vertex. The angle below can be written ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC or ∠CBA\angle CBA∠CBA, and either way the vertex is BBB. When only one angle sits at a vertex, we may name it by that single vertex letter, like ∠B\angle B∠B.

Check your understanding

In ∠PQR\angle PQR∠PQR, which point is the vertex?

Answer choices

In a three-letter angle name, the middle letter is always the vertex.

In ∠PQR\angle PQR∠PQR, the middle letter is QQQ, so QQQ is the shared endpoint of the two rays.

The size of an angle measures how far one side is turned from the other. It does not depend on how long you draw the sides. Stretching the arms longer does not change the angle, because the amount of turn between them is the same. Angle size is measured in degrees, written with a small raised circle, like 90∘90^\circ90∘.

Measuring angles in degrees

Imagine standing at the vertex and rotating one ray until it lands on the other. A full turn, all the way around back to the start, is defined as 360∘360^\circ360∘. Everything else is a fraction of that full turn:

full turn=360∘,half turn=180∘,quarter turn=90∘.\text{full turn} = 360^\circ, \qquad \text{half turn} = 180^\circ, \qquad \text{quarter turn} = 90^\circ.full turn=360∘,half turn=180∘,quarter turn=90∘.

A half turn (180∘180^\circ180∘) leaves the ray pointing in exactly the opposite direction, forming a straight line. A quarter turn (90∘90^\circ90∘) makes a square corner, called a right angle. These two landmarks, 90∘90^\circ90∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘, are worth memorizing, because every angle type is described relative to them.

The four basic types of angles

We sort angles by how their measure compares to the 90∘90^\circ90∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘ landmarks. Four basic types cover every opening up to a straight line:

  • An acute angle is greater than 0∘0^\circ0∘ and less than 90∘90^\circ90∘ (a narrow opening).
  • A right angle is exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘ (a square corner). We mark it with a small square instead of an arc.
  • An obtuse angle is between 90∘90^\circ90∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘ (a wide opening).
  • A straight angle is exactly 180∘180^\circ180∘: the two sides point in opposite directions and form a straight line.

A fifth type covers everything past a straight line. An angle larger than 180∘180^\circ180∘ but less than 360∘360^\circ360∘ is called a reflex angle. A reflex angle is the “outside” opening you get when you turn more than halfway around but not the whole way. For any opening below 180∘180^\circ180∘, the reflex angle on the other side is whatever is left of the full turn. The opening and its reflex angle therefore add to 360∘360^\circ360∘. For example, the reflex angle that goes with a 110∘110^\circ110∘ opening is 360∘−110∘=250∘360^\circ - 110^\circ = 250^\circ360∘−110∘=250∘.

Answer choices

An obtuse angle measures between 90∘90^\circ90∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Since 90∘<105∘<180∘90^\circ < 105^\circ < 180^\circ90∘<105∘<180∘, the angle is obtuse. (Acute is greater than 0∘0^\circ0∘ and less than 90∘90^\circ90∘, right is exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘, straight is exactly 180∘180^\circ180∘.)

Complementary and supplementary angles

Two angles are often described by what they add up to.

Two angles are complementary when their measures add to 90∘90^\circ90∘. If one of them is xxx, the other (its complement) is

90∘−x.90^\circ - x.90∘−x.

A complement only exists when xxx is less than 90∘90^\circ90∘, since otherwise 90∘−x90^\circ - x90∘−x would be zero or negative. For example, the complement of 30∘30^\circ30∘ is 60∘60^\circ60∘, because 30∘+60∘=90∘30^\circ + 60^\circ = 90^\circ30∘+60∘=90∘.

Two angles are supplementary when their measures add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. If one is xxx, the other (its supplement) is

180∘−x.180^\circ - x.180∘−x.

A supplement only exists when xxx is less than 180∘180^\circ180∘, since otherwise 180∘−x180^\circ - x180∘−x would be zero or negative. For example, the supplement of 110∘110^\circ110∘ is 70∘70^\circ70∘, because 110∘+70∘=180∘110^\circ + 70^\circ = 180^\circ110∘+70∘=180∘.

The two angles do not have to be drawn touching for the words to apply. Any two angles whose measures happen to add to 90∘90^\circ90∘ are complementary, and any two that add to 180∘180^\circ180∘ are supplementary, wherever they sit.

Memory tip

To keep the two words straight, line them up with the alphabet: Complementary comes before Supplementary, just as 909090 comes before 180180180. So Complementary goes with the smaller number, 909090.

Worked example 1 Find the complement and the supplement of 37∘37^\circ37∘

The complement is whatever adds to 90∘90^\circ90∘, and the supplement is whatever adds to 180∘180^\circ180∘. We just subtract.

For the complement, subtract from 90∘90^\circ90∘:

90∘−37∘=53∘.90^\circ - 37^\circ = 53^\circ.90∘−37∘=53∘.

Check: 37∘+53∘=90∘37^\circ + 53^\circ = 90^\circ37∘+53∘=90∘. Good.

For the supplement, subtract from 180∘180^\circ180∘:

180∘−37∘=143∘.180^\circ - 37^\circ = 143^\circ.180∘−37∘=143∘.

Check: 37∘+143∘=180∘37^\circ + 143^\circ = 180^\circ37∘+143∘=180∘. Good.

So the complement of 37∘37^\circ37∘ is 53∘53^\circ53∘, and the supplement is 143∘143^\circ143∘. Notice the supplement is the larger of the two, because 180180180 is larger than 909090.

Adjacent angles and linear pairs

Two angles are adjacent when they share a vertex and a side but do not overlap. Adjacent angles sit right next to each other, like two slices of the same pie.

A special case is the linear pair: two adjacent angles whose outer sides point in opposite directions, together forming a straight line. Because a straight line is a straight angle of 180∘180^\circ180∘, the two angles of a linear pair must add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. In other words, a linear pair is always supplementary. This single fact is the key to the next result.

Check your understanding

Why must the two angles in a linear pair always add to 180∘180^\circ180∘?

Answer choices

A linear pair's outer sides point in opposite directions, so together the pair forms a straight line, and a straight line is a straight angle of 180∘180^\circ180∘.

That is the whole reason, not a coincidence: any two adjacent angles whose outer sides make a straight line must add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. (Adjacent angles in general do not have to add to 180∘180^\circ180∘; only a linear pair does.)

Worked example 2 Find xxx from a linear pair

Two angles form a linear pair (their outer sides make a straight line). One measures xxx and the other measures 124∘124^\circ124∘. Find xxx.

A linear pair is supplementary, so the two angles add to 180∘180^\circ180∘:

x+124∘=180∘.x + 124^\circ = 180^\circ.x+124∘=180∘.

Subtract 124∘124^\circ124∘ from both sides to isolate xxx:

x=180∘−124∘=56∘.x = 180^\circ - 124^\circ = 56^\circ.x=180∘−124∘=56∘.

So x=56∘x = 56^\circx=56∘. As a check, 56∘+124∘=180∘56^\circ + 124^\circ = 180^\circ56∘+124∘=180∘, which is a straight line, exactly as a linear pair should be.

Vertical angles are equal

When two straight lines cross, they make four angles at the crossing point. The angles directly across from each other (sharing only the vertex, not a side) are called vertical angles, and they come in two pairs. The headline fact is that vertical angles are always equal.

In the picture, the two angles labelled aaa are a vertical pair and are equal. The two angles labelled bbb are the other vertical pair, and they are equal as well.

Why must this be true? It follows directly from linear pairs.

Why vertical angles are equal#

Number the four angles 1,2,3,41, 2, 3, 41,2,3,4 going around the crossing point, as in the figure below. There, angle 111 is on top, angle 222 on the right, angle 333 on the bottom, and angle 444 on the left. The vertical (opposite) pairs are then 111 and 333, and 222 and 444.

Angle 111 and angle 222 sit next to each other on one straight line, so they are a linear pair and therefore add to 180∘180^\circ180∘:

∠1+∠2=180∘.\angle 1 + \angle 2 = 180^\circ.∠1+∠2=180∘.

Angle 222 and angle 333 sit next to each other on the other straight line, so they are a linear pair as well:

∠2+∠3=180∘.\angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^\circ.∠2+∠3=180∘.

Both sums equal 180∘180^\circ180∘, so they equal each other:

∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3.\angle 1 + \angle 2 = \angle 2 + \angle 3.∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3.

Subtract the shared angle ∠2\angle 2∠2 from both sides:

∠1=∠3.\angle 1 = \angle 3.∠1=∠3.

So the opposite angles ∠1\angle 1∠1 and ∠3\angle 3∠3 are equal, which is exactly the vertical pair on top and bottom. Running the same argument on angles 222 and 444 (each is a linear pair with angle 111 or angle 333) gives ∠2=∠4\angle 2 = \angle 4∠2=∠4. Every pair of vertical angles is equal.

∎

In the proof above, ∠1+∠2=180∘\angle 1 + \angle 2 = 180^\circ∠1+∠2=180∘ and ∠2+∠3=180∘\angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^\circ∠2+∠3=180∘ because each pair is a linear pair. What step turns these two equations into ∠1=∠3\angle 1 = \angle 3∠1=∠3?

Answer choices

Both sums equal 180∘180^\circ180∘, so ∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3\angle 1 + \angle 2 = \angle 2 + \angle 3∠1+∠2=∠2+∠3.

Subtracting the shared angle ∠2\angle 2∠2 from both sides leaves ∠1=∠3\angle 1 = \angle 3∠1=∠3. This is exactly why vertical angles are equal: each rests on a linear pair adding to 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Worked example 3 Use vertical angles to find xxx

Two straight lines cross. One of the four angles measures 3x+103x + 103x+10 degrees, and the angle directly across from it (its vertical angle) measures 70∘70^\circ70∘. Find xxx.

Vertical angles are equal, so set the two expressions equal:

3x+10=70.3x + 10 = 70.3x+10=70.

Subtract 101010 from both sides:

3x=60.3x = 60.3x=60.

Divide both sides by 333:

x=20.x = 20.x=20.

So x=20x = 20x=20. Checking, 3(20)+10=703(20) + 10 = 703(20)+10=70, which matches the vertical angle.

Angles around a point

If several angles share a vertex and together sweep all the way around it with no gaps or overlaps, they make one full turn. So angles around a point add to 360∘360^\circ360∘. For instance, three angles meeting at a point with no gaps, measuring 120∘120^\circ120∘, 150∘150^\circ150∘, and an unknown xxx, must satisfy

120∘+150∘+x=360∘,120^\circ + 150^\circ + x = 360^\circ,120∘+150∘+x=360∘,

which gives x=90∘x = 90^\circx=90∘. This is just the full-turn idea from before: one complete rotation is 360∘360^\circ360∘, whether you split it into two pieces, four pieces, or any number.

Check your understanding

Three angles meet at a point with no gaps, measuring 100∘100^\circ100∘, 140∘140^\circ140∘, and x∘x^\circx∘. What is xxx?

Answer choices

Angles around a point add to 360∘360^\circ360∘: 100∘+140∘+x=360∘100^\circ + 140^\circ + x = 360^\circ100∘+140∘+x=360∘, so x=360∘−240∘=120∘x = 360^\circ - 240^\circ = 120^\circx=360∘−240∘=120∘.

220∘220^\circ220∘ subtracts only one of the two known angles from 360∘360^\circ360∘, and 240∘240^\circ240∘ is just the sum of the two known angles, never subtracted from 360∘360^\circ360∘ at all.

Parallel and perpendicular lines

Now that we can measure angles, two more relationships between lines have clean definitions.

Two lines in the same plane that never meet, staying the same distance apart forever, are parallel. Think of the two rails of a straight train track. We write ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m to say line ℓ\ellℓ is parallel to line mmm.

Two lines that cross to form a right angle are perpendicular, written ℓ⊥m\ell \perp mℓ⊥m. Because vertical angles are equal and a linear pair is supplementary, one 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle at the crossing makes the other three 90∘90^\circ90∘ too. The angle across from that right angle is its equal vertical angle (90∘90^\circ90∘), and each neighbour is its supplement (180∘−90∘=90∘180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ180∘−90∘=90∘). So perpendicular lines meet at four right angles, and we only need to mark one of them with the small square. The corner of this page and the cross of a plus sign are both perpendicular.

Check your understanding

Line ℓ\ellℓ crosses line mmm and forms a 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle at the crossing. What must be true of ℓ\ellℓ and mmm?

Answer choices

Two lines that cross to form a right angle are perpendicular, written ℓ⊥m\ell \perp mℓ⊥m.

Parallel lines never cross at all, so ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m cannot be true here.

Parallel lines and a transversal

A line that crosses two (or more) other lines is called a transversal. When the two lines it crosses are parallel, the angles formed line up in tidy, predictable ways. Three facts come up again and again, and each one has a clear reason behind it.

Corresponding angles (the ones in matching positions, such as the upper-right angle at each crossing, both labelled xxx below) are equal. Here is why. The two lines are parallel, which means they run in exactly the same direction. The transversal is a single straight line, so it keeps the same direction the whole way down. When the same slanted line meets two lines that point the same way, it has to open up the same amount at each meeting. The angle cannot change if nothing about the directions changed. A clean way to see it is to slide the lower crossing straight up along the transversal. Parallel lines stay the same distance apart, so the slide lands the lower parallel line exactly on the upper line. The transversal slides along itself, so that single slide carries the whole lower picture onto the whole upper picture. The angle in each position lands on the matching angle, so they must be equal.

Check your understanding

Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. A corresponding angle at the upper crossing measures 72∘72^\circ72∘. What is the matching corresponding angle at the lower crossing?

Answer choices

Corresponding angles formed when a transversal crosses two parallel lines are equal.

So the matching angle at the lower crossing is also 72∘72^\circ72∘. (108∘108^\circ108∘ is its supplement, not its corresponding angle.)

Alternate interior angles (on opposite sides of the transversal, in the space between the two parallel lines) are equal. We do not need a new idea for this one: it follows from corresponding angles plus vertical angles. Take an interior angle on one side of the transversal. The corresponding angle at the other crossing is equal to it (corresponding angles). That corresponding angle and the alternate interior angle we are after are vertical angles at that crossing. They are the opposite pair where the transversal meets the line, so they are equal too. Equal to the same angle means equal to each other, so the two alternate interior angles match.

Check your understanding

Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. An alternate interior angle at one crossing measures 118∘118^\circ118∘. What is its alternate interior partner at the other crossing?

Answer choices

Alternate interior angles formed when a transversal crosses two parallel lines are equal.

So the partner angle is also 118∘118^\circ118∘. (62∘62^\circ62∘ is the co-interior partner instead, since co-interior angles are supplementary rather than equal.)

Co-interior angles (also called same-side interior angles: both between the parallel lines, both on the same side of the transversal) are supplementary, adding to 180∘180^\circ180∘. This one we can derive as well. Look at one crossing. A co-interior angle has an interior angle right beside it, on the same line, on the other side of the transversal. Those two angles form a linear pair, so they add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. But that neighbour is the alternate interior angle of the co-interior angle at the other crossing, and we just showed alternate interior angles are equal. Replacing the neighbour with its equal partner shows the two co-interior angles add to 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Check your understanding

Two lines are marked parallel and cut by a transversal. A co-interior angle between them measures 105∘105^\circ105∘. What is its co-interior partner, also between the lines?

Answer choices

Co-interior angles lie between two parallel lines on the same side of the transversal and are supplementary, adding to 180∘180^\circ180∘. So the partner is 180∘−105∘=75∘180^\circ - 105^\circ = 75^\circ180∘−105∘=75∘.

105∘105^\circ105∘ would be right only if the pair were corresponding or alternate interior instead, since those pairs are equal, not supplementary. The rule needs the two crossed lines to be marked parallel; without that, none of these relationships is guaranteed.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Swapping complementary and supplementary. Complementary adds to 90∘90^\circ90∘; supplementary adds to 180∘180^\circ180∘. The complement of 40∘40^\circ40∘ is 50∘50^\circ50∘, but its supplement is 140∘140^\circ140∘.
  • Thinking a straight angle is 360∘360^\circ360∘. A straight angle is a half turn, 180∘180^\circ180∘. A full turn (all the way around) is 360∘360^\circ360∘.
  • Looking for the complement of a large angle. Only angles less than 90∘90^\circ90∘ have a complement. There is no complement of 120∘120^\circ120∘, since 90∘−120∘90^\circ - 120^\circ90∘−120∘ is negative.
  • Assuming any two equal-looking angles are vertical. Vertical angles must be the opposite pair formed by two crossing straight lines. Two angles that merely look the same size, but are not across a single crossing point, are not a vertical pair. Angles like that need not be equal.
  • Confusing adjacent with vertical. Adjacent angles touch (share a side); vertical angles are across from each other and share only the vertex.

Key takeaways

  • Two of the four basic objects are the point (no size) and the line (extends both ways, 000 endpoints). The other two are the segment (222 endpoints) and the ray (111 endpoint, named from that endpoint). All four live on a flat plane.
  • An angle is two rays from a common vertex; in three-letter names the middle letter is the vertex. Angles are measured in degrees, with a full turn 360∘360^\circ360∘, a straight angle 180∘180^\circ180∘, and a right angle 90∘90^\circ90∘.
  • Types: acute (greater than 0∘0^\circ0∘, less than 90∘90^\circ90∘), right (=90∘= 90^\circ=90∘), obtuse (between 90∘90^\circ90∘ and 180∘180^\circ180∘), straight (=180∘= 180^\circ=180∘), and reflex (between 180∘180^\circ180∘ and 360∘360^\circ360∘).
  • Complementary angles add to 90∘90^\circ90∘ (complement =90∘−x= 90^\circ - x=90∘−x); supplementary angles add to 180∘180^\circ180∘ (supplement =180∘−x= 180^\circ - x=180∘−x).
  • A linear pair is supplementary (180∘180^\circ180∘), vertical angles are equal, and angles around a point add to 360∘360^\circ360∘.
  • Parallel lines never meet; perpendicular lines cross at a right angle (and therefore at four right angles).
  • A transversal crossing two parallel lines makes corresponding and alternate interior angles equal, and co-interior angles supplementary (180∘180^\circ180∘). Without the parallel condition, none of the three holds.

A bit of history (optional)

How much do you have to take on trust?

That question troubled Euclid, a teacher in Alexandria, a busy port city in Egypt, around 300300300 BCE. Mathematicians before him had already proved many individual facts about shapes, and a few had even chained two or three proofs together. But no one had yet gathered so much of it into one connected system, where every result rested only on the results already proved.

So Euclid began by writing down a handful of plain statements he would simply assume. You can draw a straight line between any two points. All right angles are equal. Those are two of the assumptions he started from, and most of the list is that plain. (One assumption, about parallel lines, seemed less obvious even to his first readers, and mathematicians kept questioning it for the next two thousand years.) From those statements he proved hundreds of results, each one leaning only on the results before it.

He packed the whole of it into thirteen volumes called the Elements. Schools were still teaching from it two thousand years later.

One of the first results in that book is the proof you worked through above. Euclid shows that two crossing straight lines make equal angles opposite each other. His argument is the one you followed, resting on the straight angle of 180∘180^\circ180∘.

The Pythagorean Theorem

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

A builder marks 333 equal steps along one wall and 444 along the other, measuring from the same corner. If the corner is square, the straight gap between the marks is exactly 555 steps. The same pattern works at a larger size: 303030 and 404040 give 505050. The reason is a rule about the squares of the three lengths. It lets you find a missing length from two you can measure.

Learning goals

  • Identify the legs and the hypotenuse of a right triangle from where its right angle is
  • Explain why a2+b2=c2a^2 + b^2 = c^2a2+b2=c2 by rearranging four copies of the triangle inside one square
  • Find the hypotenuse from the two legs by adding their squares and taking the square root
  • Work out a missing leg by subtracting squares, keeping the root exact when it is not whole
  • Apply the theorem to real lengths such as ladders, diagonals and square corners

Right triangles and their sides

A right triangle has a right angle, a square corner of exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘. The two sides that meet there are its legs. The side across from the right angle is the hypotenuse. It is the only side that does not touch the right angle, whichever way the triangle is turned.

We will call the legs aaa and bbb and the hypotenuse ccc. Either leg can be aaa; what matters is that ccc stands for the side across from the right angle.

The hypotenuse is also the longest side. The area rule below will show why.

Check your understanding

Triangle PQRPQRPQR has its right angle at QQQ. Which side is the hypotenuse?

Answer choices

The hypotenuse is the side across from the right angle, so it is the one side that does not touch QQQ.

The sides PQPQPQ and QRQRQR both meet at QQQ, so they are the legs. That leaves PRPRPR. Turning the triangle around does not change which side is across from the right angle.

Squares on the three sides

Take the right triangle with legs 333 and 444 units and a hypotenuse of 555 units. Build a square outward on each side, and rule each square into unit squares so you can count its area.

The squares on the legs have areas 32=93^2 = 932=9 and 42=164^2 = 1642=16 square units. The tilted square on the hypotenuse has area 52=255^2 = 2552=25 square units:

9+16=25.9 + 16 = 25.9+16=25.

Together, the two squares on the legs have the same area as the square on the hypotenuse. We will show why this holds for every right triangle.

The rule needs a right angle. A triangle whose three sides are all 222 units long gives 22+22=82^2 + 2^2 = 822+22=8, while the third side squared is only 22=42^2 = 422=4. The sum fails, so we cannot apply the rule to just any triangle.

Why the squares always balance

Rearranging four copies of a right triangle will explain the matching areas. First, we need a fact about its angles.

The figure shows a right triangle with a short leg aaa and a long leg bbb, drawn in the ratio 555 to 121212, and the same triangle completed to an aaa by bbb rectangle. Its other two angles are marked xxx (at the far end of leg aaa) and yyy (at the far end of leg bbb).

Now place four copies of the triangle inside a square in two different ways.

Why a2+b2=c2a^2 + b^2 = c^2a2+b2=c2 in every right triangle#

Complete a right triangle to a rectangle, as in the first figure. For a numerical example, suppose its bottom-right angle is 60∘60^\circ60∘. The diagonal crosses the parallel top and bottom sides, so the alternate interior angle beside the top side is also 60∘60^\circ60∘. This is the equal-angle rule from Points, Lines, and Angles: alternate interior angles are equal when a transversal crosses parallel lines. That 60∘60^\circ60∘ angle shares the top-left right angle with the triangle’s other angle, which must be 90∘−60∘=30∘90^\circ - 60^\circ = 30^\circ90∘−60∘=30∘. The two angles add to 90∘90^\circ90∘.

Nothing depended on choosing 60∘60^\circ60∘. In any right triangle, call the other angles xxx and yyy. The angle beside yyy is also xxx, so x+y=90∘x + y = 90^\circx+y=90∘. These two angles are complementary, and each is acute.

For the area argument, take legs of 555 and 121212 units. Each triangle is half of a 555 by 121212 rectangle, so its area is 12×5×12=30\tfrac12 \times 5 \times 12 = 3021​×5×12=30 square units. The big square has side 5+12=175 + 12 = 175+12=17 and area 172=28917^2 = 289172=289 square units.

In the left arrangement, split each side into lengths 555 and 121212, going around the square in the same direction. Joining the marks cuts off four corner triangles. Each has legs of 555 and 121212 meeting at a right angle, because they meet at a corner of the big square. These match the original triangle’s legs and the angle between them, so each piece is a copy. The tilted shape left in the middle therefore has four equal sides, all hypotenuses.

At each mark, one triangle’s leg of 555 ends beside another triangle’s leg of 121212. They bring angles xxx and yyy. These angles and the middle shape’s corner fill a straight angle, so that corner is 180∘−(x+y)=180∘−90∘=90∘180^\circ - (x + y) = 180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ180∘−(x+y)=180∘−90∘=90∘. Thus the middle has four equal sides and four right angles: it is a square. Its area is the big square’s area minus the four triangles:

289−4×30=289−120=169.289 - 4 \times 30 = 289 - 120 = 169.289−4×30=289−120=169.

In the right arrangement, cut the big square along a line 555 from the left and a line 555 from the bottom. That gives a 555 by 555 square, a 121212 by 121212 square, and two 555 by 121212 rectangles. Each rectangle splits along its diagonal into two copies of the triangle. The same four triangles now leave the two squares on the legs:

289−120=25+144=169.289 - 120 = 25 + 144 = 169.289−120=25+144=169.

Both arrangements remove the same four triangles from equal squares, so the remaining areas are equal. The square on the hypotenuse has area 52+122=1695^2 + 12^2 = 16952+122=169 square units. Since 132=16913^2 = 169132=169, the hypotenuse is 131313 units.

Nothing in the argument used the numbers 555 and 121212, or needed one leg to be longer than the other. For legs aaa and bbb and hypotenuse ccc, the big square has side a+ba + ba+b and each triangle has area 12ab\tfrac12 ab21​ab. The left arrangement leaves the square on the hypotenuse, of area c2c^2c2. The right arrangement leaves the squares on the legs, of area a2+b2a^2 + b^2a2+b2. Equal leftovers from equal starts give

a2+b2=c2.a^2 + b^2 = c^2.a2+b2=c2.

The right angle is essential: it makes the corner pieces copies of our triangle and gives the middle shape right angles too.

∎

That result is the Pythagorean theorem: in a right triangle with legs aaa and bbb and hypotenuse ccc,

a2+b2=c2.a^2 + b^2 = c^2.a2+b2=c2.

In words, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the two legs. Use it once you know the triangle has a right angle.

The theorem also explains why the hypotenuse is longest. In the example, 169=25+144169 = 25 + 144169=25+144 is larger than either 252525 or 144144144, so its square root, 131313, is larger than either leg. In general, both legs have positive lengths, so c2=a2+b2c^2 = a^2 + b^2c2=a2+b2 is larger than either a2a^2a2 or b2b^2b2. A larger positive number has a larger square root, so c>ac > ac>a and c>bc > bc>b. The hypotenuse is always the longest side of a right triangle.

Finding the hypotenuse

When you know both legs, square each one and add to find c2c^2c2. Then take the square root to find ccc. Use the positive root because a triangle’s side has a positive length.

Worked example 1 Legs of 888 cm and 151515 cm

A right triangle has legs of 888 cm and 151515 cm. Find its hypotenuse.

Put the legs in for aaa and bbb:

c2=82+152=64+225=289.c^2 = 8^2 + 15^2 = 64 + 225 = 289.c2=82+152=64+225=289.

So ccc is the number whose square is 289289289. Since 17×17=28917 \times 17 = 28917×17=289,

c=289=17 cm.c = \sqrt{289} = 17 \text{ cm}.c=289​=17 cm.

Check: 171717 is longer than both legs. It is not 8+15=238 + 15 = 238+15=23, because the theorem adds squares, not sides.

The sum of the squares need not be a perfect square. When it is a whole number but not a perfect square, its root is irrational. Keep the length as an exact square root, simplifying it with perfect-square factors as you learned in Square Roots.

Worked example 2 Legs of 444 m and 666 m

A right triangle has legs of 444 m and 666 m. Find the exact length of its hypotenuse, and then about how long it is.

Add the squares of the legs:

c2=42+62=16+36=52.c^2 = 4^2 + 6^2 = 16 + 36 = 52.c2=42+62=16+36=52.

So c=52c = \sqrt{52}c=52​. The number 525252 is not a perfect square, but it has the perfect-square factor 444, since 52=4×1352 = 4 \times 1352=4×13:

c=4×13=213 m.c = \sqrt{4 \times 13} = 2\sqrt{13} \text{ m}.c=4×13​=213​ m.

That is the exact answer. To estimate it, trap 525252 between perfect squares: 49<52<6449 < 52 < 6449<52<64, so ccc is between 777 and 888, and close to 777. To round to the nearest tenth, square 7.27.27.2 and the halfway value 7.257.257.25: 7.22=51.847.2^2 = 51.847.22=51.84, which is less than 525252, and 7.252=52.56257.25^2 = 52.56257.252=52.5625, which is more. So 52\sqrt{52}52​ lies between 7.27.27.2 and 7.257.257.25, and the hypotenuse is about 7.27.27.2 m. The rounded value gets an “about”, because 7.27.27.2 squared is not quite 525252.

Finding a missing leg

The same equation works backward. Take a right triangle with a hypotenuse of 101010 and one leg of 666. The theorem says

62+b2=102,that is36+b2=100.6^2 + b^2 = 10^2, \quad\text{that is}\quad 36 + b^2 = 100.62+b2=102,that is36+b2=100.

Take 363636 away from both sides, just as you would undo an addition in any equation. That leaves b2=100−36=64b^2 = 100 - 36 = 64b2=100−36=64, so b=8b = 8b=8. Nothing depended on 101010 and 666. For any hypotenuse ccc and known leg aaa, removing a2a^2a2 from both sides gives

b2=c2−a2.b^2 = c^2 - a^2.b2=c2−a2.

So for a leg you subtract. This only works when the hypotenuse is longer than the leg you know. If it is not, the subtraction gives zero or less, and no right triangle has those sides.

Worked example 3 Hypotenuse 252525, one leg 777

A right triangle has a hypotenuse of 252525 units and one leg of 777 units. Find the other leg.

The 252525 is the hypotenuse, so it plays the part of ccc. Subtract the square of the known leg from the square of the hypotenuse:

b2=252−72=625−49=576.b^2 = 25^2 - 7^2 = 625 - 49 = 576.b2=252−72=625−49=576.

Since 202=40020^2 = 400202=400 and 252=62525^2 = 625252=625, the root of 576576576 is between 202020 and 252525. Try 242424: 24×24=57624 \times 24 = 57624×24=576, so

b=576=24 units.b = \sqrt{576} = 24 \text{ units}.b=576​=24 units.

Check: 72+242=49+576=625=2527^2 + 24^2 = 49 + 576 = 625 = 25^272+242=49+576=625=252. Had you added instead, 625+49=674625 + 49 = 674625+49=674 would give a leg longer than the hypotenuse, which is impossible.

Measuring with the theorem

Any side of a right triangle can be found this way once you know the other two, as long as all the lengths are in the same unit. A ladder against a wall is the classic case, provided the wall stands at a right angle to level ground.

Worked example 4 How high does the ladder reach?

A 6.56.56.5 m ladder leans against a wall. Its foot is 2.52.52.5 m from the wall on level ground. How high up the wall does it reach?

The ground and the wall meet at a right angle, so the ladder is the hypotenuse. The height hhh is a leg, and that means subtracting:

h2=6.52−2.52=42.25−6.25=36.h^2 = 6.5^2 - 2.5^2 = 42.25 - 6.25 = 36.h2=6.52−2.52=42.25−6.25=36.

So h=36=6h = \sqrt{36} = 6h=36​=6 m. The ladder reaches 666 m up the wall. If the wall and ground did not meet at a right angle, this calculation would not apply.

A rectangle’s diagonal is the same kind of length. Its corners are right angles, so the diagonal cuts it into two right triangles with the rectangle’s sides as legs. A park 202020 m by 212121 m has a diagonal path of 202+212=841=29\sqrt{20^2 + 21^2} = \sqrt{841} = 29202+212​=841​=29 m. Cutting across saves 121212 m on the 414141 m walk round two edges.

The builder’s marks from the start work the same way. If a corner is square, marks 333 and 444 steps along its two sides must be 555 steps apart. So if the gap comes out at anything other than 555, the corner is certainly not square. Does a gap of exactly 555 prove the corner is square? That is a different statement, called the converse: it starts from the sides and concludes the angle. Its proof belongs to Geometry.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Using the rule without a right angle. The theorem is about right triangles, and without a right angle it can fail. A triangle with all three sides 222 gives 22+22=82^2 + 2^2 = 822+22=8, not 444.
  • Putting a leg in the place of ccc. The hypotenuse is the side across from the right angle, and it goes alone on one side of the equation. With a hypotenuse of 252525 and a leg of 777, the missing leg is 625−49\sqrt{625 - 49}625−49​, not 625+49\sqrt{625 + 49}625+49​.
  • Adding the sides instead of the squares. Legs 888 and 151515 give a hypotenuse of 171717, not 8+15=238 + 15 = 238+15=23. A square root does not split across a sum.
  • Stopping at c2c^2c2. The sum of the squares is the area of the square on the hypotenuse. Take its square root to get the length.
  • Writing === for a rounded root. 52=213\sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13}52​=213​ exactly, and it is about 7.27.27.2. Write 52≈7.2\sqrt{52} \approx 7.252​≈7.2, never 52=7.2\sqrt{52} = 7.252​=7.2.

Key takeaways

  • In a right triangle the legs form the right angle and the hypotenuse is the side across from it. The hypotenuse is always the longest side.
  • The Pythagorean theorem: for a right triangle with legs aaa and bbb and hypotenuse ccc, a2+b2=c2a^2 + b^2 = c^2a2+b2=c2. Establish the right angle before using the rule.
  • It is true because one square of side a+ba + ba+b holds four copies of the triangle in two ways. One way leaves the square on the hypotenuse, and the other leaves the two squares on the legs.
  • For the hypotenuse, add the squares and take the root: c=a2+b2c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}c=a2+b2​. For a leg, subtract: b=c2−a2b = \sqrt{c^2 - a^2}b=c2−a2​, which needs the hypotenuse to be the longer side.
  • Keep an irrational length as a simplified exact radical, such as 2132\sqrt{13}213​. A root that comes out as a terminating decimal, such as 28.09=5.3\sqrt{28.09} = 5.328.09​=5.3, is exact too. Write “about” or ≈\approx≈ for any rounded value.

A bit of history (optional)

The rule carries the name of Pythagoras, a Greek teacher from about 2,5002{,}5002,500 years ago. No surviving early evidence tells us that he proved it himself. Historians distinguish the later stories from what the early sources say.

In China, the Zhou bi suan jing, an old book about astronomy and mathematics, discusses the triangle with sides 333, 444 and 555. About 1,8001{,}8001,800 years ago, the scholar Zhao Shuang added commentary and diagrams to the book. A picture preserved in a later edition is attributed to him: a square seven units wide, ruled into unit squares, with a 333, 444, 555 triangle in each corner and a tilted square in the middle.

The big square holds 494949 unit squares. Each triangle covers 666, so the four cover 242424, leaving 252525 for the tilted square. The picture makes the relationship visible without modern algebraic notation.

Clare Bycroft’s Picturing Pythagoras: An Ancient Chinese Approach to Mathematical Reasoning (PDF) shows the surviving picture and explains how diagrams helped communicate the mathematics.

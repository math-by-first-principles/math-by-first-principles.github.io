Integers and the Number Line: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Positive and negative numbers
A negative number is less than zero and sits left of zero on the number line; a positive number sits right. Read −3-3−3 as "negative three"; a bare 333 is understood to be positive.
Number line
A line with 000 marked and the integers on equally spaced ticks, negatives running left and positives right, with no last tick either way. Position on it decides order.
Integer
One of {…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…}\{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}{…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…}: a whole number or the opposite of one. A value landing between two ticks, such as 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ or 2.72.72.7, is not an integer.
Zero
Neither positive nor negative: the dividing point the two sides are measured from. It is its own opposite, and −0-0−0 is just 000.
Opposite of xxx, written −x-x−x
The number that folding the line at zero lands xxx on. The symbol −x-x−x names the opposite of xxx, not "a negative number": when xxx is negative, −x-x−x is positive.
Absolute-value bars ∣  ∣\lvert \; \rvert∣∣
A grouping symbol, like parentheses: simplify everything inside to one number first, then measure its distance from zero.
Signed quantity
A measurement whose sign records which side of a chosen reference it falls on: −8-8−8 degrees is eight below zero, −40-40−40 dollars is forty owed, −30-30−30 meters is thirty below sea level.

Formulas and theorems

  • Order by position

    a<bexactly whena lies to the left of b\begin{gathered} a < b \quad \text{exactly when} \\ a \text{ lies to the left of } b \end{gathered}a<bexactly whena lies to the left of b​
    Text description

    A number line with negative five marked further left than negative two, and arrows showing that leftward means smaller.

    Use when Every integer. Every negative number is therefore less than 000 and less than every positive number.

    e.g. −5<−2-5 < -2−5<−2 while 5>25 > 25>2; and −1000<4-1000 < 4−1000<4 without any arithmetic.

  • Opposites

    −(−a)=a-(-a) = a−(−a)=a

    Use when Every integer has exactly one opposite, the same distance from zero. 000 alone is its own opposite; every other pair sits on opposite sides.

    e.g. The opposite of −15-15−15 is 151515; the opposite of the opposite of −8-8−8 is −8-8−8.

  • Zero pair

    a+(−a)=0a + (-a) = 0a+(−a)=0

    Use when Every integer, equivalently a−a=0a - a = 0a−a=0. A number and its opposite, such as 777 and −7-7−7, form a zero pair, so matched units cancel.

  • Adding two numbers with the same sign

    (−m)+(−n)=−(m+n)(-m) + (-n) = -(m + n)(−m)+(−n)=−(m+n)

    Use when mmm and nnn are the two distances from zero, both positive. Two negatives add their distances and keep the minus sign; matching signs never cancel.

    e.g. (−9)+(−4)=−(9+4)=−13(-9) + (-4) = -(9 + 4) = -13(−9)+(−4)=−(9+4)=−13.

  • Adding two numbers with different signs

    m+(−n)=m−nif m>nm+(−n)=−(n−m)if n>m\begin{gathered} m + (-n) = m - n \quad \text{if } m > n \\ m + (-n) = -(n - m) \quad \text{if } n > m \end{gathered}m+(−n)=m−nif m>nm+(−n)=−(n−m)if n>m​

    Use when mmm and nnn are the two distances from zero, both positive. Always subtract the smaller from the larger. The answer takes the sign of the number farther from zero; equal distances give 000.

    e.g. (−12)+5=−(12−5)=−7(-12) + 5 = -(12 - 5) = -7(−12)+5=−(12−5)=−7.

  • Subtraction is adding the opposite

    a−b=a+(−b)a - b = a + (-b)a−b=a+(−b)

    Use when Every pair of integers. Subtracting a NEGATIVE is where this bites: a−(−n)=a+na - (-n) = a + na−(−n)=a+n for n>0n > 0n>0, a step to the right. Rewrite first, then use the addition rules. Order still matters: a−ba - ba−b and b−ab - ab−a are opposites.

    e.g. 3−(−8)=3+8=113 - (-8) = 3 + 8 = 113−(−8)=3+8=11.

  • Sign rule for multiplication

    in a×b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one\begin{gathered} \text{in } a \times b: \\ \text{matching signs give a positive result,} \\ \text{differing signs a negative one} \end{gathered}in a×b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one​

    Use when Both factors nonzero: multiply the two distances from zero, then attach +++ when the signs match and −-− when they differ. A factor of 000 has no sign, and any product with it is 000.

    e.g. (−7)×(−8)=+(7×8)=56(-7) \times (-8) = +(7 \times 8) = 56(−7)×(−8)=+(7×8)=56.

  • Counting negative factors

    count the negative factors:an even count gives a positive product,an odd count a negative one\begin{gathered} \text{count the negative factors:} \\ \text{an even count gives a positive product,} \\ \text{an odd count a negative one} \end{gathered}count the negative factors:an even count gives a positive product,an odd count a negative one​

    Use when A product of any number of nonzero factors. If even one factor is 000 the product is 000 and the count decides nothing.

    e.g. (−1)×(−2)×(−3)=−6(-1) \times (-2) \times (-3) = -6(−1)×(−2)×(−3)=−6: three negatives, an odd count.

  • Sign rule for division

    in a÷b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one\begin{gathered} \text{in } a \div b: \\ \text{matching signs give a positive result,} \\ \text{differing signs a negative one} \end{gathered}in a÷b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one​

    Use when The divisor bbb is not zero. Divide the two distances from zero, then attach the sign by the same match-or-differ rule as multiplication. A dividend of 000 has no sign, and 0÷b=00 \div b = 00÷b=0.

    e.g. (−30)÷(−6)=5(-30) \div (-6) = 5(−30)÷(−6)=5, checked by 5×(−6)=−305 \times (-6) = -305×(−6)=−30.

  • Division by zero

    a÷0 is undefined for every aa \div 0 \ \text{is undefined for every } aa÷0 is undefined for every a

    Use when Undefined means the expression names no number at all. It covers 0÷00 \div 00÷0, where every number fits and none can be singled out. Only division BY zero fails.

  • Absolute value

    ∣x∣=x  when x≥0∣x∣=−x  when x<0\begin{gathered} \lvert x \rvert = x \ \text{ when } x \ge 0 \\ \lvert x \rvert = -x \ \text{ when } x < 0 \end{gathered}∣x∣=x  when x≥0∣x∣=−x  when x<0​

    Use when ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ is the distance from xxx to 000, so ∣x∣≥0\lvert x \rvert \ge 0∣x∣≥0 always, while ∣x∣>0\lvert x \rvert > 0∣x∣>0 fails at x=0x = 0x=0. On the second line −x-x−x is the opposite of a negative number, so it is positive. A number and its opposite share one value: ∣x∣=∣−x∣\lvert x \rvert = \lvert -x \rvert∣x∣=∣−x∣.

    e.g. ∣−12∣=−(−12)=12\lvert -12 \rvert = -(-12) = 12∣−12∣=−(−12)=12, and ∣0∣=0\lvert 0 \rvert = 0∣0∣=0.

  • Numbers with a given absolute value

    ∣x∣=c  gives  x=c  or  x=−c\lvert x \rvert = c \ \text{ gives } \ x = c \ \text{ or } \ x = -c∣x∣=c  gives  x=c  or  x=−c

    Use when c>0c > 0c>0 gives exactly two solutions, c=0c = 0c=0 exactly one (x=0x = 0x=0), and c<0c < 0c<0 none at all, since a distance is never negative.

    e.g. ∣n∣=6\lvert n \rvert = 6∣n∣=6 has the two integer solutions 666 and −6-6−6; ∣n∣=−6\lvert n \rvert = -6∣n∣=−6 has none.

  • Distance between two numbers

    distance from a to b=∣a−b∣\text{distance from } a \text{ to } b = \lvert a - b \rvertdistance from a to b=∣a−b∣

    Use when Any two integers, subtracted in either order, since a−ba - ba−b and b−ab - ab−a are opposites. The distance is 000 exactly when a=ba = ba=b.

    e.g. The distance between −6-6−6 and 222 is ∣−6−2∣=∣−8∣=8\lvert -6 - 2 \rvert = \lvert -8 \rvert = 8∣−6−2∣=∣−8∣=8.

Problem types, step by step

Compare or order integers

  1. Place each number by position: negatives left of 000, positives right, and among negatives the bigger numeral sits farther left.
  2. Read left to right for least-to-greatest, right to left for greatest-to-least; a <<< or >>> points its narrow end at the smaller number.
  3. Translate worded comparisons first: colder, lower, deeper, and further overdrawn all mean farther left, so smaller.

e.g. 3,−4,0,−1,23, -4, 0, -1, 23,−4,0,−1,2 from least to greatest is −4,−1,0,2,3-4, -1, 0, 2, 3−4,−1,0,2,3.

Locate a point on the number line

  1. For the opposite of a number, keep its distance from zero and switch sides.
  2. To move kkk ticks, count kkk left for a decrease or kkk right for an increase from the given number.
  3. For the point halfway between two numbers, halve the distance and count that far inward from either end.

e.g. Halfway between −8-8−8 and −2-2−2: the gap is ∣−8−(−2)∣=6\lvert -8 - (-2) \rvert = 6∣−8−(−2)∣=6, so count 333 right from −8-8−8 to −5-5−5.

Add and subtract integers, singly or in a chain

  1. Simplify inside any parentheses first, treating each group as one signed number.
  2. Rewrite every subtraction as adding the opposite, leaving only additions.
  3. Matching signs: add the distances from zero and keep that sign.
  4. Differing signs: subtract the smaller distance from the larger and take the sign of the number farther from zero.
  5. In a long chain, combine left to right, or total the positives, total the negatives, and combine those two.

e.g. −8+15−(−3)−12=−8+15+3+(−12)-8 + 15 - (-3) - 12 = -8 + 15 + 3 + (-12)−8+15−(−3)−12=−8+15+3+(−12): positives 181818, negatives −20-20−20, total −2-2−2.

Multiply or divide signed numbers

  1. Multiply or divide the distances from zero as ordinary whole numbers, ignoring signs.
  2. Count every negative factor, taking a fraction bar's numerator and denominator together, then attach +++ for an even count and −-− for an odd one. A zero on top gives 000; a zero underneath is undefined; neither lets the count decide.
  3. Under a fraction bar, finish the numerator into one number before dividing.

e.g. (−3)×(−4)×(−2)−6=−24−6=4\dfrac{(-3) \times (-4) \times (-2)}{-6} = \dfrac{-24}{-6} = 4−6(−3)×(−4)×(−2)​=−6−24​=4: four negatives in all, an even count.

Evaluate an absolute-value expression

  1. Simplify everything inside each pair of bars to a single number.
  2. Replace each pair of bars with that number's distance from zero, which is never negative.
  3. Apply whatever sign or operation is still sitting outside the bars.

e.g. −∣6−13∣=−∣−7∣=−7-\lvert 6 - 13 \rvert = -\lvert -7 \rvert = -7−∣6−13∣=−∣−7∣=−7.

Find how far apart two numbers are, or how much a quantity changed

  1. Subtract the two values, in either order.
  2. Take the absolute value of that difference, keeping the size of the gap and dropping the direction.
  3. Check by counting the ticks from one number to the other, splitting at zero if they lie on opposite sides.

e.g. Divers at −40-40−40 and −25-25−25 meters: ∣−40−(−25)∣=∣−15∣=15\lvert -40 - (-25) \rvert = \lvert -15 \rvert = 15∣−40−(−25)∣=∣−15∣=15 meters apart.

Answer a question about an absolute-value condition

  1. For ∣x∣=c\lvert x \rvert = c∣x∣=c with c>0c > 0c>0, write both answers, x=cx = cx=c and x=−cx = -cx=−c, then use any extra condition to pick one.
  2. To count solutions: c>0c > 0c>0 gives two, c=0c = 0c=0 gives one, c<0c < 0c<0 gives none.
  3. To count the integers within a stated distance of zero, list them from the negative end across to the positive end, counting 000 once.

e.g. The integers no more than 222 units from zero are −2,−1,0,1,2-2, -1, 0, 1, 2−2,−1,0,1,2: five of them.

Solve a signed-quantity word problem

  1. Set what counts as zero (freezing, sea level, an empty balance) and write the starting value with its sign.
  2. Write each change as a signed number: a fall, loss, or withdrawal is negative, a rise, gain, or deposit positive; multiply a repeated change by how many times it happens.
  3. Add the signed terms in order, then read the result back into words, with units.

e.g. Midnight −6-6−6 degrees, falls 444, rises 999: −6+(−4)+9=−1-6 + (-4) + 9 = -1−6+(−4)+9=−1 degree.

Fill in a missing addend or factor

  1. Missing addend: count the walk from the starting number to the total. A walk right is a positive addend, a walk left a negative one.
  2. Missing factor: divide the product by the known nonzero factor, under the same sign rule.
  3. Substitute your answer back to confirm both its size and its sign.

e.g. (−6)×□=42(-6) \times \square = 42(−6)×□=42 gives □=42÷(−6)=−7\square = 42 \div (-6) = -7□=42÷(−6)=−7, and (−6)×(−7)=42(-6) \times (-7) = 42(−6)×(−7)=42.

Exam traps

  • Trap Ordering negatives by their digits, so −5>−2-5 > -2−5>−2 and −100>−3-100 > -3−100>−3.

    Fix Farther left is smaller, so among negatives a bigger numeral means a smaller number: −5<−2-5 < -2−5<−2 and −100<−3-100 < -3−100<−3.

  • Trap Carrying "two negatives make a positive" into addition, so (−3)+(−4)=7(-3) + (-4) = 7(−3)+(−4)=7.

    Fix Two negative factors make a positive; two negative addends pile up: (−3)+(−4)=−7(-3) + (-4) = -7(−3)+(−4)=−7.

  • Trap Adding the distances when the signs differ, or taking the sign from the wrong number, so (−8)+5(-8) + 5(−8)+5 comes out −13-13−13 or 333.

    Fix Differing signs cancel: subtract 8−5=38 - 5 = 38−5=3, then take the sign of −8-8−8, the number farther from zero, for −3-3−3.

  • Trap Dropping the rewrite when a negative is subtracted, so −9−(−4)-9 - (-4)−9−(−4) is worked as −9−4=−13-9 - 4 = -13−9−4=−13.

    Fix Subtracting a negative adds its opposite and moves right: −9−(−4)=−9+4=−5-9 - (-4) = -9 + 4 = -5−9−(−4)=−9+4=−5.

  • Trap Answering (−6)×(−2)=−12(-6) \times (-2) = -12(−6)×(−2)=−12, on the feeling that negative factors keep an answer negative.

    Fix Each negative factor flips the sign once, so two of them flip it back: (−6)×(−2)=12(-6) \times (-2) = 12(−6)×(−2)=12.

  • Trap Counting only the numerator's negative factors under a fraction bar.

    Fix Count above and below together. In (−3)×(−4)×(−2)−6\dfrac{(-3) \times (-4) \times (-2)}{-6}−6(−3)×(−4)×(−2)​ there are four negatives, an even count, so the answer is +4+4+4, not −4-4−4.

  • Trap Reading ∣x∣=−x\lvert x \rvert = -x∣x∣=−x as a negative output, so ∣−5∣=−5\lvert -5 \rvert = -5∣−5∣=−5.

    Fix There −x-x−x is the opposite of a negative number, so it is positive: ∣−5∣=−(−5)=5\lvert -5 \rvert = -(-5) = 5∣−5∣=−(−5)=5. An absolute value is never negative.

  • Trap Splitting the bars across an operation, so ∣(−3)+5∣\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert∣(−3)+5∣ is worked as ∣−3∣+∣5∣=8\lvert -3 \rvert + \lvert 5 \rvert = 8∣−3∣+∣5∣=8.

    Fix The bars group what is inside, so finish the sum first: ∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert = \lvert 2 \rvert = 2∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2.

  • Trap Letting the bars swallow a minus sign that sits outside them, so −∣−4∣=4-\lvert -4 \rvert = 4−∣−4∣=4.

    Fix Only what is inside the bars loses its sign: the bars give 444, and the outside minus makes it −4-4−4.

  • Trap Answering "which is greatest" with the greatest absolute value, or ranking −9-9−9 above 666 as a number.

    Fix Greatest asks which is farther right: 6>−96 > -96>−9. Greatest absolute value asks which is farther from zero: ∣−9∣=9>6\lvert -9 \rvert = 9 > 6∣−9∣=9>6. A larger debt is a smaller number.

  • Trap Treating −70\dfrac{-7}{0}0−7​ and 0−7\dfrac{0}{-7}−70​ as the same kind of expression.

    Fix 0−7=0\dfrac{0}{-7} = 0−70​=0, an ordinary quotient. −70\dfrac{-7}{0}0−7​ is undefined: no number times 000 gives −7-7−7.

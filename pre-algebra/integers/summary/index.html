Integers and the Number Line: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Positive and negative numbers
A negative number is less than zero and sits left of zero on the number line; a positive number sits right. Read −3-3−3 as "negative three"; a bare 333 is understood to be positive.
Number line
A line with equally spaced integer ticks: negatives left of zero, positives right. It continues in both directions, and position determines order.
Integer
A whole number or its opposite: …,−2,−1,0,1,2,…\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…. Numbers between neighboring integer ticks, such as half a unit, are not integers.
Zero
Neither positive nor negative. It is its own opposite: −0=0-0=0−0=0.
Opposite of xxx, written −x-x−x
The reflection of xxx across zero. The symbol −x-x−x means the opposite of xxx; when xxx is negative, −x-x−x is positive.
Absolute-value bars ∣  ∣\lvert \; \rvert∣∣
A grouping symbol, like parentheses: simplify everything inside to one number first, then measure its distance from zero.
Signed quantity
A measurement whose sign records which side of a chosen reference it falls on: −8-8−8 degrees is eight below zero, −40-40−40 dollars is forty owed, −30-30−30 meters is thirty below sea level.

Formulas and theorems

  • Order by position

    a<bexactly whena lies to the left of b\begin{gathered} a < b \quad \text{exactly when} \\ a \text{ lies to the left of } b \end{gathered}a<bexactly whena lies to the left of b​
    Text description

    A number line with negative five marked further left than negative two, and arrows showing that leftward means smaller.

    Use when Any two integers. Every negative is less than zero and every positive.

    e.g. −11<−4-11<-4−11<−4 even though 11>411>411>4.

  • Opposites

    −(−a)=a-(-a) = a−(−a)=a

    Use when Any integer. Nonzero opposites are equally far from zero on opposite sides; zero is its own opposite.

    e.g. The opposite of −15-15−15 is 151515.

  • Zero pair

    a+(−a)=0a + (-a) = 0a+(−a)=0

    Use when Any integer aaa. A number and its opposite form a zero pair; also a−a=0a-a=0a−a=0.

  • Adding two numbers with the same sign

    (−m)+(−n)=−(m+n)(-m) + (-n) = -(m + n)(−m)+(−n)=−(m+n)

    Use when Here mmm and nnn are positive integer distances from zero. Add the distances and keep the shared sign. Two positives also add their distances, with a positive result.

    e.g. (−9)+(−4)=−(9+4)=−13(-9) + (-4) = -(9 + 4) = -13(−9)+(−4)=−(9+4)=−13.

  • Adding two numbers with different signs

    m+(−n)=m−nif m>nm+(−n)=−(n−m)if n>m\begin{gathered} m + (-n) = m - n \quad \text{if } m > n \\ m + (-n) = -(n - m) \quad \text{if } n > m \end{gathered}m+(−n)=m−nif m>nm+(−n)=−(n−m)if n>m​

    Use when Here mmm and nnn are positive integer distances. Subtract the smaller distance from the larger and use the farther number's sign. Equal distances give zero.

    e.g. (−12)+5=−(12−5)=−7(-12) + 5 = -(12 - 5) = -7(−12)+5=−(12−5)=−7.

  • Subtraction is adding the opposite

    a−b=a+(−b)a - b = a + (-b)a−b=a+(−b)

    Use when Any integers aaa and bbb. Subtracting a negative moves right. Rewrite as addition before regrouping. Swapping the order of the two numbers gives the opposite result: b−a=−(a−b)b-a=-(a-b)b−a=−(a−b).

    e.g. 3−(−8)=3+8=113 - (-8) = 3 + 8 = 113−(−8)=3+8=11.

  • Sign rule for multiplication

    in a×b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one\begin{gathered} \text{in } a \times b: \\ \text{matching signs give a positive result,} \\ \text{differing signs a negative one} \end{gathered}in a×b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one​

    Use when For two nonzero integers: multiply the sizes, then use the sign rule. If either factor is zero, the product is zero.

    e.g. (−7)×(−8)=+(7×8)=56(-7) \times (-8) = +(7 \times 8) = 56(−7)×(−8)=+(7×8)=56.

  • Counting negative factors

    count the negative factors:an even count gives a positive product,an odd count a negative one\begin{gathered} \text{count the negative factors:} \\ \text{an even count gives a positive product,} \\ \text{an odd count a negative one} \end{gathered}count the negative factors:an even count gives a positive product,an odd count a negative one​

    Use when For nonzero integer factors. If any factor is zero, the product is zero instead.

    e.g. (−1)×(−2)×(−3)=−6(-1) \times (-2) \times (-3) = -6(−1)×(−2)×(−3)=−6: three negatives, an odd count.

  • Sign rule for division

    in a÷b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one\begin{gathered} \text{in } a \div b: \\ \text{matching signs give a positive result,} \\ \text{differing signs a negative one} \end{gathered}in a÷b:matching signs give a positive result,differing signs a negative one​

    Use when For nonzero integers aaa and bbb. If only the dividend is zero, 0÷b=00\div b=00÷b=0. The divisor must never be zero.

    e.g. (−35)÷(−7)=5(-35)\div(-7)=5(−35)÷(−7)=5, since 5×(−7)=−355\times(-7)=-355×(−7)=−35.

  • Division by zero

    a÷0 is undefined for every aa \div 0 \ \text{is undefined for every } aa÷0 is undefined for every a

    Use when Any integer aaa, including zero. No factor times zero gives a nonzero dividend; every factor works for a zero dividend. Neither case selects one quotient.

  • Absolute value

    ∣x∣=x  when x≥0∣x∣=−x  when x<0\begin{gathered} \lvert x \rvert = x \ \text{ when } x \ge 0 \\ \lvert x \rvert = -x \ \text{ when } x < 0 \end{gathered}∣x∣=x  when x≥0∣x∣=−x  when x<0​

    Use when Any integer xxx. Absolute value is distance from zero, so it is never negative and is zero only at x=0x=0x=0. In the second case, −x-x−x is positive. Opposites share a distance: ∣x∣=∣−x∣\lvert x\rvert=\lvert-x\rvert∣x∣=∣−x∣.

    e.g. ∣−12∣=−(−12)=12\lvert -12 \rvert = -(-12) = 12∣−12∣=−(−12)=12, and ∣0∣=0\lvert 0 \rvert = 0∣0∣=0.

  • Numbers with a given absolute value

    ∣x∣=cwith c>0gives x=corx=−c\begin{gathered}\lvert x\rvert=c\quad\text{with }c>0\\\text{gives }x=c\quad\text{or}\quad x=-c\end{gathered}∣x∣=cwith c>0gives x=corx=−c​

    Use when For integer xxx and integer ccc: positive ccc gives two answers, c=0c=0c=0 gives only x=0x=0x=0, and negative ccc gives none.

    e.g. The integers with absolute value 999 are 999 and −9-9−9.

  • Distance between two numbers

    distance=∣a−b∣\text{distance}=\lvert a-b\rvertdistance=∣a−b∣

    Use when For integer positions aaa and bbb. Either subtraction order gives the same distance. Equal positions give distance zero.

    e.g. −4-4−4 and 333 are 777 units apart.

Problem types, step by step

Compare or order integers

  1. Place negatives left of zero and positives right. Among negatives, the greater distance from zero gives the smaller number.
  2. Read left to right for least to greatest, or right to left for greatest to least. The narrow end of <<< or >>> points at the smaller number.
  3. In signed contexts, colder, lower, deeper, or farther overdrawn means a smaller value.

e.g. 3,−4,0,−1,23, -4, 0, -1, 23,−4,0,−1,2 from least to greatest is −4,−1,0,2,3-4, -1, 0, 2, 3−4,−1,0,2,3.

Locate a point on the number line

  1. To find an opposite, keep the distance from zero and switch sides. Zero stays at zero.
  2. On a line marked in one-unit steps, count left for a decrease or right for an increase.
  3. For the point halfway between two numbers, halve the distance and count that far inward from either end.

e.g. Halfway between −12-12−12 and −4-4−4 is −8-8−8, four units from each.

Add and subtract integers, singly or in a chain

  1. Simplify inside any parentheses first, treating each group as one signed number.
  2. Rewrite every subtraction as adding the opposite, leaving only additions.
  3. Matching signs: add the distances from zero and keep that sign.
  4. Different signs: subtract the smaller distance from the larger and use the farther number's sign. Equal distances give zero.
  5. In a long chain, combine left to right, or total the positives, total the negatives, and combine those two.

Multiply or divide signed numbers

  1. Finish grouped sums and differences first. Under a fraction bar, simplify both the numerator and denominator.
  2. Check for zero: a zero divisor makes the expression undefined; a zero product or a zero dividend with a nonzero divisor gives zero.
  3. Multiply or divide the sizes, working left to right within a chain. For nonzero factors and divisors, count their negatives: even gives positive, odd gives negative. Do not count across an unfinished sum or difference.
  4. Check a quotient by multiplying it by the divisor to recover the dividend.

e.g. (−2)×(−5)×(−3)=−30(-2)\times(-5)\times(-3)=-30(−2)×(−5)×(−3)=−30: three negative factors.

Evaluate an absolute-value expression

  1. Simplify everything inside each pair of bars to a single number.
  2. Replace each pair of bars with that number's distance from zero, which is never negative.
  3. Apply whatever sign or operation is still sitting outside the bars.

e.g. −∣2−8∣=−6-\lvert2-8\rvert=-6−∣2−8∣=−6.

Find the distance between two positions

  1. Subtract the two values, in either order.
  2. Take the absolute value to keep the distance, without its direction. For a signed change instead, subtract the starting value from the final value and keep the sign.
  3. Check by counting the ticks from one number to the other, splitting at zero if they lie on opposite sides.

e.g. Positions −18-18−18 and −5-5−5 are 131313 units apart.

Answer a question about an absolute-value condition

  1. For a positive integer distance ccc, the two integer positions are ccc and −c-c−c. Use any extra sign condition to pick one.
  2. Distance zero gives only zero. A negative distance is impossible.
  3. For integers within a stated distance, list the points from left to right. Include the ends for "at most"; exclude them for "less than." Count zero once.

e.g. The integers no more than 222 units from zero are −2,−1,0,1,2-2, -1, 0, 1, 2−2,−1,0,1,2: five of them.

Solve a signed-quantity word problem

  1. Identify the zero reference, such as sea level or a zero account balance, and record the starting value with its sign.
  2. Write each change as a signed number: a fall, loss, or withdrawal is negative, a rise, gain, or deposit positive; multiply a repeated change by how many times it happens.
  3. Add the signed terms in order, then read the result back into words, with units.

Fill in a missing addend or factor

  1. Missing addend: count the walk from the starting number to the total. A walk right is a positive addend, a walk left a negative one.
  2. Missing factor: divide the product by the known nonzero factor, under the same sign rule.
  3. Substitute your answer back to confirm both its size and its sign.

e.g. (−8)×□=40(-8)\times\square=40(−8)×□=40 needs −5-5−5, since (−8)×(−5)=40(-8)\times(-5)=40(−8)×(−5)=40.

Exam traps

  • Trap Ordering negatives by their digits, such as claiming −5>−2-5>-2−5>−2.

    Fix Farther left is smaller: −5<−2-5<-2−5<−2. For negatives, a greater distance from zero means a smaller number.

  • Trap Carrying "two negatives make a positive" into addition, so (−3)+(−4)=7(-3) + (-4) = 7(−3)+(−4)=7.

    Fix Two negative factors make a positive; two negative addends pile up: (−3)+(−4)=−7(-3) + (-4) = -7(−3)+(−4)=−7.

  • Trap Adding distances for different signs, or keeping the wrong sign.

    Fix For (−8)+5(-8)+5(−8)+5, subtract distances: 8−5=38-5=38−5=3. The farther number is negative, so the sum is −3-3−3.

  • Trap Reading −9−(−4)-9-(-4)−9−(−4) as −9−4-9-4−9−4.

    Fix Subtracting a negative adds its opposite and moves right: −9−(−4)=−9+4=−5-9 - (-4) = -9 + 4 = -5−9−(−4)=−9+4=−5.

  • Trap Giving a product of two negatives a negative sign.

    Fix Each negative factor flips the sign once, so two of them flip it back: (−6)×(−2)=12(-6) \times (-2) = 12(−6)×(−2)=12.

  • Trap Counting only the numerator's negative factors under a fraction bar.

    Fix In a product over a nonzero product, count negative factors above and below. For (−2)×(−7)−1\frac{(-2)\times(-7)}{-1}−1(−2)×(−7)​, three negatives give −14-14−14. Finish any sums first.

  • Trap Reading ∣x∣=−x\lvert x \rvert = -x∣x∣=−x as a negative output, so ∣−5∣=−5\lvert -5 \rvert = -5∣−5∣=−5.

    Fix The case ∣x∣=−x\lvert x\rvert=-x∣x∣=−x applies when x<0x<0x<0: its opposite is positive. For example, ∣−5∣=−(−5)=5\lvert-5\rvert=-(-5)=5∣−5∣=−(−5)=5.

  • Trap Splitting the bars across an operation, so ∣(−3)+5∣\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert∣(−3)+5∣ is worked as ∣−3∣+∣5∣=8\lvert -3 \rvert + \lvert 5 \rvert = 8∣−3∣+∣5∣=8.

    Fix The bars group what is inside, so finish the sum first: ∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert = \lvert 2 \rvert = 2∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2.

  • Trap Letting the bars swallow a minus sign that sits outside them, so −∣−4∣=4-\lvert -4 \rvert = 4−∣−4∣=4.

    Fix The bars measure the inside value first: ∣−4∣=4\lvert-4\rvert=4∣−4∣=4. The outside minus then gives −4-4−4.

  • Trap Confusing the greatest number with the greatest absolute value.

    Fix Greatest means farthest right: 6>−96>-96>−9. Greatest absolute value means farthest from zero: ∣−9∣=9>6\lvert-9\rvert=9>6∣−9∣=9>6.

  • Trap Treating −70\dfrac{-7}{0}0−7​ and 0−7\dfrac{0}{-7}−70​ as the same kind of expression.

    Fix 0−7=0\dfrac{0}{-7} = 0−70​=0, an ordinary quotient. −70\dfrac{-7}{0}0−7​ is undefined: no number times 000 gives −7-7−7.

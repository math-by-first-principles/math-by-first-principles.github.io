Negative Numbers and the Number Line

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

You have ten dollars in an account and spend fifteen. Your balance is now a debt of five dollars. Whole numbers cannot record that balance: it has gone below zero. Negative numbers let us write debts, temperatures below zero, and positions on the other side of a starting point.

Learning goals

  • Place a negative number left of zero, and count the steps between two marked points
  • List the integers as the whole numbers together with their negatives
  • Name a number's opposite, and say why zero is its own
  • Compare integers by position, so −5<−2-5 < -2−5<−2
  • Record a temperature, balance or elevation as a signed number, and compare two of them

Why we need numbers below zero

We write the debt as −5-5−5: the 555 gives the amount, and the minus sign records the owing side of zero.

A negative number is less than zero. A positive number is greater than zero. Read −3-3−3 as “negative three.” A positive number may carry a plus sign, like +3+3+3, but we usually leave it off and write 333.

The numbers 333 and −3-3−3 describe the same size, three units, on opposite sides of zero. If +3+3+3 means three steps in one direction, −3-3−3 means three steps in the other.

Zero is neither positive nor negative. It is the reference point between the two sides. Writing −0-0−0 does not create a different number: −0=0-0 = 0−0=0.

The number line, extended to the left

A number line places numbers along a straight line. Mark zero, then put the counting numbers 1,2,3,…1, 2, 3, \ldots1,2,3,… at one-unit steps to its right. These numbers together with zero are the whole numbers. Continue the same spacing to the left and label the ticks −1,−2,−3,…-1, -2, -3, \ldots−1,−2,−3,….

The negative numbers lie left of zero and the positive numbers lie right. There is no last number in either direction; the dots in a list mean it continues.

The integers

The whole numbers, together with all of their negatives, form a named set: the integers.

{ …,  −3,  −2,  −1,  0,  1,  2,  3,  … }\{\,\ldots,\; -3,\; -2,\; -1,\; 0,\; 1,\; 2,\; 3,\; \ldots\,\}{…,−3,−2,−1,0,1,2,3,…}

The braces mean “the collection of,” and the dots mean the list continues. Starting at zero and stepping by ones reaches every integer: the positive integers 1,2,3,…1, 2, 3, \ldots1,2,3,…, the negative integers −1,−2,−3,…-1, -2, -3, \ldots−1,−2,−3,…, and zero.

Numbers between neighboring integer ticks are not integers. For example, half a unit, written 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, lies between 000 and 111. Its opposite, −12-\tfrac{1}{2}−21​, is halfway between −1-1−1 and 000. The decimal 2.52.52.5 means two and a half, so it lies between 222 and 333. None of these is an integer.

Check your understanding

Which description gives all the integers?

Answer choices

The whole numbers are 0,1,2,…0, 1, 2, \ldots0,1,2,…. Including their negatives gives the integers.

…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…

Zero belongs to the collection. Points between neighboring integer ticks do not.

Opposites

Two numbers are opposites if folding the number line at zero puts one on the other. For example, 555 and −5-5−5 both lie five units from zero, on opposite sides. This fold is also called a reflection across zero: a number and its opposite are mirror images.

Taking the opposite twice returns to the starting number: the opposite of 555 is −5-5−5, and the opposite of −5-5−5 is 555. We write the second flip as −(−5)=5-(-5) = 5−(−5)=5.

The minus sign has three related jobs. Its position tells you how to read it:

  • A negative number: in −5-5−5, the sign marks a number below zero.
  • Taking the opposite: in −(−5)-(-5)−(−5), the outside minus asks for the opposite of −5-5−5.
  • Subtraction: in 7−57 - 57−5, the minus between the numbers means subtract.

Zero stays at the fold, so reflecting it across zero leaves it at 000. Every other number moves to the other side; zero is the only number that is its own opposite.

Check your understanding

What are the opposites of −9-9−9 and 000, and why does zero stay unchanged?

Answer choices

The point −9-9−9 is nine units left of zero. Folding at zero puts it nine units right, at 999.

−(−9)=9-(-9)=9−(−9)=9

Zero is on the fold itself, so it does not move: −0=0-0=0−0=0.

Comparing integers by position

The number 555 is greater than 222 and lies to its right. Positions compare −5-5−5 and −2-2−2 in the same way. The point −5-5−5 is five steps left of zero, while −2-2−2 is only two steps left. So −5-5−5 lies farther left:

−5<−2.-5 < -2.−5<−2.

Read this as “negative five is less than negative two.” Both pairs follow the same rule: Of two different numbers, the one farther right is greater.

The comparison symbols are the familiar <<< (“is less than”), >>> (“is greater than”), and === (“is equal to”). A reliable way to read them is that the wide open end always faces the larger number and the narrow point faces the smaller. So the statement −5<−2-5 < -2−5<−2 has its point toward −5-5−5, the smaller number.

Every negative lies left of zero and every positive lies right. So every negative is less than zero and less than every positive: for example, −1000<4-1000 < 4−1000<4.

On the number line, left always means less#

Compare −17-17−17 and −9-9−9. Nine one-unit steps left from zero reach −9-9−9; seventeen reach −17-17−17. The longer leftward walk ends farther left, so −17<−9-17 < -9−17<−9.

Nothing depends on choosing 171717 and 999. Of any two negatives, the larger count of steps carries you farther left, to the smaller number. For positives, more steps carry you farther right, to the larger number. The direction is what changes the order.

∎

What happens if you take the opposites of two different numbers? Their order reverses: −8<3-8 < 3−8<3 becomes 8>−38 > -38>−3. The Go deeper section explains why a flip always does this.

Plotting and reading points

To plot an integer, mark its position by counting one-unit steps from zero: left for a negative, right for a positive. Thus −4-4−4 is four steps left and 333 is three steps right.

To read a marked point, count its distance from zero and check which side it is on. The distance gives the size; the side gives the sign.

Check your understanding

Which statement is true?

Answer choices

Place both on the line. The point −7-7−7 is seven steps left of zero and −3-3−3 is three steps left, so −7-7−7 lies farther to the left.

−7<−3-7 < -3−7<−3

Farther left means smaller, so −7-7−7 is less than −3-3−3. (The bare digits would suggest the opposite, which is exactly the trap negatives set.)

Counting from a point other than zero

Zero is the natural place to count from, but any point will do. The distance between two points is the number of one-unit steps that take you from one to the other. From −10-10−10 to −6-6−6 is four steps, and from −6-6−6 to −2-2−2 is four steps again. So −10-10−10 and −2-2−2 sit the same distance from −6-6−6, one on each side. That is the same pattern as −5-5−5 and 555 sitting either side of zero.

When the two points fall on opposite sides of zero, count the two pieces and add them. From −3-3−3 to 444 is three steps up to zero and then four more, which is seven steps in all.

The same counting finds the point halfway between two others. Count the steps from one to the other, halve that count, then count that far in from either end. From −10-10−10 to −2-2−2 is eight steps, and half of eight is four, so four steps in from either end lands on −6-6−6. The figure above shows both counts.

The word between asks a related question. Which integers are greater than −8-8−8 and less than −4-4−4? Walk right from −8-8−8 and keep only the numbers strictly inside: −7-7−7, −6-6−6 and −5-5−5. The two ends are left out, because “greater than −8-8−8” does not include −8-8−8 itself.

Check your understanding

Point A is four one-unit steps left of zero. Point B is at 666. Where is A, and how many one-unit steps separate A and B?

Answer choices

Four steps left of zero places A at −4-4−4. Count from −4-4−4 to 000, then from 000 to 666.

4+6=104+6=104+6=10

The points are ten one-unit steps apart.

Signed quantities in the real world

A sign records which side of a chosen reference point a quantity falls on. The situation tells you what counts as zero:

  • Temperature: −8∘-8^\circ−8∘ means eight degrees below zero; +8∘+8^\circ+8∘ means eight above. A colder temperature is a smaller number.
  • Money: a balance of −40-40−40 dollars means forty dollars owed; +40+40+40 means forty available.
  • Elevation: −30-30−30 meters means thirty meters below sea level; +30+30+30 means thirty above.
  • Time: in a launch countdown, −3-3−3 seconds means three seconds before liftoff.

Comparing signed quantities uses the same position rule. For example, −8∘-8^\circ−8∘ is colder than −3∘-3^\circ−3∘ because −8<−3-8 < -3−8<−3.

Check your understanding

A diver is 30 meters below sea level and a fish is 12 meters below sea level. Which signed elevations and comparison are correct?

Answer choices

Below sea level is negative, so record the diver at −30-30−30 meters and the fish at −12-12−12 meters.

−30<−12-30 < -12−30<−12

The diver has the lower elevation and is deeper.

Worked example 1 Order a list of integers from least to greatest

Order 3,  −4,  0,  −1,  23,\; -4,\; 0,\; -1,\; 23,−4,0,−1,2 from least to greatest.

Picture each number’s position on the line and then read them off from left to right, since left is least.

The most negative number is farthest left, so it comes first. The number −4-4−4 is four steps left of zero, and −1-1−1 is one step left, so −4-4−4 is less than −1-1−1. Zero sits at the center, to the right of both negatives. Then 222 is two steps right of zero and 333 is three steps right, so 2<32 < 32<3 and both are greater than zero.

Putting the positions in order from left to right gives

−4,  −1,  0,  2,  3.-4,\; -1,\; 0,\; 2,\; 3.−4,−1,0,2,3.

Worked example 2 Compare two negatives with the correct symbol

Fill the blank with <<< or >>> so the statement is true:   −6  □  −10\;-6 \;\square\; -10−6□−10.

Both numbers are negative, so compare them by position rather than by their digits. The point −6-6−6 is six steps left of zero, while −10-10−10 is ten steps left. Since −10-10−10 is farther to the left, it is the smaller of the two, which makes −6-6−6 the larger:

−6>−10.-6 > -10.−6>−10.

You can double-check with the wide-end rule: the open side of >>> faces −6-6−6, the larger number, and the point faces −10-10−10, the smaller.

Worked example 3 Compare two temperatures

On a winter morning a weather station records −5∘-5^\circ−5∘. By noon it records −2∘-2^\circ−2∘. Which reading is warmer, and how does the number line confirm it?

A warmer temperature is a greater number, which lies farther to the right on the line. Compare the two readings by position. The point −2-2−2 is two steps left of zero, while −5-5−5 is five steps left, so −2-2−2 lies farther to the right:

−2>−5.-2 > -5.−2>−5.

The noon reading is warmer, even though both readings are below zero.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Comparing the digits instead of the positions. Because 5>25 > 25>2 it is tempting to say −5>−2-5 > -2−5>−2, but a minus sign in front changes what the digits mean. Farther left is smaller, so −5<−2-5 < -2−5<−2, and −100<−3-100 < -3−100<−3 even though 100100100 is far bigger than 333. The farther from zero a negative number sits, the smaller it is.
  • Giving zero a sign. Zero is neither positive nor negative, and it is its own opposite. Writing −0-0−0 does not create a new number; −0-0−0 is just 000.
  • Treating negatives as not “real” numbers. A negative number is a genuine value with a fixed spot on the line, not a broken or missing positive. A balance of −40-40−40 dollars is a real (if unwelcome) amount you owe.
  • Treating every point on the line as an integer. Numbers like 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ and 2.72.72.7 have positions on the line, but they fall between the integer ticks, so they are not integers.

Key takeaways

  • A negative number is less than zero and sits to the left of zero on the number line; a positive number sits to the right. The distance between two points is the number of one-unit steps from one to the other, counted from any point, not only from zero.
  • The integers are {…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…}\{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}{…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…}: the whole numbers together with their negatives. Zero is an integer but is neither positive nor negative.
  • Nonzero opposites lie the same distance from zero on opposite sides, like 555 and −5-5−5. Taking the opposite twice returns the original, and zero is its own opposite.
  • To compare, read positions: the number farther right is greater, so −5<−2-5 < -2−5<−2 even though 5>25 > 25>2. Every negative number is less than zero and less than every positive number.
  • A sign records the side of a reference point or a direction (below zero, owed, below sea level). One number line models all such signed quantities.

What a flip across zero does to a comparison

Taking opposites reverses a comparison#

Start with −8<3-8 < 3−8<3. Taking opposites puts the first point at 888 and the second at −3-3−3, so 8>−38 > -38>−3.

Folding the whole line at zero reverses left and right while preserving distance from zero. For any two different points, the one that was left of the other therefore ends up to its right. Since right means greater, taking opposites reverses every strict comparison.

Equal numbers stay equal: 2=22 = 22=2 becomes −2=−2-2 = -2−2=−2.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

In 628, the Indian astronomer Brahmagupta wrote a book that treated debts as numbers you could calculate with. He called positive quantities fortunes and negative quantities debts. His rules described what happens when fortunes and debts are added, subtracted, multiplied, or divided.

This was part of a work on mathematics and astronomy, the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, written at Bhillamala, now Bhinmal in India. The everyday language of owing and owning appeared alongside problems about numbers and the sky. A debt taken from zero became a fortune; two debts multiplied together gave a fortune. Negative numbers were participating in arithmetic, not merely labeling a loss.

Brahmagupta also tried to give rules for division by zero. Those attempts did not settle the problem correctly, a reminder that this was mathematics being developed, not a finished textbook. The University of St Andrews’ account of his life and work includes these rules.

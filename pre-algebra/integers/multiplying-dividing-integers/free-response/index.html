    1. Counting out groups of a negative number . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    Multiplication of whole numbers is repeated addition. The count of groups still has to be a whole number that is not negative. The amount in each group is free to be negative. The parts below push that reading as far as it will go, and then ask what to do once it runs out.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write 4×(−9)4 \times (-9)4×(−9) as a repeated sum and evaluate it. Then name the rule from the previous lesson that settles the sign of a sum like the one you wrote.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Now evaluate 15×(−6)15 \times (-6)15×(−6) without writing fifteen terms down. State what you multiply. State what settles the sign. Then say why this shortcut has to agree with the repeated sum it stands in for.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The product (−6)×15(-6) \times 15(−6)×15 cannot be counted out, since "negative six groups of fifteen" describes nothing. Even so, this product has the same value as the one in part B. Name the property that guarantees that. Then say what the property lets you conclude about every product of a negative number and a positive number.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      A product counts groups, and the size of one group is free to be a negative number. Once the sum is on paper, one of the two addition rules from the previous lesson settles it. Decide which one by looking at what the four terms have in common.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      You never needed the written sum itself, only what it is made of: how many steps there are and how far each one goes. Settle the total distance first and the direction afterwards.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    4×(−9)=−364 \times (-9) = -364×(−9)=−36. The sum is four negative terms, and adding numbers that share a sign keeps that sign.

    Part B

    15×(−6)=−9015 \times (-6) = -9015×(−6)=−90: the sizes multiply to 909090, and the total is negative because every one of the fifteen equal steps goes left.

    Part C

    Multiplication is commutative, so exchanging the factors never changes a product. The order that can be counted out as groups gives the value of both. So a negative number times a positive number is negative in either order. Its size is the product of the two sizes.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The product 4×(−9)4 \times (-9)4×(−9) counts four groups, each of them −9-9−9. Writing that out as a sum,

    4×(−9)=(−9)+(−9)+(−9)+(−9).4 \times (-9) = (-9) + (-9) + (-9) + (-9).4×(−9)=(−9)+(−9)+(−9)+(−9).

    Every term is negative, so every step goes the same way along the number line, to the left. Adding numbers that share a sign adds the sizes and keeps the shared sign, so the total is 9+9+9+9=369 + 9 + 9 + 9 = 369+9+9+9=36 units to the left of zero:

    4×(−9)=−36.4 \times (-9) = -36.4×(−9)=−36.

    Notice what did not happen. The two numbers were not combined into −5-5−5 or −13-13−13: the 444 counts groups, and only the −9-9−9 is the thing being added.

    Part B

    Think about what the long sum would do rather than writing it. There would be fifteen steps, each of them six units to the left. The steps never turn around, so the sizes simply pile up:

    15×6=90.15 \times 6 = 90.15×6=90.

    That is the total distance travelled, and the direction of travel is left, so the total is ninety units to the left of zero:

    15×(−6)=−90.15 \times (-6) = -90.15×(−6)=−90.

    The shortcut agrees with the sum because it is a description of that sum: multiplying the sizes counts the total distance the equal steps cover, and the single negative factor is what points every step the same way. Multiply the sizes, then settle the sign.

    Part C

    Only one of the two orders can be read as groups. Fifteen groups of −6-6−6 is a sum you could in principle write out; six negative groups of anything is not something you can count.

    The rescue is a property you already have. Multiplication is commutative, which says that for any two numbers the order of the factors does not change the product. That was stated for all numbers, not only for positive ones, so it applies here:

    (−6)×15=15×(−6)=−90.(-6) \times 15 = 15 \times (-6) = -90.(−6)×15=15×(−6)=−90.

    The value of the order that has no meaning as groups is therefore fixed by the order that does.

    The conclusion is general. Take any product of a negative number and a positive number. Exchange the factors if necessary so that the positive one counts the groups. What you then have is a repeated sum of negative terms. That sum is negative, and its size is the product of the two sizes. So the answer never depends on which side the negative factor is written:

    (−6)×15=−90and15×(−6)=−90.(-6) \times 15 = -90 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 15 \times (-6) = -90.(−6)×15=−90and15×(−6)=−90.

    In one line

    4×(−9)=−364 \times (-9) = -364×(−9)=−36, the total of four negative terms. 15×(−6)=−9015 \times (-6) = -9015×(−6)=−90: the sizes give 909090, and the single negative factor makes the total negative. (−6)×15=−90(-6) \times 15 = -90(−6)×15=−90 as well, because multiplication is commutative. So a negative number times a positive number is negative in either order.

    2. What the distributive property forces . Reasoning, 12 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Reasoning 12 pts

    A product of two negative numbers cannot be counted out as groups, so its value cannot be read off a picture. Instead it is settled by the rules of arithmetic you already trust. The lesson ran this argument on (−3)×(−5)(-3) \times (-5)(−3)×(−5); here you run it yourself on different numbers.

    The answer

    Part A

    The parentheses hold a zero pair, so the whole product is 000. Distributing gives (−5)×8+(−5)×(−8)(-5) \times 8 + (-5) \times (-8)(−5)×8+(−5)×(−8).

    Part B

    (−5)×(−8)=40(-5) \times (-8) = 40(−5)×(−8)=40. It is whatever must be added to −40-40−40 to reach zero, and only the opposite of −40-40−40 does that.

    Part C

    The student's value makes the distributed form come to −80-80−80, while adding inside the parentheses first gives 000. That disagrees with the distributive property, which says the two routes have to match.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Inside the parentheses, 888 and −8-8−8 are opposites, so they form a zero pair:

    8+(−8)=0.8 + (-8) = 0.8+(−8)=0.

    Any number times zero is zero, so the whole expression is worth nothing at all:

    (−5)×(8+(−8))=(−5)×0=0.(-5) \times \bigl(8 + (-8)\bigr) = (-5) \times 0 = 0.(−5)×(8+(−8))=(−5)×0=0.

    Now take the other route. The distributive property says a factor standing outside a sum may be multiplied across each term of that sum. Multiplying the −5-5−5 across the two terms, and leaving both products alone,

    (−5)×(8+(−8))=(−5)×8+(−5)×(−8).(-5) \times \bigl(8 + (-8)\bigr) = (-5) \times 8 + (-5) \times (-8).(−5)×(8+(−8))=(−5)×8+(−5)×(−8).

    Both routes work out the same expression, so they have to give the same number. The first route already told us that number: it is 000.

    Part B

    The two expressions of part A are two readings of one and the same thing, so they are equal, and the first reading gave zero:

    (−5)×8+(−5)×(−8)=0.(-5) \times 8 + (-5) \times (-8) = 0.(−5)×8+(−5)×(−8)=0.

    The first product has exactly one negative factor, so it is negative, with size 5×8=405 \times 8 = 405×8=40:

    (−5)×8=−40.(-5) \times 8 = -40.(−5)×8=−40.

    Put that in, and only one product is left whose value you do not know yet:

    −40+(−5)×(−8)=0.-40 + (-5) \times (-8) = 0.−40+(−5)×(−8)=0.

    Read that line as a question. It says that (−5)×(−8)(-5) \times (-8)(−5)×(−8) is the number you must add to −40-40−40 to reach zero. Only one number does that to −40-40−40, and that number is its opposite, 404040. So

    (−5)×(−8)=40.(-5) \times (-8) = 40.(−5)×(−8)=40.

    Nothing about the answer was assumed in advance, and no chart was consulted. Three facts you already trust force the value: a number and its opposite add to zero, any number times zero is zero, and multiplication distributes over addition.

    Part C

    Put the student's value into the distributed form and work it out:

    (−5)×8+(−5)×(−8)=−40+(−40)=−80.(-5) \times 8 + (-5) \times (-8) = -40 + (-40) = -80.(−5)×8+(−5)×(−8)=−40+(−40)=−80.

    Now compare that with the other route from part A. Adding inside the parentheses first gives (−5)×0(-5) \times 0(−5)×0, which is 000. So the student's value requires

    −80=0,-80 = 0,−80=0,

    which is false.

    The rule it disagrees with is the distributive property, since that is the rule saying the two routes must match. The other two rules used along the way both held up: a number and its opposite add to zero, and any number times zero is zero. Only 404040 leaves all three standing, which is why the value is not something anyone gets to choose.

    In one line

    Adding inside the parentheses gives (−5)×0=0(-5) \times 0 = 0(−5)×0=0. Distributing gives (−5)×8+(−5)×(−8)(-5) \times 8 + (-5) \times (-8)(−5)×8+(−5)×(−8). Setting the two equal forces (−5)×(−8)=40(-5) \times (-8) = 40(−5)×(−8)=40, since only 404040 brings −40-40−40 up to zero. The student's −40-40−40 fails.     Another way: Walk a pattern down past zero

    Hold one factor at −5-5−5 and let the other count down one step at a time, using only products already settled:

    (−5)×2=−10,(−5)×1=−5,(−5)×0=0.(-5) \times 2 = -10, \qquad (-5) \times 1 = -5, \qquad (-5) \times 0 = 0.(−5)×2=−10,(−5)×1=−5,(−5)×0=0.

    Each step down in the second factor adds 555 to the product. Keeping that step unbroken past zero gives (−5)×(−1)=5(-5) \times (-1) = 5(−5)×(−1)=5, then (−5)×(−2)=10(-5) \times (-2) = 10(−5)×(−2)=10, and eight steps past zero gives 404040.

    3. A steady descent, in feet per minute . Application, 10 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 10 pts

    A submersible drops at a constant rate, so every minute changes its depth by the same signed amount. Depths below the surface are recorded as negative numbers, and a change downward is negative for the same reason.

    1. A
      Part A.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The total change is 8×(−11)=−888 \times (-11) = -888×(−11)=−88 feet, so the reading is −88-88−88 feet, which is 88 feet below the surface.

    Part B

    The change is −16-16−16 feet per minute, since 6×(−16)=−966 \times (-16) = -966×(−16)=−96 feet.

    Part C

    It answers how long the descent lasts: (−84)÷(−12)=7(-84) \div (-12) = 7(−84)÷(−12)=7 minutes. The quotient counts how many equal changes make up the whole change, and a count of minutes cannot be negative.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Eight minutes each contribute the same change of −11-11−11 feet, so the total change is that amount taken eight times over:

    8×(−11)=−88 feet.8 \times (-11) = -88 \text{ feet}.8×(−11)=−88 feet.

    Exactly one factor is negative, so the total change is negative, and its size is 8×11=888 \times 11 = 888×11=88.

    The craft started at the surface, where the reading is 000 feet, so the reading afterwards is 0+(−88)=−880 + (-88) = -880+(−88)=−88 feet. In the language of the situation, it is 88 feet below the surface. The minus sign is not decoration: it is what records the direction of the change.

    Part B

    The whole change is split into six equal pieces, one per minute, so the question is which number taken six times rebuilds −96-96−96. That is a division:

    (−96)÷6=−16 feet per minute.(-96) \div 6 = -16 \text{ feet per minute}.(−96)÷6=−16 feet per minute.

    The two numbers being divided have different signs, so the quotient is negative, and the sizes divide as usual: 96÷6=1696 \div 6 = 1696÷6=16.

    Checking by multiplying back is the definition of division at work:

    6×(−16)=−96.6 \times (-16) = -96. 6×(−16)=−96.

    The original total change comes back, so −16-16−16 feet per minute is right. The sign is telling you the craft is going down, which is what the story said.

    Part C

    Three quantities are in play on any of these dives: the change per minute, the number of minutes, and the total change. The division names two of them and leaves out the number of minutes, so that is the question it asks:

    (−84)÷(−12)=7 minutes.(-84) \div (-12) = 7 \text{ minutes}.(−84)÷(−12)=7 minutes.

    Read it aloud as a question about the dive: how many changes of −12-12−12 feet does it take to make a change of −84-84−84 feet? Seven of them. The check is the multiplication it undoes:

    7×(−12)=−84.7 \times (-12) = -84. 7×(−12)=−84.

    Now the sign. The quotient counts minutes. There is no such thing as a negative number of minutes, so the answer has to be positive. The general rule agrees, because the two numbers being divided share a sign. Here you can also see why it has to. Both negatives describe the same downward direction. Asking how many of one make up the other throws that shared direction away and leaves a plain count.

    In one line

    (−6)×(−7)=42,so42÷(−7)=−6.(-6) \times (-7) = 42, \qquad \text{so} \qquad 42 \div (-7) = -6.(−6)×(−7)=42,so42÷(−7)=−6.

    Another way: Count the negative numbers instead

    As long as every number involved is nonzero, the sizes divide as usual and the sign is decided by how many of the numbers are negative.

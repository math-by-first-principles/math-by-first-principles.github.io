Multiplying and Dividing Integers

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Three groups of four give 3×4=123\times4=123×4=12. Three groups of negative four also make sense: (−4)+(−4)+(−4)=−12(-4)+(-4)+(-4)=-12(−4)+(−4)+(−4)=−12. But what could (−3)×(−4)(-3)\times(-4)(−3)×(−4) mean? You cannot count out negative three groups. To extend multiplication, we keep the arithmetic properties you already know and find what answer they require.

Learning goals

  • Read a positive times a negative as repeated addition of the negative
  • Explain why a negative times a negative has to come out positive
  • Multiply the sizes, then count the negative factors to fix the sign
  • Divide by naming the missing factor, so division needs no sign rules of its own
  • Show why dividing by zero is undefined

A positive times a negative

For a positive whole-number count, multiplication means repeated addition. Three groups of −4-4−4 give

3×(−4)=(−4)+(−4)+(−4).3\times(-4)=(-4)+(-4)+(-4).3×(−4)=(−4)+(−4)+(−4).

Three leftward steps of four units land twelve units left of zero:

So 3×(−4)=−123\times(-4)=-123×(−4)=−12. The same reasoning works for any positive number of equal negative groups: their distances add, and every step goes left.

Positive times negative: multiply the sizes and make the answer negative.

For (−3)×5(-3)\times5(−3)×5, the negative factor cannot count the groups. We keep the commutative property from the foundations chapter: swapping factors keeps the product. This lets us use five groups of −3-3−3:

(−3)×5=5×(−3)=−15.(-3)\times5=5\times(-3)=-15.(−3)×5=5×(−3)=−15.

Either order gives the same sign rule: one positive factor and one negative factor give a negative product.

A negative times a negative is positive

Swapping the factors cannot turn (−3)×(−5)(-3)\times(-5)(−3)×(−5) into a positive count of groups. We need another reason. First look for a pattern: hold the first factor at −3-3−3 and decrease the second factor by one each time.

Each step down in the second factor adds 333 to the product: −6-6−6, then −3-3−3, then 000. Keeping that same step going past zero gives 333 and then 666. So the pattern predicts (−3)×(−1)=3(-3) \times (-1) = 3(−3)×(−1)=3 and (−3)×(−2)=6(-3) \times (-2) = 6(−3)×(−2)=6, both positive.

The pattern suggests an answer; the distributive property explains why we need it. Since 5+(−5)=05+(-5)=05+(−5)=0, multiplying that sum by −3-3−3 gives

(−3)×(5+(−5))=0.(-3)\times\bigl(5+(-5)\bigr)=0.(−3)×(5+(−5))=0.

Now work out that same expression the other way. The distributive property from the foundations chapter lets a factor outside a pair of parentheses multiply each term inside:

(−3)×(5+(−5))=(−3)×5+(−3)×(−5).(-3) \times \bigl(5 + (-5)\bigr) = (-3) \times 5 + (-3) \times (-5).(−3)×(5+(−5))=(−3)×5+(−3)×(−5).

The expression is zero, and the first product on the right is −15-15−15. Therefore

−15+(−3)×(−5)=0.-15+(-3)\times(-5)=0.−15+(−3)×(−5)=0.

Only 151515 adds to −15-15−15 to make zero. So (−3)×(−5)=15(-3)\times(-5)=15(−3)×(−5)=15 is the answer that preserves the distributive property.

Nothing depends on the sizes 333 and 555. Start with any positive number and its negative: their sum is zero. Multiply by a negative factor and distribute. The first product is negative, so the second must be its positive opposite to make the total zero.

Negative times negative: multiply the sizes and make the answer positive.

Answer choices

The first product has one negative factor, so (−4)×6=−24(-4) \times 6 = -24(−4)×6=−24. Putting that in leaves

−24+(−4)×(−6)=0.-24 + (-4) \times (-6) = 0.−24+(−4)×(−6)=0.

Only 242424 brings −24-24−24 up to zero, so (−4)×(−6)=24(-4) \times (-6) = 24(−4)×(−6)=24. Any other value would make the distributed form disagree with the 000 we got by adding inside the parentheses first.

For nonzero factors, a useful memory aid is that each negative factor flips the sign once. Two negative factors flip it twice, returning to positive. The zero-pair argument above is the reason behind that shortcut.

The sign rules, gathered

For factors that are not zero, the sign of a product depends on nothing but how many of them are negative. Take (−3)×(−5)=15(-3) \times (-5) = 15(−3)×(−5)=15, which has two negative factors and lands positive. Multiply that by one more negative, 15×(−2)=−3015 \times (-2) = -3015×(−2)=−30, and now there are three negative factors and the answer lands negative.

Every case above reduces to one idea, for factors that are not zero: multiply the sizes, then count the negative factors. An even number of negative factors gives a positive product; an odd number gives a negative product, because each negative flips the sign once.

For two nonzero factors, matching signs give a positive product and different signs give a negative one. Zero is neither positive nor negative, so the chart has no row for it. If any factor is zero, the product is zero, as in 0×(−7)=00\times(-7)=00×(−7)=0. Check for zero before counting negative factors.

Worked example 1 Multiply three signed numbers: (−2)×5×(−3)(-2) \times 5 \times (-3)(−2)×5×(−3)

Work left to right, finding the size and the sign at each step. The sizes multiply just like ordinary whole numbers; only the sign needs care.

First multiply (−2)×5(-2) \times 5(−2)×5. Exactly one factor is negative, so the product is negative:

(−2)×5=−10.(-2) \times 5 = -10.(−2)×5=−10.

Now multiply that by the last factor, (−10)×(−3)(-10) \times (-3)(−10)×(−3). Both are negative, so the two sign flips cancel and the product is positive:

(−10)×(−3)=30.(-10) \times (-3) = 30.(−10)×(−3)=30.

A quick check using the shortcut: there are two negative factors in all (−2-2−2 and −3-3−3), an even count. So the final sign is positive, and the size is 2×5×3=302 \times 5 \times 3 = 302×5×3=30.

Division undoes multiplication

Division asks for a missing factor. For example, 12÷412\div412÷4 asks which number times 444 gives 121212. Since 3×4=123\times4=123×4=12, the answer is 333. This meaning works for negatives too.

Take (−12)÷4(-12) \div 4(−12)÷4. What number times 444 gives −12-12−12? Not a positive one, since a positive times a positive stays positive. The missing factor is negative, with size 333:

(−12)÷4=−3,because (−3)×4=−12.(-12) \div 4 = -3, \qquad \text{because } (-3) \times 4 = -12.(−12)÷4=−3,because (−3)×4=−12.

Now take (−12)÷(−4)(-12) \div (-4)(−12)÷(−4). What number times −4-4−4 gives −12-12−12? Here 3×(−4)=−123 \times (-4) = -123×(−4)=−12 does the job, so the missing factor is positive:

(−12)÷(−4)=3,because 3×(−4)=−12.(-12) \div (-4) = 3, \qquad \text{because } 3 \times (-4) = -12.(−12)÷(−4)=3,because 3×(−4)=−12.

That second answer is easier to believe in a real situation. A submarine drops 666 feet every minute, a change of −6-6−6 feet per minute, and over the whole dive its height changes by −24-24−24 feet. How long did the dive last? It takes four of those changes to make −24-24−24, so (−24)÷(−6)=4(-24) \div (-6) = 4(−24)÷(−6)=4 minutes. The quotient counts minutes, and a count of minutes cannot be negative.

Division uses the multiplication facts in reverse, so it follows the same sign rule.

When neither number is zero, divide the sizes. Matching signs give a positive quotient; different signs give a negative quotient.

Why you cannot divide by zero

For 6÷06\div06÷0, the missing factor would have to satisfy ?×0=6?\times0=6?×0=6. Every number times zero is zero, so none works. The quotient is undefined, meaning it has no assigned value.

For 0÷00\div00÷0, every number satisfies ?×0=0?\times0=0?×0=0. There is no single answer to choose. Compare these with zero divided by a nonzero number:

A fraction bar groups the whole top and the whole bottom, acting like parentheses around each. So work out the numerator (the top) first, then divide by the denominator (the bottom).

The numerator is a negative times a negative, which is positive:

(−5)×(−6)=30.(-5) \times (-6) = 30.(−5)×(−6)=30.

Now divide by −3-3−3. The signs differ (positive numerator, negative denominator), so the quotient is negative:

30÷(−3)=−10.30 \div (-3) = -10.30÷(−3)=−10.

So the whole expression equals −10-10−10. Counting negatives is a fast check here too, as long as no number involved is zero. There are two negatives on top and one on the bottom, which makes three negative numbers in all. That is an odd count, so the answer is negative.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding instead of multiplying. 3×(−4)3 \times (-4)3×(−4) is −12-12−12, not −7-7−7 or −1-1−1. Multiplication means groups of, so multiply the sizes (3×4=123 \times 4 = 123×4=12) and then fix the sign. Do not slip back into adding the two numbers.
  • Thinking a negative times a negative is negative. It is positive: (−6)×(−2)=12(-6) \times (-2) = 12(−6)×(−2)=12. Any other answer would break the distributive property, which is the argument you worked through above.
  • Losing track of the sign count in a long product. With no zero among the factors, count the negative factors: an even count gives a positive result, an odd count a negative one. In (−1)×(−2)×(−3)(-1) \times (-2) \times (-3)(−1)×(−2)×(−3) there are three negatives, so the answer is negative (−6-6−6).
  • Assuming division needs its own sign rules. It does not. Since a÷ba \div ba÷b is the missing factor in a multiplication, the quotient’s sign follows the very same same-signs/different-signs rule.
  • Trying to divide by zero. 50\frac{5}{0}05​ is undefined: no number times zero gives 555. Watch for a hidden zero in a denominator. Dividing zero by a nonzero number is fine and gives 000.

Key takeaways

  • Positive times negative: repeated addition gives a negative total. Swapping the factors keeps that product.
  • Negative times negative: the product is positive, as the distributive property requires.
  • Product signs: for nonzero factors, an even number of negatives gives positive, an odd number gives negative. Any zero factor makes the product zero.
  • Division: find the missing factor. For nonzero numbers, matching signs give positive and different signs give negative.
  • Zero in division: dividing by zero is undefined, including 0÷00\div00÷0. Zero divided by a nonzero number is 000.

A bit of history (optional)

As a schoolboy in France, the future novelist Stendhal kept asking why minus times minus gives plus. He liked mathematics because he expected clear reasons. This rule made him wonder whether the certainty he admired was real.

In his memoir Life of Henry Brulard, he recalled pressing his teacher, Mr. Chabert, for an explanation. Chabert repeated the lesson; Stendhal still could not see why it worked. Other strong students laughed at his question and learned the rule by heart. One explanation compared negatives with debts, but Stendhal could not see how multiplying debts could leave someone with a fortune.

Looking back years later, he said he had accepted the rule because calculations using it kept giving correct results. That was not the explanation he had wanted. The frustration survived long enough to become a scene in his memoir, which you can read in Stendhal’s own account, chapter 34, in French.

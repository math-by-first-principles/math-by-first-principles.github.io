Absolute Value

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

When multiplying integers, you found the size first and then the sign. Now we give that size a name: absolute value. A diver 303030 meters below sea level and a kite 303030 meters above it are both 303030 meters from sea level. Absolute value records that distance without its direction.

Learning goals

  • Find any integer's absolute value by reading its distance from zero, so the answer is never negative
  • Explain why a number and its opposite share one absolute value
  • Say why the rule needs two cases, and why the negative case still gives a positive answer
  • Simplify inside the bars before measuring, and handle a minus sign written outside them
  • Measure how far apart two numbers are as the absolute value of their difference

Distance, without the direction

A number’s position tells you both its distance from zero and its side of zero. Sometimes only the distance matters: −5-5−5 and 555 are on different sides, but both are five units away.

The absolute value of a number is its distance from zero on the number line. An absolute value is never negative. Zero has distance zero; every other point has a positive distance.

The notation

Put vertical bars around a number to ask for its absolute value. Read ∣−5∣\lvert-5\rvert∣−5∣ as “the absolute value of negative five.” Since −5-5−5 is five units from zero,

∣−5∣=5.\lvert-5\rvert=5.∣−5∣=5.

The bars return a number. Thus ∣7∣=7\lvert7\rvert=7∣7∣=7, ∣−7∣=7\lvert-7\rvert=7∣−7∣=7, and ∣0∣=0\lvert0\rvert=0∣0∣=0. After you measure the distance, replace the bars and their contents with that value.

Answer choices

The point −12-12−12 lies twelve units left of zero. Absolute value records the distance, not the side.

∣−12∣=12\lvert-12\rvert=12∣−12∣=12

Why the rule splits into two cases

For a written negative number, absolute value looks like removing its minus sign. But a letter might stand for a positive number, a negative number, or zero. We need a rule that handles each possibility.

Why ∣x∣=x\lvert x \rvert = x∣x∣=x when x≥0x \ge 0x≥0 and ∣x∣=−x\lvert x \rvert = -x∣x∣=−x when x<0x < 0x<0#

The point 666 is six units right of zero, so ∣6∣=6\lvert6\rvert=6∣6∣=6: keep the number itself. The point −4-4−4 is four units left of zero, so ∣−4∣=−(−4)=4\lvert-4\rvert=-(-4)=4∣−4∣=−(−4)=4: take its opposite. Zero is zero units from itself, so ∣0∣=0\lvert0\rvert=0∣0∣=0.

Nothing depends on choosing 666 and −4-4−4. Let xxx stand for any integer. If it is positive or zero, its value already gives its distance from zero. If it is negative, its positive opposite gives that distance. Every integer falls into one of these two cases.

The symbol ≥\ge≥ means “greater than or equal to.” We can write the cases as

∣x∣=xwhen x≥0,∣x∣=−xwhen x<0.\begin{gathered} \lvert x\rvert=x\quad\text{when }x\ge0,\\ \lvert x\rvert=-x\quad\text{when }x<0. \end{gathered}∣x∣=xwhen x≥0,∣x∣=−xwhen x<0.​

In the second case, −x-x−x means the opposite of a negative number, so it is positive. Neither case returns a negative distance.

∎

Check your understanding

Why does the absolute-value rule keep xxx when x≥0x\ge0x≥0 but use −x-x−x when x<0x<0x<0?

Answer choices

A positive number or zero already gives its distance from zero. A negative number does not, so use its opposite.

∣−6∣=−(−6)=6\lvert-6\rvert=-(-6)=6∣−6∣=−(−6)=6

The minus in −x-x−x means take the opposite; it does not guarantee a negative result.

A number and its opposite share one absolute value

Opposites are the same distance from zero, so ∣8∣=∣−8∣=8\lvert8\rvert=\lvert-8\rvert=8∣8∣=∣−8∣=8. Folding at zero changes the side but preserves that distance. For any integer xxx,

∣x∣=∣−x∣.\lvert x\rvert=\lvert-x\rvert.∣x∣=∣−x∣.

Only zero has absolute value zero. Every other number lies a positive distance away.

This also helps you find numbers at a given distance. Exactly two integers are four units from zero, 444 and −4-4−4. In symbols, ∣x∣=4\lvert x\rvert=4∣x∣=4 asks for those two numbers. If xxx must also be negative, only −4-4−4 works; if it must be positive, only 444 works. If the required distance is zero, only 000 works. A negative distance is impossible.

For a distance of at most one unit, list the integer points inside and on the ends: −1,0,1-1,0,1−1,0,1. For a distance of less than one unit, leave out the ends: only 000 remains.

Check your understanding

Why do 666 and −6-6−6 have equal absolute value, and which integer has absolute value zero?

Answer choices

Folding at zero preserves distance, so both opposite points are six units away.

∣6∣=∣−6∣=6\lvert6\rvert=\lvert-6\rvert=6∣6∣=∣−6∣=6

Only the point 000 has no distance from zero: ∣0∣=0\lvert0\rvert=0∣0∣=0.

The bars are grouping symbols

Absolute-value bars are grouping symbols, like parentheses. Finish the arithmetic inside before measuring its distance from zero.

Compare two expressions that look almost identical:

∣(−3)+5∣versus∣−3∣+∣5∣.\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert \qquad \text{versus} \qquad \lvert -3 \rvert + \lvert 5 \rvert.∣(−3)+5∣versus∣−3∣+∣5∣.

In the first, the bars group the sum, so add inside first and then take the absolute value:

∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2.\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert = \lvert 2 \rvert = 2.∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2.

In the second, each number has its own bars, so take each absolute value first and then add:

∣−3∣+∣5∣=3+5=8.\lvert -3 \rvert + \lvert 5 \rvert = 3 + 5 = 8.∣−3∣+∣5∣=3+5=8.

The first measures the sum; the second measures the two numbers separately and adds their distances. Since 222 and 888 differ, you cannot split bars across a sum.

A minus sign written outside the bars

A minus sign outside the bars acts after the absolute value. For example,

−∣−4∣=−(4)=−4.-\lvert-4\rvert=-(4)=-4.−∣−4∣=−(4)=−4.

The bars returned the positive distance 444. The outside minus then took its opposite. The final result is negative because it is the opposite of an absolute value.

Answer choices

(−8)+3=−5(-8)+3=-5(−8)+3=−5

The bars give the distance 555, and then the outside minus takes its opposite.

−∣−5∣=−(5)=−5-\lvert-5\rvert=-(5)=-5−∣−5∣=−(5)=−5

Worked examples

Worked example 1 Evaluate ∣6−10∣\lvert 6 - 10 \rvert∣6−10∣

The bars are grouping symbols, so do the subtraction inside before taking the absolute value. Subtract first:

6−10=−4.6 - 10 = -4.6−10=−4.

Now take the absolute value of that single number. The point −4-4−4 is four units from zero:

∣6−10∣=∣−4∣=4.\lvert 6 - 10 \rvert = \lvert -4 \rvert = 4.∣6−10∣=∣−4∣=4.

A common slip is to compute ∣6∣−∣10∣=6−10=−4\lvert 6 \rvert - \lvert 10 \rvert = 6 - 10 = -4∣6∣−∣10∣=6−10=−4, but that ignores the grouping and even produces a negative result, which an absolute value can never be. Inside first, then the bars.

Worked example 2 Compare ∣−7∣\lvert -7 \rvert∣−7∣ and ∣5∣\lvert 5 \rvert∣5∣

Replace each absolute value with the distance it stands for, then compare those numbers the usual way.

The first is the distance of −7-7−7 from zero:

∣−7∣=7.\lvert -7 \rvert = 7.∣−7∣=7.

The second is the distance of 555 from zero:

∣5∣=5.\lvert 5 \rvert = 5.∣5∣=5.

Now compare the results: 7>57 > 57>5, so

∣−7∣>∣5∣.\lvert -7 \rvert > \lvert 5 \rvert.∣−7∣>∣5∣.

Even though −7<5-7 < 5−7<5 as signed numbers, −7-7−7 is farther from zero than 555 is, so it has the larger absolute value. Distance and order are different questions: one asks how far, the other asks which number is greater.

Distance between two numbers

The distance between 333 and 888 is five one-unit steps. Subtracting in the two orders gives opposites, but absolute value gives the same distance:

∣3−8∣=∣−5∣=5,∣8−3∣=∣5∣=5.\begin{gathered} \lvert3-8\rvert=\lvert-5\rvert=5,\\ \lvert8-3\rvert=\lvert5\rvert=5. \end{gathered}∣3−8∣=∣−5∣=5,∣8−3∣=∣5∣=5.​

The difference records the gap with a direction. Reversing the subtraction reverses that direction, but not the gap’s size. Absolute value keeps just the size.

This does not depend on 333 and 888. For any integers aaa and bbb, subtracting bbb from aaa records the move from bbb to aaa: positive for a rightward move, negative for a leftward move. Its absolute value is the number of one-unit steps between them. If the points coincide, the difference and the distance are both zero.

distance=∣a−b∣.\text{distance}=\lvert a-b\rvert.distance=∣a−b∣.

Subtract in either order: a−ba-ba−b and b−ab-ab−a are opposites and have the same absolute value.

This works across zero too. The distance between −2-2−2 and 444 is

∣−2−4∣=∣−6∣=6,\lvert -2 - 4 \rvert = \lvert -6 \rvert = 6,∣−2−4∣=∣−6∣=6,

and the walk splits neatly at zero: two steps from −2-2−2 up to zero, then four more out to 444.

Worked example 3 Find the distance between −6-6−6 and 222

Use the distance rule: subtract the two numbers and take the absolute value of the result. Subtract first, remembering that subtracting a positive moves left:

−6−2=−8.-6 - 2 = -8.−6−2=−8.

Now take the absolute value to discard the sign and keep the size of the gap:

∣−6−2∣=∣−8∣=8.\lvert -6 - 2 \rvert = \lvert -8 \rvert = 8.∣−6−2∣=∣−8∣=8.

The two numbers are 888 units apart. You can confirm the answer by walking the line. Six one-unit steps take you from −6-6−6 up to zero, and two more reach 222, which is eight steps in all.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Thinking ∣x∣=−x\lvert x \rvert = -x∣x∣=−x is negative. When xxx is negative, −x-x−x is its opposite, and the opposite of a negative number is positive. So ∣−5∣=−(−5)=5\lvert -5 \rvert = -(-5) = 5∣−5∣=−(−5)=5, not −5-5−5.
  • Splitting the bars across an operation. The bars group what is inside, so ∣a+b∣\lvert a + b \rvert∣a+b∣ is not ∣a∣+∣b∣\lvert a \rvert + \lvert b \rvert∣a∣+∣b∣ in general. Compare ∣(−3)+5∣=2\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert = 2∣(−3)+5∣=2 with ∣−3∣+∣5∣=8\lvert -3 \rvert + \lvert 5 \rvert = 8∣−3∣+∣5∣=8. Finish the inside first.
  • Dropping a minus sign that sits outside the bars. The bars measure only what stands between them. In −∣−4∣-\lvert -4 \rvert−∣−4∣ they return 444, and the minus in front then makes it −4-4−4. That answer is allowed to be negative, because it is the opposite of an absolute value rather than one.
  • Confusing “largest” with “largest absolute value.” As signed numbers −9<6-9 < 6−9<6, yet ∣−9∣=9\lvert -9 \rvert = 9∣−9∣=9 beats ∣6∣=6\lvert 6 \rvert = 6∣6∣=6, because a very negative number is a long way from zero.
  • Forgetting that distance has no direction. In ∣a−b∣\lvert a - b \rvert∣a−b∣, subtracting in either order gives the same answer, since a−ba - ba−b and b−ab - ab−a are opposites and share one absolute value.

Key takeaways

  • The absolute value ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣ is the distance from xxx to zero on the number line, so it is always zero or positive.
  • The rule splits into two cases: ∣x∣=x\lvert x \rvert = x∣x∣=x when x≥0x \ge 0x≥0, and ∣x∣=−x\lvert x \rvert = -x∣x∣=−x (the opposite, which is positive) when x<0x < 0x<0.
  • A number and its opposite share one absolute value: ∣x∣=∣−x∣\lvert x \rvert = \lvert -x \rvert∣x∣=∣−x∣, for example ∣8∣=∣−8∣=8\lvert 8 \rvert = \lvert -8 \rvert = 8∣8∣=∣−8∣=8. The only number with absolute value 000 is 000.
  • The bars are grouping symbols: simplify what is inside down to one number first, then take its distance, so ∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2\lvert (-3) + 5 \rvert = \lvert 2 \rvert = 2∣(−3)+5∣=∣2∣=2. A minus sign written outside the bars is not measured, so −∣−4∣=−4-\lvert -4 \rvert = -4−∣−4∣=−4.
  • The distance between two numbers aaa and bbb is ∣a−b∣\lvert a - b \rvert∣a−b∣, and the order of subtraction does not change it.

A bit of history (optional)

The two absolute-value bars look simple, but their history has a puzzle. Writers once described the idea in words. Some later shortened those words; others used round brackets. The familiar pair of vertical lines was not always the obvious choice.

Karl Weierstrass is often credited with introducing the bars in 1841. But the work attached to that date was printed much later, in 1894. Historian Klaus Viertel compared it with notes taken by Weierstrass’s students. Notes from the 1860s still used words or round brackets; the modern bars appear in a set from 1870 and 1871.

That evidence makes a neat invention date harder to pin down. It also gives us a glimpse of mathematical notation while it was still changing: the teacher’s words, students’ handwritten notes, and a later printed book do not always use the same symbols. Viertel discusses the evidence in his study of Weierstrass’s lectures.

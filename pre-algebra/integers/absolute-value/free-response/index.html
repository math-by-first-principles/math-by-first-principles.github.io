    The answer

    Part A

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Each number has a position on the line, and the bars ask only how far that position is from zero.

    The number −13-13−13 sits thirteen units to the left of zero, 444 sits four units to the right, and 111111 sits eleven units to the right:

    ∣−13∣=13,∣4∣=4,∣11∣=11.\lvert -13 \rvert = 13, \qquad \lvert 4 \rvert = 4, \qquad \lvert 11 \rvert = 11.∣−13∣=13,∣4∣=4,∣11∣=11.

    One of the three inputs was negative and all three outputs came out positive. That is not luck. The bars report a count of unit steps, and the side of zero a number sits on is not part of that count.

    Part B

    An integer with absolute value 666 is an integer six units from zero, so start at zero and walk six units. There are two directions to walk, so there are two places to land:

    ∣6∣=6,∣−6∣=6.\lvert 6 \rvert = 6, \qquad \lvert -6 \rvert = 6.∣6∣=6,∣−6∣=6.

    No third integer qualifies, because every other integer sits some other number of units from zero. The two that do qualify are opposites, and that is exactly why they tie: opposites are mirror images across zero, so they are the same distance from it.

    Now walk zero units from zero. You never leave, so there is only one place to land:

    ∣0∣=0.\lvert 0 \rvert = 0.∣0∣=0.

    The second list is shorter than the first because zero is the one number whose opposite is itself.

    Part C

    No integer has an absolute value of −4-4−4, and the reason sits in the definition rather than in any calculation.

    The bars report a distance: the number of unit steps between the number and zero on the line. A count of steps starts at none and grows from there, so the smallest value it can take is 000, and that value belongs to zero alone:

    ∣x∣≥0for every integer x.\lvert x \rvert \ge 0 \quad \text{for every integer } x.∣x∣≥0for every integer x.

    A value of −4-4−4 is less than 000, so nothing produces it.

    Trying numbers could not have settled this. Testing twenty integers rules out twenty integers, and the claim is about every integer. The argument above needs no list, because it works from what the bars measure.

    In one line

    ∣−13∣=13\lvert -13 \rvert = 13∣−13∣=13, ∣4∣=4\lvert 4 \rvert = 4∣4∣=4 and ∣11∣=11\lvert 11 \rvert = 11∣11∣=11, so those numbers sit 131313, 444 and 111111 units from zero; the integers with absolute value 666 are 666 and −6-6−6, and that list is finished because six units can be walked from zero in only two directions, while the only integer with absolute value 000 is 000, since a walk of no units never leaves zero; and no integer has an absolute value of −4-4−4, because the bars report a count of unit steps from zero and no count is below none. Testing numbers could not have settled that last question, since each test rules out only the number tried while the claim is about every integer.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The two expressions differ only in where the bars are drawn, and that is enough to change what gets measured.

    In the first, one pair of bars encloses the whole sum, so the addition is finished before any distance is read. Adding a positive number to a negative one moves right along the line, and starting at −7-7−7 and moving two units right lands at −5-5−5:

    ∣(−7)+2∣=∣−5∣=5.\lvert (-7) + 2 \rvert = \lvert -5 \rvert = 5.∣(−7)+2∣=∣−5∣=5.

    In the second, each number carries its own pair of bars, so two distances are read off first and only then added:

    ∣−7∣+∣2∣=7+2=9.\lvert -7 \rvert + \lvert 2 \rvert = 7 + 2 = 9.∣−7∣+∣2∣=7+2=9.

    Same two numbers, same addition sign, two different results.

    Part B

    In the first expression the bars enclose a subtraction, so carry it out before measuring. Taking eleven away from 444 moves eleven units left and crosses zero:

    ∣4−11∣=∣−7∣=7.\lvert 4 - 11 \rvert = \lvert -7 \rvert = 7.∣4−11∣=∣−7∣=7.

    The second expression has a symbol written outside the bars, which means the bars never enclosed it. They act on −8-8−8 alone and return the distance 888, and the minus sign standing in front then takes the opposite of that:

    −∣−8∣=−(8)=−8.-\lvert -8 \rvert = -(8) = -8.−∣−8∣=−(8)=−8.

    The second result is negative and nothing has gone wrong, because it is not an absolute value. It is the opposite of one. The rule that an absolute value is never negative is a promise about what comes out from between the bars, and this minus sign was never between them.

    Part C

    Read each expression as an instruction about what to do first.

    In ∣(−7)+2∣\lvert (-7) + 2 \rvert∣(−7)+2∣ the bars enclose the whole sum, so the sum is the thing whose distance is measured. The numbers combine into one number before anything is measured, and the positive 222 pulls that number back toward zero:

    ∣(−7)+2∣=∣−5∣=5.\lvert (-7) + 2 \rvert = \lvert -5 \rvert = 5.∣(−7)+2∣=∣−5∣=5.

    In ∣−7∣+∣2∣\lvert -7 \rvert + \lvert 2 \rvert∣−7∣+∣2∣ each number is enclosed separately, so nothing combines before measuring. Two distances are read off and then added, and neither distance remembers which side of zero its number came from:

    ∣−7∣+∣2∣=7+2=9.\lvert -7 \rvert + \lvert 2 \rvert = 7 + 2 = 9.∣−7∣+∣2∣=7+2=9.

    So the two forms may not be exchanged. Stripping the signs before adding throws away the cancellation that adding a positive number to a negative one produces, which is why the second arrangement comes out the larger of the two here. Rewriting ∣a+b∣\lvert a + b \rvert∣a+b∣ as ∣a∣+∣b∣\lvert a \rvert + \lvert b \rvert∣a∣+∣b∣ is the same kind of error as replacing a quantity grouped in parentheses by the loose pieces inside it.

    3. How far the temperature moved . Application, 10 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 10 pts

    A weather station logs its temperature at dawn and again in the afternoon, and the two readings can land on either side of zero. How much the temperature moved is the gap between two points on the number line, which is the absolute value of their difference.

    1. A
      Part A.

      At dawn a station read −14-14−14 degrees Celsius, and in the afternoon it read 555 degrees Celsius. Find how many degrees the temperature moved between the two readings, showing the subtraction that goes inside the bars.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Two other stations logged the same pair of times. Station Fern went from −9-9−9 degrees Celsius to 444 degrees Celsius, and station Ridge went from 777 degrees Celsius to −5-5−5 degrees Celsius. Find how far each station's temperature moved, and say which of the two moved further.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why the size of a temperature movement comes out the same whether the dawn reading is subtracted from the afternoon one or the other way round, and say what information the bars discard when they turn that difference into a distance.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      The gap between two points on the line is the absolute value of their difference. The sign the bars throw away is what records which of the two readings you started from.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Settle each station on its own before comparing anything, and take care with the station whose second reading lies below zero, since taking away a negative number moves you the opposite way.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Subtract one pair of readings both ways round and look at the two results together. What is the relationship between them, and what do you already know about the absolute values of a pair like that?

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    ∣−14−5∣=19\lvert -14 - 5 \rvert = 19∣−14−5∣=19, so the temperature moved by 191919 degrees Celsius.

    Part B

    ∣−9−4∣=13\lvert -9 - 4 \rvert = 13∣−9−4∣=13 for Fern and ∣7−(−5)∣=12\lvert 7 - (-5) \rvert = 12∣7−(−5)∣=12 for Ridge, so Fern's temperature moved 131313 degrees Celsius, Ridge's moved 121212, and Fern moved further.

    Part C

    The two subtractions give opposite results, and a number and its opposite are the same distance from zero, so the bars report one size either way. What they discard is the sign, which under afternoon minus dawn records a rise when positive and a fall when negative.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    How far apart two numbers sit on the line is the absolute value of their difference, so subtract the two readings and then measure what comes out.

    Subtracting 555 from −14-14−14 moves five units further left:

    −14−5=−19.-14 - 5 = -19.−14−5=−19.

    The bars then discard the sign and keep the size of the gap:

    ∣−14−5∣=∣−19∣=19.\lvert -14 - 5 \rvert = \lvert -19 \rvert = 19.∣−14−5∣=∣−19∣=19.

    The temperature moved by 191919 degrees Celsius. Counting along the line agrees: fourteen degrees to climb from −14-14−14 up to zero, then five more to reach 555.

    Part B

    Take one station at a time, each as the absolute value of a difference.

    Station Fern went from −9-9−9 to 444:

    ∣−9−4∣=∣−13∣=13.\lvert -9 - 4 \rvert = \lvert -13 \rvert = 13.∣−9−4∣=∣−13∣=13.

    Station Ridge went from 777 to −5-5−5. Subtracting a negative number moves right rather than left, which is where a dropped sign would cost the answer:

    ∣7−(−5)∣=∣7+5∣=12.\lvert 7 - (-5) \rvert = \lvert 7 + 5 \rvert = 12.∣7−(−5)∣=∣7+5∣=12.

    So Fern's temperature moved 131313 degrees Celsius and Ridge's moved 121212 degrees Celsius, and Fern moved further.

    Notice what the comparison is between. It is between the two gaps, not between the readings: Ridge finished at the colder temperature of the two stations and still covered the shorter distance.

    Part C

    Subtract one pair of readings both ways round and set the results beside each other. For a dawn reading of −14-14−14 degrees and an afternoon reading of 555 degrees,

    −14−5=−19,5−(−14)=19.-14 - 5 = -19, \qquad 5 - (-14) = 19.−14−5=−19,5−(−14)=19.

    The two results are opposites, and that is what always happens: reversing a subtraction reverses the direction you travelled, and reversing a direction leaves the number of steps alone. A number and its opposite are mirror images across zero, so they are the same distance from it, and one pair of bars turns both differences into one answer:

    ∣−14−5∣=∣5−(−14)∣=19.\lvert -14 - 5 \rvert = \lvert 5 - (-14) \rvert = 19.∣−14−5∣=∣5−(−14)∣=19.

    What the bars discard is the sign, and the sign is not part of the size. It records the direction. Subtract afternoon minus dawn, and a positive result means the temperature rose while a negative one means it fell. That is real information, but it answers a different question: a station that rose nineteen degrees and a station that fell nineteen degrees moved by the same amount, and only the signed difference tells them apart.

    In one line

    ∣−14−5∣=19\lvert -14 - 5 \rvert = 19∣−14−5∣=19, so the first station's temperature moved 191919 degrees Celsius; ∣−9−4∣=13\lvert -9 - 4 \rvert = 13∣−9−4∣=13 for Fern and ∣7−(−5)∣=12\lvert 7 - (-5) \rvert = 12∣7−(−5)∣=12 for Ridge, so Fern moved further; and the order of subtraction cannot matter because the two differences are opposites, which share one absolute value, while the sign the bars discard records the direction of the movement rather than its size, once an order such as afternoon minus dawn has been fixed.

    Another way: Count up to zero, then out the other side

    When two readings straddle zero, the gap can be counted in two easy pieces instead of one subtraction. Count from the reading below zero up to zero, then from zero out to the other reading, and add the two counts. From −14-14−14 degrees up to zero is fourteen degrees, and from zero up to 555 degrees is five more:

    14+5=19.14 + 5 = 19.14+5=19.

    When it is worth it As a check on a subtraction that involved a negative number, where a dropped sign is easy to miss, and whenever you would rather see the answer on the line than trust the arithmetic.

    4. Two cases, and the sign in the second one . Reasoning, 11 points. Question 4 of 5.

    The definition of absolute value comes in two cases: for an input that is zero or positive it returns the number itself, and for a negative input it returns the opposite of the number. The second case is written with a minus sign in front of the input, and that minus sign is where most of the trouble with absolute value begins.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The two orderings answer different questions, so carry them out one at a time.

    As signed numbers, position along the line decides, and further left means smaller. The number −16-16−16 sits furthest left, then −3-3−3, then 999, then 121212:

    −16<−3<9<12.-16 < -3 < 9 < 12.−16<−3<9<12.

    ∣−3∣=3,∣9∣=9,∣12∣=12,∣−16∣=16.\lvert -3 \rvert = 3, \qquad \lvert 9 \rvert = 9, \qquad \lvert 12 \rvert = 12, \qquad \lvert -16 \rvert = 16.∣−3∣=3,∣9∣=9,∣12∣=12,∣−16∣=16.

    Part B

    −14<11.-14 < 11.−14<11.

Variables and Expressions

Intuition

Suppose a notebook costs 333 dollars and you do not yet know how many you will buy. You cannot write the total as a single number, because the total depends on a quantity you have not fixed. So instead of a number, write a letter in its place. If nnn stands for the number of notebooks, then the cost is 3×n3 \times n3×n dollars. That one short phrase covers every case at once: two notebooks, ten notebooks, a hundred. A letter used this way is called a variable: a symbol that holds the place of a number you do not know, or one that is free to change. That one idea is where algebra begins.

Learning goals

  • Use a letter for a number that is unknown or free to change
  • Tell an expression from an equation, and say what you do to each
  • Translate a phrase into algebra, minding the reversal in less than
  • Name the terms, coefficient and constant of an expression
  • Evaluate by substituting each value in parentheses

What a variable is

A variable is a letter that stands for a number. The letter does not have a fixed value of its own; it is a placeholder, exactly the way a blank box would be. Writing

3×n3 \times n3×n

says “three times whatever number nnn is.” If nnn is 444, the phrase is worth 3×4=123 \times 4 = 123×4=12; if nnn is 101010, it is worth 3×10=303 \times 10 = 303×10=30. The same letter can take different values on different occasions, which is precisely why it is called variable: its value can vary.

There are two everyday reasons to reach for a letter instead of a number.

The first is that the number is unknown. You may be trying to find it, the way you will in the next few lessons when you solve for xxx. Until you pin it down, a letter holds its place so you can still write down everything you do know about it.

The second is that the number is changing, or you want a single statement to cover many cases at once. The cost of nnn notebooks is 3×n3 \times n3×n for every possible nnn; you do not want to rewrite the rule for each separate purchase. One expression with a variable replaces an endless list of arithmetic examples.

Any letter will do, and the choice is up to you. People lean on xxx and yyy out of long habit. Even so, a letter that hints at its meaning often reads better: ttt for time, ddd for distance, nnn for a count of things. The letter is just a name; what matters is that you state clearly what it stands for.

The hidden multiplication sign

In algebra we almost never write the ×\times× symbol between a number and a letter, because ×\times× looks too much like the letter xxx. Instead we write them side by side: 3n3n3n means 3×n3 \times n3×n, and ababab means a×ba \times ba×b. A number written right next to a variable always means multiply. The number 333 in 3n3n3n has a name, the coefficient, which you will meet shortly. One warning: this shorthand is only for multiplication by a variable. The bare digits 343434 still mean thirty-four, not 3×43 \times 43×4.

Expressions versus equations

These two words name different things, and keeping them apart will save you constant confusion later.

An algebraic expression names a single value, once you know its variables. It can be a number or a variable on its own, or several of those combined with operations (+++, −-−, ×\times×, ÷\div÷, exponents). It is a phrase, the algebraic version of a number. Each of these is an expression:

7,x,3n,x+5,2a−7,y4,5x2.7, \qquad x, \qquad 3n, \qquad x + 5, \qquad 2a - 7, \qquad \frac{y}{4}, \qquad 5x^2.7,x,3n,x+5,2a−7,4y​,5x2.

An expression has no equals sign. On its own it makes no claim; it simply names an amount, the way 3×43 \times 43×4 names an amount before you work it out.

An equation is two expressions joined by an equals sign, stating that they have the same value. Whether that statement is true can depend on which number the variable turns out to be. It is a complete sentence, and like any sentence it can be true or false. Each of these is an equation:

3n=12,x+5=9,2a−7=a.3n = 12, \qquad x + 5 = 9, \qquad 2a - 7 = a.3n=12,x+5=9,2a−7=a.

The difference is exactly the difference between a phrase and a sentence. “Three more than xxx” is a phrase; it points at a quantity but asserts nothing. “Three more than xxx equals nine” is a sentence; it makes a claim you could check. You evaluate an expression (work out its value), but you solve an equation (find the variable value that makes the sentence true). Solving is the work of the coming lessons; this lesson is about reading and evaluating expressions.

Check your understanding

Which of these is an equation (not just an expression)?

Answer choices

An equation must contain an equals sign joining two expressions; an expression alone has none.

4a+3=114a + 3 = 114a+3=11

The other three are expressions: they name a value but assert nothing, because no equals sign is present.

Check your understanding

The expression 2n+52n + 52n+5 and the equation 2n+5=112n + 5 = 112n+5=11 share the same left side. What do you do with each one?

Answer choices

2n+52n + 52n+5 has no equals sign, so it names a value once you pick nnn; you evaluate it by substitution, the way Worked Example 3 does.

2n+5=112n + 5 = 112n+5=11 has an equals sign, so it is a claim that could be true or false; you solve it to find the one value of nnn that makes the claim true, which is the work of the coming lessons.

An expression is never "solved," because it makes no claim to check; an equation is never merely "evaluated," because a specific unknown value is being pinned down.

Translating words into expressions

Most of the time a problem arrives in words, and the first real skill of algebra is turning those words into an expression. Start by naming the unknown with a letter. Then read the whole phrase for its meaning: what quantity are you starting from, what happens to it, and in what order? Once you can answer that, this vocabulary helps you write down each operation:

WordsOperationExample phraseExpression
sum, more than, increased by, total+++seven more than xxxx+7x + 7x+7
difference, less than, decreased by, fewer−-−five less than xxxx−5x - 5x−5
product, times, of, twice, double×\times×three times xxx3x3x3x
quotient, divided by, per, split÷\div÷xxx divided by 444x4\dfrac{x}{4}4x​

Two of these phrases hide a trap worth pausing on. “Five less than xxx” is x−5x - 5x−5, not 5−x5 - x5−x. The phrase tells you to start with xxx and take five away, so the xxx comes first even though 555 is spoken first. The same flip happens with “fewer than” and “subtracted from.” Read these by their meaning, not by the left-to-right order of the words.

When several operations combine, build the expression one phrase at a time, and use parentheses whenever a whole group must be operated on together.

Worked example 1 Translate three phrases into expressions

Name the unknown number xxx in each, then convert phrase by phrase.

“Eight more than twice a number.” Twice the number is 2x2x2x, and eight more than that adds 888:

2x+8.2x + 8.2x+8.

“The product of 555 and a number, decreased by 333.” The product of 555 and the number is 5x5x5x, and decreased by 333 subtracts 333:

5x−3.5x - 3.5x−3.

“A number divided by 222, then increased by 111.” Dividing by 222 gives x2\dfrac{x}{2}2x​, and increasing by 111 adds 111:

x2+1.\frac{x}{2} + 1.2x​+1.

In each case the unknown is held by the letter xxx, and it was the meaning of the phrase, not just its words, that decided the operation and the order.

The trickiest translations are the ones where a sum or difference must be operated on as a whole. Here parentheses do the grouping, exactly as they did in the order-of-operations chapter.

Worked example 2 Translate 'twice the sum of a number and four'

The phrase has two layers. The inner phrase, “the sum of a number and four,” is x+4x + 4x+4. The outer word “twice” then doubles that entire sum, not just the xxx.

To double the whole sum, wrap it in parentheses before multiplying:

2(x+4).2(x + 4).2(x+4).

Without the parentheses, 2x+42x + 42x+4 would double only the xxx and leave the 444 alone, a different expression. Compare the two by trying x=3x = 3x=3: the correct 2(3+4)=142(3 + 4) = 142(3+4)=14, but 2x+42x + 42x+4 would give 2⋅3+4=102 \cdot 3 + 4 = 102⋅3+4=10. The parentheses are what make sure “twice the sum” doubles the whole sum, not just part of it.

Which expression means "four less than a number nnn"?

Answer choices

"Four less than nnn" means start at nnn and take four away, so nnn comes first.

n−4n - 4n−4

The order 4−n4 - n4−n is the common trap: the words say four less than nnn, not nnn less than four.

The parts of an expression

To work with an expression you need names for its pieces. Consider

5x+3y−7.5x + 3y - 7.5x+3y−7.

The expression is built from parts that are added or subtracted together; each such part is a term. This expression has three terms: 5x5x5x, 3y3y3y, and −7-7−7. Terms are the chunks separated by a +++ or −-− sign written at the main level of the expression, not one tucked inside parentheses; a grouped piece like (x+4)(x + 4)(x+4) from the last example stays together as one piece until the parentheses are removed. By convention a term carries the sign written in front of it, so the subtracted piece here is the term −7-7−7, not a bare 777.

Inside a term like 5x5x5x, the number 555 is the coefficient: the number multiplied by the variable. The coefficient of 3y3y3y is 333. A variable written with no visible number, such as plain xxx, has a coefficient of 111, because xxx means 1x1x1x, one copy of xxx. Likewise −x-x−x means −1x-1x−1x, so its coefficient is −1-1−1.

A term that is just a number, with no variable at all, is a constant. In 5x+3y−75x + 3y - 75x+3y−7 the constant is −7-7−7. It is called constant because it never changes: whatever the variables do, that term contributes the same fixed amount.

Check your understanding

Which statement about the expression 9k+29k + 29k+2 is correct?

Answer choices

The +++ sign splits the expression into two terms: 9k9k9k and 222.

9k+2  →  terms 9k, 29k + 2 \;\to\; \text{terms } 9k,\ 29k+2→terms 9k, 2

In 9k9k9k the number multiplying the variable is 999, so that is the coefficient. The term with no variable, 222, is the constant. The other options either merge the two terms into one, swap which number is the coefficient and which is the constant, or split the expression at the wrong place.

Evaluating an expression by substitution

To evaluate an expression you replace each variable with a given number, then do the arithmetic. Replacing a letter with a number is called substitution, and it turns an algebraic phrase back into an ordinary calculation.

There is one habit that prevents nearly every mistake. When you substitute, put the number inside parentheses where the variable used to be. The parentheses keep the original operations intact, especially multiplication and signs, so nothing gets accidentally merged or dropped.

Why parentheses matter so much is easiest to see with a coefficient. In 5x5x5x the variable is being multiplied by 555. If x=7x = 7x=7 and you simply write the 777 next to the 555, you get "575757", the number fifty-seven, which is the wrong number entirely, not the product 5×75 \times 75×7. Written with parentheses, the same substitution gives 5(7)=355(7) = 355(7)=35, which is what 5x5x5x actually means.

Worked example 3 Evaluate 3x+43x + 43x+4 when x=5x = 5x=5

Substitute 555 for xxx, writing it in parentheses so the multiplication stays clear:

3x+4=3(5)+4.3x + 4 = 3(5) + 4.3x+4=3(5)+4.

Now follow the order of operations: multiply before you add.

3(5)+4=15+4=19.3(5) + 4 = 15 + 4 = 19.3(5)+4=15+4=19.

So when x=5x = 5x=5, the expression 3x+43x + 43x+4 is worth 191919.

Why the same expression gives different values for different inputs#

An expression with a variable is not one fixed number. It is a set of instructions that produces a number once you choose the variable’s value; the instructions stay the same, but what you choose does not.

Take 2x+12x + 12x+1. Substituting x=3x = 3x=3 gives 2(3)+1=6+1=72(3) + 1 = 6 + 1 = 72(3)+1=6+1=7. Substituting x=5x = 5x=5 instead gives 2(5)+1=10+1=112(5) + 1 = 10 + 1 = 112(5)+1=10+1=11. The instructions, multiply by two and add one, are identical both times. Only the number substituted for xxx changed, from 333 to 555, and that alone is what changed the result, from 777 to 111111.

This is exactly what the word variable promises: because xxx can be any number, the expression can produce a different value for each number you choose. Getting a different answer when you substitute a different value is expected, not a mistake.

Worked example 4 Evaluate 2a2−b2a^2 - b2a2−b when a=3a = 3a=3 and b=5b = 5b=5

Replace each variable by its value, every one in parentheses:

2a2−b=2(3)2−(5).2a^2 - b = 2(3)^2 - (5).2a2−b=2(3)2−(5).

By the order of operations the exponent goes first, then the multiplication, then the subtraction. Square the 333:

2(3)2=2⋅9=18.2(3)^2 = 2 \cdot 9 = 18.2(3)2=2⋅9=18.

Then subtract the value of bbb:

18−5=13.18 - 5 = 13.18−5=13.

So the expression equals 131313. Notice the exponent applied only to the 333, the value of aaa, because a2a^2a2 means aaa is squared, not the whole term.

Substitution works just as smoothly when the value is negative. The parentheses matter most in that case, because they keep the whole value, sign included, together as one piece.

Worked example 5 Evaluate 3x+13x + 13x+1 when x=−2x = -2x=−2

Substitute −2-2−2 for xxx, keeping it in parentheses so the multiplication and the sign both stay clear:

3x+1=3(−2)+1.3x + 1 = 3(-2) + 1.3x+1=3(−2)+1.

The parentheses matter here more than ever. Without them, "3−23-23−2" would read as a subtraction instead of the multiplication 3×(−2)3 \times (-2)3×(−2) that 3x3x3x actually means, and the sign on the 222 could be lost along the way.

Now follow the order of operations, multiplying before adding:

3(−2)+1=−6+1=−5.3(-2) + 1 = -6 + 1 = -5.3(−2)+1=−6+1=−5.

So when x=−2x = -2x=−2, the expression 3x+13x + 13x+1 is worth −5-5−5.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reversing “less than” and “subtracted from.” “Five less than xxx” is x−5x - 5x−5, not 5−x5 - x5−x. Start from the quantity named, then take the amount away. The same flip catches “fewer than” and “subtracted from.”
  • Reading 3n3n3n as a two-digit number. Side-by-side notation means multiply, but only between a number and a variable: 3n=3×n3n = 3 \times n3n=3×n. The plain digits 343434 still mean thirty-four.
  • Treating an expression like an equation. An expression has no equals sign, so you evaluate it; you do not “solve” it. Only an equation, with its equals sign, gets solved.
  • Forgetting the invisible coefficient 111. Plain xxx means 1x1x1x and −x-x−x means −1x-1x−1x. The coefficient is 111 or −1-1−1, not zero and not missing.
  • Dropping parentheses when substituting. Replace xxx with (7)(7)(7), not a bare 777, so 5x5x5x becomes 5(7)=355(7) = 355(7)=35 rather than the wrong number 575757, and a negative input keeps its sign. This single habit removes most substitution errors.
  • Multiplying before grouping in “twice the sum.” “Twice the sum of xxx and 444” is 2(x+4)2(x + 4)2(x+4), not 2x+42x + 42x+4. Parentheses must hold the whole sum together before it is doubled.

Key takeaways

  • A variable is a letter standing for a number that is unknown or free to change; the same letter can take different values on different occasions. A number written right beside a variable means multiply, so 3n3n3n is 3×n3 \times n3×n.
  • An expression is a phrase (3n3n3n, x+5x + 5x+5) that names a value. An equation joins two expressions with an equals sign (3n=123n = 123n=12) and can be true or false. You evaluate expressions and solve equations.
  • Translate words by naming the unknown with a letter and converting each phrase to an operation. Mind the reversal in “less than,” and use parentheses for “twice the sum” and similar groupings.
  • The parts of an expression are its terms (separated by a +++ or −-− at the main level), its coefficient, and its constant (a number term). A coefficient is the number multiplying a variable, with plain xxx meaning 1x1x1x.
  • To evaluate, substitute each variable’s value in parentheses and follow the order of operations: 3x+43x + 43x+4 at x=5x = 5x=5 is 3(5)+4=193(5) + 4 = 193(5)+4=19.

A bit of history (optional)

A book showing you how to share thirty coins among five people, written out entirely in words, is how algebra problems were solved and recorded for a very long time.

Francois Viete was a French lawyer who did mathematics at night. In 159115911591 he wrote a short book with one very large idea: letters, he said, should stand not only for the number you are hunting for, but also for the numbers you already know. He used consonants for the known amounts and vowels for the unknown ones.

That was a small change of notation with a big effect. One line of letters could now stand for every problem of one shape at once, written in symbols instead of paragraphs. Which numbers to use became a choice you made later, by substitution.

You used that same power in this lesson. The cost of nnn notebooks is 3n3n3n, and that one expression covers every trip to the shop you will ever make.

  1. Problem 1 Two machines, two orders

    ★ Stretch Difficulty: 1 of 3 stars, Stretch

    Machine A doubles its input and then adds 3. Machine B triples its input and then adds a fixed number ccc. Each output becomes the next machine's input.

    (a) When c=−4c=-4c=−4, compare A followed by B with B followed by A, starting with the same arbitrary real number. Which result is larger, and by how much?

    (b) Find every real value of ccc for which the two orders give the same result for every starting number. Prove your answer.

    1. Hint 1

      Name the starting number xxx and write the output after each machine.

    2. Hint 2

      When comparing the final expressions, check which terms cancel. Do not assume the starting number needs to be solved for.

    Answer

    For c=−4c=-4c=−4, A followed by B is always larger by 10. The two orders agree for every input exactly when c=6c=6c=6.

    Full solution

    Let the starting number be xxx.

    A followed by B produces

    3(2x+3)+c=6x+9+c.3(2x+3)+c=6x+9+c.3(2x+3)+c=6x+9+c.

    B followed by A produces

    2(3x+c)+3=6x+2c+3.2(3x+c)+3=6x+2c+3.2(3x+c)+3=6x+2c+3.

    For c=−4c=-4c=−4, the two outputs are 6x+56x+56x+5 and 6x−56x-56x−5.

    Their difference is 10, regardless of whether xxx is positive, negative, zero, or fractional.

    The input cancels from the comparison.

    For general ccc, the first output minus the second is

    (6x+9+c)−(6x+2c+3)=6−c.(6x+9+c)-(6x+2c+3)=6-c.(6x+9+c)−(6x+2c+3)=6−c.

    Therefore, the outputs agree precisely when 6−c=06-c=06−c=0, or c=6c=6c=6.

    Substitution gives 6x+156x+156x+15 in either order, verifying that this choice works for every real input.

    If ccc is any other value, the difference is a fixed nonzero number.

    Then no starting number makes the outputs equal.

    Writing an arbitrary input made it possible to prove a statement about all inputs at once.

    Answer

    For c=−4c=-4c=−4, A followed by B is always larger by 10. The two orders agree for every input exactly when c=6c=6c=6.

    Key idea

    Simplifying a difference can reveal an input-independent relationship.

  2. Problem 2 Two erased numbers

    ★ Stretch Difficulty: 1 of 3 stars, Stretch

    Five consecutive positive integers are written in increasing order. Two of them are erased. The remaining three numbers have sum 45.

    Find every possible original block of five numbers and every erased pair that works. Prove that your list is complete.

    1. Hint 1

      Write the original numbers as n,n+1,n+2,n+3,n+4n,n+1,n+2,n+3,n+4n,n+1,n+2,n+3,n+4. Bound the smallest and largest possible remaining sums.

    2. Hint 2

      After bounding nnn, the three retained offsets from 0,1,2,3,40,1,2,3,40,1,2,3,4 must have one of only three possible sums.

    Answer

    Block 12 through 16: erase 12 and 13. Block 13 through 17: erase 13 and 17, or erase 14 and 16. Block 14 through 18: erase 17 and 18.

    Full solution

    n+(n+1)+(n+2)=3n+3.n+(n+1)+(n+2)=3n+3.n+(n+1)+(n+2)=3n+3.

    Hence 3n+3≤45≤3n+93n+3\leq45\leq3n+93n+3≤45≤3n+9, giving

    12≤n≤14.12\leq n\leq14.12≤n≤14.

    When n=12n=12n=12, the retained offsets must sum to 45−36=945-36=945−36=9.

    The maximum offset sum is 2+3+4=92+3+4=92+3+4=9, so the only retained numbers are 14, 15, and 16.

    Erase 12 and 13.

    When n=13n=13n=13, the offsets must sum to 6.

    If 0 is included, the other two distinct offsets must be 2 and 4.

    If 0 is absent, the smallest possible sum is 1+2+3=61+2+3=61+2+3=6, giving the only other choice.

    When n=14n=14n=14, the offsets must have the minimum sum 3, forcing 0,1,20,1,20,1,2.

    Retain 14, 15, 16 and erase 17, 18.

    Each listed retained triple sums to 45, and the bounds and offset cases exclude every other possibility.

    Answer

    Block 12 through 16: erase 12 and 13. Block 13 through 17: erase 13 and 17, or erase 14 and 16. Block 14 through 18: erase 17 and 18.

    Key idea

    A variable and two extreme cases can turn a large search into a few complete cases.

  3. Problem 3 A row controlled by one number

  4. Problem 4 Which targets can the machine reach?

    ★★ Challenge Difficulty: 2 of 3 stars, Challenge

    A machine starts at 1. On each move, you may either double the current number or add 3. You may make any finite number of moves, including no moves.

    Describe exactly which positive integers can appear. Prove both that the excluded integers are impossible and that every integer in your description can be reached.

    1. Hint 1

      Track whether the current number is divisible by 3.

    2. Hint 2

      For reachability, work backward from a proposed target. An even target can be halved; an odd target greater than 1 can first have 3 subtracted.

    Answer

    Exactly the positive integers that are not divisible by 3.

    Full solution

    Starting from 1, a number is never divisible by 3.

    Adding 3 preserves its remainder upon division by 3.

    Doubling also preserves nondivisibility: a number of the form 3k+13k+13k+1 doubles to 3(2k)+23(2k)+23(2k)+2, and a number of the form 3k+23k+23k+2 doubles to 3(2k+1)+13(2k+1)+13(2k+1)+1.

    Neither result is a multiple of 3.

    To show that every other positive integer is reachable, reverse the moves.

    Start with any positive target not divisible by 3.

    If it is even, halve it.

    The result is positive, smaller, and still not divisible by 3; otherwise doubling it would have made the original target divisible by 3.

    If the target is odd and greater than 1, it is at least 5, because 3 is excluded.

    Subtract 3.

    The result is an even positive integer, smaller than before and still not divisible by 3.

    Repeat these backward steps.

    The numbers strictly decrease while remaining positive, so the process must stop.

    Every allowed number greater than 1 has a backward step, so it can stop only at 1.

    Reversing the entire path uses only the permitted forward moves and reaches the original target.

    Answer

    Exactly the positive integers that are not divisible by 3.

    Key idea

    An invariant proves impossibility; a decreasing reverse process proves that the remaining cases are all possible.

  7. Problem 7 The center is forced

    ★★ Challenge Difficulty: 2 of 3 stars, Challenge

    The nine numbers 2,5,8,11,14,17,20,23,262,5,8,11,14,17,20,23,262,5,8,11,14,17,20,23,26 are to be placed once each in a 3×33\times33×3 square. Each of the three rows, each of the three columns, and both main diagonals must have the same sum.

    (a) Prove that the center must be 14 and that every pair of opposite cells sums to 28.

    (b) Prove that 2 cannot occupy a corner.

    (c) Give one arrangement that satisfies all the conditions. You may describe it by its three rows.

    1. Hint 1

      The sum of all nine entries determines each row sum. Add the four line sums passing through the center.

    2. Hint 2

      If a corner contained 2, the two remaining entries in its row would have a fixed sum. Which unused pair could produce that sum?

    Answer

    The common line sum is 42, the center is 14, and opposite cells sum to 28. The number 2 cannot be a corner. One arrangement has rows (23,2,17)(23,2,17)(23,2,17), (8,14,20)(8,14,20)(8,14,20), and (11,26,5)(11,26,5)(11,26,5).

    Full solution

    Each line through the center now has its other two entries summing to 42−14=2842-14=2842−14=28.

    This proves the opposite-pair claim.

    Suppose 2 were in a corner.

    Its diagonally opposite corner would have to contain 26.

    The two other entries in 2's row would sum to 40, and so would the two other entries in its column.

    Among the available numbers, the only possible distinct pairs summing to 40 are 14 with 26, or 17 with 23.

    But 14 is already the center and 26 is in the opposite corner; neither belongs in that row or column.

    Finally, rows (23,2,17)(23,2,17)(23,2,17), (8,14,20)(8,14,20)(8,14,20), and (11,26,5)(11,26,5)(11,26,5) use every number once.

    Answer

    The common line sum is 42, the center is 14, and opposite cells sum to 28. The number 2 cannot be a corner. One arrangement has rows (23,2,17)(23,2,17)(23,2,17), (8,14,20)(8,14,20)(8,14,20), and (11,26,5)(11,26,5)(11,26,5).

    Counting the same entries through overlapping sums can force a hidden value before any search.

  9. Problem 9 A surprising replacement rule

  10. Problem 10 Four additions and four doublings

    ★★★ Deep challenge Difficulty: 3 of 3 stars, Deep challenge

    Start with the number 1. Make exactly eight moves: four moves of type A, which add 1, and four moves of type D, which double the current number. You may arrange the eight moves in any order.

    If one of those is 8, the other two must be 2 and 2.

    If none is 8, all three are at most 4, so all three must be 4.

    This gives contribution groups (16,8,2,2)(16,8,2,2)(16,8,2,2) and (16,4,4,4)(16,4,4,4)(16,4,4,4).

    If there is no 16, all contributions are at most 8.

    With at most two 8s, their sum is at most

    8+8+4+4=24.8+8+4+4=24.8+8+4+4=24.

    Four 8s sum to 32.

    Therefore there must be three 8s and one 4, giving (8,8,8,4)(8,8,8,4)(8,8,8,4).

    A contribution specifies its addition's position relative to the four doublings: 16 means before all four, 8 after the first, 4 after the second, 2 after the third, and 1 after the fourth.

    The three groups therefore give exactly the three stated sequences.

    Each totals 16+28=4416+28=4416+28=44, and the exhaustive contribution cases prove no others work.

    1. Two walls, and the different jobs of the two numbers . Foundational, 9 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 9 pts

    A community centre has two blank walls of exactly the same size. Painters mark the first wall into 999 equal panels and the second wall into 666 equal panels. By the end of the morning, 444 panels of the first wall have been painted and the second wall has not been started. Nothing about the two walls differs except how many panels each was marked into.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write the fraction of the first wall that has been painted and the fraction of it that is still blank. Then say what the bottom number of those fractions records about the wall and what the top number records.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Now take one single panel from each wall. Write the fraction of a whole wall that each of those two panels covers, and say which of the two single panels covers more wall.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A third blank wall of the same size is marked into 121212 equal panels. A visitor looks at it beside the wall marked into 666 and says: "The third wall was marked into more panels, and 121212 is bigger than 666, so each panel of the third wall must be the bigger panel." Identify the step in that reasoning that fails, and rewrite the claim so that it is correct.

      Find and correct the error Say which line first goes wrong, why it is wrong, and then do it correctly. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Everything here is measured against one whole wall at a time. Settle what counts as the whole, then what one panel of it is, and only then compare anything.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      The two walls are the same size, so the only thing that differs is how many pieces that size was shared among. Ask what happens to one share when the same amount is shared among more of them.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      The visitor compared two numbers correctly. Ask what each of those numbers is a count of before deciding what their comparison is entitled to say about a panel.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    49\frac{4}{9}94​ of the wall is painted and 59\frac{5}{9}95​ is blank. The denominator records the 999 equal panels the wall was marked into; the numerator counts how many of them the fraction takes.

    Part B

    One panel of the first wall is 19\frac{1}{9}91​ of it and one panel of the second wall is 16\frac{1}{6}61​ of it. A single panel of the wall marked into 666 covers more wall.

    Part C

    The step that fails is reading a larger denominator as a larger part. The walls are the same size, so marking one into more panels makes each of its panels smaller. Corrected: the third wall was marked into more panels, so each of its panels is the smaller one.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The wall is one whole, and it was marked into 999 equal panels, so every fraction in this question counts ninths.

    Four of those nine panels carry paint, so the painted amount is four ninths:

    49\frac{4}{9}94​

    The blank panels are the ones left over, and there are 9−4=59 - 4 = 59−4=5 of them, each one a ninth of the wall:

    59\frac{5}{9}95​

    The two numbers are doing different jobs, and that is why one of them moved and the other did not. The 999 underneath is a report about the cutting: the wall was divided into 999 equal panels, so a ninth is the size of one panel. The number on top is a plain count of those panels, in the same way that 444 metres is a count of metres. Painting more panels changes the count, not the size of a panel, so the denominator stays at 999 in both fractions while the numerator changes.

    Part B

    One panel is one of the equal parts its wall was marked into, so each of these fractions has numerator 111.

    The first wall carries 999 equal panels, so one of them is

    19\frac{1}{9}91​

    of that wall. The second wall carries 666 equal panels, so one of them is

    16\frac{1}{6}61​

    of that wall.

    The two walls are the same size, so the two panels can be set side by side and compared. Sharing one wall among 999 panels leaves each panel less wall than sharing the same wall among 666 panels does, because more cuts leave smaller pieces:

    19<16\frac{1}{9} < \frac{1}{6}91​<61​

    So a single panel of the second wall covers more, even though 999 is the larger number.

    One detail is worth keeping straight. The inequality 19<16\frac{1}{9} < \frac{1}{6}91​<61​ is a statement about two numbers, and it holds whatever the walls happen to look like. What the walls being the same size buys is the step from that inequality to a claim about the panels themselves: a fraction measures against the whole it came from, so if the two walls differed in size, knowing 19<16\frac{1}{9} < \frac{1}{6}91​<61​ would not on its own settle which panel covers more.

    Part C

    The visitor's arithmetic is not in question. It is perfectly true that 121212 is bigger than 666. The failure is in what that comparison is taken to be about.

    A denominator does not measure a panel. It counts how many equal panels one whole wall was divided into, and the wall being divided is the same size in both cases. A fixed amount of wall shared among more panels leaves less wall for each of them, so pushing the count of panels up forces the size of one panel down:

    112<16\frac{1}{12} < \frac{1}{6}121​<61​

    The corrected claim keeps the visitor's observation and reverses what it is taken to show: the third wall was marked into more panels, so each of its panels is the smaller one.

    One test keeps this straight in future. Push the count as far as you like: mark a wall into 100100100 equal panels and one panel is a thin strip. Nobody expects a hundred panels to make each panel large, and the same reasoning settles 121212 against 666. The visitor read the denominator as an amount of wall per panel, when it is a count of panels per wall.

    In one line

    49\frac{4}{9}94​ of the first wall is painted and 59\frac{5}{9}95​ is blank, where the 999 records the equal panels the wall was marked into and the numerator counts them. One panel of that wall is 19\frac{1}{9}91​ of it, against 16\frac{1}{6}61​ for one panel of the second wall, so the panel from the wall marked into 666 is the larger one. The visitor's reasoning fails at the step from 12>612 > 612>6 to a claim about panel size: one wall shared among more panels leaves each panel less, so 112<16\frac{1}{12} < \frac{1}{6}121​<61​.

    2. The bar as an instruction to divide . Foundational, 9 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 9 pts

    The bar in a fraction is a division sign as well as a description of parts, so for a nonzero bbb the fraction ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ and the division a÷ba \div ba÷b name the same number. This question works from that reading throughout: first on a share, then on a list of fractions, and finally on two fractions built from the same pair of numbers in the two possible orders.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Five identical banana loaves are shared equally among 666 people, with nothing left over. Write one person's share first as a division and then as a fraction of a loaf, and say whether one person receives a whole loaf.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 2 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Decide which of 279\frac{27}{9}927​, 1313\frac{13}{13}1313​, 151\frac{15}{1}115​, 229\frac{22}{9}922​ and 07\frac{0}{7}70​ name whole numbers, and give the whole number wherever there is one.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The fractions 07\frac{0}{7}70​ and 70\frac{7}{0}07​ are built from the same two numbers in the two possible orders. Decide for each one whether it names a number, and explain both decisions from the reading of the bar as a division.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every part of this question can be started by turning the bar into a division sign and reading off what that division is asking for. Do that before deciding anything about the fraction.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      A division comes out even exactly when the bottom number divides the top one with nothing left over, which is a test you already have from the divisibility rules.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Multiplication is what answers a division: a÷ba \div ba÷b asks which number times bbb gives aaa. Ask that question of both fractions and see which one gets an answer back.

    That is every hint for this question.

    The answer

    Part A

    One share is 5÷65 \div 65÷6, which is 56\frac{5}{6}65​ of a loaf. Nobody receives a whole loaf.

    Part B

    279=3\frac{27}{9} = 3927​=3, 1313=1\frac{13}{13} = 11313​=1, 151=15\frac{15}{1} = 15115​=15 and 07=0\frac{0}{7} = 070​=0 name whole numbers. 229\frac{22}{9}922​ does not, since 999 does not divide 222222 evenly.

    Part C

    07\frac{0}{7}70​ names the number 000, since none of seven equal parts is a real amount. 70\frac{7}{0}07​ names no number, because 7÷07 \div 07÷0 asks for a number that gives 777 when multiplied by 000, and everything multiplied by 000 gives 000.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Sharing equally is a division, and the two numbers arrive in a fixed order: the amount being shared goes first, the number of people it is shared among goes second.

    5÷65 \div 65÷6

    The whole numbers have no answer to that division, but the fraction bar does, because the bar is that division written another way:

    5÷6=565 \div 6 = \frac{5}{6}5÷6=65​

    It is worth seeing why five sixths is believable and not merely formal. Cut each of the 555 loaves into 666 equal pieces, which gives 303030 pieces, every one of them a sixth of a loaf. Deal those pieces out to the 666 people:

    30÷6=5 pieces each30 \div 6 = 5 \text{ pieces each}30÷6=5 pieces each

    Five pieces, each a sixth, is five sixths of a loaf.

    One whole loaf would be six sixths, and each share is one sixth short of that, so nobody receives a whole loaf.

    Part B

    Read each bar as a division and ask whether that division comes out even.

    279=27÷9=3\frac{27}{9} = 27 \div 9 = 3927​=27÷9=3

    1313=13÷13=1\frac{13}{13} = 13 \div 13 = 11313​=13÷13=1

    The second needs no arithmetic at all: a numerator equal to its denominator says the whole was cut into 131313 equal parts and all 131313 were taken back, which is the whole itself.

    151=15÷1=15\frac{15}{1} = 15 \div 1 = 15115​=15÷1=15

    A denominator of 111 says the whole was never cut, so each part is a whole and taking 151515 of them gives 151515.

    07=0÷7=0\frac{0}{7} = 0 \div 7 = 070​=0÷7=0

    Here the whole was cut into 777 equal parts and none was taken, which is a perfectly good amount: none.

    That leaves 229\frac{22}{9}922​. Nine goes into 222222 twice with 444 left over, so that division does not come out even and no whole number equals it. This is not a gap in the answer. The fraction 229\frac{22}{9}922​ is itself the answer to 22÷922 \div 922÷9, and it is a complete one.

    Part C

    A division always asks the same shape of question: a÷ba \div ba÷b asks which number, multiplied by bbb, gives back aaa. Put both fractions to that test.

    First 07\frac{0}{7}70​, which is 0÷70 \div 70÷7. The question is which number multiplied by 777 gives 000, and 000 answers it:

    0×7=00 \times 7 = 00×7=0

    so 07=0\frac{0}{7} = 070​=0. The parts reading agrees. The whole was cut into 777 equal parts and none of them was taken, and taking none of something is a real amount, namely nothing at all.

    Now 70\frac{7}{0}07​, which is 7÷07 \div 07÷0. The question is which number multiplied by 000 gives 777. Every number multiplied by 000 gives 000, never 777:

    n×0=0 for every number nn \times 0 = 0 \text{ for every number } nn×0=0 for every number n

    So nothing answers the question, and the fraction names no number. We say it is undefined.

    The parts reading breaks in the same place. A denominator of 000 claims the whole was cut into no parts at all, and then there are no parts for a numerator to count.

    The asymmetry is the thing to hold on to. The two positions in a fraction are not interchangeable: a numerator of 000 is an ordinary count that happens to be none, while a denominator of 000 removes the very thing a fraction is built out of.

    In one line

    One share is 5÷6=565 \div 6 = \frac{5}{6}5÷6=65​ of a loaf, which is short of a whole loaf. On the list, 279=3\frac{27}{9} = 3927​=3, 1313=1\frac{13}{13} = 11313​=1, 151=15\frac{15}{1} = 15115​=15 and 07=0\frac{0}{7} = 070​=0 name whole numbers, while 229\frac{22}{9}922​ does not, because 999 does not divide 222222 evenly. And 07\frac{0}{7}70​ names 000, while 70\frac{7}{0}07​ names no number at all, since no number multiplied by 000 gives 777.

    Another way: Test the list with multiples instead of divisions

    Part B can be settled without dividing anything, by listing what the denominator's multiples are. For 279\frac{27}{9}927​ the multiples of 999 run 999, 181818, 272727, and 272727 is among them, in the third place:

    27=9×327 = 9 \times 327=9×3

    so the fraction names 333. For 229\frac{22}{9}922​ the same list runs straight past 222222 without landing on it, so no whole number multiplied by 999 gives 222222 and the fraction names no whole number.

    The same test explains the zero cases in part C. Every multiple of 000 is 000, so a list of them never reaches 777, which is exactly why 70\frac{7}{0}07​ has no answer.

    When it is worth it When the denominator is small, so its multiples are quicker to write out than a division is to carry through, and when you want one test that handles the awkward cases the same way as the ordinary ones.

    3. Splitting a choir into equal groups . Application, 11 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 11 pts

    A community choir has 353535 singers this season. The conductor wants 27\frac{2}{7}72​ of the choir on the low harmony line and everybody else on the melody, and the singers have to be counted out before the first rehearsal.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Work out how many singers take the low harmony line, showing the two steps that the two numbers of the fraction ask for. Report the result as a number of singers.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Working from the way the choir is split rather than from your count in part A, give the fraction of the choir that takes the melody and how many singers that is. Then say what fraction of the choir all seven groups make together.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Next season the choir grows to 383838 singers and the conductor asks for the same 27\frac{2}{7}72​ split. Carry out the first step of the method on 383838 singers, say what the fraction that division produces names in this situation, and say what that tells the conductor about splitting 383838 singers into seven equal groups.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

    Answer and solution

    The answer

    Part A

    101010 singers take the low harmony line.

    Part B

    The melody takes 57\frac{5}{7}75​ of the choir, which is 252525 singers. All seven groups together are 77\frac{7}{7}77​ of the choir, that is 111, the whole choir.

    Part C

    The first step is 38÷738 \div 738÷7, which does not come out even. Its answer is 387\frac{38}{7}738​, the exact size of one group in singers, and that is not a whole number, so 383838 singers cannot be split into seven equal groups of whole people.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The denominator and the numerator give two instructions, and the denominator moves first.

    The denominator 777 says to split the choir into 777 equal groups, so begin by sizing one group:

    35÷7=535 \div 7 = 535÷7=5

    Each group holds 555 singers, so one seventh of the choir is 555 singers. The numerator 222 says to take two of those groups:

    2×5=102 \times 5 = 102×5=10

    So 101010 singers take the low harmony line.

    The order of the two steps is not a matter of taste. Divide by the denominator to find the size of one part, then multiply by the numerator to count how many of those parts you want. And the split lands on whole singers only because 777 divides 353535 evenly, which is what let the first step come out as a whole number.

    Part B

    The choir is split into 777 equal groups and 222 of them take the harmony, so the groups left for the melody number

    7−2=57 - 2 = 57−2=5

    out of the seven. That is five sevenths of the choir.

    Now size the groups and count them, exactly as before. One group holds

    35÷7=5 singers35 \div 7 = 5 \text{ singers}35÷7=5 singers

    and five groups hold

    5×5=25 singers5 \times 5 = 25 \text{ singers}5×5=25 singers

    so 252525 singers take the melody.

    All seven groups together are 77\frac{7}{7}77​ of the choir. A fraction whose numerator equals its denominator is one whole, because taking all 777 of the 777 equal groups is taking the choir back entire, with nobody left out and nobody counted twice. That is the check on the whole arrangement: the two lines between them use up every group, and every group holds 555 singers.

    Part C

    The method starts the same way. Split the new choir into 777 equal groups:

    38÷738 \div 738÷7

    Seven goes into 383838 five times with 333 left over, so this division does not come out even and no whole number answers it. The fraction bar does answer it:

    38÷7=38738 \div 7 = \frac{38}{7}38÷7=738​

    So one seventh of 383838 singers is exactly 387\frac{38}{7}738​ singers, a quantity larger than 555 and smaller than 666.

    That is a perfectly good number, and for a length of rope or a weight of flour it would end the question. Singers are different: a group cannot hold a fraction of a person. So the arithmetic is reporting something real about the situation rather than failing at it. A choir of 383838 cannot be divided into seven equal groups of whole singers at all, and no clever arrangement of the rehearsal will change that.

    What the conductor has to change is something else: the total, by recruiting or resting singers until it is a multiple of 777, or the plan, by giving up on groups of exactly equal size.

    The season of 353535 worked only because 777 divides 353535 evenly. The fraction 27\frac{2}{7}72​ was never the difficulty. What matters is whether the denominator divides the number being split.

    In one line

    One seventh of 353535 singers is 35÷7=535 \div 7 = 535÷7=5 singers, so the harmony takes 2×5=102 \times 5 = 102×5=10 singers. The melody takes the other five sevenths, which is 252525 singers, and all seven groups together are 77\frac{7}{7}77​ of the choir, that is one whole choir of 353535. With 383838 singers the first step gives 38÷7=38738 \div 7 = \frac{38}{7}38÷7=738​, which is not a whole number of singers, so a choir of 383838 cannot be split into seven equal groups of whole people at all.

    4. Naming the marks on a line cut into fifths . Reasoning, 10 points. Question 4 of 5.

    Reasoning 10 pts

    A number line is drawn from 000 to 222, and the segment between each pair of neighbouring whole numbers is divided into 555 equal steps. Count the marks from 000 upward, so that the first mark after 000 is the first one, the mark after that is the second, and so on.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Name the fraction at the seventh mark counting from 000. Say whether it is a proper or an improper fraction, and name the two whole numbers it lies between.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Give the fraction at the mark that coincides with 111 and the fraction at the mark that coincides with 222. Then give a test on the two numbers of a fraction that decides, without any picture, whether it lands exactly on a whole number.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A classmate says that every fraction names a point somewhere between 000 and 111, because a fraction is a part of a whole. Decide whether that claim holds, and support your decision from the way a fraction is placed on the line.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Placing a fraction is a two-stage instruction: the denominator decides how big one step is, and the numerator decides how many steps to count. Keep those two stages apart and every part below becomes a count.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Ask what the counting has done when it arrives exactly on a whole number, then read the bar as a division and ask what that division must do for the same thing to happen.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Before arguing anything general, look along the line described above for a single mark that would settle the claim on its own.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The seventh mark is 75\frac{7}{5}57​. It is improper, because 777 is larger than 555, and it lies between 111 and 222.

    Part B

    The mark at 111 is 55\frac{5}{5}55​ and the mark at 222 is 105\frac{10}{5}510​. A fraction lands on a whole number exactly when its denominator divides its numerator evenly.

    Part C

    The claim fails. Counting steps does not stop at the 111 mark: 75\frac{7}{5}57​ takes seven fifth-steps and lands past it. What is true is the narrower claim that a fraction lands before 111 when its numerator is smaller than its denominator, which is the proper case.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Each step is one of 555 equal parts of a unit, so every step is 15\frac{1}{5}51​ and the marks count fifths. Seven steps from 000 is seven fifths:

    75\frac{7}{5}57​

    Its numerator is larger than its denominator, so it is an improper fraction, and an improper fraction has reached or passed one whole.

    Where it lands can be counted rather than guessed. Five steps carry you to

    55=1\frac{5}{5} = 155​=1

    and the two steps after that are still short of the next whole, which would need five more. So 75\frac{7}{5}57​ sits between 111 and 222, two fifths of the way along that second unit.

    Nothing about the counting changed when the marks passed 111. The line simply kept going, the steps stayed the same size, and the numerator went on counting them.

    Part B

    Counting in fifths, five steps make one whole:

    55=1\frac{5}{5} = 155​=1

    because taking all 555 of the 555 equal parts of a unit is taking that unit back. Ten steps cross a second unit in the same way:

    105=10÷5=2\frac{10}{5} = 10 \div 5 = 2510​=10÷5=2

    The test that needs no picture comes from reading the bar as a division. A fraction names a whole number exactly when the division it stands for comes out even, that is, when the denominator divides the numerator with nothing left over. It works in both directions: if the denominator divides the numerator, the division has a whole-number answer, and if the fraction equals a whole number, then that whole number times the denominator is the numerator, so the denominator divides it.

    Here 555 divides 555 and 555 divides 101010, so both of those marks land on whole numbers, while 555 does not divide 777, which is why the seventh mark landed strictly between two of them. The whole-number marks on this line are exactly the ones whose numerator is a multiple of 555: 55\frac{5}{5}55​ at 111, 105\frac{10}{5}510​ at 222, and on a longer line 155\frac{15}{5}515​ at 333.

    Part C

    One mark from this very line settles the claim.

    Placing a fraction has two stages: the denominator fixes the size of a step, and the numerator says how many steps to count from 000. Nothing in the second stage says to stop at the 111 mark. The line continues, the steps continue, and the count runs as far as the numerator says:

    75>1\frac{7}{5} > 157​>1

    because seven steps of a fifth carry you past the five steps that make one whole. A single case like that is enough to sink a claim about every fraction.

    What survives is a sharper statement, and the line shows all three cases at once. When the numerator is smaller than the denominator, the count runs out before it has taken the number of steps a whole requires, so the mark lands short of 111: those are the proper fractions. When the numerator equals the denominator, the count lands exactly on 111. When the numerator is larger, the count carries past it, which is the improper case.

    The phrase that misled the classmate is "a part of a whole". A fraction is a count of equal parts, and nothing forbids counting out more parts than a single whole contains, provided there is more than one whole to take them from.

    In one line

    The seventh mark is 75\frac{7}{5}57​, an improper fraction lying between 111 and 222. The mark at 111 is 55\frac{5}{5}55​ and the mark at 222 is 105\frac{10}{5}510​, and a fraction lands on a whole number exactly when its denominator divides its numerator evenly. The classmate's claim fails, since 75\frac{7}{5}57​ lies past 111; only the fractions whose numerator is smaller than their denominator, the proper ones, land short of 111.

    Another way: Find the whole-number marks first

    Counting seven steps one at a time is easy to lose track of on a long line. Mark the wholes first instead. The denominator says five steps make one whole, so the mark at 111 is the fifth step and the mark at 222 is the tenth. Then count only what is left over past the whole below:

    7−5=27 - 5 = 27−5=2

    The seventh mark is therefore the second step after the mark at 111, which places it between 111 and 222 without counting all the way from 000. The same reading hands you part B for nothing, since it locates the whole-number marks before anything else.

    When it is worth it On a line drawn past 111, or whenever the numerator is large: finding the whole below is quicker and far less error-prone than counting every step from zero.

    Answer and solution

    The answer

    Part A

    716<1116\frac{7}{16} < \frac{11}{16}167​<1611​, from the matching denominators, and 67>613\frac{6}{7} > \frac{6}{13}76​>136​, from the matching numerators.

    Part B

    For example 511\frac{5}{11}115​, since with the denominators matched the numerators run 3<5<83 < 5 < 83<5<8; and 314\frac{3}{14}143​, since with the numerators matched the larger denominator names the smaller parts.

    Part C

    The claim is too strong. This pair shares neither a numerator nor a denominator, so neither shortcut reaches it: one needs a shared denominator so that only the counts differ, the other a shared numerator, not zero, so that only the sizes differ.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The first pair counts the same size of part. Both fractions are built from sixteenths, so the only thing that differs is how many sixteenths each one holds, and eleven of a thing beats seven of the very same thing:

    716<1116\frac{7}{16} < \frac{11}{16}167​<1611​

    The second pair counts the same number of parts, six each, but the parts are not the same size. One whole cut into 777 pieces gives larger pieces than the same whole cut into 131313, so six of the larger pieces outweigh six of the smaller ones:

    67>613\frac{6}{7} > \frac{6}{13}76​>136​

    The two shortcuts read the numbers in opposite directions, and it is worth saying why. With the denominators matching, the larger numerator wins, because it counts more of an identical part. With the numerators matching, and that shared count not zero, the smaller denominator wins, because fewer cuts leave bigger parts.

    That second condition earns its place. Taking none of a part is nothing at all, whatever the parts are like, so a shared numerator of 000 leaves two fractions equal rather than one of them ahead:

    03=0=07\frac{0}{3} = 0 = \frac{0}{7}30​=0=70​

    With a genuine count of parts the rule holds, and it only feels backwards if the denominator is read as a size; it is a count of how many parts the whole was cut into.

    Part B

    Both requests come down to choosing which of the two numbers to hold fixed.

    For the first, hold the denominator at 111111 so that everything counts elevenths. The order of the fractions is then the order of their numerators, and any numerator strictly between 333 and 888 does the job:

    311<511<811\frac{3}{11} < \frac{5}{11} < \frac{8}{11}113​<115​<118​

    The numerators 444, 666 and 777 serve just as well. There are exactly four fractions with denominator 111111 that qualify, because there are exactly four whole numbers strictly between 333 and 888.

    For the second, hold the numerator at 333 so that both fractions take three parts, and then make the parts smaller by cutting the whole into more of them. Any denominator larger than 111111 works:

    314<311\frac{3}{14} < \frac{3}{11}143​<113​

    Three fourteenths is three of a smaller piece than three elevenths, so it is the smaller amount. A denominator of 121212, of 202020 or of 100100100 would do equally well.

    The two requests are mirror images of one another. The first freezes the size of the part and varies the count; the second freezes the count and varies the size. Each shortcut exists because exactly one thing is being allowed to move.

    Part C

    Test the claim against the pair rather than arguing about it in general.

    The first shortcut needs the two fractions to be built from the same size of part, which is to say it needs them to share a denominator. Here the denominators are 777 and 999, so it does not apply.

    The second shortcut needs the two fractions to take the same number of parts, which is to say it needs them to share a numerator, and that shared count must not be zero. Here the numerators are 444 and 555, so it does not apply either.

    The extra condition on the second one is worth stating rather than assuming. If the shared numerator were 000, both fractions would be 000 however their parts were sized:

    03=0=07\frac{0}{3} = 0 = \frac{0}{7}30​=0=70​

    and the smaller denominator would win nothing, because the two amounts are equal. A shortcut is only as good as the conditions attached to it.

    Neither shortcut reaches the pair, so the classmate's claim is too strong. Now be exact about what that does and does not show. The two fractions certainly have a definite order: each names a single point on the number line, and one of those two points lies to the left of the other. What is missing is a method, and both shortcuts are missing it for the same reason. A shortcut works by freezing one of the two numbers so that only one thing varies, and

    47against59\frac{4}{7} \quad \text{against} \quad \frac{5}{9}74​against95​

    lets the count of parts and the size of the parts change at once. Comparing 444 against 555 ignores that the parts differ; comparing 777 against 999 ignores that the counts differ. Neither number can be read on its own.

    The repair is to rewrite one or both fractions so that they do share a denominator, which is the business of the next lesson. Until then, the honest report on this pair is that these two shortcuts do not decide it.

    In one line

    716<1116\frac{7}{16} < \frac{11}{16}167​<1611​, since matching denominators leave the numerators to decide, and 67>613\frac{6}{7} > \frac{6}{13}76​>136​, since matching numerators, when that shared count is not zero, leave the denominators to decide backwards. A fraction such as 511\frac{5}{11}115​ lies between 311\frac{3}{11}113​ and 811\frac{8}{11}118​, and 314\frac{3}{14}143​ is smaller than 311\frac{3}{11}113​. The classmate's claim is too strong: 47\frac{4}{7}74​ and 59\frac{5}{9}95​ share neither number, so neither shortcut reaches them, and ordering that pair waits on the method of the next lesson.

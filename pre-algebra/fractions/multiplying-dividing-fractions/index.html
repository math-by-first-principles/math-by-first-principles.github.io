Multiplying and Dividing Fractions

Intuition

Adding fractions was fussy: the pieces had to be the same size before you could combine them. Multiplying is different. What is 23\frac{2}{3}32​ of 45\frac{4}{5}54​? That question does not need matching denominators at all, because “of” is what multiplication means: 23×45\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}32​×54​ means two thirds of four fifths, and a picture turns that into counting equal pieces. Hiding inside that picture is a shortcut: multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms, and you are done. Later in this lesson, dividing asks a different question: how many of one fraction fit into another?

Learning goals

  • Multiply numerators and denominators using an area model, with no common denominator needed
  • Cancel a common factor before multiplying to keep numbers small
  • Find the reciprocal of a nonzero fraction and explain why it multiplies back to 1
  • Divide by a nonzero fraction by flipping the divisor, and explain why that undoes multiplication

What “a fraction of a fraction” means

When you see 12×6\frac{1}{2} \times 621​×6, you already read it as half of six, which is 333. The word of is what multiplication means here, and it does not change when the second number is also a fraction. So 12×13\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3}21​×31​ means one half of one third: start with one third of a whole, then take half of that piece.

Picture a single whole as a square. Slice it into thirds with two vertical cuts and shade one of the three strips. That shaded strip is 13\frac{1}{3}31​ of the square. Now take half of just that strip by cutting it across the middle. Half of the strip is a smaller rectangle, and the question is what fraction of the whole square that small rectangle is. Extend the horizontal cut all the way across and you can count. The square is now divided into 666 equal small rectangles, and the doubly shaded piece is exactly one of them. So one half of one third is one sixth:

12×13=16.\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}.21​×31​=61​.

Look at where the 666 came from. The whole square was cut into thirds across its width and into halves down its height. The two sets of cuts make the number of small rectangles the denominators multiplied together, 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6. The doubly shaded piece is the overlap of “one of the thirds” and “one of the halves.” So the number of shaded rectangles is the numerators multiplied together, 1×1=11 \times 1 = 11×1=1. Multiply the bottoms to count the pieces in the new whole, and multiply the tops to count how many of those pieces you keep.

The rule for multiplying fractions

The same picture works when each fraction keeps more than one part. Take 23×45\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}32​×54​, which reads as two thirds of four fifths:

23×45=2×43×5=815.\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 5} = \frac{8}{15}.32​×54​=3×52×4​=158​.

The five columns and three rows cut the square into 151515 equal rectangles, which is where the 151515 comes from. The kept block is 222 rows crossing 444 columns, so it covers 888 of them, and that is the 888. Simplify the answer whenever you can: here 888 and 151515 share no factor above 111, so 815\frac{8}{15}158​ is already in lowest terms.

Multiplication rule. For any fractions ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ and cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ with bbb and ddd not zero, ab×cd=a×cb×d.\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}.ba​×dc​=b×da×c​.

The square diagram shows the rule for fractions between 000 and 111, where you shade part of one whole. The very same rule, multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms, carries over to improper fractions and to whole numbers. A whole number is a fraction over 111, so 5×27=51×27=1075 \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{5}{1} \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{10}{7}5×72​=15​×72​=710​. You just may end up with a result larger than one whole.

Cancelling before you multiply

Multiplying the tops and bottoms first and simplifying afterward always works, but the numbers can grow large and then need a big simplification. There is a shortcut that keeps the numbers small: cancel any common factor between a numerator and a denominator before multiplying.

Take 34×89\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{9}43​×98​. Before multiplying, notice that 333 on top shares the factor 333 with 999 on the bottom. The 888 on top also shares the factor 444 with the 444 on the bottom. Divide each pair by its common factor. The 333 becomes 111 and the 999 becomes 333 after dividing both by 333. The 888 becomes 222 and the 444 becomes 111 after dividing both by 444:

34×89=11×23=1×21×3=23.\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{9} = \frac{1}{1} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1 \times 2}{1 \times 3} = \frac{2}{3}.43​×98​=11​×32​=1×31×2​=32​.

Multiplying the small leftovers gives 23\frac{2}{3}32​ directly, in lowest terms, with no large product to reduce. To see that this is the same answer the long way gives, multiply first instead: 34×89=2436\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{9} = \frac{24}{36}43​×98​=3624​. Then 2436\frac{24}{36}3624​ simplifies by its greatest common factor 121212 to 23\frac{2}{3}32​. Same result, but cancelling first kept the numbers to single digits.

That cancel paired the 333 on top of one fraction with the 999 on the bottom of the other. It is allowed because the product is one fraction, 3×84×9\frac{3 \times 8}{4 \times 9}4×93×8​. In that single fraction the 333 is a numerator and the 999 is a denominator. Simplifying a fraction means dividing its top and its bottom by a common factor, exactly the rule from the equivalent-fractions lesson. So it makes no difference which of the two starting fractions each number came from.

The full proof shows that cancelling before you multiply always lands on the same fraction as multiplying first and simplifying afterwards. It holds for any shared factor, in either direction across the two fractions. See the full argument in Go deeper.

Worked example 1 Compute 29×38\frac{2}{9} \times \frac{3}{8}92​×83​ by cancelling first

Look for common factors across the two fractions before multiplying. The numerator 222 and the denominator 888 share a factor of 222, and the numerator 333 and the denominator 999 share a factor of 333.

Divide the first pair by 222: the 222 becomes 111 and the 888 becomes 444. Divide the second pair by 333: the 333 becomes 111 and the 999 becomes 333. The product is now built from small numbers:

29×38=13×14=1×13×4=112.\frac{2}{9} \times \frac{3}{8} = \frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1 \times 1}{3 \times 4} = \frac{1}{12}.92​×83​=31​×41​=3×41×1​=121​.

Cancelling first landed directly on 112\frac{1}{12}121​, in lowest terms, without ever multiplying up to a large fraction that then needs simplifying.

The reciprocal of a nonzero fraction

The reciprocal of a nonzero fraction is what you get by swapping its numerator and denominator, that is by flipping it over. The reciprocal of 35\frac{3}{5}53​ is 53\frac{5}{3}35​, and the reciprocal of 27\frac{2}{7}72​ is 72\frac{7}{2}27​. A whole number such as 444 is 41\frac{4}{1}14​, so its reciprocal is 14\frac{1}{4}41​.

The reason the reciprocal matters is a single clean fact: a nonzero fraction times its reciprocal is always 111.

A nonzero fraction times its reciprocal equals 111#

Start with 58\frac{5}{8}85​, whose reciprocal is 85\frac{8}{5}58​. Multiplying tops and bottoms gives 58×85=5×88×5=4040\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{8}{5} = \frac{5 \times 8}{8 \times 5} = \frac{40}{40}85​×58​=8×55×8​=4040​. Top and bottom are the same product 404040, and a fraction whose top equals its bottom is 111.

Nothing there used the digits 555 and 888. Take any nonzero fraction ab\frac{a}{b}ba​, meaning aaa and bbb are both nonzero, so flipping it gives the fraction ba\frac{b}{a}ab​. Multiplying the two gives

ab×ba=a×bb×a=1,\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{a} = \frac{a \times b}{b \times a} = 1,ba​×ab​=b×aa×b​=1,

because the numerator a×ba \times ba×b and the denominator b×ab \times ab×a are the same product in a different order, and a fraction whose numerator equals its denominator is 111. Both aaa and bbb have to be nonzero: flipping 0b\frac{0}{b}b0​ would put a 000 in the denominator, which is undefined.

∎

Two numbers whose product is 111 are called reciprocals of each other. You can read the relation both ways: 35×53=1\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{5}{3} = 153​×35​=1, so each is the reciprocal of the other. That is the whole reason a reciprocal matters: multiplying by it undoes a multiplication by the original nonzero fraction.

Answer choices

The reciprocal flips the numerator and denominator, so the reciprocal of 74\frac{7}{4}47​ is 47\frac{4}{7}74​. Multiply to check.

74×47=7×44×7=2828=1\frac{7}{4} \times \frac{4}{7} = \frac{7 \times 4}{4 \times 7} = \frac{28}{28} = 147​×74​=4×77×4​=2828​=1

A nonzero fraction times its reciprocal is always 111, because the top and bottom end up as the same product.

Why dividing by a nonzero fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal

Division asks a counting question: a÷ba \div ba÷b asks “how many bbb‘s fit into aaa?” Keep that meaning and apply it to fractions. The expression 34÷14\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{1}{4}43​÷41​ asks “how many fourths fit into three fourths?” The picture below answers that directly.

That picture answers the question for one size of piece. Below you can change the size. The top bar is fixed at three fourths. The bottom bar is that same three fourths, with each of its parts cut into as many pieces as you choose.

The numerator on the bottom bar counts how many of the smaller pieces fit into three fourths. Cut each fourth into 222 and the bar reads 68\frac{6}{8}86​, so six eighths cover the same amount. That means six one-eighth pieces fit into three fourths, and 34÷18=6\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{1}{8} = 643​÷81​=6. Notice what flipping does here: 6=34×816 = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{1}6=43​×18​, which is 34\frac{3}{4}43​ times the reciprocal of 18\frac{1}{8}81​. Cut into 333 instead and the bar reads 912\frac{9}{12}129​, so 34÷112=9\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{1}{12} = 943​÷121​=9, matching 34×121=364=9\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{12}{1} = \frac{36}{4} = 943​×112​=436​=9.

Now run the cut all the way to its finest and watch which direction the count moves. The pieces keep shrinking and more of them fit, which is exactly why dividing by a smaller fraction gives a larger answer.

Why does flipping the divisor work in general? Dividing undoes multiplying: the answer to 34÷23\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{3}43​÷32​ is whatever number, multiplied by 23\frac{2}{3}32​, gets back to 34\frac{3}{4}43​. Multiplying by 23\frac{2}{3}32​‘s reciprocal 32\frac{3}{2}23​ is exactly what cancels 23\frac{2}{3}32​ away, because 23×32=1\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{2} = 132​×23​=1, the reciprocal fact from the last section. So multiplying by the reciprocal of the divisor always finds the number that division asks for.

Dividing by 23\frac{2}{3}32​ means multiplying by which fraction, and why that one?

Answer choices

Dividing by 23\frac{2}{3}32​ asks for the number that, multiplied by 23\frac{2}{3}32​, gets back to the original amount. Multiplying by the reciprocal 32\frac{3}{2}23​ is what does that, because a nonzero fraction times its reciprocal is always 111: 23×32=1\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{2} = 132​×23​=1. That is the reason flipping the divisor works, not a coincidence about the size of the numbers.

Division rule. To divide by a nonzero fraction, multiply by its reciprocal: ab÷cd=ab×dc.\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}.ba​÷dc​=ba​×cd​.

The rule needs ccc to be nonzero, because the reciprocal of 0d\frac{0}{d}d0​ would be d0\frac{d}{0}0d​, and nothing can be divided by zero.

The full proof shows that flipping and multiplying is not a trick to memorise. It produces the only number that answers the division question, for any two fractions with a nonzero divisor, by treating a quotient as a missing factor. The full argument is in Go deeper, below.

See why flipping is forced

So to divide by a nonzero fraction, leave the first fraction alone, flip the second, and multiply, using the cancelling shortcut if it helps. For example,

23÷49=23×94=2×93×4=1812=32,\frac{2}{3} \div \frac{4}{9} = \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{9}{4} = \frac{2 \times 9}{3 \times 4} = \frac{18}{12} = \frac{3}{2},32​÷94​=32​×49​=3×42×9​=1218​=23​,

where the last step divides top and bottom by their greatest common factor 666. A common slip is to flip the wrong fraction. Only the divisor, the fraction you are dividing by, gets turned over; the first fraction stays exactly as it is.

Worked example 2 Compute 56÷23\frac{5}{6} \div \frac{2}{3}65​÷32​

Dividing by 23\frac{2}{3}32​ is multiplying by its reciprocal 32\frac{3}{2}23​. Rewrite the division as a multiplication, keeping the first fraction as it is:

56÷23=56×32.\frac{5}{6} \div \frac{2}{3} = \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{3}{2}.65​÷32​=65​×23​.

Cancel before multiplying. The numerator 333 and the denominator 666 share a factor of 333, so the 333 becomes 111 and the 666 becomes 222:

56×32=52×12=5×12×2=54.\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{5}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{5 \times 1}{2 \times 2} = \frac{5}{4}.65​×23​=25​×21​=2×25×1​=45​.

So 56÷23=54\frac{5}{6} \div \frac{2}{3} = \frac{5}{4}65​÷32​=45​. Since 555 and 444 share no factor above 111, this is in lowest terms, and it does not have to be a whole number of groups: 56\frac{5}{6}65​ holds one full group of 23\frac{2}{3}32​ (that is 46\frac{4}{6}64​), with 16\frac{1}{6}61​ left over. That leftover is one fourth of a 23\frac{2}{3}32​ group, since 16\frac{1}{6}61​ is one fourth of 46\frac{4}{6}64​. One full group plus one fourth of another is one and one fourth groups in all, which is 54\frac{5}{4}45​.

Worked example 3 Compute 6÷346 \div \frac{3}{4}6÷43​

A whole number divides by a nonzero fraction the same way, once you write it over 111. Here 6=616 = \frac{6}{1}6=16​, and dividing by 34\frac{3}{4}43​ means multiplying by its reciprocal 43\frac{4}{3}34​:

6÷34=61×43.6 \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{6}{1} \times \frac{4}{3}.6÷43​=16​×34​.

Cancel the common factor 333 between the 666 on top and the 333 on the bottom. That turns the 666 into 222 and the 333 into 111:

61×43=21×41=2×41×1=8.\frac{6}{1} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{2}{1} \times \frac{4}{1} = \frac{2 \times 4}{1 \times 1} = 8.16​×34​=12​×14​=1×12×4​=8.

So 6÷34=86 \div \frac{3}{4} = 86÷43​=8. This reads correctly as a counting question: how many three-quarter pieces fit into 666 wholes? Each whole is 444 fourths, so 666 wholes are 242424 fourths, and each 34\frac{3}{4}43​ piece uses 333 of those fourths. That is 24÷3=824 \div 3 = 824÷3=8 pieces.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating multiplication like addition. Multiplying fractions does not need a common denominator, and you never add the tops or the bottoms. 23×45\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}32​×54​ is not 68\frac{6}{8}86​ and is not found by matching denominators first; multiply straight across to 2×43×5=815\frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 5} = \frac{8}{15}3×52×4​=158​. Common denominators are only for adding and subtracting.
  • Flipping the wrong fraction, or not flipping at all. In ab÷cd\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d}ba​÷dc​ with ccc nonzero, only the divisor cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ is turned over; the first fraction stays as it is. 56÷23\frac{5}{6} \div \frac{2}{3}65​÷32​ is 56×32\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{3}{2}65​×23​, never 56×23\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{2}{3}65​×32​ or 65×23\frac{6}{5} \times \frac{2}{3}56​×32​.
  • Expecting the product to be bigger. Multiplying two positive proper fractions makes the result smaller than either one, because you are taking a part of a part. And dividing a positive number by a positive fraction less than 111 makes the result bigger. These feel backwards only until you read them as “of” and “how many fit.”
  • Cancelling tops with tops, or bottoms with bottoms. A cancel must pair a numerator with a denominator, since simplifying divides the top and the bottom of the product by the same factor. Cancelling two numerators against each other changes the value.

Key takeaways

  • Multiplying fractions multiplies the numerators and the denominators: ab×cd=a×cb×d\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}ba​×dc​=b×da×c​, because you are taking a part of a part, which an area model counts as one grid of equal pieces. A whole number is a fraction over 111, so it multiplies the same way.
  • Cancel a common factor between any numerator and any denominator (from either fraction) before multiplying. It gives the same answer with smaller numbers, and you should still simplify the result to lowest terms; an improper-fraction answer such as 54\frac{5}{4}45​ is complete and correct as it is.
  • The reciprocal of a nonzero fraction ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ is ba\frac{b}{a}ab​, the fraction flipped over, and a nonzero fraction times its reciprocal is always 111.
  • Dividing by a nonzero fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal: ab÷cd=ab×dc\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}ba​÷dc​=ba​×cd​ (with c≠0c \neq 0c=0), because that is the number that undoes multiplying by cd\frac{c}{d}dc​. Only the divisor is flipped; the first fraction is left alone.

Why the grid counts every product, not just the ones drawn

The lesson draws the grid for 12×13\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3}21​×31​ and for 23×45\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}32​×54​. This runs the same count on any two fractions between 000 and 111 at once, which is what makes multiplying the tops and the bottoms a rule rather than a pattern.

Why multiplying fractions multiplies the tops and the bottoms#

Run the same grid argument once with letters, so it covers every pair of fractions between 000 and 111 at once, and nothing in it depends on which numbers you started with. Read the product as a fraction of a fraction: ab×cd\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}ba​×dc​ is ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ of cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ of one whole. Draw the whole as a square.

First mark off cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ of the square. Cut the square into ddd equal vertical strips and shade ccc of them, so the shaded region is cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ of the whole. Now take ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ of that shaded region. Cut the square into bbb equal horizontal rows, and keep aaa of those rows over the part already shaded. The piece shaded both ways is ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ of cd\frac{c}{d}dc​, which is the product you want.

Count it with the grid the two sets of cuts create. The vertical cuts made ddd columns and the horizontal cuts made bbb rows. So the whole square is divided into b×db \times db×d equal small rectangles, all the same size. The doubly shaded region is aaa rows of the kept part crossing ccc shaded columns, so it covers a×ca \times ca×c of those small rectangles. A region of a×ca \times ca×c equal pieces out of b×db \times db×d equal pieces is, by the meaning of a fraction, a×cb×d\frac{a \times c}{b \times d}b×da×c​. Therefore

ab×cd=a×cb×d.\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}.ba​×dc​=b×da×c​.

No common denominator entered anywhere, because we were never combining same-size parts the way addition does. We were cutting one whole two ways at once, and the grid did the counting. The same formula, multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms, keeps working once a fraction goes above one whole; only the single-square picture stops fitting, the way the core lesson already noted.

∎

Why cancelling early and simplifying late give the same answer

The lesson cancels 34×89\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{9}43​×98​ down to 23\frac{2}{3}32​ and checks it against the long way. This shows the two routes agree for any shared factor, so cancelling early can never cost you the right answer.

Why cancelling across the two fractions is legal#

Try it on 512×87\frac{5}{12} \times \frac{8}{7}125​×78​, where the 888 on top and the 121212 on the bottom share the factor 444. Written as one fraction the product is 5×812×7\frac{5 \times 8}{12 \times 7}12×75×8​. The shared 444 shows up on both: 888 is 4×24 \times 24×2 and 121212 is 4×34 \times 34×3. So the product is 5×4×24×3×7\frac{5 \times 4 \times 2}{4 \times 3 \times 7}4×3×75×4×2​. Dividing top and bottom by that 444 leaves 5×23×7=1021\frac{5 \times 2}{3 \times 7} = \frac{10}{21}3×75×2​=2110​, which is what cancelling first gives.

The same steps run on letters. Suppose a numerator and a denominator from the two fractions share a common factor kkk. Say the numerator ccc is k×uk \times uk×u and the denominator bbb is k×vk \times vk×v. Write the product as a single fraction, and the shared factor appears once on top and once on the bottom:

ab×cd=a×cb×d=a×k×uk×v×d.\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d} = \frac{a \times k \times u}{k \times v \times d}.ba​×dc​=b×da×c​=k×v×da×k×u​.

The same nonzero number kkk now multiplies the top and the bottom. By the simplifying rule, dividing both by that common kkk does not change the value:

a×k×uk×v×d=a×uv×d.\frac{a \times k \times u}{k \times v \times d} = \frac{a \times u}{v \times d}.k×v×da×k×u​=v×da×u​.

Nothing in that depended on kkk being 444. So cancelling early and simplifying late give the identical fraction, and cancelling early means you never build the large product.

∎

Why flipping a nonzero second fraction is what division asks for

The lesson counts how many eighths fit into three fourths and notices that flipping gives the same answer. This shows that flipping is forced: it produces the only number that answers the division question, for any two fractions with a nonzero divisor.

A bit of history (optional)

Generations of students met division by a fraction as a rhyme: “Ours is not to reason why, just invert and multiply.” No reason was given, just two words to remember.

The rhyme borrows its rhythm from an older line. In 1854 the English poet Alfred Tennyson wrote a poem about soldiers given an order nobody explained. “Theirs not to reason why,” he wrote, and they obeyed anyway.

A teacher later noticed how well that line fit an unexplained rule, and the joke stuck in classrooms. Flip the divisor, no questions asked. It gets the right answer, and it teaches nothing.

You already know better than that rhyme. A nonzero fraction times its reciprocal is one. That single fact is the whole reason flipping the divisor clears it away. The rhyme is easier to remember than the proof. The proof, from earlier in this lesson, is the reason the rule is actually true.

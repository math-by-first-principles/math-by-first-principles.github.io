    1. One sheet, cut two ways . Foundational, 9 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 9 pts

    A sheet of paper stands for one whole. A student shades 35\frac{3}{5}53​ of the sheet in yellow, using cuts that run down the height so the yellow region is a set of vertical strips. Working inside the yellow region only, the student then shades 27\frac{2}{7}72​ of that yellow region in blue, using bands that run across the width. Part of the sheet now carries both colours.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Extend the blue bands across the whole sheet, so that both sets of cuts run edge to edge. Report how many equal small rectangles the sheet is then divided into, how many of those rectangles carry both colours, and what fraction of the whole sheet carries both colours.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Now state the general case. Using letters, write what ab×cd\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}ba​×dc​ comes to as a single fraction, and say which count on the grid each of the two multiplications in your answer matches.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Adding two fractions cannot start until the two amounts are cut into pieces of the same size, which is the job a common denominator does. Explain why no common denominator was needed anywhere in parts A and B, in terms of what the two shadings do to the single sheet.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Read the multiplication sign as the word of, and keep track of what each shading is taken from: the second one was applied inside the first region, not to the bare sheet.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Look at how each of your two counts in part A was produced. One came from multiplying the numbers of cuts, the other from multiplying the numbers of strips that were kept.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask what has to be true of two amounts before you are allowed to add them, then ask whether anything in the stem ever set two separate amounts side by side.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The sheet is divided into 353535 equal rectangles, 666 of them carry both colours, and that is 635\frac{6}{35}356​ of the sheet.

    Part B

    ab×cd=a×cb×d\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}ba​×dc​=b×da×c​. The bottom product counts every small rectangle the cuts make; the top product counts the rectangles kept by both shadings.

    • acbd\frac{ac}{bd}bdac​ with the multiplication signs left out is the same statement
    • the two fractions may be named the other way round, as long as each letter keeps the same job throughout

    Part C

    Nothing was being put together. The two shadings cut one sheet in two different directions, and the grid they make is a single set of equal pieces, so the answer is read straight off that one set. Adding needs matching piece sizes because it combines two separate amounts, and a part of a part never does.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The vertical cuts divide the width into 555 equal strips and 333 of them are yellow, which is what 35\frac{3}{5}53​ of the sheet means. The bands divide the height into 777 equal rows and 222 of them are blue, which is 27\frac{2}{7}72​ of the yellow region.

    Carry both sets of cuts edge to edge. Every one of the 555 columns is crossed by every one of the 777 rows, so the sheet is cut into

    5×7=355 \times 7 = 355×7=35

    equal small rectangles, all the same size.

    A rectangle carries both colours exactly when it sits in one of the yellow columns and in one of the blue rows at the same time, so the doubly shaded region is 333 columns crossing 222 rows:

    3×2=6.3 \times 2 = 6.3×2=6.

    Six equal pieces out of 353535 equal pieces is, by what a fraction means, 635\frac{6}{35}356​ of the sheet:

    27×35=635.\frac{2}{7} \times \frac{3}{5} = \frac{6}{35}.72​×53​=356​.

    Nothing was measured. The two sets of cuts made a grid, and the grid did the counting. Since 666 and 353535 share no factor above 111, this is already in lowest terms.

    Part B

    Build the same picture with letters. Cut the sheet into ddd equal columns and shade ccc of them, which is cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ of the sheet. Then cut it into bbb equal rows and keep aaa of them over the shaded part, which takes ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ of what was shaded.

    The two sets of cuts cross, so the whole sheet is divided into

    b×db \times db×d

    equal small rectangles. The region carrying both shadings is aaa rows crossing ccc columns, so it covers

    a×ca \times ca×c

    of those rectangles. A region of a×ca \times ca×c equal pieces out of b×db \times db×d equal pieces is

    ab×cd=a×cb×d.\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}.ba​×dc​=b×da×c​.

    Part A is this statement with numbers in place of letters. The bottom product was the 5×75 \times 75×7 that counted every rectangle on the sheet, and the top product was the 3×23 \times 23×2 that counted the rectangles kept by both shadings.

    The rule holds when one of the factors is a whole number too, since a whole number is a fraction over 111: for instance 6×49=61×49=2496 \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{6}{1} \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{24}{9}6×94​=16​×94​=924​, which simplifies to 83\frac{8}{3}38​.

    Part C

    The two operations are doing different jobs, and the picture makes the difference visible.

    Adding starts with two amounts that already exist side by side. Thirds and fifths are pieces of different sizes, and there is no honest way to say how many pieces you hold until both amounts are re-cut into pieces of one common size. Building a common denominator is that re-cutting.

    A part of a part starts with one amount. The yellow shading did not arrive beside the blue shading; the blue shading was taken from inside the yellow. So there were never two piles to reconcile. What the second set of cuts did was slice the same sheet a second way, and the moment both sets run edge to edge the sheet carries one grid of rectangles that are all the same size as each other:

    5×7=35 equal rectangles.5 \times 7 = 35 \text{ equal rectangles}.5×7=35 equal rectangles.

    Equal-sized pieces are exactly what addition has to work for, and here they came free, because they were produced by the cutting itself rather than assembled from two different amounts.

    That is why the multiplication rule mentions no common denominator at all. The denominators are multiplied because they count cuts in two directions, and the numerators are multiplied because they count the strips kept in each direction. A common denominator would answer a question about combining, and no combining ever happened.

    In one line

    Extending both sets of cuts divides the sheet into 5×7=355 \times 7 = 355×7=35 equal rectangles, of which 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6 carry both colours, so 27×35=635\frac{2}{7} \times \frac{3}{5} = \frac{6}{35}72​×53​=356​. In letters, ab×cd=a×cb×d\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}ba​×dc​=b×da×c​, where the bottom product counts every rectangle the two sets of cuts make and the top product counts the ones kept by both shadings. No common denominator appears because nothing is being combined: one whole is cut two ways, and the grid that results is already a single set of equal pieces.

    2. Trimming before the product grows . Foundational, 12 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    Two fractions can be multiplied straight across and simplified at the end, or trimmed before anything is multiplied. This question runs one product both ways, then examines a trim taken from another student's page.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Compute 914×712\frac{9}{14} \times \frac{7}{12}149​×127​ by cancelling common factors before you multiply, naming each pair you cancel and the factor you divide it by. Then compute the same product by multiplying straight across and simplifying at the end, and report what each route gives.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Another student computes 625×49\frac{6}{25} \times \frac{4}{9}256​×94​ in two moves: first divide the 666 and the 444 each by 222, giving 325×29\frac{3}{25} \times \frac{2}{9}253​×92​, then multiply across, giving 6225\frac{6}{225}2256​. Say which of those two moves is the first to go wrong and why, and say what that leaves you able to conclude about the other move. Then give the correct value of the product.

      Find and correct the error Say which line first goes wrong, why it is wrong, and then do it correctly. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      In the product ab×cd\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}ba​×dc​, suppose ccc and bbb share a factor above 111, so the two numbers come from different fractions. Decide whether that factor may be cancelled before multiplying, and argue for your decision from the rule for multiplying fractions and from what simplifying does.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every product of two fractions is one fraction in disguise: all the tops end up on top and all the bottoms end up underneath. Ask what that single fraction allows you to do.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

      A trim is available only where a top number and a bottom number share a factor above 111, so check each numerator against each denominator before any multiplying starts.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Simplifying has exactly one rule: divide above and below by the same number. Write the product as a single fraction and ask whether the proposed move obeys that rule.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Both routes give 38\frac{3}{8}83​.

    Part B

    The first move is the one that fails: the two numbers divided by 222 are both numerators, so the top of the product was shrunk with no matching change underneath. The second move is faultless arithmetic on the pair it was handed. The product is 875\frac{8}{75}758​, not the 275\frac{2}{75}752​ that line reaches.

    Part C

    It may. The product is one fraction whose numerator holds both aaa and ccc and whose denominator holds both bbb and ddd, so a factor shared by ccc and bbb is a factor of that single top and of that single bottom, and dividing both by it leaves the value unchanged.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Look across the two fractions for a numerator and a denominator that share a factor. The 999 on top shares 333 with the 121212 underneath, and the 777 on top shares 777 with the 141414 underneath.

    Divide each pair by its shared factor. The 999 becomes 333 and the 121212 becomes 444; the 777 becomes 111 and the 141414 becomes 222. Only small numbers are left to multiply:

    914×712=32×14=3×12×4=38\frac{9}{14} \times \frac{7}{12} = \frac{3}{2} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3 \times 1}{2 \times 4} = \frac{3}{8}149​×127​=23​×41​=2×43×1​=83​

    Now the long route. Multiply straight across first:

    914×712=9×714×12=63168\frac{9}{14} \times \frac{7}{12} = \frac{9 \times 7}{14 \times 12} = \frac{63}{168}149​×127​=14×129×7​=16863​

    The greatest common factor of 636363 and 168168168 is 212121, so

    63168=63÷21168÷21=38.\frac{63}{168} = \frac{63 \div 21}{168 \div 21} = \frac{3}{8}.16863​=168÷2163÷21​=83​.

    The two routes name the same number, which they must: cancelling early and simplifying late divide the same product by the same factors, only in a different order. The difference is the size of the numbers you handle. The long route asked you to notice that 212121 divides both 636363 and 168168168; the trimmed route never built a number above 888.

    Part B

    Carry the student's line through to a value first, so the comparison rests on two numbers rather than on an impression:

    325×29=3×225×9=6225=275\frac{3}{25} \times \frac{2}{9} = \frac{3 \times 2}{25 \times 9} = \frac{6}{225} = \frac{2}{75}253​×92​=25×93×2​=2256​=752​

    Now the product as it was written. Multiplying across gives a single fraction:

    625×49=6×425×9=24225=875\frac{6}{25} \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{6 \times 4}{25 \times 9} = \frac{24}{225} = \frac{8}{75}256​×94​=25×96×4​=22524​=758​

    Both of those last steps divide top and bottom by 333. The two values disagree, so the opening move was not a trim, it was a change of value.

    The second move is worth acquitting explicitly. Multiplying 325×29\frac{3}{25} \times \frac{2}{9}253​×92​ across to 6225\frac{6}{225}2256​ is exactly right for the fractions it was handed; it inherits a wrong pair and processes them faultlessly. A line can be perfect arithmetic and still carry a wrong value forward, which is why the first move is the one to name.

    Here is why that first move fails. Cancelling is simplifying, and simplifying divides the top and the bottom of one fraction by the same number. In this product the 666 and the 444 both end up on TOP, since every numerator joins the one numerator 6×46 \times 46×4. Dividing each of them by 222 therefore divides the numerator by 2×2=42 \times 2 = 42×2=4 and leaves the denominator untouched, so the value falls to a quarter of what it should be:

    275×4=875.\frac{2}{75} \times 4 = \frac{8}{75}.752​×4=758​.

    A legitimate trim on this same product does exist, and it pairs a top with a bottom. The 666 on top and the 999 underneath share 333:

    625×49=225×43=2×425×3=875,\frac{6}{25} \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{2}{25} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{2 \times 4}{25 \times 3} = \frac{8}{75},256​×94​=252​×34​=25×32×4​=758​,

    which reaches the correct value with smaller numbers, exactly as a trim is supposed to.

    Part C

    Start by writing the product as the single fraction it is, which is what the multiplication rule says it comes to:

    ab×cd=a×cb×d\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}ba​×dc​=b×da×c​

    The two starting fractions have gone. There is now one numerator, a×ca \times ca×c, and one denominator, b×db \times db×d.

    Suppose ccc and bbb share a factor kkk above 111, so c=k×c′c = k \times c'c=k×c′ and b=k×b′b = k \times b'b=k×b′. Substituting shows kkk standing once on top and once underneath:

    a×cb×d=a×k×c′k×b′×d\frac{a \times c}{b \times d} = \frac{a \times k \times c'}{k \times b' \times d}b×da×c​=k×b′×da×k×c′​

    Simplifying has one rule: dividing the top and the bottom by the same nonzero number leaves the value alone. Applying it to kkk gives

    a×k×c′k×b′×d=a×c′b′×d,\frac{a \times k \times c'}{k \times b' \times d} = \frac{a \times c'}{b' \times d},k×b′×da×k×c′​=b′×da×c′​,

    which is exactly the product you get by replacing ccc with c′c'c′ and bbb with b′b'b′ before multiplying. So cancelling first is legitimate, and it produces the same number as multiplying first and simplifying afterwards.

    Notice what the argument never used: which of the two starting fractions ccc and bbb came from. Once the product is formed, every numerator has joined the one top and every denominator has joined the one bottom, so a numerator cannot tell whether the denominator it is cancelling against was its own or its neighbour's. What the argument does use is that one number sat on top and the other underneath. Two numerators share no such relationship, which is why part B's move failed.

    In one line

    914×712=38\frac{9}{14} \times \frac{7}{12} = \frac{3}{8}149​×127​=83​ by both routes: cancelling the 999 against the 121212 and the 777 against the 141414 never builds a number above 888, while multiplying first gives 63168\frac{63}{168}16863​ and needs the factor 212121 to be spotted. The student's opening move is where the second product goes wrong, because the 666 and the 444 both sit on top of the single fraction 6×425×9\frac{6 \times 4}{25 \times 9}25×96×4​, so dividing each by 222 shrinks the numerator by a factor of 444 and leaves the denominator alone: the product is 875\frac{8}{75}758​, not 275\frac{2}{75}752​. Their multiply-across step is sound arithmetic on the pair it inherited, which is what makes the first move the one to name. Cancelling a numerator against the other fraction's denominator is legitimate, because once the product is written as a×cb×d\frac{a \times c}{b \times d}b×da×c​ the shared factor stands once above and once below, and dividing both by it is simply simplifying.

    Another way: Trim with prime factorizations

    Write every numerator and denominator as a product of primes, then strike out each prime that appears both above and below the bar. For the product in part A,

    914×712=3×32×7×72×2×3\frac{9}{14} \times \frac{7}{12} = \frac{3 \times 3}{2 \times 7} \times \frac{7}{2 \times 2 \times 3}149​×127​=2×73×3​×2×2×37​

    One 333 on top matches the 333 in the bottom of the second fraction, and the 777 on top matches the 777 in the bottom of the first. Striking both pairs leaves

    32×2×2=38.\frac{3}{2 \times 2 \times 2} = \frac{3}{8}.2×2×23​=83​.

    Nothing had to be spotted by eye, because the prime factorizations put every shared factor on display at once.

    When it is worth it When the numbers are large enough that a shared factor is easy to miss, or when a first trim leaves numbers that still have something in common.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The recipe calls for 23\frac{2}{3}32​ of the oil in the jug, and the word of is the multiplication:

    23×910\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{9}{10}32​×109​

    Trim before multiplying. The 222 on top shares 222 with the 101010 underneath, and the 999 on top shares 333 with the 333 underneath. The 222 becomes 111 and the 101010 becomes 555; the 999 becomes 333 and the 333 becomes 111:

    23×910=11×35=35\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{9}{10} = \frac{1}{1} \times \frac{3}{5} = \frac{3}{5}32​×109​=11​×53​=53​

    Part B

    Each bottle holds 320\frac{3}{20}203​ of a litre, so the question is how many helpings of that size fit inside the oil that was measured out. That is a division:

Because the cases cover their range and never overlap, each half also reads backwards on that range. A starting number of 000 has to be excluded, since every multiplier leaves it unchanged.

    Another way: Predict the direction before computing

    For a positive number the direction can be settled by reading the multiplier as a number of copies, with no arithmetic at all. Multiplying by 25\frac{2}{5}52​ asks for two fifths of one copy of the number, which is part of a copy, so the result must be less than the number. Multiplying by 52\frac{5}{2}25​ asks for five halves of a copy, which is more than one whole copy, so the result must be more than the number.

    Division reads the same way through the reciprocal:

    38÷25=38×52\frac{3}{8} \div \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3}{8} \times \frac{5}{2}83​÷52​=83​×25​

    Since 25\frac{2}{5}52​ is below 111 and its reciprocal is above 111, dividing a positive number by a positive fraction below 111 has to grow it. The word positive is doing work in that sentence: a negative divisor sends the quotient below 000 and so below the number you started from.

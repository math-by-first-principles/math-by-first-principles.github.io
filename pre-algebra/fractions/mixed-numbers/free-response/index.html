    1. Two numbers, one space . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    A mixed number is written by setting a whole number directly beside a fraction, with nothing between them. In algebra, and in plain arithmetic once a fraction is involved, that arrangement normally means multiply, so this notation is carrying an instruction that never appears on the page. This question makes both readings explicit and then asks what keeps them from being mistaken for one another.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Take 3273\tfrac{2}{7}372​. Work out the single fraction it names under the mixed-number convention, and then work out the single fraction those same two numbers would name if the space between them were read as a multiplication. Report both, each labelled with the reading that produced it.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Pin each of your two values from part A between two consecutive whole numbers. Then say which of the two readings agrees with the whole number written at the front of the notation, and why that agreement matters to someone reading the symbol.

      Carry your own answer forward Use the two values you reached in part A, whatever they came out to. If part A did not come out, you can still place any fraction between whole numbers by asking how many times its denominator fits inside its numerator.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The mixed-number convention contradicts the usual reading of two numbers written side by side, which in algebra and wherever a fraction is involved means multiply, and yet readers almost never take one for the other. Explain what property of the fraction part keeps the two readings apart, and say what would become of that safeguard if someone set a fraction larger than 111 beside a whole number.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing here is settled by the two numbers themselves. Work out what the symbols would name under each of the two possible readings first, and only then ask which one a reader is meant to take.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Any fraction can be placed between whole numbers by asking how many times the denominator fits inside the numerator. Do that for both values and see where each of them lands.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Compare what adding a quantity smaller than one whole does to a number with what multiplying by that same quantity does. One of those can never make the number smaller and the other always does, and that gap is the whole safeguard.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The mixed-number reading names 237\tfrac{23}{7}723​. The multiplication reading names 67\tfrac{6}{7}76​.

    Part B

    The mixed-number reading lands between 333 and 444, so it keeps the promise made by the 333 in front. The multiplication reading lands between 000 and 111, below the whole number it is written beside, which is not what a leading 333 leads a reader to expect.

    Part C

    The fraction part of a mixed number is proper, so multiplying by it always lands strictly below the whole number, while adding it never lands below and lands strictly above whenever that fraction part is above zero. With a fraction larger than 111 there, both readings land above the whole number and the safeguard is gone.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The two readings are two different calculations, so carry both out in full before comparing them.

    Under the mixed-number convention the space means add, so the whole number joins the fraction. Write the 333 in sevenths, since one whole is 77\tfrac{7}{7}77​ and three wholes are 3×7=213 \times 7 = 213×7=21 sevenths:

    327=217+27=237.3\tfrac{2}{7} = \frac{21}{7} + \frac{2}{7} = \frac{23}{7}.372​=721​+72​=723​.

    Read the same space as a multiplication and the whole number scales the fraction instead, which is the multiply-across rule with 333 written as 31\tfrac{3}{1}13​:

    3×27=31×27=67.3 \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{3}{1} \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{6}{7}.3×72​=13​×72​=76​.

    Both calculations are correct arithmetic. What separates them is not the arithmetic but the instruction the notation is understood to carry, and the two results are nothing like each other.

    Part B

    Place each value by asking how many times the denominator fits inside the numerator.

    For the mixed-number reading, seven fits into twenty-three three times with two sevenths left over, so the value has passed 333 but has not reached 444:

    3<237<4.3 < \frac{23}{7} < 4.3<723​<4.

    For the multiplication reading, seven does not fit into six at all, so the value has not even reached one whole:

    0<67<1.0 < \frac{6}{7} < 1.0<76​<1.

    Now read the notation the way a reader does. The 333 at the front announces three complete wholes and a little more. Only one of the two values delivers that. The addition reading sits in the interval the leading 333 points at, while the multiplication reading sits below 111 and leaves the 333 describing nothing at all.

    That is what makes the convention worth its oddity. A notation whose leading number told you nothing about the size of the value would be harder to read than the improper fraction it replaced, and the whole point of a mixed number is that its size can be seen at a glance.

    Part C

    The safeguard is the condition a mixed number always satisfies: its fraction part is proper, so it is less than 111, and its whole part is at least 111.

    Take the multiplication reading first, because its inequality needs nothing checked. Multiplying by a number below 111 takes only part of a quantity, and the whole part is at least 111, so the product lands strictly below the whole number:

    3×27<3×1=3.3 \times \frac{2}{7} < 3 \times 1 = 3.3×72​<3×1=3.

    The addition reading can never go below the whole number, since a fraction part is never negative, and it goes strictly above as soon as that fraction part is above zero, which it is in every mixed number anyone writes:

    3≤3+27.3 \le 3 + \frac{2}{7}.3≤3+72​.

    So the two readings land on opposite sides of the number written at the front: the multiplication reading strictly below it, the addition reading never below it. A reader who glances at the leading number knows at once which side the value is meant to be on, and the convention costs nothing in practice because the rival reading is never plausible. (The single case where the addition reading lands exactly on the whole number is a fraction part of zero, such as 3093\tfrac{0}{9}390​, and nobody writes that, because it is just 333.)

    Now drop the condition and watch the safeguard fail. Suppose someone writes 2532\tfrac{5}{3}235​, with a fraction part larger than 111. The addition reading gives 2+53=1132 + \tfrac{5}{3} = \tfrac{11}{3}2+35​=311​ and the multiplication reading gives 2×53=1032 \times \tfrac{5}{3} = \tfrac{10}{3}2×35​=310​:

    113>2and103>2.\frac{11}{3} > 2 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{10}{3} > 2.311​>2and310​>2.

    Both land above the 222, and they sit only 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ apart besides, so nothing about the size of the result tells a reader which was meant. That is why a mixed number is required to carry a proper fraction. The requirement is not tidiness; it is what keeps the notation readable.

    In one line

    Under the mixed-number convention 327=2373\tfrac{2}{7} = \tfrac{23}{7}372​=723​, which sits between 333 and 444; read as a multiplication the same symbols give 3×27=673 \times \tfrac{2}{7} = \tfrac{6}{7}3×72​=76​, which sits below 111. Only the addition reading keeps the promise made by the 333 at the front. The two readings stay apart because the fraction part of a mixed number is proper: multiplying by it always lands strictly below the whole number, while adding it never lands below and lands strictly above whenever that fraction part is above zero. Set a fraction larger than 111 there, as in 2532\tfrac{5}{3}235​, and both readings land above the 222, so the safeguard disappears.

    2. Reading a division back into a fraction . Foundational, 14 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 14 pts

    Every conversion from an improper fraction to a mixed number is a division with a remainder, and the two pieces of that division do different jobs: one counts wholes, the other counts leftover pieces. This question runs three conversions and then tests a rule a classmate has written down about which fractions are improper.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write 538\tfrac{53}{8}853​ as a mixed number by dividing the numerator by the denominator. Then say what the quotient counts and what the remainder counts, naming the pieces the denominator has cut each whole into.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Two more improper fractions come out of the same piece of work: 346\tfrac{34}{6}634​ and 729\tfrac{72}{9}972​. Convert each of them, giving every fraction part in lowest terms and each result in whatever form the division actually produces.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A classmate writes down this test: a fraction is improper exactly when writing it as a mixed number gives a whole number with a fraction beside it. Decide whether that test is right in both directions, support each verdict with a case, and if either direction goes wrong, repair it.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 6 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every conversion in this question is one division question asked carefully: how many whole groups of the denominator fit inside the numerator, and how many pieces are left standing outside them?

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Divide first and simplify afterwards, then read each result against what a mixed number is supposed to carry. Be ready for a conversion that produces no fraction part at all.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      A rule that says "exactly when" is really two rules pointing opposite ways. Check one, then swap the two halves round and check the other, and remember that a single case is enough to bring a direction down.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    538=658\tfrac{53}{8} = 6\tfrac{5}{8}853​=685​. The quotient counts complete wholes and the remainder counts the eighths left over, too few to make another whole.

    Part B

    346=546=523\tfrac{34}{6} = 5\tfrac{4}{6} = 5\tfrac{2}{3}634​=564​=532​, and 729=8\tfrac{72}{9} = 8972​=8, a whole number with no fraction part beside it.

    Part C

    One direction holds, the other fails. A value made of a whole number of at least 111 plus a proper fraction is at least 111, so its numerator is at least its denominator: improper. But an improper fraction whose numerator is a multiple of its denominator converts to a whole number alone. Test the definition instead.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The fraction bar was a division before it was anything else, so ask the plain division question: how many complete groups of 888 fit inside 535353, and what is left standing outside them?

    53÷8=6 remainder 5.53 \div 8 = 6 \text{ remainder } 5.53÷8=6 remainder 5.

    Now read those two numbers back through the fraction. Eight eighths make one whole, so each complete group of 888 eighths is one whole unit: the quotient 666 counts wholes. The remainder 555 is the eighths that could not be gathered into another whole, so it counts leftover eighths, and it keeps the denominator it was already measured in:

    538=658.\frac{53}{8} = 6\tfrac{5}{8}.853​=685​.

    The leftover is guaranteed to be a proper fraction, and that is not luck. A remainder is always smaller than the number you divided by, so the remainder is always smaller than the denominator, which is exactly the condition a mixed number's fraction part has to meet.

    Part B

    Take them one at a time, and simplify only once the division is done.

    Six fits into 343434 five times, using 303030, and leaves 444:

    34÷6=5 remainder 4,346=546.34 \div 6 = 5 \text{ remainder } 4, \qquad \frac{34}{6} = 5\tfrac{4}{6}.34÷6=5 remainder 4,634​=564​.

    The fraction part is not yet in lowest terms, since 444 and 666 share the factor 222, so divide top and bottom by it:

    546=523.5\tfrac{4}{6} = 5\tfrac{2}{3}.564​=532​.

    The second conversion behaves differently. Nine fits into 727272 exactly eight times and leaves nothing over:

    72÷9=8 remainder 0,729=8.72 \div 9 = 8 \text{ remainder } 0, \qquad \frac{72}{9} = 8.72÷9=8 remainder 0,972​=8.

    There is no fraction part to write. The conversion does not record a zero remainder at all, so what it hands you is the whole number 888. Writing 8098\tfrac{0}{9}890​ instead names the same value and breaks no rule, but it is not the output of the conversion: the fraction part exists to record what was left over, and here nothing was. Either way the standard result is the whole number 888, which is a reminder that an improper fraction does not always convert into a whole number with a fraction beside it.

    Part C

    A claim of the form "exactly when" is two claims facing opposite ways, and each needs its own test.

    Start with the direction that works. Suppose a fraction converts to a mixed number: a whole number www of at least 111 with a proper fraction ab\tfrac{a}{b}ba​ beside it. A fraction part is never negative, so the value is at least the whole part:

    w+ab≥w≥1.w + \frac{a}{b} \ge w \ge 1.w+ba​≥w≥1.

    A fraction of whole numbers whose value is at least 111 has a numerator at least as large as its denominator, and that is exactly what improper means, so this direction is sound. It is ≥1\ge 1≥1 that does the work here rather than >1> 1>1: a value of exactly 111 is still improper, as 99\tfrac{9}{9}99​ shows below.

    Now the other direction, and part B has already supplied the case that settles it. The fraction 729\tfrac{72}{9}972​ is improper, since its numerator is far larger than its denominator, and yet the conversion leaves no remainder at all:

    729=8.\frac{72}{9} = 8.972​=8.

    The result is a whole number with nothing standing beside it, so the classmate's test would refuse to call 729\tfrac{72}{9}972​ improper. It is improper. One case is enough to sink a direction.

    The boundary is worth naming too, because it fails the same way and is easy to miss. When the numerator equals the denominator,

    99=1,\frac{9}{9} = 1,99​=1,

    the fraction is improper under the usual definition, and again it converts to a whole number with no fraction part.

    The repair is to stop testing by the shape of the converted form and go back to the definition: for a fraction of whole numbers, it is improper exactly when the numerator is greater than or equal to the denominator. That version holds in both directions, and it covers the whole-number cases the classmate's version leaves out.

    In one line

    538=658\tfrac{53}{8} = 6\tfrac{5}{8}853​=685​, where the quotient 666 counts complete wholes and the remainder 555 counts leftover eighths. The other two conversions give 346=523\tfrac{34}{6} = 5\tfrac{2}{3}634​=532​ after simplifying, and 729=8\tfrac{72}{9} = 8972​=8 with no fraction part at all. The classmate's test is sound in one direction, since a whole number of at least 111 with a proper fraction beside it has a value of at least 111, so its numerator is at least its denominator and it is improper; but it fails in the other, because 729\tfrac{72}{9}972​ and 99\tfrac{9}{9}99​ are improper and convert to whole numbers alone. The repair is to test the definition itself, numerator greater than or equal to denominator.

    3. Edging the flower beds . Application, 12 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 12 pts

    A gardener is putting a flexible timber strip around the flower beds in a community garden. One full roll of the strip is 161216\tfrac{1}{2}1621​ feet long. A standard bed takes 3233\tfrac{2}{3}332​ feet of strip, and a large bed takes 1121\tfrac{1}{2}121​ times as much strip as a standard bed.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write a single expression, with both mixed numbers rewritten as improper fractions, for the length of strip one large bed takes. Then evaluate it and give the length as a mixed number of feet.

      Model the situation Name your unknown first, then write every other quantity in terms of that one letter. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      How many standard-bed lengths of strip does one full roll hold? Divide the roll's length by the length one standard bed takes, working in improper fractions, and report the quotient as a mixed number.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The quotient in part B is not a whole number. Say what its fraction part counts, in the words of the situation, and then work out how many feet of strip are actually left on the roll once as many standard beds as possible have been edged. Say why those two numbers are not the same.

      Carry your own answer forward Continue from the quotient you reached in part B, whatever it came out to. If part B did not come out, you can still answer the second half by multiplying one bed's length by the number of complete beds you can edge and taking that away from the roll.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Both operations in this question want single fractions rather than mixed numbers. Convert everything before multiplying or dividing, and convert back only when you are ready to state an answer someone can picture.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Asking how many of one length fit inside another is a division. Multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor, and cancel common factors before multiplying out, or the numerators get large fast.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask what the division in part B was counting, and then ask what unit the strip still on the roll is measured in. If those two answers are not the same word, the two leftovers cannot be the same number.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The expression is 32×113\tfrac{3}{2} \times \tfrac{11}{3}23​×311​, and it comes to 5125\tfrac{1}{2}521​ feet.

    Part B

    The quotient is 4124\tfrac{1}{2}421​, reached as 332×311\tfrac{33}{2} \times \tfrac{3}{11}233​×113​.

    Part C

    The fraction part counts beds, not feet: it says half of one more standard bed could be edged. Four beds use 142314\tfrac{2}{3}1432​ feet, so 1561\tfrac{5}{6}165​ feet of strip remain, and that is half of one bed's 3233\tfrac{2}{3}332​ feet.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    "One and a half times as much" is a multiplication, so the length wanted is 1121\tfrac{1}{2}121​ of the standard bed's 3233\tfrac{2}{3}332​ feet. Neither number can be multiplied in mixed form, so convert both first: multiply the whole part by the denominator, add the numerator, and keep the denominator.

    112=1×2+12=32,323=3×3+23=113.1\tfrac{1}{2} = \frac{1 \times 2 + 1}{2} = \frac{3}{2}, \qquad 3\tfrac{2}{3} = \frac{3 \times 3 + 2}{3} = \frac{11}{3}.121​=21×2+1​=23​,332​=33×3+2​=311​.

    Now multiply across, cancelling the 333 that stands on the top of one fraction and the bottom of the other:

    32×113=3×112×3=112.\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{11}{3} = \frac{3 \times 11}{2 \times 3} = \frac{11}{2}.23​×311​=2×33×11​=211​.

    The answer is wanted as a mixed number, so divide: 11÷2=511 \div 2 = 511÷2=5 remainder 111.

    112=512.\frac{11}{2} = 5\tfrac{1}{2}.211​=521​.

    A large bed takes 5125\tfrac{1}{2}521​ feet of strip. The size is believable, and a second route confirms it: half as much again as 3233\tfrac{2}{3}332​ feet means adding 1561\tfrac{5}{6}165​ feet to it, and 346+156=496=5123\tfrac{4}{6} + 1\tfrac{5}{6} = 4\tfrac{9}{6} = 5\tfrac{1}{2}364​+165​=469​=521​.

    Part B

    Dividing asks how many copies of one length fit inside another, which is exactly what the question wants. Convert both mixed numbers first:

    1612=332,323=113.16\tfrac{1}{2} = \frac{33}{2}, \qquad 3\tfrac{2}{3} = \frac{11}{3}.1621​=233​,332​=311​.

    Dividing by 113\tfrac{11}{3}311​ means multiplying by its reciprocal 311\tfrac{3}{11}113​:

    332÷113=332×311.\frac{33}{2} \div \frac{11}{3} = \frac{33}{2} \times \frac{3}{11}.233​÷311​=233​×113​.

    Cancel before multiplying, since 333333 and 111111 share the factor 111111 and leave 333 on top:

    332×311=3×32=92=412.\frac{33}{2} \times \frac{3}{11} = \frac{3 \times 3}{2} = \frac{9}{2} = 4\tfrac{1}{2}.233​×113​=23×3​=29​=421​.

    So one roll goes 4124\tfrac{1}{2}421​ of the way through the standard beds. Reporting 92\tfrac{9}{2}29​ would be the same value, but the mixed form is the one that shows at a glance how many beds are covered.

    Part C

    A quotient is counted in whatever unit the division was asking about, and the division asked how many standard beds fit. So the quotient is measured in beds. Its whole part, 444, says four complete beds can be edged, and its fraction part, 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, says that after those four there is enough strip for half of another bed. It is not half of a foot.

    To find the strip actually left, work in feet. Four beds use

    4×323=41×113=443=1423 feet.4 \times 3\tfrac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{1} \times \frac{11}{3} = \frac{44}{3} = 14\tfrac{2}{3} \text{ feet}.4×332​=14​×311​=344​=1432​ feet.

    Take that away from the roll, over the common denominator 666:

    332−443=996−886=116=156 feet.\frac{33}{2} - \frac{44}{3} = \frac{99}{6} - \frac{88}{6} = \frac{11}{6} = 1\tfrac{5}{6} \text{ feet}.233​−344​=699​−688​=611​=165​ feet.

    So 1561\tfrac{5}{6}165​ feet of strip remain on the roll.

    The two leftovers differ because they count different things: one counts beds and the other counts feet. They agree perfectly once the unit is converted, and the check is a single multiplication, half of one bed's length:

    12×113=116=156.\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{11}{3} = \frac{11}{6} = 1\tfrac{5}{6}.21​×311​=611​=165​.

    Reading the fraction part of a quotient in the wrong unit is the standard trap in a division like this one. The cure is to ask what the division was counting before reading its answer back into the situation.

    In one line

    A large bed takes 32×113=112=512\tfrac{3}{2} \times \tfrac{11}{3} = \tfrac{11}{2} = 5\tfrac{1}{2}23​×311​=211​=521​ feet of strip. One roll edges 332÷113=412\tfrac{33}{2} \div \tfrac{11}{3} = 4\tfrac{1}{2}233​÷311​=421​ standard beds, and that fraction part is counted in beds: there is enough left for half of another bed, not half of a foot. Four beds use 142314\tfrac{2}{3}1432​ feet, so 1561\tfrac{5}{6}165​ feet of strip remain, which is exactly 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ of one bed's 3233\tfrac{2}{3}332​ feet.

Marking rubrics

Work every question first. Then mark your own paper against these, giving yourself the points for each element you actually wrote down.

1. Two numbers, one space 10 points

Part A 3

  • Carries both readings out to a single fraction, rather than working one and describing the other. 2
  • Attaches each of the two fractions to the reading that produced it. 1

Part B 3

  • Places each of the two values between a pair of consecutive whole numbers, rather than restating the fractions. 2
  • Says which reading agrees with the whole number written at the front, and what that agreement gives a reader. 1

Part C 4

  • Names the property of the fraction part that the two readings turn on, and says what each reading does to the whole number because of it. 3
  • Says what becomes of the safeguard when that property is dropped, using a case rather than an assertion. 1

2. Reading a division back into a fraction 14 points

Part A 4

  • Divides the numerator by the denominator and reports both the quotient and the remainder. 2
  • Says what each of the quotient and the remainder counts, in terms of the pieces the denominator names. 2

Part B 4

  • Divides each fraction and reports the whole-number part and the remainder for both. 2
  • Gives each result in the form the division actually produces. 1
  • Reduces a fraction part that is not already in lowest terms. 1

Part C 6

  • Treats the claim as two separate statements and tests each of them in turn. 3
  • Supports each verdict with a specific fraction rather than a general assertion about improper fractions. 2
  • States a verdict on each direction separately, and supplies a repaired test wherever one is needed. 1

3. Edging the flower beds 12 points

Part A 4

  • Turns the comparison "times as much" into a product of the two given quantities before any arithmetic happens. 2
  • Converts both mixed numbers to improper fractions and multiplies across. 1
  • States the result as a mixed number with the unit of length attached. 1

Part B 3

  • Sets the division up as the roll divided by one standard bed, and multiplies by the reciprocal once both are improper fractions. 2
  • Reports the quotient as a mixed number rather than leaving it top-heavy. 1

Part C 5

  • Names the unit the quotient's fraction part is counted in, and says what it claims in the words of the situation. 2
  • Works out the strip remaining from the number of complete beds, rather than from the quotient itself. 2
  • Distinguishes the units of the two quantities and shows that they agree. 1

4. Carrying one way, exchanging the other 15 points

Part A 4

  • Adds the fraction parts over a common denominator and the whole parts separately. 2
  • Ends with a fraction part that is proper and in lowest terms, moving any extra whole into the whole-number column. 1
  • Names the properties of addition that license splitting one sum into two columns. 1

Part B 6

  • Puts the two fraction parts over a common denominator before deciding whether an exchange is needed. 2
  • Writes down the exchanged form of the first mixed number, with one whole moved into the fraction column at its correct worth. 2
  • Says why the exchanged form is the same number as the one it replaces, rather than only writing it down. 1
  • Reports a final mixed number whose fraction part is proper. 1

Part C 5

  • Locates the wrong step in the fraction column and says what reversing that column does to the value. 2
  • Quantifies the gap between the reported value and a correct one. 1

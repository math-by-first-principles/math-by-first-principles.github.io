    1. What one cutting does to both counts . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    A strip of card is divided into 777 equal parts and 222 of them are coloured. Nobody touches the colouring after that. Every one of the 777 parts is then cut into 333 equal pieces, so the card now carries more cut lines and exactly the same colour. This question follows the two counts through that cutting.

    1. A
      Part A.

      After the second cutting, count the pieces the whole strip now holds and count the pieces that are coloured. Report both counts, and write the coloured share as a fraction in terms of the new pieces.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A second strip is divided into 999 equal parts with 444 of them coloured, and every one of those parts is then cut into 222 equal pieces. A classmate records that cutting as 49=89\frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{9}94​=98​, on the grounds that the coloured pieces went from 444 to 888 while the card stayed the same card. Say what a denominator of 999 claims about the pieces that strip is now in, set that claim against what the cutting does, and give the line that records it.

      Find and correct the error Say which line first goes wrong, why it is wrong, and then do it correctly. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain, from the cutting rather than from the rule, why one re-cutting scales the numerator and the denominator by the same number and therefore leaves the value alone. Say also what happens to the amount if only one of the two counts is scaled, on a strip that has some colour on it. Then say why the multiplier is never allowed to be 000.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing here needs a rule quoted at it. Follow the cut lines instead, and ask what happens to the number of pieces in the whole strip and to the number of coloured pieces when every single part is sliced the same way.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Read the classmate's fraction on its own terms first. The number underneath is a count of the pieces the whole is in, so ask how many pieces their line says the card holds, and whether the card is in that many after the slicing.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Name what each of the two numbers counts before you say what happens to it. The reason has to come from the cut lines, since the rule is only the record of what the cutting does.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The strip holds 212121 pieces and 666 of them are coloured, so the coloured share is 621\frac{6}{21}216​ of the strip.

    Part B

    A denominator of 999 says the strip is still in 999 pieces, and after the cutting it is in 181818. The classmate scaled the coloured count and left the piece count alone. The cutting is recorded as 49=818\frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{18}94​=188​.

    Part C

    One cutting scales both counts at once: every part of the whole becomes ccc pieces and so does every coloured part, with no card added or removed. On a coloured strip, scaling one count alone colours in card or rubs it out, so the amount moves. A multiplier of 000 leaves nothing to count, and a denominator of 000 names no number.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Follow the two counts separately, because one cutting acts on both of them.

    The strip was in 777 equal parts and every part became 333 pieces, so the whole strip now holds

    7×3=21 pieces.7 \times 3 = 21 \text{ pieces.}7×3=21 pieces.

    The 222 coloured parts went under the same knife, so each of them became 333 pieces too:

    2×3=6 coloured pieces.2 \times 3 = 6 \text{ coloured pieces.}2×3=6 coloured pieces.

    Six of the 212121 pieces are coloured, so in terms of the new pieces the coloured share is

    621.\frac{6}{21}.216​.

    No card was added and none was taken away; only the cut lines changed. So 621\frac{6}{21}216​ and 27\frac{2}{7}72​ are two descriptions of one and the same coloured amount.

    Part B

    Take the classmate's line at its word before judging it. A denominator counts the pieces the whole is in, so 89\frac{8}{9}98​ claims the strip is in 999 pieces with 888 of them coloured.

    That strip is not in 999 pieces any more. Every one of its parts was cut into 222, so

    9×2=189 \times 2 = 189×2=18

    pieces make up the strip now, and the 444 coloured parts became

    4×2=84 \times 2 = 84×2=8

    coloured pieces. The classmate scaled the coloured count and left the piece count exactly as it was, which describes a strip whose parts were never cut at all.

    Read the two lines back as amounts of colour. 49\frac{4}{9}94​ colours 444 of the 999 parts, under half the strip; 89\frac{8}{9}98​ colours 888 of them, nearly all of it. Drawing cut lines cannot colour in more card, so the classmate's line cannot be a record of this cutting.

    With both counts scaled by the same 222, the record is

    49=818.\frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{18}.94​=188​.

    Part C

    The two numbers in a fraction count different things, and one act of cutting changes both of them at once.

    Start from what the fraction says: the whole is in bbb equal parts and aaa of them are coloured. Cut every part into ccc equal pieces. The whole was in bbb parts and each became ccc pieces, so the whole is now in b×cb \times cb×c pieces. The coloured parts met the same knife, so the aaa coloured parts became a×ca \times ca×c coloured pieces. Neither count could change without the other, because a single cutting produced both:

    ab=a×cb×c.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times c}.ba​=b×ca×c​.

    No card was added and none removed, so the coloured amount is exactly what it was, described in smaller pieces.

    Now suppose only one of the two numbers is scaled, on a strip that has some colour on it. Scaling the numerator alone says more pieces are coloured while the whole is still in its original parts, which is somebody colouring in extra card. Scaling the denominator alone says the whole was cut finer while the coloured count stood still, which is somebody rubbing colour out. Either way the amount moves, and the two fractions no longer name the same number.

    The words about colour are doing real work there. A strip with no colour at all is the one case where a lopsided change does no damage: 07\frac{0}{7}70​ and 014\frac{0}{14}140​ both name 000, because no colour is no colour however finely the card is cut. That is not a loophole in the rule. The building rule scales both counts and gets the value right in every case, this one included; what the exception shows is that scaling both is what always works, not merely the only thing that ever can.

    The multiplier can never be 000. Cutting each part into 000 pieces leaves no pieces to count, and both counts collapse at once, since the fraction it would produce is

    a×0b×0=00.\frac{a \times 0}{b \times 0} = \frac{0}{0}.b×0a×0​=00​.

    A denominator of 000 names no number, because it asks for a division by 000, and no such division picks out one number: for 60\frac{6}{0}06​ nothing multiplied by 000 gives 666, while for 00\frac{0}{0}00​ every number would qualify.

    In one line

    Cutting each of the 777 parts into 333 leaves the strip in 212121 pieces with 666 of them coloured, so that cutting is recorded as 27=621\frac{2}{7} = \frac{6}{21}72​=216​. On the second strip, the line 49=89\frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{9}94​=98​ keeps a denominator saying the strip is still in 999 pieces, when the cutting has already left it in 181818; the record is 49=818\frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{18}94​=188​. One cutting scales both counts at once, the parts in the whole and the coloured parts alike, which is why the same multiplier has to reach the numerator and the denominator; and it can never be 000, since a whole cut into no pieces has nothing to count and a denominator of 000 names no number.

    2. A plan in eighths, a bed in plots . Application, 13 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Application 13 pts

    A community garden bed is divided into 242424 equal plots. The planting plan is written as a fraction rather than a plot count, because it has to serve beds of different sizes: it gives 58\frac{5}{8}85​ of a bed to herbs. Before anything can be marked out, that fraction has to be turned into plots.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Work out how many of the 242424 plots the herbs take under the plan. Show the multiplier you used to get there, and state the result with its unit.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A second bed is divided into 303030 equal plots, and 181818 of them are already planted with vegetables. Write the planted share as a fraction of that bed in lowest terms, and say how you know nothing is left to divide out.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A supplier sells beds that arrive already divided into 202020 equal plots. Decide whether the plan's herb fraction can be marked out on such a bed in whole plots, support the decision, and describe every bed size, counted in plots, on which it can.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      A fraction of a bed becomes a count of plots only once it is written with the bed's own plot count underneath it. That rewriting is the whole job, and it starts with the multiplier between the two denominators.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Put the planted plots over the plots in that bed, then look for the largest number dividing both. Their prime factorizations hand you that number without any listing.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask what the multiplier would have to be, and whether a whole number can be it. The multiples of the plan's denominator are the list worth checking against.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    151515 plots, using the multiplier 333, since 8×3=248 \times 3 = 248×3=24.

    Part B

    1830=35\frac{18}{30} = \frac{3}{5}3018​=53​ of the second bed, and 333 and 555 share no factor above 111, so nothing is left to divide out.

    Part C

    It cannot: no whole number multiplies 888 to give 202020, since 202020 is not a multiple of 888. The plan marks out in whole plots exactly on beds whose plot count is a multiple of 888, that is 888, 161616, 242424, 323232, and so on.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The plan is a fraction of a bed and the bed is a number of plots, so rewrite 58\frac{5}{8}85​ with 242424 underneath it.

    The multiplier comes from the denominators. The plan counts eighths and this bed is in 242424 plots, and

    8×3=24,8 \times 3 = 24,8×3=24,

    so the multiplier is 333. In plain terms, one eighth of this bed is 333 plots. The building rule sends that same 333 to the numerator:

    58=5×38×3=1524.\frac{5}{8} = \frac{5 \times 3}{8 \times 3} = \frac{15}{24}.85​=8×35×3​=2415​.

    So the herbs take 151515 plots. The unit is worth keeping straight: 151515 is a count of plots, while 1524\frac{15}{24}2415​ is the share of the bed those plots make up, and the two are the same fact stated in different currencies.

    Part B

    The planted share is 181818 plots out of the 303030 the bed holds, which is 1830\frac{18}{30}3018​ before any simplifying. Reach lowest terms in one division by dividing by the greatest common factor, so find that first:

    18=2×3×3,30=2×3×5.18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3, \qquad 30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5.18=2×3×3,30=2×3×5.

    The primes they share are one 222 and one 333, so GCF⁡(18,30)=6\operatorname{GCF}(18, 30) = 6GCF(18,30)=6. Divide both terms by it:

    1830=18÷630÷6=35.\frac{18}{30} = \frac{18 \div 6}{30 \div 6} = \frac{3}{5}.3018​=30÷618÷6​=53​.

    Now check that the job is finished. The only factor 333 and 555 share is 111, so there is nothing left to divide out and 35\frac{3}{5}53​ is in lowest terms.

    Read it back at the bed. Grouping the 303030 plots into 555 blocks of 666, the vegetables fill 333 of those blocks, which is the same land the 181818 plots covered.

    Part C

    The question is whether the plan's fraction can be written with 202020 underneath it using a whole-number multiplier.

    That multiplier would have to carry 888 to 202020:

    8×c=20.8 \times c = 20.8×c=20.

    The multiples of 888 run 8,16,24,32,…8, 16, 24, 32, \ldots8,16,24,32,…, and 202020 is not among them, so no whole number ccc does it. Marking 58\frac{5}{8}85​ of a 202020-plot bed would need part of a plot, and a plot is the smallest piece this bed has.

    The same argument says exactly which beds do work. A bed of nnn plots serves the plan when 8×c=n8 \times c = n8×c=n for some whole number ccc, and that is precisely the statement that nnn is a multiple of 888:

    n=8,  16,  24,  32,  40,  …n = 8, \; 16, \; 24, \; 32, \; 40, \; \ldotsn=8,16,24,32,40,…

    Both directions hold here: every such bed can be marked out, because ccc plots then make one eighth and 5×c5 \times c5×c plots make the herb share, and no other bed can, because the multiplier would not be whole. The first bed in this question has 242424 plots, which is on the list, and 202020 is not.

    The supplier's bed is not useless, of course. It can be marked out if the plots themselves are subdivided, but then the bed is no longer in 202020 pieces, which is a different bed from the one that was bought.

    In one line

    The plan gives the herbs 58=1524\frac{5}{8} = \frac{15}{24}85​=2415​ of the first bed, which is 151515 plots. On the second bed the planted share is 1830=35\frac{18}{30} = \frac{3}{5}3018​=53​, and since 333 and 555 share no factor above 111 that fraction is in lowest terms. A 202020-plot bed cannot carry the plan in whole plots, because no whole number multiplies 888 to give 202020; the beds that can are exactly those whose plot count is a multiple of 888, namely 888, 161616, 242424, 323232, and so on.

    Another way: Price one eighth first

    Part A can be done without writing a second fraction at all, using the meaning of the two numbers. The denominator says the bed splits into 888 equal shares, so work out how big one share is:

    24÷8=3 plots in one eighth.24 \div 8 = 3 \text{ plots in one eighth.}24÷8=3 plots in one eighth.

    The numerator says take 555 of those shares:

    5×3=15 plots.5 \times 3 = 15 \text{ plots.}5×3=15 plots.

    The arithmetic is the same 333 and the same 555 as the building route, which is no accident: dividing the bed into 888 shares is what finding the multiplier was measuring all along.

    When it is worth it When you want the count and not the fraction, and the plot total divides cleanly by the denominator. It also fails loudly on a bed the plan cannot serve, since the first division does not come out whole.

    3. Two routes, and the test for a finished fraction . Foundational, 11 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    The fraction 3256\frac{32}{56}5632​ can be made simpler by more than one route, and the routes differ in how many divisions they ask for. This question runs two of them on the same fraction and then turns to what tells you a fraction is finished.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find the greatest common factor of 323232 and 565656, and use it to simplify 3256\frac{32}{56}5632​ in a single division. Report the greatest common factor and the fraction it produces, and say what that division does to the fraction's value.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A classmate simplifies the same fraction by halving the numerator and the denominator, repeating for as long as both stay whole numbers. Carry that route out, writing every fraction it passes through and saying what stops it. Then set it beside the single-division route: how many divisions each takes, and what each one ends at.

      Carry your own answer forward Set the halving route beside the fraction your single division ended on in part A. If part A did not come out, carry the halving route out in full anyway and compare the number of divisions.

      Compare the two methods Say what each one costs you, and when you would reach for it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A classmate offers a rule of their own: dividing by any common factor bigger than 111 leaves a fraction in lowest terms. Decide whether that holds, support the decision from work already done in this question, and state the test that tells you a fraction has nothing left to divide out.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Both routes on offer are the same rule used more than once: divide the top and the bottom by something that divides both. What separates them is how much gets divided out per step.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Write down each fraction as it appears and watch for the step that cannot be taken. Then multiply the numbers you divided by along the way and see what they come to together.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      A claim of the form always is beaten by a single case. Look at the fraction the very first halving produced, and ask whether anything still divides both of its terms.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    GCF⁡(32,56)=8\operatorname{GCF}(32, 56) = 8GCF(32,56)=8, and dividing both terms by it gives 3256=47\frac{32}{56} = \frac{4}{7}5632​=74​, which names the same amount, since both terms were divided by the same number.

    Part B

    Halving runs 3256\frac{32}{56}5632​, 1628\frac{16}{28}2816​, 814\frac{8}{14}148​, 47\frac{4}{7}74​, and stops because 777 cannot be halved to a whole number. That is three divisions against one, and both routes end at the same fraction.

    Part C

    It does not hold. Dividing 3256\frac{32}{56}5632​ by 222 leaves 1628\frac{16}{28}2816​, whose terms are both still even. A fraction is in lowest terms when its numerator and denominator share no factor above 111, which is what dividing by the greatest common factor secures in one step.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    One division is enough only if it divides out everything the two numbers share, so find the greatest common factor first. Write each as a product of primes:

    32=2×2×2×2×2,56=2×2×2×7.32 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2, \qquad 56 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 7.32=2×2×2×2×2,56=2×2×2×7.

    The copies of 222 they share number three, and there is no other shared prime, so

    GCF⁡(32,56)=2×2×2=8.\operatorname{GCF}(32, 56) = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8.GCF(32,56)=2×2×2=8.

    Divide the numerator and the denominator by that one number:

    3256=32÷856÷8=47.\frac{32}{56} = \frac{32 \div 8}{56 \div 8} = \frac{4}{7}.5632​=56÷832÷8​=74​.

    Both terms were divided by the same 888, so the result names the same amount as the fraction it came from. And 444 and 777 share no factor above 111, so there is nothing further to take out.

    Part B

    Halving acts on both terms at once, so it is the simplifying rule with a common factor of 222, applied over and over:

    3256=1628=814=47.\frac{32}{56} = \frac{16}{28} = \frac{8}{14} = \frac{4}{7}.5632​=2816​=148​=74​.

    At each of those steps both numbers were even, so both halved to whole numbers. The route stops at 47\frac{4}{7}74​ because halving again would ask for half of 777, which is not a whole number.

    Now set the two routes side by side. The halving route took three divisions; the single-division route took one, and that is not a coincidence:

    2×2×2=8,2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8,2×2×2=8,

    so the three halvings between them divided out exactly the number the one division divided out at a stroke.

    Both routes end at the same fraction here, and that is not luck, though it is not automatic either. Every step of either route divides both terms by a common factor, so neither route moves the value. The three halvings between them removed everything 323232 and 565656 share, so the halving route lands in lowest terms as well, and a fraction has only one lowest-terms form for the two of them to land on.

    That second condition is the one to watch, because it can fail. Halving stops as soon as one of the terms is odd, which need not be lowest terms at all: on a fraction whose numerator and denominator share an odd factor above 111, the halving route runs out early and leaves that factor sitting there. It finished the job here only because everything 323232 and 565656 share is built out of 222s.

    The difference between the routes is therefore bookkeeping and risk. The halving route needs a check after every step, and a check again when it stops, to see whether anything is left.

    Part C

    A claim that says always is settled by one example, and there is one already on the page.

    The first halving divides by the common factor 222, which is certainly bigger than 111:

    3256=1628.\frac{32}{56} = \frac{16}{28}.5632​=2816​.

    By the claim, 1628\frac{16}{28}2816​ should now be in lowest terms. It is not: 161616 and 282828 are both even, so 222 divides both of them again, and in fact 444 does. One counterexample is enough, so the claim does not hold.

    What went wrong is that 222 is a common factor but not the greatest one. Dividing by a common factor removes that factor and nothing else, so whatever else the two numbers share is still sitting there afterwards. The greatest common factor is the one number that carries everything they share, which is why dividing by it finishes the job at a stroke.

    The test for a finished fraction says nothing about the route taken to it: a fraction is in lowest terms when its numerator and denominator share no factor above 111. Applied to 1628\frac{16}{28}2816​ that test says keep going. Applied to 47\frac{4}{7}74​ it says stop, since the only factor 444 and 777 share is 111.

    In one line

    GCF⁡(32,56)=8\operatorname{GCF}(32, 56) = 8GCF(32,56)=8, and one division by it gives 3256=47\frac{32}{56} = \frac{4}{7}5632​=74​. Halving repeatedly runs 3256\frac{32}{56}5632​, 1628\frac{16}{28}2816​, 814\frac{8}{14}148​, 47\frac{4}{7}74​ and stops there, since 777 cannot be halved: three divisions instead of one, ending at the same fraction, because 2×2×2=82 \times 2 \times 2 = 82×2×2=8 is what those halvings divided out between them. Dividing by any common factor above 111 does not by itself finish the job, as 1628\frac{16}{28}2816​ shows with both terms still even. A fraction is in lowest terms exactly when its numerator and denominator share no factor above 111.

    Part A 4 points

    1

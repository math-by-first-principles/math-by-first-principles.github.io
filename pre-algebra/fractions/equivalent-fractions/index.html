Equivalent Fractions and Simplifying

Intuition

Cut a pizza in half and eat one piece, or cut the same pizza into four equal pieces and eat two of them. Either way you have eaten the same amount of pizza, even though one description says 12\frac{1}{2}21​ and the other says 24\frac{2}{4}42​. These two fractions look different but name the very same number, and we call them equivalent. That freedom lets you rewrite a fraction in whatever form a problem needs. It also lets you boil a clumsy fraction like 1824\frac{18}{24}2418​ down to the cleanest version of itself, 34\frac{3}{4}43​.

Learning goals

  • Build an equivalent fraction by scaling top and bottom alike
  • Explain why scaling by a nonzero whole number leaves the value alone
  • Simplify by dividing out a common factor, the rule in reverse
  • Reach lowest terms in one step by dividing by the GCF
  • Test two fractions for equality with cross products

When two fractions name the same amount

Two fractions are equivalent when they stand for the same number: the same point on the number line, the same amount of pizza. The clearest way to see it is with the bars you met in the last lesson. Draw one whole cut into 222 equal parts with 111 shaded, and below it the same whole cut into 444 equal parts with 222 shaded. The shaded region is identical in both: exactly half the bar is coloured.

To go from the top bar to the bottom one, we cut each of the 222 parts into 222 smaller parts. That doubles the number of parts in the whole, so the denominator goes from 222 to 444. But that same cutting also doubles the number of shaded parts, because each shaded part got cut in two as well. So the numerator goes from 111 to 222. The shaded amount never moved; we only drew more cut lines. That is why 12\frac{1}{2}21​ and 24\frac{2}{4}42​ are the same number:

12=24.\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4}.21​=42​.

You can keep going. Cut each part into three instead, and the same half of the bar is now 36\frac{3}{6}63​; into five, and it is 510\frac{5}{10}105​. Every one of these is just a finer description of the same shaded region:

12=24=36=510=50100.\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{50}{100}.21​=42​=63​=105​=10050​.

You can do that cutting yourself. In the figure below, the top bar is fixed at one half. The bottom bar is the same whole, and you choose how many pieces each of its parts is cut into.

Watch the edge of the shaded region as you change the cut. It does not move. The numerator and the denominator both climb, so the fraction gets new names, but the amount of bar coloured in stays exactly where it started. That is what makes all these fractions equal.

Why multiplying top and bottom by the same number is allowed

The picture suggests a rule: to rewrite a fraction, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same nonzero whole number.

Try that on 25\frac{2}{5}52​ with the multiplier 333. Multiplying top and bottom by 333 turns it into 615\frac{6}{15}156​, and the bars say the same thing. Cut each of the 555 parts into 333 pieces and the whole holds 151515 pieces. Each of the 222 shaded parts becomes 333 pieces too, so 666 pieces are shaded. The extra cuts moved no shading, so 25\frac{2}{5}52​ and 615\frac{6}{15}156​ are the same number.

The same reasoning works for any fraction and any nonzero whole-number multiplier. Cut 47\frac{4}{7}74​‘s 777 parts in two, and its 444 shaded parts in two as well: 7×2=147 \times 2 = 147×2=14 parts in all, 4×2=84 \times 2 = 84×2=8 of them shaded, so 47=814\frac{4}{7} = \frac{8}{14}74​=148​.

Both times the multiplier did the same two jobs. It cut every part of the whole, and it cut every shaded part by the identical amount. That is why the top and the bottom both have to be multiplied, and any starting fraction with any nonzero whole-number multiplier behaves the same way.

Building rule. For any whole numbers aaa and bbb with bbb not zero, and any nonzero whole number ccc, ab=a×cb×c.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times c}.ba​=b×ca×c​.

The two examples above show the pattern. The full proof in Go deeper shows it holds for every fraction and every nonzero whole-number multiplier, including fractions bigger than one.

See why this always works

This is also why the multiplier has to hit both numbers. Change only one of them and the picture no longer matches the fraction you started with, unless the numerator is already 000: 0b\frac{0}{b}b0​ equals 000 for every nonzero bbb, so changing only its denominator changes nothing. The requirement that ccc is not zero matters too: you cannot cut each part into 000 pieces, and a denominator of 000 is undefined.

Building an equivalent fraction

The most common job is to rewrite a fraction so it has a particular denominator you need. Rewriting to a required denominator is exactly what you will do when adding fractions in the next lesson.

To rewrite 23\frac{2}{3}32​ with a denominator of 121212, ask ”333 times what gives 121212?” Since 3×4=123 \times 4 = 123×4=12, the multiplier is 444. Now multiply the numerator by that same 444:

23=2×43×4=812.\frac{2}{3} = \frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 4} = \frac{8}{12}.32​=3×42×4​=128​.

The rule guarantees these name the same number, because we multiplied top and bottom by the same 444. That gives the method: find what the old denominator was multiplied by, then multiply the numerator by that same number. The fixed idea is that whatever you do to the bottom, you must do to the top. Cutting every part into the same number of equal pieces is what keeps the value the same.

Check your understanding

Rewrite 35\frac{3}{5}53​ as an equivalent fraction with denominator 202020. What is the new numerator?

Answer choices

Find the multiplier first: the denominator went from 555 to 202020, and 5×4=205 \times 4 = 205×4=20, so the multiplier is 444. Multiply the numerator by that same 444.

35=3×45×4=1220\frac{3}{5} = \frac{3 \times 4}{5 \times 4} = \frac{12}{20}53​=5×43×4​=2012​

The new numerator is 121212. You must multiply the top by the same number you multiplied the bottom by, so the value stays the same. Writing 320\frac{3}{20}203​ instead, by multiplying only the denominator, does not match any real cut of the bar: cutting every one of the 555 parts into 444 pieces always turns the 333 shaded parts into 121212 shaded pieces too. Leaving the numerator at 333 describes only a quarter of the correctly shaded amount, not an equivalent fraction.

Simplifying: the rule run in reverse

The building rule works in both directions. Building up cut each part into smaller pieces; simplifying runs the same picture backwards, grouping the small pieces back into larger ones.

Take 68\frac{6}{8}86​. Both 666 and 888 are divisible by 222, so divide each by 222:

68=6÷28÷2=34.\frac{6}{8} = \frac{6 \div 2}{8 \div 2} = \frac{3}{4}.86​=8÷26÷2​=43​.

Look at what that division does to the picture. The whole was cut into 888 parts, and gathering those parts two at a time leaves 444 larger parts. The 666 shaded pieces gather into 333 shaded larger parts. No area was added or removed, only cut lines erased, so the shaded amount is unchanged.

That same gathering works for any number that divides both the numerator and the denominator, which gives the rule.

Simplifying rule. If a whole number ccc divides both aaa and bbb, then ab=a÷cb÷c.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \div c}{b \div c}.ba​=b÷ca÷c​.

The number ccc you divide by must be a common factor of the numerator and denominator, a factor of both. That requirement is exactly the idea from the factors chapter. Dividing by a common factor is the only way to make a fraction simpler while keeping its value.

Lowest terms and the one-step shortcut

A fraction is in lowest terms (also called simplest form) when the numerator and denominator have no common factor larger than 111. At that point there is nothing left to divide out, so the fraction cannot be written with smaller whole numbers. For example 34\frac{3}{4}43​ is in lowest terms, because the only factor 333 and 444 share is 111.

You could simplify 1824\frac{18}{24}2418​ by dividing out common factors one at a time: divide by 222 to get 912\frac{9}{12}129​, then by 333 to get 34\frac{3}{4}43​. That works, but it takes several steps and you have to keep checking whether you are done. There is a faster way that always finishes in a single division, and it uses the greatest common factor from the previous chapter.

For 1824\frac{18}{24}2418​, the greatest common factor of 181818 and 242424 is 666, so

1824=18÷624÷6=34,\frac{18}{24} = \frac{18 \div 6}{24 \div 6} = \frac{3}{4},2418​=24÷618÷6​=43​,

and you are guaranteed to be finished. If 333 and 444 still shared a factor, that factor times 666 would divide both 181818 and 242424. But 666 is already the biggest number that does. If you instead divide by a common factor that is not the greatest, you simplify the fraction but you are not done yet.

So the shortcut is: divide the numerator and the denominator by their GCF, once.

The 1824\frac{18}{24}2418​ example shows the pattern. The full proof in Go deeper shows dividing by the greatest common factor can never leave a shared factor behind, for any fraction with a nonzero numerator.

Checking whether two fractions are equal

Sometimes you are handed two fractions and asked whether they are equal, with no obvious cutting between them. One reliable test is to simplify both to lowest terms. Compare 69\frac{6}{9}96​ and 1015\frac{10}{15}1510​. The first simplifies by 333 to 23\frac{2}{3}32​, and the second simplifies by 555 to 23\frac{2}{3}32​. Same simplest form, so they are equal. That test works because equivalent fractions have the same lowest-terms form. If the simplified versions match the fractions are equal, and if they differ the fractions are not.

A second test avoids simplifying entirely, and it is especially handy with larger numbers. Give 46\frac{4}{6}64​ and 69\frac{6}{9}96​ the same denominator, 6×9=546 \times 9 = 546×9=54, by multiplying each fraction’s top and bottom by the other fraction’s bottom:

46=4×96×9=3654and69=6×69×6=3654.\frac{4}{6} = \frac{4 \times 9}{6 \times 9} = \frac{36}{54} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{6}{9} = \frac{6 \times 6}{9 \times 6} = \frac{36}{54}.64​=6×94×9​=5436​and96​=9×66×6​=5436​.

Both fractions are now over the same denominator, and their numerators, 4×94 \times 94×9 and 6×66 \times 66×6, both come out to 363636. Equal numerators over an equal denominator means the fractions are equal.

Notice which numbers got multiplied together: the numerator of one fraction with the denominator of the other, crossing between the two fractions. Those two products are called the cross products, and checking them is a shortcut that skips writing out the shared denominator. In general, for ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ and cd\frac{c}{d}dc​ with bbb and ddd not zero,

ab=cdexactly whena×d=b×c.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \quad \text{exactly when} \quad a \times d = b \times c.ba​=dc​exactly whena×d=b×c.

Worked example 1 Write 34\frac{3}{4}43​ with a denominator of 282828

Find the multiplier by asking what turns the old denominator into the new one. The denominator goes from 444 to 282828, and

4×7=28,4 \times 7 = 28,4×7=28,

so the multiplier is 777. The building rule says multiply the numerator by that same 777:

34=3×74×7=2128.\frac{3}{4} = \frac{3 \times 7}{4 \times 7} = \frac{21}{28}.43​=4×73×7​=2821​.

Both fractions name the same number, because we multiplied the top and bottom by the same 777. That multiplier just cuts each of the four parts into seven smaller pieces.

Worked example 2 Simplify 2436\frac{24}{36}3624​ to lowest terms

Use the one-step shortcut: divide by the greatest common factor of 242424 and 363636. List their factors and find the largest they share.

24:  1,2,3,4,6,8,12,2436:  1,2,3,4,6,9,12,18,3624: \; 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24 \qquad 36: \; 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 3624:1,2,3,4,6,8,12,2436:1,2,3,4,6,9,12,18,36

The common factors are 1,2,3,4,6,121, 2, 3, 4, 6, 121,2,3,4,6,12, and the greatest is 121212, so GCF⁡(24,36)=12\operatorname{GCF}(24, 36) = 12GCF(24,36)=12. Divide the numerator and denominator by 121212:

2436=24÷1236÷12=23.\frac{24}{36} = \frac{24 \div 12}{36 \div 12} = \frac{2}{3}.3624​=36÷1224÷12​=32​.

Now check that you are finished: 222 and 333 share no factor larger than 111, so 23\frac{2}{3}32​ is in lowest terms. Because we divided by the greatest common factor, one division was enough.

Worked example 3 Are 812\frac{8}{12}128​ and 1015\frac{10}{15}1510​ equivalent?

Use the cross-product test. Multiply the numerator of each by the denominator of the other:

8×15=120and12×10=120.8 \times 15 = 120 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 12 \times 10 = 120.8×15=120and12×10=120.

The two products are both 120120120, so the fractions are equivalent:

812=1015.\frac{8}{12} = \frac{10}{15}.128​=1510​.

You can confirm it by simplifying: 812\frac{8}{12}128​ divides by 444 to give 23\frac{2}{3}32​, and 1015\frac{10}{15}1510​ divides by 555 to give 23\frac{2}{3}32​. Same lowest-terms form, which agrees with the cross-product test.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Changing only the top or only the bottom. Writing 12=22\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{2}21​=22​ is wrong, because the numerator was doubled but the denominator was not. To keep the value, multiply the top and the bottom by the same nonzero whole number, or divide both by the same common factor. That way, all you are doing is cutting each part into equal pieces, or gathering them back up. The one exception is a zero numerator: 05=010\frac{0}{5} = \frac{0}{10}50​=100​, because 0b\frac{0}{b}b0​ is 000 no matter what nonzero bbb is.
  • Adding instead of multiplying. 12\frac{1}{2}21​ does not become 1+12+1=23\frac{1 + 1}{2 + 1} = \frac{2}{3}2+11+1​=32​. The building rule multiplies top and bottom by the same number; it never adds the same number to both. Adding the same nonzero number to two terms that differ changes the value too, as long as the new denominator is still nonzero.
  • Stopping before lowest terms. 68=34\frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4}86​=43​ is fully simplified, but dividing 1824\frac{18}{24}2418​ by only 222 to get 912\frac{9}{12}129​ is not finished. The reason is that 999 and 121212 still share a factor of 333. Check that the numerator and denominator share no factor above 111 before you call a fraction simplified.
  • Dividing by a number that is not a common factor. You can only divide out a number that divides both the numerator and the denominator. You cannot simplify 58\frac{5}{8}85​ by halving the 888 alone; nothing divides both 555 and 888 except 111, so 58\frac{5}{8}85​ is already in lowest terms.
  • Cross-multiplying in a tangle. The cross-product test pairs each numerator with the other fraction’s denominator: a×da \times da×d against b×cb \times cb×c. Multiplying a×ba \times ba×b or c×dc \times dc×d instead tests nothing useful.

Key takeaways

  • Two fractions are equivalent when they name the same number. 12=24=36\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6}21​=42​=63​ are all the same amount, drawn with finer and finer cuts.
  • Building rule: ab=a×cb×c\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times c}ba​=b×ca×c​ for any nonzero whole number ccc. Multiplying the top and bottom by the same number just cuts each part into ccc equal pieces, so the value never changes.
  • To build a fraction with a chosen denominator, find what the old denominator was multiplied by, then multiply the numerator by that same number.
  • Simplifying rule: ab=a÷cb÷c\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \div c}{b \div c}ba​=b÷ca÷c​ when ccc is a common factor. Run the building rule in reverse to make a fraction simpler.
  • A fraction is in lowest terms when the numerator and denominator share no factor above 111. Dividing both by their GCF reaches lowest terms in a single step.
  • Two fractions are equal exactly when they share a lowest-terms form, or equivalently when their cross products match: ab=cd\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}ba​=dc​ when a×d=b×ca \times d = b \times ca×d=b×c.

Why cutting every part the same way leaves the fraction alone

The lesson cuts 25\frac{2}{5}52​ into fifteenths and 47\frac{4}{7}74​ into fourteenths, and the shading never moves either time. This settles it for every fraction and every nonzero whole-number multiplier at once, then checks the same rule a second way by reading the bar as a division.

Why ab=a×cb×c\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times c}ba​=b×ca×c​#

Take 38\frac{3}{8}83​ first, with the multiplier 555: a whole cut into 888 equal parts, with 333 of them shaded. Cut each of those 888 parts into 555 equal pieces. The whole was in 888 parts and every part became 555 pieces, so the whole now holds 8×5=408 \times 5 = 408×5=40 pieces. Each of the 333 shaded parts also became 555 pieces, so the number of shaded pieces is 3×5=153 \times 5 = 153×5=15. The new lines added and removed no area, so the shaded amount is exactly what it was. That same amount, described with the smaller pieces, is 1540\frac{15}{40}4015​, so 38=1540\frac{3}{8} = \frac{15}{40}83​=4015​.

Now the same argument in letters. Start from what ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ means: parts of size 1b\frac{1}{b}b1​, with aaa of them shaded, using as many whole shapes as needed to hold them (one whole is enough whenever aaa is at most bbb; use more, cut the same way, whenever aaa is bigger than bbb). Now cut every one of those 1b\frac{1}{b}b1​ parts into ccc equal smaller pieces. Each whole was in bbb parts and every part became ccc pieces, so each whole now holds b×cb \times cb×c equal pieces. Each of the aaa shaded parts also became ccc pieces, so the number of shaded pieces is a×ca \times ca×c. You drew new cut lines but added and removed no area, so the shaded amount is exactly what it was. That same amount, described with the smaller pieces, is a×ca \times ca×c shaded out of b×cb \times cb×c per whole, which is a×cb×c\frac{a \times c}{b \times c}b×ca×c​. Since the two fractions describe the identical shaded amount,

ab=a×cb×c.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times c}.ba​=b×ca×c​.

Nothing in that argument depended on the values 333, 888 and 555 the first pass used.

The division reading agrees and uses only whole-number arithmetic. Suppose the division a÷ba \div ba÷b comes out to a whole number qqq, meaning a=q×ba = q \times ba=q×b. Multiplying both sides by ccc gives a×c=q×(b×c)a \times c = q \times (b \times c)a×c=q×(b×c), so (a×c)÷(b×c)(a \times c) \div (b \times c)(a×c)÷(b×c) is the same qqq.

∎

Why dividing by the GCF always finishes the job

The lesson divides 1824\frac{18}{24}2418​ by 666 and finds nothing left to cancel. This shows the same thing holds for every fraction with a nonzero numerator, by working out what a leftover shared factor would have to be.

Dividing by the GCF gives lowest terms in one step#

Take 4270\frac{42}{70}7042​ first. The greatest common factor of 424242 and 707070 is 141414, and dividing both by 141414 gives 35\frac{3}{5}53​. Suppose 333 and 555 still shared some factor kkk bigger than 111. Then 14×k14 \times k14×k would divide both 424242 and 707070, and 14×k14 \times k14×k is bigger than 141414, so 141414 would not be the greatest common factor. It is, so no such kkk exists and 35\frac{3}{5}53​ is in lowest terms. A smaller common factor is what leaves something behind: dividing 4270\frac{42}{70}7042​ by 777 gives 610\frac{6}{10}106​, whose terms still share 222. Those leftovers point straight back at the factor that was missed, because 7×2=147 \times 2 = 147×2=14.

Now the same argument in letters, for a nonzero numerator aaa. Let g=GCF⁡(a,b)g = \operatorname{GCF}(a, b)g=GCF(a,b) be the greatest common factor of the numerator and denominator. Because ggg divides both, the simplifying rule lets us write

ab=a÷gb÷g.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \div g}{b \div g}.ba​=b÷ga÷g​.

The claim is that a÷gb÷g\frac{a \div g}{b \div g}b÷ga÷g​ is already in lowest terms, with nothing left to cancel. Suppose it were not. Then a÷ga \div ga÷g and b÷gb \div gb÷g would share some common factor kkk bigger than 111. But if kkk divides both a÷ga \div ga÷g and b÷gb \div gb÷g, then g×kg \times kg×k would divide both aaa and bbb. Since g×kg \times kg×k divides both, it is a common factor of aaa and bbb, and k>1k > 1k>1 makes it larger than ggg. That contradicts ggg being the greatest common factor. So no such kkk exists, which means a÷ga \div ga÷g and b÷gb \div gb÷g share no factor above 111. The fraction is in lowest terms, and nothing in the argument depended on the values 424242, 707070 and 141414 the first pass used.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

For centuries a page of music named the notes but said nothing about their lengths. A singer picked up the timing by ear. You learned a tune from somebody who already knew it. If two singers disagreed, the page could not settle the argument.

Around 1280 a music teacher named Franco, working in what is now Germany, wrote down a fix. The shape of a note, he said, should announce how long to hold it. Lengths could then be measured against each other. Musicians eventually settled on halving.

That is why the notes carry fraction names today. There is a whole note, a half note, a quarter note and an eighth note. Hold one half note and you have filled the time of two quarter notes. Four eighth notes fill it too. Splitting a long note into shorter ones changes the counting and never the music.

That is the building rule of this lesson, performed out loud. One half, two fourths and four eighths are a single duration under three names.

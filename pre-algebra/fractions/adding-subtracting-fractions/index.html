Adding and Subtracting Fractions

Intuition

Adding whole numbers is easy because every unit is the same: three apples plus two apples is five apples, no questions asked. Fractions add the same way, but only once you make sure the pieces you are counting are the same size. The trouble starts when the pieces are different sizes, like adding a half to a third. You cannot say “one plus one” of anything until both are cut into a common size of piece first.

Learning goals

  • Explain why parts must be the same size before adding or subtracting fractions
  • Add or subtract fractions with the same denominator by combining the numerators
  • Rewrite unlike fractions, including whole numbers, over their least common denominator
  • Simplify the answer to lowest terms after combining

Why the parts must be the same size

Think about why 3+2=53 + 2 = 53+2=5 never needs explaining. You are counting copies of a single unit, the apple, so you total the copies. A fraction is exactly the same kind of object: 25\frac{2}{5}52​ means two copies of the unit “one fifth.” In the same way, 15\frac{1}{5}51​ means one more copy of that same unit. Counting the copies gives three fifths:

25+15=35.\frac{2}{5} + \frac{1}{5} = \frac{3}{5}.52​+51​=53​.

The denominator stayed 555 because the size of the piece never changed; only the count went up. Fractions add by counting parts, and counting only works when the parts are identical. A fifth and a fifth can be tallied together, the same way two apples and three more apples can. A half and a third cannot, for the same reason two apples and three oranges cannot just be added: they are not counts of the same-sized thing. Unlike apples and oranges, though, a half and a third can be forced to match: cut each into smaller, equally sized pieces until both are measured in that same small piece. That recutting is the whole trick, and it is what the next section does. So the rule splits into two cases: when the denominators already match, you add at once. When they do not match, your first job is to make them match.

Adding and subtracting with the same denominator

When two fractions share a denominator, they are built from identical pieces, so you combine them by working only with the numerators. For example,

38+18=3+18=48=12,\frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{8} = \frac{3 + 1}{8} = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2},83​+81​=83+1​=84​=21​,

Three eighths is three of those pieces, and one eighth is one more, so together you are holding four of them. Only the count changed, so the denominator is still 888. The last step divides the top and bottom by their greatest common factor 444, exactly the simplifying skill from the previous lesson.

Like-denominator rule. For any whole numbers aaa, bbb, and ccc with ccc not zero, ac+bc=a+bcandac−bc=a−bc.\frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a + b}{c} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{a}{c} - \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a - b}{c}.ca​+cb​=ca+b​andca​−cb​=ca−b​. Here aaa and bbb are the two numerators, and ccc is the denominator they share. (The subtraction examples in this lesson keep a≥ba \ge ba≥b, so the difference is 000 or a positive fraction; you already know from the integers chapter what a negative result would mean.)

Both ac\frac{a}{c}ca​ and bc\frac{b}{c}cb​ are copies of the very same unit piece, 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​. Lining up aaa copies with bbb more copies of that piece gives a+ba + ba+b copies in total, which is exactly a+bc\frac{a + b}{c}ca+b​. Subtraction runs the same argument backwards: taking bbb copies away from aaa copies leaves a−ba - ba−b copies.

This holds for any whole numbers aaa, bbb, and ccc (with a≥ba \ge ba≥b for subtraction, as in every example above), not only the small cases used here. You can read the complete argument in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full argument

So the procedure with like denominators is short: add (or subtract) the numerators and keep the common denominator. Then simplify the result to lowest terms. A common slip is to add the denominators too, writing 38+18=416\frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{8} = \frac{4}{16}83​+81​=164​. That is wrong, because the denominator names the size of the piece. The pieces also did not get smaller when you put two groups of them together, so 888 stays 888.

Answer choices

The denominators already match, so add the numerators and keep the denominator 999.

59+29=5+29=79\frac{5}{9} + \frac{2}{9} = \frac{5 + 2}{9} = \frac{7}{9}95​+92​=95+2​=97​

Since 777 and 999 share no factor above 111, 79\frac{7}{9}97​ is already in lowest terms. Do not add the denominators: each piece stays a ninth.

Unlike denominators: make the pieces match first

When the denominators differ, the pieces are different sizes and cannot be counted together yet. The fix is the building rule from the last lesson: rewrite each fraction as an equivalent one over a denominator they share. Then the like-denominator rule finishes the job.

Take 12+13\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}21​+31​. Halves and thirds are different sizes, so picture cutting both into a size they have in common. Sixths work: a half is three sixths, and a third is two sixths. Once both are written in sixths, they are made of identical pieces and you can count:

To rewrite 12\frac{1}{2}21​ in sixths, the denominator goes from 222 to 666 by multiplying by 333, so the numerator does too: 12=1×32×3=36\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1 \times 3}{2 \times 3} = \frac{3}{6}21​=2×31×3​=63​. To rewrite 13\frac{1}{3}31​ in sixths, the denominator goes from 333 to 666 by multiplying by 222: 13=1×23×2=26\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1 \times 2}{3 \times 2} = \frac{2}{6}31​=3×21×2​=62​. Now both are sixths, so add the numerators:

12+13=36+26=3+26=56.\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{3 + 2}{6} = \frac{5}{6}.21​+31​=63​+62​=63+2​=65​.

Notice what the common denominator 666 is: it is a multiple of both 222 and 333. Being a multiple of both is what lets each fraction be rebuilt over 666. Any common multiple of the two denominators would serve. We could rewrite both over 121212 instead and still get the right answer, just with larger numbers to simplify at the end. The cleanest choice is the smallest common multiple, and you already know how to find it.

Try it with a harder pair: 23+14\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{4}32​+41​. Fourths cannot be counted alongside thirds, so find the first denominator that is a multiple of both 333 and 444.

Cut each part of the bar below more finely, and watch the denominator on the bottom bar. Stop at the first cut where that denominator is also a whole number of fourths: that is the least common denominator of thirds and fourths.

Cutting each third into 444 pieces gives twelfths, and a fourth can be rewritten in twelfths too, so that is the cut to use: 23=812\frac{2}{3} = \frac{8}{12}32​=128​. Cutting into 888 pieces would also work, giving 1624\frac{16}{24}2416​, just with larger numbers for the same sum.

Whichever cut you pick, keep an eye on the shaded length. It never moves, because the bar is still two thirds and only its name has changed. That is what makes rewriting a fraction safe in the middle of an addition.

The least common denominator

A common denominator of two fractions is any common multiple of their denominators. The least common denominator (LCD) is the smallest one. For 12\frac{1}{2}21​ and 13\frac{1}{3}31​ the denominators are 222 and 333, whose common multiples run 6,12,186, 12, 186,12,18 and on. The smallest is 666, which is why 666 was the natural choice above.

So the LCD is exactly the least common multiple of the two denominators, from the factors chapter. Using the LCD keeps the numbers as small as possible, so there is less arithmetic and usually less simplifying at the end. The full method for unlike denominators is four steps:

  1. Find the LCD, the least common multiple of the two denominators.
  2. Rewrite each fraction as an equivalent fraction over the LCD, using the building rule.
  3. Add or subtract the numerators, keeping the common denominator.
  4. Simplify the result to lowest terms.

Each rewrite in step 2 is an equality, so nothing about the two amounts changes on the way to the common denominator. All that changes is the size of the pieces they are counted in. Once both are counted in the same pieces, step 3 is the like-denominator rule again.

The full proof shows that this four-step method gives the true sum, whichever common denominator you pick. A bigger common multiple only makes the numbers larger, never the answer different, which is why the least one is safe to prefer. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See why the rewrite is safe

Worked example 1 Compute 23+14\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{4}32​+41​

The denominators 333 and 444 are different, so find the least common denominator first. The least common multiple of 333 and 444 is 121212, so the LCD is 121212.

Rewrite each fraction over 121212 with the building rule. For 23\frac{2}{3}32​, the denominator goes from 333 to 121212 by multiplying by 444, so the numerator does too:

23=2×43×4=812.\frac{2}{3} = \frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 4} = \frac{8}{12}.32​=3×42×4​=128​.

For 14\frac{1}{4}41​, the denominator goes from 444 to 121212 by multiplying by 333:

14=1×34×3=312.\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1 \times 3}{4 \times 3} = \frac{3}{12}.41​=4×31×3​=123​.

Now both are twelfths, so add the numerators and keep the denominator:

23+14=812+312=8+312=1112.\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{8}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{8 + 3}{12} = \frac{11}{12}.32​+41​=128​+123​=128+3​=1211​.

Finally, check lowest terms: 111111 and 121212 share no factor above 111, so 1112\frac{11}{12}1211​ is the final answer.

Worked example 2 Compute 56−38\frac{5}{6} - \frac{3}{8}65​−83​

Subtraction follows the very same plan. The denominators 666 and 888 differ, so find the LCD. The least common multiple of 666 and 888 is 242424, so the LCD is 242424.

Rewrite each fraction over 242424. For 56\frac{5}{6}65​, the denominator goes from 666 to 242424 by multiplying by 444:

56=5×46×4=2024.\frac{5}{6} = \frac{5 \times 4}{6 \times 4} = \frac{20}{24}.65​=6×45×4​=2420​.

For 38\frac{3}{8}83​, the denominator goes from 888 to 242424 by multiplying by 333:

38=3×38×3=924.\frac{3}{8} = \frac{3 \times 3}{8 \times 3} = \frac{9}{24}.83​=8×33×3​=249​.

Both are twenty-fourths now, so subtract the numerators:

56−38=2024−924=20−924=1124.\frac{5}{6} - \frac{3}{8} = \frac{20}{24} - \frac{9}{24} = \frac{20 - 9}{24} = \frac{11}{24}.65​−83​=2420​−249​=2420−9​=2411​.

Check lowest terms: 111111 and 242424 share no factor above 111, so 1124\frac{11}{24}2411​ is the final answer.

To compute 14+56\frac{1}{4} + \frac{5}{6}41​+65​, what is the least common denominator you should use?

Answer choices

The least common denominator is the least common multiple of the denominators 444 and 666. List multiples: 4,8,12,…4, 8, 12, \ldots4,8,12,… and 6,12,…6, 12, \ldots6,12,…, and the smallest shared one is 121212.

lcm⁡(4,6)=12\operatorname{lcm}(4, 6) = 12lcm(4,6)=12

So the LCD is 121212. The product 242424 is a common denominator too, but not the least, so it would leave larger numbers to simplify.

When one denominator is already a multiple of the other

Sometimes the larger denominator is itself a multiple of the smaller one. Take 16+712\frac{1}{6} + \frac{7}{12}61​+127​. Since 121212 is a multiple of 666 (because 6×2=126 \times 2 = 126×2=12), the least common multiple of 666 and 121212 is 121212, so the LCD is 121212. The fraction 712\frac{7}{12}127​ is already over 121212 and stays as it is; only 16\frac{1}{6}61​ needs rebuilding:

16=1×26×2=212,so16+712=212+712=912=34.\frac{1}{6} = \frac{1 \times 2}{6 \times 2} = \frac{2}{12}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{1}{6} + \frac{7}{12} = \frac{2}{12} + \frac{7}{12} = \frac{9}{12} = \frac{3}{4}.61​=6×21×2​=122​,so61​+127​=122​+127​=129​=43​.

The last step simplifies 912\frac{9}{12}129​ by dividing the top and bottom by their greatest common factor 333. Whenever the larger denominator is a multiple of the smaller one, the LCD is that larger denominator. Only the fraction with the smaller denominator has to be rewritten, so spotting a pair like this saves half the work.

Adding a whole number to a fraction

A whole number can join a fraction the same way, once you remember that every whole number is a fraction with denominator 111. To add 2+352 + \frac{3}{5}2+53​, write 222 as 21\frac{2}{1}12​, then give it the denominator 555 so the pieces match. The LCD of 111 and 555 is 555, and 21=2×51×5=105\frac{2}{1} = \frac{2 \times 5}{1 \times 5} = \frac{10}{5}12​=1×52×5​=510​, which just says two wholes is ten fifths. Then add:

2+35=105+35=10+35=135.2 + \frac{3}{5} = \frac{10}{5} + \frac{3}{5} = \frac{10 + 3}{5} = \frac{13}{5}.2+53​=510​+53​=510+3​=513​.

The improper fraction 135\frac{13}{5}513​ is a complete and correct answer. (Rewriting it as a whole-number part plus a fraction is the subject of the later lesson on mixed numbers.) The same idea handles a subtraction like 1−271 - \frac{2}{7}1−72​: write 1=771 = \frac{7}{7}1=77​, so that 1−27=77−27=571 - \frac{2}{7} = \frac{7}{7} - \frac{2}{7} = \frac{5}{7}1−72​=77​−72​=75​.

What is 310+16\frac{3}{10} + \frac{1}{6}103​+61​ in lowest terms?

Answer choices

The LCD of 101010 and 666 is 303030. Rewrite both fractions: 310=930\frac{3}{10} = \frac{9}{30}103​=309​ and 16=530\frac{1}{6} = \frac{5}{30}61​=305​. Add the numerators: 930+530=1430\frac{9}{30} + \frac{5}{30} = \frac{14}{30}309​+305​=3014​. That is correct but not finished: 141414 and 303030 share the factor 222, so divide both by 222 to reach 715\frac{7}{15}157​, which is in lowest terms.

Do not add the denominators (that gives 416\frac{4}{16}164​), and do not stop before simplifying (that leaves 1430\frac{14}{30}3014​ unfinished).

What is 1−381 - \frac{3}{8}1−83​?

Answer choices

Write the whole number 111 as a fraction over 888 so the pieces match: 1=881 = \frac{8}{8}1=88​. Then subtract the numerators.

1−38=88−38=8−38=581 - \frac{3}{8} = \frac{8}{8} - \frac{3}{8} = \frac{8 - 3}{8} = \frac{5}{8}1−83​=88​−83​=88−3​=85​

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Adding the denominators. 25+15\frac{2}{5} + \frac{1}{5}52​+51​ is 35\frac{3}{5}53​, not 310\frac{3}{10}103​. The denominator names the size of the piece, and combining groups of fifths does not make the pieces smaller. Only the numerators are added.
  • Adding unlike fractions without a common denominator. 12+13\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}21​+31​ is not 25\frac{2}{5}52​. Halves and thirds are different sizes, so you must rewrite both over a common denominator (sixths) before you can count them together.
  • Rewriting only the denominator. When you change a denominator to the LCD, you must multiply the numerator by the same number. Turning 12\frac{1}{2}21​ into sixths means 36\frac{3}{6}63​, not 16\frac{1}{6}61​, because the top and bottom are both multiplied by 333.
  • Forgetting to simplify. An answer like 48\frac{4}{8}84​ is correct but not finished; it reduces to 12\frac{1}{2}21​. After adding or subtracting, always check whether the numerator and denominator share a factor above 111.
  • Picking a needlessly large common denominator. Any common multiple works, but the product of the denominators is often bigger than the LCD. Using the least common denominator keeps the numbers small and means less simplifying at the end.

Key takeaways

  • Fractions add and subtract by counting parts, which works only when the parts are the same size, that is when the denominators are the same.
  • Like denominators: add or subtract the numerators and keep the common denominator. Never add the denominators.
  • Unlike denominators: first rewrite each fraction, and any whole number, over their least common denominator (the least common multiple of the denominators), then combine the numerators as above.
  • Simplify every answer to lowest terms at the end by dividing out the greatest common factor.

Why same-denominator fractions add by adding the numerators

The lesson states the like-denominator rule and gives a short reason for it. This shows the reason in full, for any whole numbers aaa, bbb, and ccc with c>0c > 0c>0 (and a≥ba \ge ba≥b for the subtraction case), not just the small examples used above.

Why same-denominator fractions add by adding the numerators#

Run the argument on 411+311\frac{4}{11} + \frac{3}{11}114​+113​ first. Cutting a whole into 111111 equal parts makes each part 111\frac{1}{11}111​. So 411\frac{4}{11}114​ is 444 copies of 111\frac{1}{11}111​, and 311\frac{3}{11}113​ is 333 more copies of that same piece. Lining them up gives 777 copies of 111\frac{1}{11}111​, and 777 copies of 111\frac{1}{11}111​ is the fraction 711\frac{7}{11}117​. Running it backwards, taking those 333 copies away again leaves 444 copies, which is 411\frac{4}{11}114​.

Nothing in that depended on the numbers 444, 333 and 111111. Recall from the first fractions lesson that a fraction is built from unit fractions: ac\frac{a}{c}ca​ is aaa copies of the unit 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​. The reason is that cutting a whole into ccc equal parts makes each part 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​, and ac\frac{a}{c}ca​ takes aaa of them.

So ac\frac{a}{c}ca​ is aaa copies of 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​, and bc\frac{b}{c}cb​ is bbb more copies of the very same unit 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​. Lining them all up, you are holding a+ba + ba+b copies of 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​ in total. By that same meaning, a+ba + ba+b copies of 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​ is the fraction a+bc\frac{a + b}{c}ca+b​. Therefore

ac+bc=a+bc.\frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a + b}{c}.ca​+cb​=ca+b​.

Subtraction is the same argument run the other way. Taking bbb copies of 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​ away from aaa copies leaves a−ba - ba−b copies, which is a−bc\frac{a - b}{c}ca−b​. The denominator never moves, because the size of the unit piece, 1c\frac{1}{c}c1​, is exactly what stays fixed. Only the number of those pieces changes.

∎

Why a common denominator does not change the answer

The lesson rewrites both fractions over a common denominator and then adds the numerators. This shows that the rewrite never moves either amount, so the sum it produces is the true one, for any common denominator at all.

Why rewriting over a common denominator gives the correct sum#

Run it on 79+512\frac{7}{9} + \frac{5}{12}97​+125​ first, rewriting both over 363636, a common multiple of 999 and 121212. Since 36=9×436 = 9 \times 436=9×4, the building rule turns 79\frac{7}{9}97​ into 7×436=2836\frac{7 \times 4}{36} = \frac{28}{36}367×4​=3628​. Since 36=12×336 = 12 \times 336=12×3, it turns 512\frac{5}{12}125​ into 5×336=1536\frac{5 \times 3}{36} = \frac{15}{36}365×3​=3615​. Both are thirty-sixths now, so the like-denominator rule gives 2836+1536=4336\frac{28}{36} + \frac{15}{36} = \frac{43}{36}3628​+3615​=3643​. Each rewrite was an equality, so 4336\frac{43}{36}3643​ is exactly the sum we started with.

Nothing in those steps depended on the particular numbers, only on 363636 being a common multiple of 999 and 121212. So let the two fractions be ab\frac{a}{b}ba​ and cd\frac{c}{d}dc​, and let mmm be any nonzero common multiple of bbb and ddd. Then there are nonzero whole numbers sss and ttt with m=b×sm = b \times sm=b×s and m=d×tm = d \times tm=d×t.

By the building rule, multiplying the top and bottom of a fraction by the same number does not change its value, so

ab=a×smandcd=c×tm.\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times s}{m} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c \times t}{m}.ba​=ma×s​anddc​=mc×t​.

Now both rewrites have the same denominator mmm, meaning they are made of pieces of the identical size 1m\frac{1}{m}m1​. The like-denominator rule then applies exactly:

ab+cd=a×sm+c×tm=(a×s)+(c×t)m.\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times s}{m} + \frac{c \times t}{m} = \frac{(a \times s) + (c \times t)}{m}.ba​+dc​=ma×s​+mc×t​=m(a×s)+(c×t)​.

The choice of which common multiple to use for mmm affects only the size of the numbers, never the value. Taking m=lcm⁡(b,d)m = \operatorname{lcm}(b, d)m=lcm(b,d) simply makes that mmm as small as possible.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

In the 1980s an American burger chain built a new sandwich. It carried a third of a pound of beef. The quarter pound its rivals sold was smaller, and cost the same money. Almost nobody bought it.

The chain went looking for the reason, and the answer has been retold ever since. Customers believed they were being cheated. Three is smaller than four, so a third of a pound sounded like less. The number on top of a fraction had swallowed all their attention.

The real trouble is comparing 333 against 444 as ordinary counts, which is what the customers did. The pieces are different sizes, so the counts on top are counting different things. Cut both into twelfths and the argument is over. A third becomes four twelfths, and a quarter becomes three twelfths. The pieces match now, so the bigger count really is the bigger amount. Finding that shared denominator is the step this lesson turns into a rule.

GCF and LCM

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

You have 121212 apples and 181818 oranges to share among identical bags, using every piece of fruit. What is the greatest number of bags you can fill? The bag count must divide both totals. Now imagine two bells ringing every 444 and 666 minutes. They ring together now. How many minutes until they next ring together? That elapsed time must be a multiple of both intervals. These questions lead to the greatest common factor and least common multiple. Prime factorization will help you find both, and explain why the methods work.

Learning goals

  • Find a GCF and an LCM by listing factors or multiples
  • Compute a GCF and an LCM from prime factorizations, taking the lowest or highest prime power
  • Explain why lowest serves the GCF and highest serves the LCM
  • Apply the product rule for two numbers, and explain why it can fail for three
  • Read greatest number of equal groups as GCF and coinciding cycles as LCM

Common factors and the GCF

A common factor of two positive whole numbers is a number that is a factor of both. Take 121212 and 181818. Their factors are

12:  1,2,3,4,6,1218:  1,2,3,6,9,18.12: \; 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 \qquad\qquad 18: \; 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18.12:1,2,3,4,6,1218:1,2,3,6,9,18.

The numbers that appear in both lists are 1,2,3,1, 2, 3,1,2,3, and 666: these are the common factors of 121212 and 181818. Every pair of positive whole numbers shares at least the factor 111, so the list of common factors is never empty. The largest entry in it is the one we care about most.

The greatest common factor (GCF) of two positive whole numbers is the largest number that is a factor of both. For 121212 and 181818 the common factors are 1,2,3,61, 2, 3, 61,2,3,6, and the greatest of these is 666, so

GCF⁡(12,18)=6.\operatorname{GCF}(12, 18) = 6.GCF(12,18)=6.

Dividing by 666 gives 12÷6=212\div6=212÷6=2 and 18÷6=318\div6=318÷6=3. So six identical bags can each hold two apples and three oranges, with nothing left over. No larger number divides both totals. In the next chapter, this same common-factor idea helps simplify fractions.

Common multiples and the LCM

Now flip the question around. A common multiple of two positive whole numbers is a number that is a multiple of both. List a few multiples of 444 and of 666:

4:  4,8,12,16,20,24,28,…6:  6,12,18,24,30,…4: \; 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, \ldots \qquad 6: \; 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, \ldots4:4,8,12,16,20,24,28,…6:6,12,18,24,30,…

The positive numbers shared by the lists start 12,24,36,…12,24,36,\ldots12,24,36,…. They keep going without end, so there is no greatest common multiple. The least common multiple (LCM) is the smallest positive common multiple, so

LCM⁡(4,6)=12.\operatorname{LCM}(4,6)=12.LCM(4,6)=12.

The word “positive” matters: 000 is also a multiple of both numbers, but it is not the LCM. Counting by fours and sixes, 121212 is the first positive place the patterns meet. This makes the LCM useful for repeating events and, later, common denominators in fractions.

The GCF is a factor of the numbers, so it is never bigger than either one. The LCM is a multiple of the numbers, so it is never smaller than either one. For 444 and 666, the GCF is 222, at most as big as the smaller number. The LCM is 121212, at least as big as the larger. If a value you have called the GCF comes out larger than the numbers themselves, you have the two swapped. The same is true if an LCM comes out smaller than those numbers.

The listing method

The listing method finds the answer by comparing lists:

  • GCF: list the factors of each number and choose the largest shared factor.
  • LCM: list positive multiples in increasing order and choose the first shared multiple.

For example, the factors of 888 are 1,2,4,81,2,4,81,2,4,8, and those of 121212 are 1,2,3,4,6,121,2,3,4,6,121,2,3,4,6,12. Their common factors are 1,2,41,2,41,2,4, so their GCF is 444. The factor 333 does not qualify: it divides 121212 but not 888.

Listing is useful when the numbers are small. With larger numbers the lists can become long, so after practicing it we will use the prime factors instead.

The prime-factorization method

Every whole number greater than 111 has a unique prime factorization. That factorization records exactly how many copies of each prime the number is built from. That bookkeeping is precisely what the GCF and LCM need. Start each number in prime-power form. Take 121212 and 181818:

12=22×3,18=2×32.12 = 2^2 \times 3, \qquad 18 = 2 \times 3^2.12=22×3,18=2×32.

Think of each number as a bag of prime factors. The bag for 121212 holds two 222s and one 333; the bag for 181818 holds one 222 and two 333s. With the numbers written this way, both answers come from one simple rule each.

  • GCF: for each shared prime, take the lowest power it has in the two numbers. The prime 222 appears as 222^222 in 121212 but only 212^121 in 181818, so take 212^121. The prime 333 appears as 313^131 in 121212 and 323^232 in 181818, so take 313^131. Multiply: GCF⁡(12,18)=2×3=6\operatorname{GCF}(12, 18) = 2 \times 3 = 6GCF(12,18)=2×3=6. A prime that sits in only one of the numbers is not shared, so it contributes nothing to the GCF.
  • LCM: for each prime, take the highest power that appears in either number. For 222 take 222^222; for 333 take 323^232. Multiply: LCM⁡(12,18)=22×32=4×9=36\operatorname{LCM}(12, 18) = 2^2 \times 3^2 = 4 \times 9 = 36LCM(12,18)=22×32=4×9=36.

The GCF agrees with the factor lists, and 363636 is the first common multiple of 121212 and 181818. The figure separates shared prime copies from the extra copies each number needs.

Why lowest power for the GCF

A common factor can use only the prime copies that both numbers contain.

Why the GCF takes the lowest power of each shared prime#

Take 40=23×540=2^3\times540=23×5 and 60=22×3×560=2^2\times3\times560=22×3×5. A common factor can use at most two 222s, because 606060 has only two. Both numbers have one 555, so it can use that too. It cannot use 333, which is missing from 404040. Taking all the shared copies gives 22×5=202^2\times5=2022×5=20.

The same reasoning works for any two positive whole numbers. For each prime, a common factor cannot use more copies than either number contains. The smaller count is therefore the limit. A prime missing from one number cannot be included at all.

Take every shared prime at that limit. The product divides both numbers, because neither is missing any required copies. Every other common factor uses some or all of those copies, so it divides this product and cannot be larger. This proves that the product is the GCF.

∎

If the numbers share no prime, their only common factor is 111. Such numbers are called coprime (or relatively prime). For example, 888 and 999 are coprime, although neither is prime. If one input is 111, the GCF is 111 too.

Why highest power for the LCM

A common multiple has the opposite job: it must contain enough prime copies for each number.

Why the LCM takes the highest power of each prime that appears in either number#

Again use 40=23×540=2^3\times540=23×5 and 60=22×3×560=2^2\times3\times560=22×3×5. A common multiple needs three 222s to contain 404040‘s factors, one 333 to contain 606060‘s, and one 555, which serves both. Taking exactly these copies gives 23×3×5=1202^3\times3\times5=12023×3×5=120, which both numbers divide.

For any two positive whole numbers, compare their counts of each prime. A common multiple needs at least the larger count to contain either number’s factors. Even a prime present in just one number must be included, at its full count there.

Take exactly those required counts. Both numbers divide the resulting product. Every positive common multiple contains at least these copies, so it is a multiple of this product and cannot be smaller. This proves that the product is the LCM.

∎

If one input is 111, the LCM is the other number. To remember the contrast: the GCF takes what both numbers can supply; the LCM supplies what either number needs. That is why the GCF takes the lower count and the LCM the higher.

Worked example 1 Find the GCF and LCM of 242424 and 363636 from their prime factorizations

Write each number in prime-power form first:

24=23×3,36=22×32.24 = 2^3 \times 3, \qquad 36 = 2^2 \times 3^2.24=23×3,36=22×32.

Go prime by prime. For the prime 222, the two powers are 232^323 and 222^222. For the prime 333, they are 313^131 and 323^232.

For the GCF, take the lower power of each:

GCF⁡(24,36)=22×31=4×3=12.\operatorname{GCF}(24, 36) = 2^2 \times 3^1 = 4 \times 3 = 12.GCF(24,36)=22×31=4×3=12.

For the LCM, take the higher power of each:

LCM⁡(24,36)=23×32=8×9=72.\operatorname{LCM}(24, 36) = 2^3 \times 3^2 = 8 \times 9 = 72.LCM(24,36)=23×32=8×9=72.

As a check, 121212 divides both 242424 and 363636 (giving 222 and 333), and both 242424 and 363636 divide 727272 (giving 333 and 222). So the answers behave exactly as a GCF and an LCM should.

GCF and LCM together: the product rule

Look back at 121212 and 181818. The GCF was 666 and the LCM was 363636, and

GCF⁡(12,18)×LCM⁡(12,18)=6×36=216=12×18.\operatorname{GCF}(12, 18) \times \operatorname{LCM}(12, 18) = 6 \times 36 = 216 = 12 \times 18.GCF(12,18)×LCM(12,18)=6×36=216=12×18.

That is not a coincidence of this one pair. For any two positive whole numbers aaa and bbb,

GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b.\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b.GCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b.

To see why, picture blocks before comparing prime counts. Split each number into the part it shares with the other and the part it does not. That gives 12=2×612 = 2 \times 612=2×6 and 18=6×318 = 6 \times 318=6×3, with the shared block 666 in the middle. The GCF is that shared block by itself, and the LCM is all three blocks together. So the GCF times the LCM calls on the shared block twice and each outside block once, which is exactly what 12×1812 \times 1812×18 does.

Why the GCF and the LCM of two numbers multiply back to the product#

Use 40=23×540=2^3\times540=23×5 and 60=22×3×560=2^2\times3\times560=22×3×5. For the prime 222, the counts are three and two: the GCF takes two and the LCM three, totaling all five copies in 40×6040\times6040×60. For 333, the counts are zero and one, so the GCF takes none and the LCM one. For 555, both counts are one, so each answer takes one. Together, the GCF 202020 and the LCM 120120120 use the same prime copies as the two original numbers:

20×120=2,400=40×60.20\times120=2{,}400=40\times60.20×120=2,400=40×60.

For any prime in any pair of numbers, there are just two counts to compare. The GCF takes the smaller and the LCM the larger, so together they take both counts. If the counts are equal, both answers take that same count. If one count is zero, the GCF takes none and the LCM takes the nonzero count.

Thus GCF times LCM has the same prime copies as the product of the original numbers. The products are equal. If either input is 111, its GCF with the other is 111 and its LCM is the other number, so the rule holds in that case too.

∎

This gives a handy shortcut. Once you have the GCF, you can get the LCM without listing anything, by dividing the product of the numbers by the GCF:

LCM⁡(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF⁡(a,b).\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = (a \times b) \div \operatorname{GCF}(a, b).LCM(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF(a,b).

For 121212 and 181818 that is (12×18)÷6=216÷6=36(12 \times 18) \div 6 = 216 \div 6 = 36(12×18)÷6=216÷6=36, matching what we found the long way.

Worked example 2 Use the product rule to find LCM⁡(8,36)\operatorname{LCM}(8, 36)LCM(8,36)

First find the GCF. In prime-power form,

8=23,36=22×32.8 = 2^3, \qquad 36 = 2^2 \times 3^2.8=23,36=22×32.

The only shared prime is 222, and the lower power is 222^222, so GCF⁡(8,36)=4\operatorname{GCF}(8, 36) = 4GCF(8,36)=4. The prime 333 appears only in 363636, so it is not part of the common factor.

Now apply the product rule instead of listing multiples:

LCM⁡(8,36)=(8×36)÷GCF⁡(8,36)=288÷4=72.\operatorname{LCM}(8, 36) = (8 \times 36) \div \operatorname{GCF}(8, 36) = 288 \div 4 = 72.LCM(8,36)=(8×36)÷GCF(8,36)=288÷4=72.

The same answer falls out of the highest-power rule: LCM⁡(8,36)=23×32=8×9=72\operatorname{LCM}(8, 36) = 2^3 \times 3^2 = 8 \times 9 = 72LCM(8,36)=23×32=8×9=72. Either route works, and the product rule is quickest once the GCF is in hand.

The power rules extend, but the product rule does not

Take 444, 666 and 101010. Only the prime 222 sits in all three, and the fewest copies any of them holds is one, so GCF⁡(4,6,10)=2\operatorname{GCF}(4, 6, 10) = 2GCF(4,6,10)=2. For the LCM, take the most copies of each prime that appears anywhere. That is two 222s from 444, one 333 from 666 and one 555 from 101010, giving 22×3×5=602^2 \times 3 \times 5 = 6022×3×5=60. Both answers check out, since 222 divides all three numbers and all three divide 606060.

Nothing there was special to three numbers. A common factor has to divide all of them, so at each prime it is capped by whichever number holds the fewest copies. A common multiple has to cover all of them, so at each prime it needs enough for whichever number holds the most. Just more numbers to compare at each prime.

Worked example 3 Find the GCF and LCM of 181818, 424242 and 636363

Write all three in prime-power form:

18=2×32,42=2×3×7,63=32×7.18 = 2 \times 3^2, \qquad 42 = 2 \times 3 \times 7, \qquad 63 = 3^2 \times 7.18=2×32,42=2×3×7,63=32×7.

Now go prime by prime across all three at once. The prime 222 sits in 181818 and 424242 but not in 636363, since 636363 is odd. The prime 333 sits in all three, as 323^232, 313^131 and 323^232. The prime 777 sits in 424242 and 636363 but not in 181818.

For the GCF, keep only the primes every number has, each at its lowest power. That leaves the prime 333 on its own, and the lowest of its three powers is 313^131:

GCF⁡(18,42,63)=3.\operatorname{GCF}(18, 42, 63) = 3.GCF(18,42,63)=3.

The primes 222 and 777 drop out for the same reason an unshared prime drops out with two numbers. The number 636363 has no copy of 222 to give, and 181818 has no copy of 777.

For the LCM, take the highest power of every prime that appears anywhere, which is 212^121, 323^232 and 717^171:

LCM⁡(18,42,63)=2×32×7=2×9×7=126.\operatorname{LCM}(18, 42, 63) = 2 \times 3^2 \times 7 = 2 \times 9 \times 7 = 126.LCM(18,42,63)=2×32×7=2×9×7=126.

Check both ends. The GCF divides all three: 18÷3=618 \div 3 = 618÷3=6, 42÷3=1442 \div 3 = 1442÷3=14, and 63÷3=2163 \div 3 = 2163÷3=21. And all three divide the LCM: 126÷18=7126 \div 18 = 7126÷18=7, 126÷42=3126 \div 42 = 3126÷42=3, and 126÷63=2126 \div 63 = 2126÷63=2. Every division comes out whole, which is exactly what a GCF and an LCM are for.

The product rule does not extend, and 222, 333 and 444 show why. Their GCF is 111 and their LCM is 121212, so the two answers multiply to 121212, while 2×3×4=242 \times 3 \times 4 = 242×3×4=24.

Watch the prime 222 in those three numbers. The number 222 holds one copy, 333 holds none, and 444 holds two. The GCF takes the fewest, none, and the LCM takes the most, two. The middle count, the single copy inside the number 222, is taken by neither, and that missing copy is the whole gap: 24=12×224 = 12 \times 224=12×2. With two numbers there is no middle count to miss.

GCF in a word problem: the greatest number of equal groups

Word problems hide the GCF and LCM behind everyday situations. The signal for a GCF problem is that you are splitting things into the greatest possible number of equal groups. The same signal appears when you are finding the biggest size that fits evenly into several totals at once.

Worked example 4 Packing into the greatest number of equal boxes

A baker has 484848 muffins and 606060 cookies. She wants to pack them into identical gift boxes so that every box has the same number of muffins and the same number of cookies. No muffins or cookies may be left over, and she wants to make as many boxes as possible. How many boxes can she make?

Each box gets an equal share of the muffins and an equal share of the cookies. So the number of boxes must divide both 484848 and 606060. The largest such number is the greatest common factor. In prime-power form,

48=24×3,60=22×3×5.48 = 2^4 \times 3, \qquad 60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5.48=24×3,60=22×3×5.

Take the lower power of each shared prime: 222^222 for the prime 222, and 313^131 for the prime 333. The prime 555 is only in 606060, so it drops out.

GCF⁡(48,60)=22×3=12.\operatorname{GCF}(48, 60) = 2^2 \times 3 = 12.GCF(48,60)=22×3=12.

So she can make 121212 boxes. Each box holds 48÷12=448 \div 12 = 448÷12=4 muffins and 60÷12=560 \div 12 = 560÷12=5 cookies. Asking for the greatest number of equal groups is the tell that you want the GCF.

The value 222 is a common factor of 181818 and 242424 but not the greatest one, and 181818 is just one of the two original numbers.

LCM in a word problem: when patterns coincide

The signal for an LCM problem is that two repeating cycles start together and you want to know when they will next line up. The same signal appears when you want the smallest amount that several given sizes can all build up to exactly.

Worked example 5 When do the two bells ring together again?

At a train station, one bell rings every 888 minutes and another rings every 121212 minutes. They ring together at 9:009{:}009:00. How many minutes until they next ring at the same time?

The first bell rings at minutes 8,16,24,…8, 16, 24, \ldots8,16,24,… (the multiples of 888), and the second at minutes 12,24,36,…12, 24, 36, \ldots12,24,36,… (the multiples of 121212). They coincide at any common multiple, and they next coincide at the smallest one, the least common multiple. In prime-power form,

8=23,12=22×3.8 = 2^3, \qquad 12 = 2^2 \times 3.8=23,12=22×3.

Take the highest power of each prime: 232^323 for the prime 222, and 313^131 for the prime 333.

LCM⁡(8,12)=23×3=8×3=24.\operatorname{LCM}(8, 12) = 2^3 \times 3 = 8 \times 3 = 24.LCM(8,12)=23×3=8×3=24.

So the bells next ring together after 242424 minutes, at 9:249{:}249:24. Checking: 24÷8=324 \div 8 = 324÷8=3 rings of the first bell and 24÷12=224 \div 12 = 224÷12=2 rings of the second, both whole, so 242424 really is a moment they share. Asking when repeating events next coincide is the tell that you want the LCM.

Check your understanding

Two runners start together on a track. One finishes a lap every 666 minutes, the other every 999 minutes. After how many minutes do they next cross the start line at the same time?

Answer choices

They meet at the start line at a common multiple of their lap times, and next at the least common multiple. Write 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3 and 9=329 = 3^29=32, then take the highest power of each prime.

LCM⁡(6,9)=2×32=2×9=18\operatorname{LCM}(6, 9) = 2 \times 3^2 = 2 \times 9 = 18LCM(6,9)=2×32=2×9=18

The value 333 is the GCF, and 54=6×954 = 6 \times 954=6×9 is the plain product, not the least common multiple.

A youth group has 282828 volunteers and 424242 supply kits. It wants to form identical teams, giving every team the same number of volunteers and the same number of kits with none left over, using as many teams as it possibly can. Which computation finds the number of teams?

Answer choices

Splitting into as many identical teams as possible, with nothing left over, is the GCF signal: the number of teams has to divide both totals, and you want the largest number that does.

GCF⁡(28,42)=14\operatorname{GCF}(28, 42) = 14GCF(28,42)=14

LCM⁡(28,42)=84\operatorname{LCM}(28, 42) = 84LCM(28,42)=84 would be the right computation for a when do two repeating cycles of 282828 and 424242 next coincide question instead, not this one. Adding or multiplying the totals answers neither question.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Swapping GCF and LCM. The GCF is a factor, so it is at most as big as either number. The LCM is a multiple, so it is at least as big as either number. If your GCF is the larger answer, you have the two reversed.
  • Mixing up the powers. GCF takes the lowest power of each shared prime; LCM takes the highest. A quick check: the GCF should divide both numbers, and both numbers should divide the LCM.
  • Dropping a prime from the LCM. A prime that appears in only one number still belongs in the LCM, at its full power there, because that number alone demands it. For 8=238 = 2^38=23 and 9=329 = 3^29=32, the LCM is 23×32=722^3 \times 3^2 = 7223×32=72, not 111; do not discard 232^323 or 323^232 just because they are not shared.
  • Using the plain product as the LCM. Multiplying the two numbers gives a common multiple, but not the least one unless the numbers share no prime. For 444 and 666, the product is 242424 but the LCM is 121212; divide the product by the GCF to correct it.
  • Carrying the product rule to three numbers. The rule GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times bGCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b is about a pair, and the 2,3,42, 3, 42,3,4 example above shows it failing for three. With three or more numbers, read both answers straight off the prime powers instead.
  • Stopping the multiple lists too soon. With the listing method, keep extending both lists until a value truly appears in both. The first shared value is the LCM, not merely the first largish number you recognize.

Key takeaways

  • The GCF of two or more positive whole numbers is the largest number that divides all of them. The LCM of those numbers is the smallest positive number that is a common multiple of all of them.
  • Listing method: for the GCF, list the factors of each number and take the largest shared one. For the LCM, list the multiples of each number until the lists first coincide.
  • Prime-factorization method: write each number in prime-power form, then for the GCF take the lowest power of each prime shared by all of them. For the LCM, take the highest power of every prime that appears.
  • Take the lowest power of each shared prime for the GCF, because a common factor can use only the copies both numbers have. Take the highest power of every prime for the LCM, because a common multiple needs enough copies for each number.
  • Two numbers: the answers are linked by GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times bGCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b, so LCM⁡(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF⁡(a,b)\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = (a \times b) \div \operatorname{GCF}(a, b)LCM(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF(a,b). The reason is that a pair of counts holds only a lower one and a higher one, with nothing in between.
  • Three or more numbers: the lowest-power and highest-power rules work unchanged, but the product rule does not travel with them, as the 2,3,42, 3, 42,3,4 example showed. Read the GCF and the LCM straight off the prime powers instead.
  • In words: the greatest number of equal groups signals a GCF; when repeating cycles next coincide signals an LCM.

A bit of history (optional)

Around 300 BCE, Euclid recorded a way to find a GCF without prime factorization in Book VII of the Elements. Although the Elements is famous for geometry, Book VII studies whole numbers. His method repeatedly subtracts the smaller number from the larger, keeping the smaller and the difference. For 484848 and 606060, subtracting 484848 leaves 121212; further subtractions lead to two 121212s, revealing the GCF.

Euclid treated 111 as a unit, not as a number. That distinction shaped how he wrote the argument: one proposition handles pairs whose only common measure is the unit, and the next handles pairs with a larger common measure. Today we call both answers a GCF. The commentary on Book VII, Proposition 2 explains why Euclid needed the two separate statements.

The method had a much later role in secret messages. In their 1978 paper on RSA encryption, Ronald Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman used an extended version of Euclid’s algorithm to help calculate a public encryption key from secret information. Their original paper connects an ancient number method with the then-emerging world of electronic mail.

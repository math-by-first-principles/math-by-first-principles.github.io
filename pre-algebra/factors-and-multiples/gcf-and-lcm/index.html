GCF and LCM

Intuition

Two questions come up again and again with positive whole numbers. Given two numbers, what is the biggest number that divides into both of them? And what is the smallest number that both of them divide into? The first is the greatest common factor, written GCF; the second is the least common multiple, written LCM. They sound like opposites, and in a precise sense they are. One looks down at the factors the numbers share, and the other looks up at the multiples they share. You already know how to find factors and multiples, and you already know how to break a number into primes. This lesson puts those tools together into two quick methods, and shows why each method does exactly the right thing.

Learning goals

  • Find a GCF and an LCM by listing factors or multiples
  • Compute a GCF and an LCM from prime factorizations, taking the lowest or highest prime power
  • Explain why lowest serves the GCF and highest serves the LCM
  • Apply the product rule for two numbers, and say why three break it
  • Read greatest number of equal groups as GCF and coinciding cycles as LCM

Common factors and the GCF

A common factor of two positive whole numbers is a number that is a factor of both. Take 121212 and 181818. Their factors are

12:  1,2,3,4,6,1218:  1,2,3,6,9,18.12: \; 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 \qquad\qquad 18: \; 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18.12:1,2,3,4,6,1218:1,2,3,6,9,18.

The numbers that appear in both lists are 1,2,3,1, 2, 3,1,2,3, and 666: these are the common factors of 121212 and 181818. Every pair of positive whole numbers shares at least the factor 111, so the list of common factors is never empty. The largest entry in it is the one we care about most.

The greatest common factor (GCF) of two positive whole numbers is the largest number that is a factor of both. For 121212 and 181818 the common factors are 1,2,3,61, 2, 3, 61,2,3,6, and the greatest of these is 666, so

GCF⁡(12,18)=6.\operatorname{GCF}(12, 18) = 6.GCF(12,18)=6.

This is the largest number you could divide both 121212 and 181818 by and still land on whole numbers: 12÷6=212 \div 6 = 212÷6=2 and 18÷6=318 \div 6 = 318÷6=3. That is exactly what makes the GCF useful. When you reach fractions in the next chapter, you will divide the top and bottom of a fraction by their GCF. That is what reduces the fraction as far as it will go in a single step.

Common multiples and the LCM

Now flip the question around. A common multiple of two positive whole numbers is a number that is a multiple of both. List a few multiples of 444 and of 666:

4:  4,8,12,16,20,24,28,…6:  6,12,18,24,30,…4: \; 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, \ldots \qquad 6: \; 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, \ldots4:4,8,12,16,20,24,28,…6:6,12,18,24,30,…

The numbers in both lists, 12,24,36,…12, 24, 36, \ldots12,24,36,…, are the common multiples of 444 and 666. Unlike common factors, there are infinitely many of them, because you can always go further out and find more. But there is a smallest one, and that is the one worth naming.

The least common multiple (LCM) of two positive whole numbers is the smallest positive common multiple of the two. The common multiples of 444 and 666 start 12,24,36,…12, 24, 36, \ldots12,24,36,…, so

LCM⁡(4,6)=12.\operatorname{LCM}(4, 6) = 12.LCM(4,6)=12.

This is the soonest the two counting patterns line up: count by fours and by sixes, and 121212 is the first place they meet. That is why the LCM is the natural common denominator when you add fractions later: it is the smallest number into which both denominators fit.

The GCF is a factor of the numbers, so it is never bigger than either one. The LCM is a multiple of the numbers, so it is never smaller than either one. For 444 and 666, the GCF is 222, at most as big as the smaller number. The LCM is 121212, at least as big as the larger. If a value you have called the GCF comes out larger than the numbers themselves, you have the two swapped. The same is true if an LCM comes out smaller than those numbers.

The listing method

The lists above are the listing method (writing the factors or the multiples out and reading the answer off the lists), and they always work:

  • For the GCF, write out the factors of each number, find the ones common to both, and take the largest.
  • For the LCM, write out multiples of each number until a value shows up in both lists, and take the first such value.

Listing is reliable and easy to picture, which makes it the right way to meet these ideas. Its drawback shows up with bigger numbers. The factor lists get long, and you might count out a great many multiples before the lists finally meet. For 888 and 363636 you would list multiples up to 727272 before they coincide. So we want a method that does not depend on the numbers being small, and prime factorization provides exactly that.

Before leaving the listing method, it is worth running it once from start to finish. Write out the factors of each number, then read off the ones that appear in both lists:

8:  1,2,4,812:  1,2,3,4,6,12.8: \; 1, 2, 4, 8 \qquad\qquad 12: \; 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.8:1,2,4,812:1,2,3,4,6,12.

The numbers in both lists are 111, 222 and 444, so those are the common factors of 888 and 121212. The largest of them is 444, so GCF⁡(8,12)=4\operatorname{GCF}(8, 12) = 4GCF(8,12)=4. Notice what disqualifies a number like 333: it divides 121212 but not 888, so it appears in one list and not the other. A factor has to survive both lists to count as a common one.

The prime-factorization method

Every whole number greater than 111 has a unique prime factorization. That factorization records exactly how many copies of each prime the number is built from. That bookkeeping is precisely what the GCF and LCM need. Start each number in prime-power form. Take 121212 and 181818:

12=22×3,18=2×32.12 = 2^2 \times 3, \qquad 18 = 2 \times 3^2.12=22×3,18=2×32.

Think of each number as a bag of prime factors. The bag for 121212 holds two 222s and one 333; the bag for 181818 holds one 222 and two 333s. With the numbers written this way, both answers come from one simple rule each.

  • GCF: for each shared prime, take the lowest power it has in the two numbers. The prime 222 appears as 222^222 in 121212 but only 212^121 in 181818, so take 212^121. The prime 333 appears as 313^131 in 121212 and 323^232 in 181818, so take 313^131. Multiply: GCF⁡(12,18)=2×3=6\operatorname{GCF}(12, 18) = 2 \times 3 = 6GCF(12,18)=2×3=6. A prime that sits in only one of the numbers is not shared, so it contributes nothing to the GCF.
  • LCM: for each prime, take the highest power that appears in either number. For 222 take 222^222; for 333 take 323^232. Multiply: LCM⁡(12,18)=22×32=4×9=36\operatorname{LCM}(12, 18) = 2^2 \times 3^2 = 4 \times 9 = 36LCM(12,18)=22×32=4×9=36.

Both agree with the lists (GCF⁡=6\operatorname{GCF} = 6GCF=6 from before, and 363636 is indeed the first common multiple of 121212 and 181818). The figure below shows why “lowest” and “highest” are the right choices. The figure sets the shared core that sits inside both numbers against the combined pile that contains each number whole.

Why lowest power for the GCF

The rule is not arbitrary. A factor of both numbers can only use prime copies that both numbers actually own, and that limit is what forces “lowest power.”

Look at what each bag can spare. The bag for 121212 holds two 222s, but the bag for 181818 holds only one, so a common factor can use at most one 222: nothing can divide 181818 by asking for a 222 it does not have. The 333s work the other way, since here it is 121212 that holds only one 333. Take each shared prime at the smaller of its two counts and you get 2×3=62 \times 3 = 62×3=6, the GCF.

The full proof shows that the lower power is not merely a sensible choice, it is the only one available. Whatever two numbers you start with, a common factor can carry no more copies of a prime than the number holding fewer of them. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See why the lower power wins

So the GCF is built only from the primes the two numbers truly have in common. Each of those primes is capped at the count in whichever number has fewer copies of it. Take a prime to a higher power than that, and the result would no longer divide the number with fewer copies.

Why highest power for the LCM

The LCM argument is the mirror image. A multiple of both numbers must contain enough copies of each prime to cover each number on its own. That is what forces “highest power.”

Check 121212 and 181818 against that demand. A common multiple has to hold two 222s, because 121212 does and every multiple of 121212 carries its factors along. It has to hold two 333s for the same reason, from the 181818 side. Two of each satisfies both demands at once, and 22×32=362^2 \times 3^2 = 3622×32=36 is what the demands come to.

The full proof shows that the higher power is the smallest one that will do. For any two numbers, a common multiple must hold at least as many copies of a prime as whichever number has more of them. Taking exactly that many is always enough. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See why the higher power wins

Side by side, the two rules are easy to remember once you see the reason. The GCF can only keep what both numbers can spare, so it takes the lower power. The LCM must satisfy whichever number needs more of a given prime, so it takes the higher power.

Worked example 1 Find the GCF and LCM of 242424 and 363636 from their prime factorizations

Write each number in prime-power form first:

24=23×3,36=22×32.24 = 2^3 \times 3, \qquad 36 = 2^2 \times 3^2.24=23×3,36=22×32.

Go prime by prime. For the prime 222, the two powers are 232^323 and 222^222. For the prime 333, they are 313^131 and 323^232.

For the GCF, take the lower power of each:

GCF⁡(24,36)=22×31=4×3=12.\operatorname{GCF}(24, 36) = 2^2 \times 3^1 = 4 \times 3 = 12.GCF(24,36)=22×31=4×3=12.

For the LCM, take the higher power of each:

LCM⁡(24,36)=23×32=8×9=72.\operatorname{LCM}(24, 36) = 2^3 \times 3^2 = 8 \times 9 = 72.LCM(24,36)=23×32=8×9=72.

As a check, 121212 divides both 242424 and 363636 (giving 222 and 333), and both 242424 and 363636 divide 727272 (giving 333 and 222). So the answers behave exactly as a GCF and an LCM should.

GCF and LCM together: the product rule

Look back at 121212 and 181818. The GCF was 666 and the LCM was 363636, and

GCF⁡(12,18)×LCM⁡(12,18)=6×36=216=12×18.\operatorname{GCF}(12, 18) \times \operatorname{LCM}(12, 18) = 6 \times 36 = 216 = 12 \times 18.GCF(12,18)×LCM(12,18)=6×36=216=12×18.

That is not a coincidence of this one pair. For any two positive whole numbers aaa and bbb,

GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b.\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b.GCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b.

The reason lives in the prime powers again. Look at the prime 222 in 121212 and 181818. There are two of them in 121212 and one in 181818. The GCF takes the one and the LCM takes the two, so between them the two answers take all three. Multiplying 121212 by 181818 takes all three as well.

That happens at every prime, because a prime is offered just two counts, one from each number. The GCF takes the smaller and the LCM takes the larger, so between them they take both. The two counts can also be equal, as the 555s are in 404040 and 606060. Then each answer takes one of the pair, and the tally comes out the same.

The same bookkeeping is easier to see with blocks than with exponents. Split each number into the part it shares with the other and the part it does not. That gives 12=2×612 = 2 \times 612=2×6 and 18=6×318 = 6 \times 318=6×3, with the shared block 666 in the middle. The GCF is that shared block by itself, and the LCM is all three blocks together. So the GCF times the LCM calls on the shared block twice and each outside block once, which is exactly what 12×1812 \times 1812×18 does.

The full proof shows that the GCF times the LCM equals the two numbers multiplied. It holds for any two positive whole numbers, not just 121212 and 181818. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full proof

This gives a handy shortcut. Once you have the GCF, you can get the LCM without listing anything, by dividing the product of the numbers by the GCF:

LCM⁡(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF⁡(a,b).\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = (a \times b) \div \operatorname{GCF}(a, b).LCM(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF(a,b).

For 121212 and 181818 that is (12×18)÷6=216÷6=36(12 \times 18) \div 6 = 216 \div 6 = 36(12×18)÷6=216÷6=36, matching what we found the long way.

Worked example 2 Use the product rule to find LCM⁡(8,36)\operatorname{LCM}(8, 36)LCM(8,36)

First find the GCF. In prime-power form,

8=23,36=22×32.8 = 2^3, \qquad 36 = 2^2 \times 3^2.8=23,36=22×32.

The only shared prime is 222, and the lower power is 222^222, so GCF⁡(8,36)=4\operatorname{GCF}(8, 36) = 4GCF(8,36)=4. The prime 333 appears only in 363636, so it is not part of the common factor.

Now apply the product rule instead of listing multiples:

LCM⁡(8,36)=(8×36)÷GCF⁡(8,36)=288÷4=72.\operatorname{LCM}(8, 36) = (8 \times 36) \div \operatorname{GCF}(8, 36) = 288 \div 4 = 72.LCM(8,36)=(8×36)÷GCF(8,36)=288÷4=72.

The same answer falls out of the highest-power rule: LCM⁡(8,36)=23×32=8×9=72\operatorname{LCM}(8, 36) = 2^3 \times 3^2 = 8 \times 9 = 72LCM(8,36)=23×32=8×9=72. Either route works, and the product rule is quickest once the GCF is in hand.

The power rules extend, but the product rule does not

Take 444, 666 and 101010. Only the prime 222 sits in all three, and the fewest copies any of them holds is one, so GCF⁡(4,6,10)=2\operatorname{GCF}(4, 6, 10) = 2GCF(4,6,10)=2. For the LCM, take the most copies of each prime that appears anywhere. That is two 222s from 444, one 333 from 666 and one 555 from 101010, giving 22×3×5=602^2 \times 3 \times 5 = 6022×3×5=60. Both answers check out, since 222 divides all three numbers and all three divide 606060.

Nothing there was special to three numbers. A common factor has to divide all of them, so at each prime it is capped by whichever number holds the fewest copies. A common multiple has to cover all of them, so at each prime it needs enough for whichever number holds the most. Just more numbers to compare at each prime.

Worked example 3 Find the GCF and LCM of 181818, 424242 and 636363

Write all three in prime-power form:

18=2×32,42=2×3×7,63=32×7.18 = 2 \times 3^2, \qquad 42 = 2 \times 3 \times 7, \qquad 63 = 3^2 \times 7.18=2×32,42=2×3×7,63=32×7.

Now go prime by prime across all three at once. The prime 222 sits in 181818 and 424242 but not in 636363, since 636363 is odd. The prime 333 sits in all three, as 323^232, 313^131 and 323^232. The prime 777 sits in 424242 and 636363 but not in 181818.

For the GCF, keep only the primes every number has, each at its lowest power. That leaves the prime 333 on its own, and the lowest of its three powers is 313^131:

GCF⁡(18,42,63)=3.\operatorname{GCF}(18, 42, 63) = 3.GCF(18,42,63)=3.

The primes 222 and 777 drop out for the same reason an unshared prime drops out with two numbers. The number 636363 has no copy of 222 to give, and 181818 has no copy of 777.

For the LCM, take the highest power of every prime that appears anywhere, which is 212^121, 323^232 and 717^171:

LCM⁡(18,42,63)=2×32×7=2×9×7=126.\operatorname{LCM}(18, 42, 63) = 2 \times 3^2 \times 7 = 2 \times 9 \times 7 = 126.LCM(18,42,63)=2×32×7=2×9×7=126.

Check both ends. The GCF divides all three: 18÷3=618 \div 3 = 618÷3=6, 42÷3=1442 \div 3 = 1442÷3=14, and 63÷3=2163 \div 3 = 2163÷3=21. And all three divide the LCM: 126÷18=7126 \div 18 = 7126÷18=7, 126÷42=3126 \div 42 = 3126÷42=3, and 126÷63=2126 \div 63 = 2126÷63=2. Every division comes out whole, which is exactly what a GCF and an LCM are for.

The product rule does not extend, and 222, 333 and 444 show why. Their GCF is 111 and their LCM is 121212, so the two answers multiply to 121212, while 2×3×4=242 \times 3 \times 4 = 242×3×4=24.

Watch the prime 222 in those three numbers. The number 222 holds one copy, 333 holds none, and 444 holds two. The GCF takes the fewest, none, and the LCM takes the most, two. The middle count, the single copy inside the number 222, is taken by neither, and that missing copy is the whole gap: 24=12×224 = 12 \times 224=12×2. With two numbers there is no middle count to miss.

Check your understanding

For 222, 333 and 444, why does GCF⁡(2,3,4)×LCM⁡(2,3,4)\operatorname{GCF}(2,3,4) \times \operatorname{LCM}(2,3,4)GCF(2,3,4)×LCM(2,3,4) come out smaller than 2×3×42 \times 3 \times 42×3×4?

Answer choices

With two numbers, each prime offers only two counts, a lower one and a higher one, and the GCF and LCM take one each, covering both. With three numbers, a prime can offer a middle count that neither the GCF (which takes the lowest) nor the LCM (which takes the highest) ever picks up. Here the prime 222 appears 000 times in 333, 111 time in 222, and 222 times in 444: the GCF takes the 000, the LCM takes the 222, and the middle count of 111, from the number 222, is never taken by either. That missing copy is exactly the factor of 222 separating 121212 from 242424.

The GCF and LCM here are computed correctly (GCF⁡(2,3,4)=1\operatorname{GCF}(2,3,4) = 1GCF(2,3,4)=1 and LCM⁡(2,3,4)=12\operatorname{LCM}(2,3,4) = 12LCM(2,3,4)=12). The mismatch is not a coincidence; it happens whenever three numbers give a prime a genuine middle count. And a GCF of 111 does not by itself break anything, since two numbers can also have a GCF of 111 without the product rule failing.

The lowest-power and highest-power rules work for any number of numbers, but this product rule works only for two. With three or more, read the GCF and the LCM straight off the prime powers instead: this shortcut, (a×b)÷GCF⁡(a,b)(a \times b) \div \operatorname{GCF}(a, b)(a×b)÷GCF(a,b), does not carry over to three numbers in the same simple way.

GCF in a word problem: the greatest number of equal groups

Word problems hide the GCF and LCM behind everyday situations. The signal for a GCF problem is that you are splitting things into the greatest possible number of equal groups. The same signal appears when you are finding the biggest size that fits evenly into several totals at once.

Worked example 4 Packing into the greatest number of equal boxes

A baker has 484848 muffins and 606060 cookies. She wants to pack them into identical gift boxes so that every box has the same number of muffins and the same number of cookies. No muffins or cookies may be left over, and she wants to make as many boxes as possible. How many boxes can she make?

Each box gets an equal share of the muffins and an equal share of the cookies. So the number of boxes must divide both 484848 and 606060. The largest such number is the greatest common factor. In prime-power form,

48=24×3,60=22×3×5.48 = 2^4 \times 3, \qquad 60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5.48=24×3,60=22×3×5.

Take the lower power of each shared prime: 222^222 for the prime 222, and 313^131 for the prime 333. The prime 555 is only in 606060, so it drops out.

GCF⁡(48,60)=22×3=12.\operatorname{GCF}(48, 60) = 2^2 \times 3 = 12.GCF(48,60)=22×3=12.

So she can make 121212 boxes. Each box holds 48÷12=448 \div 12 = 448÷12=4 muffins and 60÷12=560 \div 12 = 560÷12=5 cookies. Asking for the greatest number of equal groups is the tell that you want the GCF.

GCF⁡(18,24)=2×3=6\operatorname{GCF}(18, 24) = 2 \times 3 = 6GCF(18,24)=2×3=6

The value 727272 is LCM⁡(18,24)\operatorname{LCM}(18, 24)LCM(18,24), the number you would want instead if the question asked when two repeating cycles of 181818 and 242424 next line up. The value 222 is a common factor of 181818 and 242424 but not the greatest one, and 181818 is just one of the two original numbers.

LCM in a word problem: when patterns coincide

The signal for an LCM problem is that two repeating cycles start together and you want to know when they will next line up. The same signal appears when you want the smallest amount that several given sizes can all build up to exactly.

Worked example 5 When do the two bells ring together again?

At a train station, one bell rings every 888 minutes and another rings every 121212 minutes. They ring together at 9:009{:}009:00. How many minutes until they next ring at the same time?

The first bell rings at minutes 8,16,24,…8, 16, 24, \ldots8,16,24,… (the multiples of 888), and the second at minutes 12,24,36,…12, 24, 36, \ldots12,24,36,… (the multiples of 121212). They coincide at any common multiple, and they next coincide at the smallest one, the least common multiple. In prime-power form,

8=23,12=22×3.8 = 2^3, \qquad 12 = 2^2 \times 3.8=23,12=22×3.

Take the highest power of each prime: 232^323 for the prime 222, and 313^131 for the prime 333.

LCM⁡(8,12)=23×3=8×3=24.\operatorname{LCM}(8, 12) = 2^3 \times 3 = 8 \times 3 = 24.LCM(8,12)=23×3=8×3=24.

So the bells next ring together after 242424 minutes, at 9:249{:}249:24. Checking: 24÷8=324 \div 8 = 324÷8=3 rings of the first bell and 24÷12=224 \div 12 = 224÷12=2 rings of the second, both whole, so 242424 really is a moment they share. Asking when repeating events next coincide is the tell that you want the LCM.

Check your understanding

Two runners start together on a track. One finishes a lap every 666 minutes, the other every 999 minutes. After how many minutes do they next cross the start line at the same time?

Answer choices

They meet at the start line at a common multiple of their lap times, and next at the least common multiple. Write 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3 and 9=329 = 3^29=32, then take the highest power of each prime.

LCM⁡(6,9)=2×32=2×9=18\operatorname{LCM}(6, 9) = 2 \times 3^2 = 2 \times 9 = 18LCM(6,9)=2×32=2×9=18

The value 333 is the GCF, and 54=6×954 = 6 \times 954=6×9 is the plain product, not the least common multiple.

A word problem never announces which of the two you need; you have to read for the signal.

Check your understanding

A youth group has 282828 volunteers and 424242 supply kits. It wants to form identical teams, giving every team the same number of volunteers and the same number of kits with none left over, using as many teams as it possibly can. Which computation finds the number of teams?

Answer choices

Splitting into as many identical teams as possible, with nothing left over, is the GCF signal: the number of teams has to divide both totals, and you want the largest number that does.

GCF⁡(28,42)=14\operatorname{GCF}(28, 42) = 14GCF(28,42)=14

LCM⁡(28,42)=84\operatorname{LCM}(28, 42) = 84LCM(28,42)=84 would be the right computation for a when do two repeating cycles of 282828 and 424242 next coincide question instead, not this one. Adding or multiplying the totals answers neither question.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Swapping GCF and LCM. The GCF is a factor, so it is at most as big as either number. The LCM is a multiple, so it is at least as big as either number. If your GCF is the larger answer, you have the two reversed.
  • Mixing up the powers. GCF takes the lowest power of each shared prime; LCM takes the highest. A quick check: the GCF should divide both numbers, and both numbers should divide the LCM.
  • Dropping a prime from the LCM. A prime that appears in only one number still belongs in the LCM, at its full power there, because that number alone demands it. For 8=238 = 2^38=23 and 9=329 = 3^29=32, the LCM is 23×32=722^3 \times 3^2 = 7223×32=72, not 111; do not discard 232^323 or 323^232 just because they are not shared.
  • Using the plain product as the LCM. Multiplying the two numbers gives a common multiple, but not the least one unless the numbers share no prime. For 444 and 666, the product is 242424 but the LCM is 121212; divide the product by the GCF to correct it.
  • Carrying the product rule to three numbers. The rule GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times bGCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b is about a pair, and the 2,3,42, 3, 42,3,4 example above shows it failing for three. With three or more numbers, read both answers straight off the prime powers instead.
  • Stopping the multiple lists too soon. With the listing method, keep extending both lists until a value truly appears in both. The first shared value is the LCM, not merely the first largish number you recognize.

Key takeaways

  • The GCF of two or more positive whole numbers is the largest number that divides all of them. The LCM of those numbers is the smallest positive number that is a common multiple of all of them.
  • Listing method: for the GCF, list the factors of each number and take the largest shared one. For the LCM, list the multiples of each number until the lists first coincide.
  • Prime-factorization method: write each number in prime-power form, then for the GCF take the lowest power of each prime shared by all of them. For the LCM, take the highest power of every prime that appears.
  • Take the lowest power of each shared prime for the GCF, because a common factor can use only the copies both numbers have. Take the highest power of every prime for the LCM, because a common multiple needs enough copies for each number.
  • For two numbers the answers are linked by GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times bGCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b, so LCM⁡(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF⁡(a,b)\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = (a \times b) \div \operatorname{GCF}(a, b)LCM(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF(a,b). The reason is that a pair of counts holds only a lower one and a higher one, with nothing in between.
  • Three or more numbers: the lowest-power and highest-power rules work unchanged, but the product rule does not travel with them, as the 2,3,42, 3, 42,3,4 example showed. Read the GCF and the LCM straight off the prime powers instead.
  • In words: the greatest number of equal groups signals a GCF; when repeating cycles next coincide signals an LCM.

Why the GCF takes the lowest power of each shared prime

The lesson shows the cap biting on 121212 and 181818: a common factor may carry one 222, because 181818 has only one to give. This runs the same argument on a fresh pair, then on any two numbers at once.

Why the GCF takes the lowest power of each shared prime#

Try it on 40=23×540 = 2^3 \times 540=23×5 and 60=22×3×560 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 560=22×3×5. Suppose ddd divides both of them, and ask how many 222s ddd is allowed. Every 222 inside ddd has to sit inside 404040 as well, which caps the count at three. Every one has to sit inside 606060 too, which caps it at two. Two is the lower cap, so ddd holds at most two 222s. Asking the same of 555 gives one from each side, and asking it of 333 gives none, since 404040 has no 333 to offer. Taking every cap at once builds 22×5=202^2 \times 5 = 2022×5=20, which is GCF⁡(40,60)\operatorname{GCF}(40, 60)GCF(40,60).

Now the general form of that. Suppose a number ddd divides both aaa and bbb. Look at any prime ppp in the factorization of ddd, and say that ddd contains ppp exactly kkk times. Since ddd divides aaa, those kkk copies must already sit inside aaa, so aaa holds at least kkk of them. The same reasoning gives bbb at least kkk. So kkk is at most the smaller of those two counts.

For every prime, then, a common factor can carry no more copies than the number that has fewer of them. The greatest common factor is the one that carries as many as it possibly can. For each shared prime that is the lower of the two powers. A prime sitting in only one of the numbers drops out entirely, because the other has none of it to offer.

Multiply the lowest power of each prime together. The result divides both aaa and bbb, because it never asks for more copies than either number has. It is also as large as any common factor can be, since it takes the most copies allowed. That is the GCF.

∎

Why the LCM takes the highest power of each prime that appears in either number

The lesson shows the demand landing on 121212 and 181818: a common multiple has to hold two 222s, because every multiple of 121212 carries them along. This runs the same argument on a fresh pair, then on any two numbers at once.

Why the LCM takes the highest power of each prime that appears in either number#

Take the same 40=23×540 = 2^3 \times 540=23×5 and 60=22×3×560 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 560=22×3×5, and let mmm be a multiple of both. Because 404040 divides mmm, that mmm carries at least three 222s. Because 606060 divides mmm, it carries at least two, a demand the three has already met. So three is what the pair asks for, the larger of the two counts. One 333 is demanded by 606060 and one 555 by either number, so the smallest mmm meeting every demand is 23×3×5=1202^3 \times 3 \times 5 = 12023×3×5=120, which is LCM⁡(40,60)\operatorname{LCM}(40, 60)LCM(40,60).

Now the general form. Suppose a number mmm is a multiple of both aaa and bbb, so both of them divide mmm. Look at any prime ppp. Because aaa divides mmm, that mmm holds at least as many copies of ppp as aaa does. Because bbb divides mmm, it holds at least as many as bbb does. To meet both demands at once, mmm needs at least the larger of the two counts.

For every prime, then, a common multiple must carry no fewer copies than the number that has more of them. The least common multiple carries exactly that many and not one extra, which for each prime is the higher of the two powers. A prime sitting in only one number still appears, at the full power it has there, because that number alone demands it.

Multiply the highest power of each prime together. The result is a number that both aaa and bbb divide, because each prime is present in enough quantity for either one. It is also as small as any common multiple can be, because no prime is taken to more than the largest power required. That is the LCM.

∎

Why the counts always add up, for any two positive numbers

The lesson shows the product rule working on 121212 and 181818, and says why. At each prime the GCF takes the smaller count and the LCM takes the larger, so together they take both. This does that count for every prime at once.

Why the GCF and the LCM of two numbers multiply back to the product#

Take 40=23×540 = 2^3 \times 540=23×5 and 60=22×3×560 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 560=22×3×5 first. Their GCF is 22×52^2 \times 522×5 and their LCM is 23×3×52^3 \times 3 \times 523×3×5. Count the 222s. The GCF took two and the LCM took three, so five between them, and 40×6040 \times 6040×60 holds five as well. The 333s give 0+10 + 10+1 against 0+10 + 10+1, and the 555s give 1+11 + 11+1 against 1+11 + 11+1. Every prime matches, and 20×120=2400=40×6020 \times 120 = 2400 = 40 \times 6020×120=2400=40×60.

Now with letters. If either number is 111 there is nothing to prove: the GCF is 111, the LCM is the other number, and both sides read the same. So take aaa and bbb above 111.

Multiplying two numbers stands their prime lists side by side, and a number has only one prime list, so that combined list is the product’s. Counting a prime inside a product means adding the two counts.

Fix a prime ppp. Let xxx be how many copies of it sit in aaa, and yyy how many sit in bbb. Either count may be 000. The two rules already proved say the GCF takes the lower of xxx and yyy, and the LCM takes the higher. So GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b)GCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b) holds

(lower of x and y)+(higher of x and y)(\text{lower of } x \text{ and } y) + (\text{higher of } x \text{ and } y)(lower of x and y)+(higher of x and y)

copies of ppp. Here is the whole rule in one step. Take the smaller of xxx and yyy as the “lower” value, and either one if they are equal. Then “lower” and “higher” are xxx and yyy again, just named in size order. Sorting two numbers does not change their total, so

(lower of x and y)+(higher of x and y)=x+y,(\text{lower of } x \text{ and } y) + (\text{higher of } x \text{ and } y) = x + y,(lower of x and y)+(higher of x and y)=x+y,

and x+yx + yx+y is how many copies of ppp sit inside a×ba \times ba×b.

So at every prime the two sides hold the same number of copies. A number has only one prime list, so two numbers built from identical lists cannot be different. Therefore

GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b.\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b.GCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b.

Notice the step that needed a pair. Two counts are a smaller one and a larger one, and the two answers take one each. Three counts have a middle one that neither answer takes, which is why this rule is about two numbers only.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

You found the GCF by breaking both numbers into primes. That works while the numbers stay small. Hand someone a number built by secretly multiplying two large primes, each hundreds of digits long, and the method stalls: factoring a number like that back into its two primes can take far too long to be practical, even for a computer.

An older way never factors anything. A Greek writer named Euclid set it down around 300 BCE, in the geometry book called the Elements, and the recipe carries his name today. Take the two numbers and subtract the smaller from the larger, over and over, until the two are equal. That final value is the GCF.

Run it on 484848 and 606060. Subtracting drives the pair down to 121212 and 121212, so the GCF is 121212. One line explains why. A number that divides both of them also divides their difference. So the difference is safe to use, and it is smaller.

Subtracting one at a time is slow on huge numbers. Modern versions divide instead, replacing the larger number by the remainder, which lands in one step where subtracting took thousands. That change, plus never factoring at all, keeps it quick on numbers hundreds of digits long. A close relative of this algorithm is part of how today’s internet codes, the ones built from two secret large primes, get set up in the first place. Two thousand years on, it still returns the 121212 your prime powers did.

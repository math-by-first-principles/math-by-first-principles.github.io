    1. The same two questions, asked two ways . Foundational, 12 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    Take the numbers 272727 and 454545. Two questions can be asked about any such pair: what is the largest whole number that divides into both of them, and what is the smallest whole number that both of them divide into. Each question can be answered by writing out lists, and each can be answered from the prime factorizations. The two routes are answering the same question, so they have to agree.

    1. A
      Part A.

      List every factor of 272727 and every factor of 454545. Then write down the factors that appear in both lists, and state which of them is the greatest common factor of the two numbers.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Write 272727 and 454545 in prime-power form. Then apply the prime-factorization rule for the greatest common factor, working one prime at a time, and give the result both as a product of prime powers and as a single number.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Find the least common multiple of 272727 and 454545 from their prime-power forms. Then say how many multiples of 272727 you would have had to write down to reach it by listing instead.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    4. D
      Part D.

      One of these two numbers contains a prime that the other does not. Identify that prime, then decide for each of the greatest common factor and the least common multiple in turn whether it can appear there, and explain from what each of those two things has to do why it must be that way.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 4

      Part A wants the two factor lists themselves, built systematically so that nothing is missed. The parts after it turn instead on the primes each number is built from: which primes both numbers own, and how many copies each one holds. Have both breakdowns in front of you before comparing anything.

    2. Hint 2 of 4 · Part B

      For the greatest common factor, ask of each prime in turn how many copies the poorer of the two numbers can spare. A common factor can never call for more copies of a prime than one of the numbers actually holds.

    3. Hint 3 of 4 · Part C

      For the least common multiple, ask instead how many copies are needed to cover each number on its own. Whichever number is hungrier for a given prime sets the count, and no prime present in either number may be left out.

    4. Hint 4 of 4 · Part D

      Find a prime standing in one factorization and not the other, then test it twice: could a number containing it still divide the number that has none of it, and could a number missing it still be a multiple of the number that has it?

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Factors of 272727: 1,3,9,271, 3, 9, 271,3,9,27. Factors of 454545: 1,3,5,9,15,451, 3, 5, 9, 15, 451,3,5,9,15,45. The common factors are 111, 333 and 999, so GCF⁡(27,45)=9\operatorname{GCF}(27, 45) = 9GCF(27,45)=9.

    Part B

    27=3327 = 3^327=33 and 45=32×545 = 3^2 \times 545=32×5, and the rule gives GCF⁡(27,45)=32=9\operatorname{GCF}(27, 45) = 3^2 = 9GCF(27,45)=32=9.

    Part C

    LCM⁡(27,45)=33×5=135\operatorname{LCM}(27, 45) = 3^3 \times 5 = 135LCM(27,45)=33×5=135, and reaching it by listing would have taken 555 multiples of 272727.

    Part D

    The prime is 555, which sits in 454545 but not in 272727. It cannot appear in the GCF, because a factor of 272727 cannot contain a prime that 272727 does not have, and it must appear in the LCM, because a multiple of 454545 has to contain everything 454545 is built from.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Work up from 111 and pair each factor with its partner, so that nothing is missed.

    27:  1×27,  3×9  ⇒  1,3,9,2727: \; 1 \times 27, \; 3 \times 9 \;\Rightarrow\; 1, 3, 9, 2727:1×27,3×9⇒1,3,9,27

    45:  1×45,  3×15,  5×9  ⇒  1,3,5,9,15,4545: \; 1 \times 45, \; 3 \times 15, \; 5 \times 9 \;\Rightarrow\; 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 4545:1×45,3×15,5×9⇒1,3,5,9,15,45

    Now compare the two lists. The numbers standing in both are 111, 333 and 999, and those are the common factors of 272727 and 454545. Every pair of whole numbers has 111 in common, so such a list is never empty, and here the largest entry is 999:

    GCF⁡(27,45)=9.\operatorname{GCF}(27, 45) = 9.GCF(27,45)=9.

    Check it against the definition: 27÷9=327 \div 9 = 327÷9=3 and 45÷9=545 \div 9 = 545÷9=5, both whole, so 999 divides both numbers. Nothing larger can, because the two lists hold nothing larger in common.

    Part B

    Break each number down. 27=3×3×327 = 3 \times 3 \times 327=3×3×3, and 45=3×3×545 = 3 \times 3 \times 545=3×3×5, so in prime-power form

    27=33,45=32×5.27 = 3^3, \qquad 45 = 3^2 \times 5.27=33,45=32×5.

    Go prime by prime. The prime 333 appears three times in 272727 and twice in 454545, so a number dividing both can carry it at most twice: take 323^232. The prime 555 appears in 454545 only, and 272727 has none of it to offer, so it cannot appear in a common factor at all.

    GCF⁡(27,45)=32=9.\operatorname{GCF}(27, 45) = 3^2 = 9.GCF(27,45)=32=9.

    This is the same 999 the lists produced, which is what had to happen. The lists and the prime powers are two ways of asking one question, not two questions.

    Part C

    A multiple of both numbers has to carry enough copies of every prime to cover each number on its own. The prime 333 appears three times in 272727 and twice in 454545, so three copies are needed; the prime 555 appears in 454545, so it has to be there as well.

    LCM⁡(27,45)=33×5=27×5=135.\operatorname{LCM}(27, 45) = 3^3 \times 5 = 27 \times 5 = 135.LCM(27,45)=33×5=27×5=135.

    Check it both ways round: 135÷27=5135 \div 27 = 5135÷27=5 and 135÷45=3135 \div 45 = 3135÷45=3, so 135135135 really is a multiple of each.

    By listing, the multiples of 272727 run 27,54,81,108,13527, 54, 81, 108, 13527,54,81,108,135, so 135135135 is the fifth of them, and the multiples of 454545 run 45,90,13545, 90, 13545,90,135, so it is the third of those. Five rows is no hardship. The point is that listing gives no warning of how long it will take: for 272727 and 505050, which share no prime at all, the first coincidence is 1,3501{,}3501,350, and the same count would run to 505050 rows.

    Part D

    The prime factorizations show it at once:

    27=33,45=32×5.27 = 3^3, \qquad 45 = 3^2 \times 5.27=33,45=32×5.

    The prime 555 belongs to 454545 alone.

    A common factor has to divide 272727. Every prime inside a factor of 272727 already sits inside 272727, and 272727 is built from copies of 333 and nothing else, so no factor of 272727 can contain a 555. That bars 555 from the greatest common factor entirely: it is not that the rule chooses to drop it, it is that 272727 has none of it to give.

    A common multiple has to be a multiple of 454545, and 454545 cannot divide a number with no 555 in it. So every common multiple carries a 555, the least one included. Here the demand comes from one number alone, and one number is enough.

    Compare the two answers and the difference is visible:

    GCF⁡(27,45)=9=32,LCM⁡(27,45)=135=33×5.\operatorname{GCF}(27, 45) = 9 = 3^2, \qquad \operatorname{LCM}(27, 45) = 135 = 3^3 \times 5.GCF(27,45)=9=32,LCM(27,45)=135=33×5.

    That is the general pattern for an unshared prime: barred from the greatest common factor, because the other number cannot supply it, and compulsory in the least common multiple, at the full power it has, because the number that owns it demands it.

    In one line

    The common factors of 272727 and 454545 are 111, 333 and 999, so GCF⁡(27,45)=9\operatorname{GCF}(27, 45) = 9GCF(27,45)=9, which the prime powers give as 323^232. The least common multiple is 33×5=1353^3 \times 5 = 13533×5=135, the fifth multiple of 272727. The prime 555 sits in 454545 alone, which bars it from the greatest common factor and makes it compulsory in the least common multiple.

    2. Two numbers given only by their prime powers . Reasoning, 10 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Reasoning 10 pts

    Two whole numbers are described by their prime factorizations rather than by their digits: a=24×32×7a = 2^4 \times 3^2 \times 7a=24×32×7 and b=22×33×5b = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 5b=22×33×5. Written this way, each number is a tally of how many copies of each prime it is built from, and that tally is everything either rule needs.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Give the greatest common factor and the least common multiple of aaa and bbb, each as a product of prime powers. Neither one needs to be multiplied out.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A student writes: "The prime 555 is in bbb only and the prime 777 is in aaa only, so neither of them is shared. Unshared primes get dropped, so 555 and 777 appear in neither answer." Say which part of that reasoning is sound and which part is not, correct the faulty part, and give the reason it fails.

      Find and correct the error Say which line first goes wrong, why it is wrong, and then do it correctly. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      There is a limit on how many copies of the prime 222 a common factor of aaa and bbb may contain, and a minimum number of copies of the prime 333 that every common multiple of aaa and bbb must contain. State each of those two bounds and prove it. Then say how the two arguments, run at every prime at once, become the lowest-power and highest-power rules.

      Complete the derivation Each line should follow from the one above it. Say what lets you take each step. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Neither number needs to be multiplied out at any stage. A prime factorization is a tally of how many copies of each prime a number holds, and both rules are decided one prime at a time from the two tallies.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Test the student's rule in the direction they did not. Ask what would go wrong if a common multiple were missing a prime that one of the two numbers is built from, and whether such a number could be a multiple of it at all.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Both arguments start from a supposition and push it until it collides with a factorization. Write down what it would mean for a common factor to hold three copies of a prime, then look for the number that cannot supply them.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    GCF⁡(a,b)=22×32\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) = 2^2 \times 3^2GCF(a,b)=22×32 and LCM⁡(a,b)=24×33×5×7\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = 2^4 \times 3^3 \times 5 \times 7LCM(a,b)=24×33×5×7.

    Part B

    Dropping 555 and 777 from the greatest common factor is sound; dropping them from the least common multiple is not. Both must appear there at their full power, because a multiple of aaa has to carry the 777 that aaa is built from and a multiple of bbb has to carry its 555.

    Part C

    A common factor divides bbb, which holds only two copies of 222, so it can carry at most two. A common multiple is a multiple of bbb, which holds three copies of 333, so it must carry at least three. Repeated at every prime, the first argument is the lowest-power rule and the second is the highest-power rule.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Set the two tallies side by side, prime by prime. For the prime 222 the powers are 242^424 in aaa and 222^222 in bbb; for the prime 333 they are 323^232 and 333^333; the prime 555 appears in bbb alone, and the prime 777 in aaa alone.

    The greatest common factor takes the lower power of each prime the two numbers share, and takes nothing from a prime only one of them has:

    GCF⁡(a,b)=22×32.\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) = 2^2 \times 3^2.GCF(a,b)=22×32.

    The least common multiple takes the higher power of every prime that appears anywhere, unshared primes included:

    LCM⁡(a,b)=24×33×5×7.\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = 2^4 \times 3^3 \times 5 \times 7.LCM(a,b)=24×33×5×7.

    Multiplied out these come to 363636 and 15,12015{,}12015,120, but the prime powers are the more useful form. In them you can see directly that the first divides both numbers, since every power in it is matched or beaten in each, and that both numbers divide the second.

    Part B

    The student holds one correct rule and has applied it to a question it does not govern.

    For the greatest common factor the reasoning holds. A common factor divides aaa, so every prime inside it already sits inside aaa; it divides bbb, so every prime inside it sits inside bbb as well. The prime 555 is nowhere in aaa and the prime 777 is nowhere in bbb, so neither can appear in any common factor whatsoever:

    GCF⁡(a,b)=22×32.\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) = 2^2 \times 3^2.GCF(a,b)=22×32.

    For the least common multiple the reasoning reverses. A common multiple must be divisible by aaa, and aaa carries a 777, so the multiple carries a 777 too. It must be divisible by bbb, and bbb carries a 555, so the multiple carries a 555. An unshared prime is not optional in a common multiple; it is demanded by the one number that owns it.

    LCM⁡(a,b)=24×33×5×7.\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = 2^4 \times 3^3 \times 5 \times 7.LCM(a,b)=24×33×5×7.

    Test the student's version and it collapses at once. Dropping both unshared primes leaves 24×33=4322^4 \times 3^3 = 43224×33=432, and neither a=1,008a = 1{,}008a=1,008 nor b=540b = 540b=540 divides 432432432, since both are larger than it. A number that is a multiple of neither number can hardly be their least common multiple.

    Part C

    Take the prime 222 first. Suppose ddd is a common factor of aaa and bbb, and suppose ddd contained three copies of 222. Since ddd divides bbb, those three copies would have to sit inside bbb already. But

    b=22×33×5b = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 5b=22×33×5

    holds exactly two copies of 222 and no third can be extracted from it, so the supposition fails. A common factor carries at most two copies of 222. The number aaa has four of them to spare, and that turns out not to matter: the poorer of the two numbers sets the cap.

    Now the prime 333. Suppose mmm is a common multiple of aaa and bbb. Being a multiple of bbb means

    m=b×k=22×33×5×km = b \times k = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 5 \times km=b×k=22×33×5×k

    for some whole number kkk, so every copy of 333 inside bbb is already inside mmm, which is at least three of them. Here aaa holds only two copies of 333, and again that does not matter: the richer of the two numbers sets the demand.

    Run those two arguments at every prime at once and the rules fall out. A common factor is capped, prime by prime, by whichever number holds fewer copies, so the greatest one takes the lower power throughout. A common multiple is forced, prime by prime, to match whichever number holds more, so the least one takes the higher power throughout. Lowest and highest are not a convention to be memorised. They are the two directions the definitions push in.

    In one line

    GCF⁡(a,b)=22×32=36\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) = 2^2 \times 3^2 = 36GCF(a,b)=22×32=36 and LCM⁡(a,b)=24×33×5×7=15,120\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = 2^4 \times 3^3 \times 5 \times 7 = 15{,}120LCM(a,b)=24×33×5×7=15,120. The unshared primes 555 and 777 are barred from the first and compulsory in the second. Any common factor holds at most two copies of 222 and any common multiple at least three copies of 333, and those two caps, applied at every prime, are the lowest-power and highest-power rules.

    The answer

    Part A

    The largest card is 202020 centimetres on a side, and the board takes 101010 of them, lying 222 along the short edge and 555 along the long edge.

    Part B

    The 555 centimetre card works, giving 888 along the short edge and 202020 along the long one, 160160160 cards in all. The 252525 centimetre card fails: it divides 100100100 but not 404040, so one card leaves 151515 centimetres of the short edge bare and a second would hang over it.

    LCM⁡(40,100)=200\operatorname{LCM}(40, 100) = 200LCM(40,100)=200.     Worked solution

    Part A

    A card of side sss fits along the 404040 centimetre edge only if sss divides 404040, and along the 100100100 centimetre edge only if sss divides 100100100. Both conditions apply at once, so sss must be a common factor of the two measurements, and the largest card is the greatest common factor. In prime-power form,

    40=23×5,100=22×52.40 = 2^3 \times 5, \qquad 100 = 2^2 \times 5^2.40=23×5,100=22×52.

    Take the lower power of each shared prime, which is 222^222 for the prime 222 and one copy of 555 for the prime 555:

    GCF⁡(40,100)=22×5=20.\operatorname{GCF}(40, 100) = 2^2 \times 5 = 20.GCF(40,100)=22×5=20.

    So the cards are 202020 centimetres square. The short edge takes 40÷20=240 \div 20 = 240÷20=2 of them and the long edge 100÷20=5100 \div 20 = 5100÷20=5, so the board takes

    2×5=10 cards.2 \times 5 = 10 \text{ cards.}2×5=10 cards.

    Both divisions came out whole, which is the check that the cards really do fit.

    Part B

    Both cases are settled by asking whether the side divides both measurements, not just one.

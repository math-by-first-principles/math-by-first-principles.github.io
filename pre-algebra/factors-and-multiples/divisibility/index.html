Divisibility Rules

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

Is 48,213,76048{,}213{,}76048,213,760 divisible by 555? Yes: it ends in 000, so no long division is needed. A divisibility rule is a quick test for whether division leaves a remainder of zero. Most of these rules come from place value: split off a piece that divides evenly, then check what is left. The rule for 666 combines two tests you will learn here. Knowing the reasons lets you rebuild a rule if you forget it.

Learning goals

  • Test for 222, 555 and 101010 by reading the last digit
  • Add the digits to settle 333 and 999
  • Check the last two or three digits for 444 and 888
  • Combine the 222 and 333 tests for 666, since they share no factor above 111
  • Explain why each rule works, from place value for most, and from combining two rules for 666
  • State what divisible means, and tie factor to multiple

What “divisible” means

A whole number is divisible by a positive whole number when the division leaves remainder 000. For example, 20÷5=420 \div 5 = 420÷5=4 with nothing left over, but 23÷523 \div 523÷5 leaves 333. We do not divide by zero. Zero itself is divisible by every positive whole number: 0÷5=00 \div 5 = 00÷5=0, for example.

The same division fact has three useful descriptions:

  • Divisible: 202020 is divisible by 555.
  • Factor (or divisor): 555 is a factor of 202020.
  • Multiple: 202020 is a multiple of 555.

Each says 20=5×420 = 5 \times 420=5×4: four groups of five use all twenty objects. In the picture, change the count and the row width. A full set of rows means the width is a factor; the hollow dots in a short last row show the remainder.

Set the count to 101010 and step the row width from 222 up to 666. Exactly two of those five widths leave nothing hollow, and each of the other three leaves a remainder you can count on the picture.

Since 999 is a factor of 636363, which statement says the same thing?

Answer choices

A factor divides the number with no remainder.

63=9×763 = 9 \times 763=9×7

So 636363 is divisible by 999 and is a multiple of 999. Reversing the factor and multiple reverses the division; 9÷639 \div 639÷63 is not a whole number.

The one idea behind most rules

Most tests use one idea: split off a part that divides evenly, then test the leftover.

For example, 28=20+828 = 20 + 828=20+8. The 202020 is five groups of 444 and the 888 is two groups of 444. Together they make seven groups, so 282828 is divisible by 444.

The same idea works with subtraction. The numbers 181818 and 121212 are six threes and four threes. Adding gives ten threes; subtracting gives two threes:

18+12=3×(6+4),18−12=3×(6−4).18 + 12 = 3 \times (6 + 4), \qquad 18 - 12 = 3 \times (6 - 4).18+12=3×(6+4),18−12=3×(6−4).

For any positive divisor ddd, adding or subtracting two multiples of ddd just adds or subtracts their counts of groups. This is the distributive property in reverse. If the two counts are mmm and nnn, then

(d×m)+(d×n)=d×(m+n),(d×m)−(d×n)=d×(m−n).\begin{aligned} (d \times m) + (d \times n) &= d \times (m+n),\\ (d \times m) - (d \times n) &= d \times (m-n). \end{aligned}(d×m)+(d×n)(d×m)−(d×n)​=d×(m+n),=d×(m−n).​

For a whole-number difference, subtract the smaller count from the larger. A divisor of both numbers therefore divides their sum and difference. This explains the shortcut in both directions. If the leftover is a multiple of ddd, so is the whole, because it is a sum of two multiples. If the whole is a multiple, so is the leftover, because it is a difference of two multiples. The whole passes exactly when the leftover does.

For example, 836=830+6836=830+6836=830+6, and 830830830 is 838383 tens. The picture separates that known multiple of 101010 from the ones digit left to test.

Divisibility by 2, 5, and 10: read the last digit

Every place-value column above the ones is made from tens. Since 10=2×510 = 2 \times 510=2×5, that part already divides evenly by 222, 555, and 101010.

Why only the last digit matters for 222, 555, and 101010#

Split 574574574 into 570+4570 + 4570+4. The 570570570 is 575757 tens, so all three divisors divide it evenly. Only the 444 is left to test: it is divisible by 222, but not by 555 or 101010.

The digits of 574574574 were not essential. Any whole number splits into a whole number of tens and its ones digit. By the sum-and-difference reasoning above, the whole number passes each test exactly when that ones digit does.

∎

  • Divisible by 222: the last digit is 000, 222, 444, 666, or 888.
  • Divisible by 555: the last digit is 000 or 555.
  • Divisible by 101010: the last digit is 000.

A whole number divisible by 222 is even; any other whole number is odd. Zero is even, because 0=2×00 = 2 \times 00=2×0.

Worked example 1 Test 4,1704{,}1704,170 for divisibility by 222, 555, and 101010

Everything depends on the ones digit, which is 000.

To see why the rest drops out, split off that last column:

4,170=(417×10)+0,4{,}170 = (417 \times 10) + 0,4,170=(417×10)+0,

where the 417×10417 \times 10417×10 piece is 417417417 tens, already a multiple of 101010. The leftover ones digit is 000, and 000 is a multiple of 222, of 555, and of 101010 at once.

So 4,1704{,}1704,170 is divisible by 222 (the digit 000 is even), by 555 (the digit is 000), and by 101010 (the digit is 000). Any number ending in 000 clears all three tests together.

2,450=245×102{,}450 = 245 \times 102,450=245×10

Every ten contains five twos or two fives, so the number also divides evenly by 222 and 555. Zero is an even digit; it does not make the 222 test fail.

Why do 5,9025{,}9025,902 and its last digit 222 give the same results for divisibility by 222, 555, and 101010?

Answer choices

5,902=5,900+25{,}902=5{,}900+25,902=5,900+2

The 5,9005{,}9005,900 is 590590590 tens. Since 10=2×510=2\times510=2×5, this part leaves remainder 000 for each divisor. Only the 222 remains to test, so the whole number passes 222 and fails 555 and 101010.

Divisibility by 3 and 9: add the digits

The last digit cannot settle divisibility by 333: 121212 passes and 323232 fails, although both end in 222. Instead, add the digits. For 531531531, the sum 5+3+1=95+3+1=95+3+1=9 passes both the 333 and 999 tests. Place value explains why this works.

Why the digit sum decides divisibility by 999 (and by 333)#

Use 347434743474 so that four columns contribute. Each place value above the ones is one more than a multiple of 999:

10=9+1,100=99+1,1000=999+1.\begin{aligned} 10 &= 9+1,\\ 100 &= 99+1,\\ 1000 &= 999+1. \end{aligned}101001000​=9+1,=99+1,=999+1.​

Split each column that way and distribute:

3474=3(999+1)+4(99+1)+7(9+1)+4=2997+396+63+(3+4+7+4)=3456+18.\begin{aligned} 3474 &= 3(999+1)+4(99+1)\\ &\quad +7(9+1)+4\\ &= 2997+396+63\\ &\quad +(3+4+7+4)\\ &= 3456+18. \end{aligned}3474​=3(999+1)+4(99+1)+7(9+1)+4=2997+396+63+(3+4+7+4)=3456+18.​

The first part is 9×3849 \times 3849×384, so it divides evenly by 999. Only the digit sum, 181818, remains. It too divides evenly by 999, giving 3474=9×3863474=9\times3863474=9×386.

The same split works for any digits and any number of columns. A hundred contributes a group of 999999 and one leftover unit; a thousand contributes 999999999 and one; each larger place behaves the same way. Multiplying by the digit in that column leaves exactly that many units. All the other pieces are multiples of 999, so the whole number and its digit sum leave the same remainder on division by 999. Since every multiple of 999 is also a multiple of 333, the argument works for 333 too.

∎

A number is divisible by 333 or by 999 exactly when its digit sum is divisible by that divisor. You may add the digits again if the sum is still awkward: 9,9969{,}9969,996 gives 333333, then 666. So 9,9969{,}9969,996 passes the 333 test and fails the 999 test.

Check your understanding

Why does the digit sum test 642642642 for divisibility by both 333 and 999?

Answer choices

Splitting each place value leaves its digit and a multiple of 999.

642=630+(6+4+2)642 = 630 + (6+4+2)642=630+(6+4+2)

The 630630630 is divisible by both 999 and 333. The digit sum is 121212, so 642642642 passes the 333 test and fails the 999 test. The place values are one more than multiples of 999, not multiples themselves. Dropping digits or testing only the last digit does not preserve these tests.

Worked example 2 Is 4,7284{,}7284,728 divisible by 333? By 999?

Add the digits:

4+7+2+8=21.4 + 7 + 2 + 8 = 21.4+7+2+8=21.

Now test the digit sum 212121. It is divisible by 333, since 21=3×721 = 3 \times 721=3×7, but not by 999, since 9×2=189 \times 2 = 189×2=18 and 9×3=279 \times 3 = 279×3=27 step right over 212121.

If you cannot read 212121 at a glance, fold it once more: 2+1=32 + 1 = 32+1=3, which is divisible by 333 and not by 999, the same verdict. So 4,7284{,}7284,728 is divisible by 333 but not by 999.

This also explains why every multiple of 999 is automatically a multiple of 333. If the digit sum clears the higher bar of 999, it has certainly cleared 333, because 999 is itself a multiple of 333.

Check your understanding

Add the digits of 7,2547{,}2547,254 to test divisibility by 333 and 999. Which conclusion follows?

Answer choices

Add the digits first: 7+2+5+4=187+2+5+4=187+2+5+4=18. Test that sum against each divisor.

18=3×6=9×218 = 3 \times 6 = 9 \times 218=3×6=9×2

Both divisions leave remainder 000, so 7,2547{,}2547,254 is divisible by both 333 and 999. A multiple of 999 always passes the 333 test too.

A missing-digit question uses the same rule. To make 51□51\square51□ divisible by 999, start with 5+1=65+1=65+1=6. The missing digit must raise the sum to 999, so it is 333. The next multiple, 181818, would require a digit of 121212, which is impossible. Thus 513=9×57513=9\times57513=9×57 is the only answer.

Divisibility by 4 and 8: check the last two or three digits

For 444 and 888, keep a block of digits instead of adding them.

Why the last two digits decide divisibility by 444#

Split 7,3167{,}3167,316 into 7,300+167{,}300+167,300+16. Each hundred is 4×254\times254×25, so the 737373 hundreds divide evenly by 444. The leftover 16=4×416=4\times416=4×4 does too, so 7,3167{,}3167,316 passes the 444 test.

For 888, split the same number into 7,000+3167{,}000+3167,000+316. Each thousand is 8×1258\times1258×125, so only 316316316 needs testing. Since 316=8×39+4316=8\times39+4316=8×39+4, the number fails the 888 test.

For any whole number, the part above the last two digits is a whole number of hundreds, all divisible by 444. Thus the last two digits, read as one number, decide the 444 test. Similarly, the part above the last three digits is a whole number of thousands, all divisible by 888. The last three digits decide the 888 test. If the number has fewer digits than the rule asks for, test the whole number.

∎

  • Divisible by 444: the last two digits form a multiple of 444.
  • Divisible by 888: the last three digits form a multiple of 888.

Every hundred is divisible by 444, but 100=8×12+4100=8\times12+4100=8×12+4 is not divisible by 888. That is why the 888 test needs three digits: every thousand is divisible by 888.

Check your understanding

Why can you test 5,5365{,}5365,536 using 363636 for 444, but 536536536 for 888?

Answer choices

The discarded pieces must divide evenly by the divisor being tested.

5,500=4×1,3755{,}500=4\times1{,}3755,500=4×1,375

5,000=8×6255{,}000=8\times6255,000=8×625

That leaves 363636 for the 444 test and 536536536 for the 888 test. Discarding 5,5005{,}5005,500 for 888 would fail:

5,500=8×687+45{,}500=8\times687+45,500=8×687+4

So hundreds need not be multiples of 888, and passing 444 does not guarantee passing 888. Keeping only the last digit fails too: 101010 is a multiple of 101010 but passes neither 444 nor 888.

Worked example 3 Is 53,61653{,}61653,616 divisible by 444? By 888?

For 444, ignore everything but the last two digits, which form 161616:

16=4×4,16 = 4 \times 4,16=4×4,

so 53,61653{,}61653,616 is divisible by 444.

For 888, keep the last three digits, which form 616616616, and test that smaller number:

616=8×77,616 = 8 \times 77,616=8×77,

so 53,61653{,}61653,616 is divisible by 888 as well. You have replaced a five-digit division with a check on 616616616, and even that is quick: 8×70=5608 \times 70 = 5608×70=560, and the remaining 565656 is 8×78 \times 78×7.

The last two digits give:

Divisibility by 6: combine 2 and 3

A number is divisible by 666 exactly when it passes both the 222 and 333 tests. For example, 424242 is even and its digit sum is 666; indeed, 42=6×742=6\times742=6×7.

Why passing both the 222 test and the 333 test means divisible by 666#

Take 787878, which is even and divisible by 333. Write it as three equal rows of 262626 objects. Each row can be paired, since 26=2×1326=2\times1326=2×13. The three rows together contain 3×2×13=6×133\times2\times13=6\times133×2×13=6×13 objects.

For any multiple of 333, arrange the objects in three equal rows. If the row length is even, the rows split into pairs and the total is a multiple of 666. If the row length is odd, each row leaves one unpaired object; the three leftovers make one pair and one extra, so the total is odd. Thus an even multiple of 333 must have even row length and must be a multiple of 666. Conversely, every group of 666 splits into three twos and also into two threes, so every multiple of 666 passes both tests.

∎

Combining two divisor tests works when the divisors share no factor above 111, as 222 and 333 do. Passing the 222 test twice does not test 444: 666 passes both copies of the same test but is not divisible by 444. The tests have to supply the factors without counting a shared factor twice.

Check your understanding

Why must an even number that is divisible by 333 also be divisible by 666?

Answer choices

Divisibility by 333 gives three equal rows; it is the row length that must be even. Three odd-length rows leave three unpaired objects, hence an odd total. Since the total is even, each row has a whole number of pairs.

Three rows of pairs make groups of 666. For example, three rows of 141414 give 3×2×7=6×73\times2\times7=6\times73×2×7=6×7.

Combining two tests need not test the product of their divisors when the divisors share a factor. The number 666 passes the 222 test twice but is not divisible by 444.

Worked example 4 Is 2,5142{,}5142,514 divisible by 666?

Run the two tests that make up 666.

First, divisibility by 222: the last digit is 444, which is even, so 2,5142{,}5142,514 passes.

Next, divisibility by 333: the digit sum is

2+5+1+4=12,2 + 5 + 1 + 4 = 12,2+5+1+4=12,

and 12=3×412 = 3 \times 412=3×4 is a multiple of 333, so 2,5142{,}5142,514 passes that test too.

It clears both halves, so 2,5142{,}5142,514 is divisible by 666. As a check, 2,514=6×4192{,}514 = 6 \times 4192,514=6×419.

Which number is divisible by 666?

Answer choices

A number is divisible by 666 when it passes both the 222 test and the 333 test. That means an even last digit, and a digit sum that is a multiple of 333.

The last digit, 444, is even. Add the digits:

3+4+4+4=15=3×53+4+4+4=15=3\times53+4+4+4=15=3×5

So 3,4443{,}4443,444 clears both tests. Of the rest, 2,0132{,}0132,013 is odd, while 5,1025{,}1025,102 (digit sum 888) and 1,0401{,}0401,040 (digit sum 555) fail the 333 test.

Putting the rules to work

Together these tests are far quicker than long division. The rules pay off most when you stack several on one number to learn a lot in a single pass. Here is the whole toolkit:

Worked example 5 Which of 2,3,4,5,6,8,9,102, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 102,3,4,5,6,8,9,10 divide 7,3807{,}3807,380?

Run each test on 7,3807{,}3807,380 in turn.

The last digit is 000, so the number is even and divisible by 222, by 555, and by 101010 all at once.

The digit sum is 7+3+8+0=187 + 3 + 8 + 0 = 187+3+8+0=18, which is 9×29 \times 29×2, so the number is divisible by 333 and by 999.

Because it passes the 222 test and the 333 test, it is divisible by 666.

The last two digits form 808080, and 80=4×2080 = 4 \times 2080=4×20, so it is divisible by 444.

The last three digits form 380380380, and 380=(8×47)+4380 = (8 \times 47) + 4380=(8×47)+4, a remainder of 444, so 7,3807{,}3807,380 is not divisible by 888.

So 7,3807{,}3807,380 is divisible by every one of 2,3,4,5,6,9,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9,2,3,4,5,6,9, and 101010, but not by 888. Notice how one digit sum (181818) settled both the 333 and the 999 questions, the single trailing zero settled three tests by itself, and the two block tests for 444 and 888 gave different verdicts even though they look alike. The rules share their work, and each one still has to be checked on its own terms.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading the last digit for 333 or 999. The last-digit shortcut works only for 222, 555, and 101010. For 333 and 999 you must add all the digits; 323232 ends in an even digit yet is not divisible by 333.
  • Forgetting the remainder is the whole point. “Divisible” means the remainder is 000. Since 23÷523 \div 523÷5 gives 444 with 333 left over, 232323 is not divisible by 555, even though the division “almost” works.
  • Thinking “even” is enough for 444. A number can be even without being divisible by 444: both 141414 and 181818 are even, yet neither is divisible by 444. Check the last two digits as one number, using 100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25. (Dividing by 222 twice, with nothing left over each time, works too, but it is slower to do by hand on a large number.)
  • Combining rules that share a factor. “Divisible by 666” splits cleanly into 222 and 333 because they share no factor above 111. You cannot test 888 as ”222 and 444” or 121212 as ”222 and 666”; the shared factor gets counted twice. When unsure, fall back on the dedicated rule.
  • Stopping the digit sum too soon. If the digit sum is still large, like 484848, fold it again (4+8=124 + 8 = 124+8=12, then 1+2=31 + 2 = 31+2=3) until the answer is clear. A multiple of 333 stays a multiple of 333 at every fold.

Key takeaways

  • A whole number is divisible by a positive divisor ddd when the division leaves remainder 000. The same fact says that ddd is a factor of the number and that the number is a multiple of ddd.
  • Most rules come from splitting the number using place value: peel off a part that is obviously a multiple of ddd, and test only the small leftover. The rule for 666 instead combines two other rules.
  • The last digit decides 222 (even), 555 (000 or 555), and 101010 (000), because every column above the ones is a multiple of 101010.
  • The digit sum decides 333 and 999, because each of 10,100,1000,…10, 100, 1000, \ldots10,100,1000,… is one more than a multiple of 999. Each of those place values therefore leaves one copy of its own digit behind.
  • The last two or three digits decide 444 and 888, respectively: 100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25 and 1000=8×1251000 = 8 \times 1251000=8×125.
  • Divisibility by 666: combine the 222 and 333 tests, which is valid because 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3 and those factors share nothing above 111.

A bit of history (optional)

A merchant adds a long column of figures. How can the total be checked without repeating all that work?

One old answer was casting out nines: replace each number by its remainder after division by 999, using digit sums. Add the small leftovers, then compare that sum’s remainder with the claimed total’s remainder. A mismatch exposes an error. A match is not a guarantee, since an error of 999 or a multiple of 999 goes unnoticed.

Luca Pacioli, a Franciscan friar and mathematics teacher, included the method in his Summa, printed in Venice in 1494. He was passing on an existing practice: the printed Treviso Arithmetic had described it in 1478. MacTutor’s history of the term records both appearances.

Pacioli’s book became famous for another practical subject: the first printed account of double-entry bookkeeping. Merchants could read how to organize accounts as well as check arithmetic in the same volume. ICAEW’s account of Pacioli’s life describes how his teaching and writing connected mathematics with the counting house.

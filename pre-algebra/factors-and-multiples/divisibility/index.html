Divisibility Rules

Intuition

Is 48,213,76048{,}213{,}76048,213,760 divisible by 555? You do not have to divide anything. It ends in 000, so yes, and you knew that the moment your eye reached the last digit. That instant verdict is a divisibility rule. A divisibility rule is a quick test that tells you whether one number splits evenly into another, with no remainder, without doing the division. There is a rule for 222, one for 333, one for 555, and several more, and not one of them is a trick to memorize. Most of them come straight out of place value, the way our columns are built from tens; the rest, like the rule for 666, are built by combining two place-value rules. Once you see why a rule works, you can rebuild it any time you forget it.

Learning goals

  • Test for 222, 555 and 101010 by reading the last digit
  • Add the digits to settle 333 and 999
  • Check the last two or three digits for 444 and 888
  • Combine the 222 and 333 tests for 666, since they share no factor above 111
  • Explain why each rule works, from place value for most, and from combining two rules for 666
  • State what divisible means, and tie factor to multiple

What “divisible” means

One whole number is divisible by another when you can divide them and nothing is left over. The number 202020 is divisible by 555, because 20÷5=420 \div 5 = 420÷5=4 with a remainder of 000. The number 232323 is not divisible by 555, because 23÷5=423 \div 5 = 423÷5=4 with a remainder of 333; that leftover 333 is what disqualifies it. Divisibility is all about the remainder being zero, nothing else.

The same fact can be stated three ways, and reading them as interchangeable will save you confusion later:

  • 202020 is divisible by 555.
  • 555 is a factor (also called a divisor) of 202020.
  • 202020 is a multiple of 555.

Each sentence says that 202020 can be cut into a whole number of equal groups of 555, here four groups, leaving none behind.

The remainder is the only thing worth watching, so it is worth seeing rather than trusting. Below you choose how many dots there are and how many go in each row. Dots that cannot complete a row are drawn hollow, and those hollow dots are the remainder.

Set the count to 101010 and step the row width from 222 up to 666. Exactly two of those five widths leave nothing hollow, and each of the other three leaves a remainder you can count on the picture.

Check your understanding

Since 999 is a factor of 636363, which statement says the same thing a different way?

Answer choices

"999 is a factor of 636363", "636363 is divisible by 999", and "636363 is a multiple of 999" are three names for one fact: 63÷9=763 \div 9 = 763÷9=7 with remainder 000.

63=9×763 = 9 \times 763=9×7

The other two factor-or-multiple options have the direction backwards: 636363 is the larger number built from 999, so it is 636363 that is the multiple, never the smaller 999. And a remainder of 000 is exactly what makes 999 a factor in the first place, not something that rules it out.

The one idea behind most rules

There is a single move underneath all of these tests: break the number into pieces along its columns. Then throw away the pieces that are obviously multiples of your divisor, and test only what is left.

Try it on 282828 with a divisor of 444. Split 282828 into 20+820 + 820+8. Each piece is a multiple of 444 on its own, since 20=4×520 = 4 \times 520=4×5 and 8=4×28 = 4 \times 28=4×2. Push them back together and you are holding 555 fours alongside 222 fours, which is 777 fours in total. So 28=4×728 = 4 \times 728=4×7, and 444 divides it with nothing over.

The numbers 202020 and 888 did no special work there. Their counts of fours, 555 and 222, could simply be added because both pieces were measured in fours to begin with.

Watch that happen again with a divisor of 333. Both 181818 and 121212 are multiples of 333, since 181818 is six threes and 121212 is four threes. Put them together and you are holding six threes alongside four threes, which is ten threes. So 18+12=3018 + 12 = 3018+12=30 is a multiple of 333 as well. Take them apart instead, and six threes minus four threes leaves two threes. So 18−12=618 - 12 = 618−12=6 is a multiple of 333 too.

Nothing there depended on the numbers being 181818 and 121212. Two piles counted in the same unit can be added or subtracted just by adding or subtracting how many units each one holds. Write ddd for that shared unit, and mmm and nnn for the two counts. What you get is the distributive property you have already met, read in reverse:

(d×m)+(d×n)=d×(m+n),(d×m)−(d×n)=d×(m−n).(d \times m) + (d \times n) = d \times (m + n), \qquad (d \times m) - (d \times n) = d \times (m - n).(d×m)+(d×n)=d×(m+n),(d×m)−(d×n)=d×(m−n).

So a number that divides two others also divides their sum and their difference. Every rule for 222, 555, 101010, 333, 999, 444, and 888 below uses this once. We split the number into two parts: a big part that is plainly a multiple of ddd, contributing remainder 000, and a small leftover. Then the whole question rests on that leftover alone. The rule for 666 works differently: it does not split the number at all, and instead combines two of these rules, as the last section of the lesson shows.

Why does testing 836836836 for divisibility by 222, 555, and 101010 only require looking at the last digit, 666?

Answer choices

The whole idea is splitting off a part that is already known to divide evenly, so only the leftover has to be tested.

836=830+6,830=10×83836 = 830 + 6, \qquad 830 = 10 \times 83836=830+6,830=10×83

Because 830830830 is a multiple of 101010, and 10=2×510 = 2 \times 510=2×5, that part already leaves remainder 000 for 222, 555, and 101010 all at once. Only the leftover 666 can still change the verdict. That 666 happening to be even is a separate fact about this particular number, not the reason the shortcut works in general, and the digit count plays no role at all. The addition fact 830+6=836830 + 6 = 836830+6=836 does not by itself explain the shortcut either, since it says nothing about 830830830; the decisive fact is that 830830830, specifically, is a multiple of 222, 555, and 101010.

Divisibility by 2, 5, and 10: read the last digit

These three are the easiest tests, and they share one reason: every column except the ones is a multiple of 101010.

Take 836836836 again. The 800800800 and the 303030 combine into 830830830, which is 838383 tens, a clean multiple of 101010. And 101010 is 2×52 \times 52×5, so those 838383 tens are a multiple of 222 and a multiple of 555 as well. All three divisions therefore come out even on the 830830830 part alone. Only the 666 is still waiting to be judged.

Nothing in that split was special to 836836836. The tens, hundreds, thousands, and all columns to the left are made of tens, so any whole number, however long, can be written as

(a multiple of 10)+(the ones digit).(\text{a multiple of } 10) + (\text{the ones digit}).(a multiple of 10)+(the ones digit).

So whatever decides divisibility by 222, 555, or 101010 has to live in the last digit alone. That is because everything else was already a multiple of all three.

The reasoning above works for every whole number, not only 836836836: split off the ones digit, and everything left is always a multiple of 101010. You can read that argument written for any whole number in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

A number whose last digit is even is called an even number, and any other whole number is odd. So “even” and “divisible by 222” are two names for the same property, and you check both by glancing at the final digit.

Worked example 1 Test 4,1704{,}1704,170 for divisibility by 222, 555, and 101010

Everything depends on the ones digit, which is 000.

To see why the rest drops out, split off that last column:

4,170=(417×10)+0,4{,}170 = (417 \times 10) + 0,4,170=(417×10)+0,

where the 417×10417 \times 10417×10 piece is 417417417 tens, already a multiple of 101010. The leftover ones digit is 000, and 000 is a multiple of 222, of 555, and of 101010 at once.

So 4,1704{,}1704,170 is divisible by 222 (the digit 000 is even), by 555 (the digit is 000), and by 101010 (the digit is 000). Any number ending in 000 clears all three tests together.

Answer choices

Look only at each last digit. Divisible by 222 needs an even last digit; divisible by 555 needs a last digit of 000 or 555.

1,284→ends in 4  ⇒  even, and not 0 or 51{,}284 \to \text{ends in } 4 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{even, and not } 0 \text{ or } 51,284→ends in 4⇒even, and not 0 or 5

So 1,2841{,}2841,284 is divisible by 222 but not by 555. Both 3,7303{,}7303,730 and 2,0052{,}0052,005 are divisible by 555, and 6,9156{,}9156,915 ends in an odd digit, so it is not divisible by 222.

Divisibility by 3 and 9: add the digits

For 333 and 999 the last digit is useless: 121212 is divisible by 333 but 323232 is not, and both end in 222. The right test is the well-known one, add every digit and check that sum. The digits of 531531531 add to 5+3+1=95 + 3 + 1 = 95+3+1=9, which is divisible by both 333 and 999, so 531531531 is too. This looks like a coincidence, but it comes out of place value as neatly as the last-digit rules. You only have to notice one thing about the numbers 10,100,1000,10, 100, 1000,10,100,1000, and so on.

The key fact is that each of those place values is exactly one more than a multiple of 999:

10=9+1,100=99+1,1000=999+1,10 = 9 + 1, \qquad 100 = 99 + 1, \qquad 1000 = 999 + 1,10=9+1,100=99+1,1000=999+1,

and 999, 999999, and 999999999 are all multiples of 999 (and of 333). So suppose a digit ddd sits in the tens column. That digit’s contribution d×10d \times 10d×10 breaks into d×9d \times 9d×9, which is a multiple of 999, plus a single leftover ddd. Every column behaves the same way: it donates a multiple of 999 and leaves one copy of its own digit. Sweep up all those leftovers and you have the digit sum.

The reasoning above works for a number of any length, because every place value from the tens up is one more than a multiple of 999. You can read that argument written for any whole number in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

If the digit sum is still large, add its digits again, and repeat until the total is small enough to read at a glance. For 9,9969{,}9969,996 the digits give 9+9+9+6=339 + 9 + 9 + 6 = 339+9+9+6=33, and adding once more gives 3+3=63 + 3 = 63+3=6. Because 666 is divisible by 333 but not by 999, the original 9,9969{,}9969,996 is divisible by 333 but not by 999.

Why does testing 531531531 for divisibility by 999 come down to just adding its digits, 5+3+15 + 3 + 15+3+1?

Answer choices

The whole idea is splitting off a part that is already known to be a multiple of 999, so only the leftover has to be tested.

531=(5×99+3×9)⏟a multiple of 9+(5+3+1)⏟digit sum531 = \underbrace{(5 \times 99 + 3 \times 9)}_{\text{a multiple of } 9} + \underbrace{(5 + 3 + 1)}_{\text{digit sum}}531=a multiple of 9(5×99+3×9)​​+digit sum(5+3+1)​​

Each place value (100=99+1100 = 99 + 1100=99+1, 10=9+110 = 9 + 110=9+1) hands over a multiple of 999 and leaves behind one copy of its own digit, so the first group is a multiple of 999 no matter what the digits are. Only the digit sum can still change the verdict. The digit count is a fact about this number's length, not the reason the shortcut works in general, and 5+3+15 + 3 + 15+3+1 landing exactly on 999 is special to this number, not the mechanism itself; the shortcut replaces division exactly, it does not merely go faster than it.

Worked example 2 Is 4,7284{,}7284,728 divisible by 333? By 999?

Add the digits:

4+7+2+8=21.4 + 7 + 2 + 8 = 21.4+7+2+8=21.

Now test the digit sum 212121. It is divisible by 333, since 21=3×721 = 3 \times 721=3×7, but not by 999, since 9×2=189 \times 2 = 189×2=18 and 9×3=279 \times 3 = 279×3=27 step right over 212121.

If you cannot read 212121 at a glance, fold it once more: 2+1=32 + 1 = 32+1=3, which is divisible by 333 and not by 999, the same verdict. So 4,7284{,}7284,728 is divisible by 333 but not by 999.

This also explains why every multiple of 999 is automatically a multiple of 333. If the digit sum clears the higher bar of 999, it has certainly cleared 333, because 999 is itself a multiple of 333.

Divisibility by 4 and 8: check the last two or three digits

The rule for 333 used digit sums, but 444 goes back to the splitting idea, just one column deeper. The fact we need is that 100100100 is a multiple of 444, since 100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25.

Take 1,7321{,}7321,732 and cut it at that point, into 1,700+321{,}700 + 321,700+32. The 1,7001{,}7001,700 piece is 171717 hundreds, and every hundred is 4×254 \times 254×25, so those 171717 hundreds come to 4×4254 \times 4254×425 exactly. That piece leaves nothing over. The whole question drops onto the 323232, and 32=4×832 = 4 \times 832=4×8, so 1,7321{,}7321,732 is divisible by 444.

The same cut works on any number, because the hundreds, thousands, and higher part always counts a whole number of hundreds:

N=(a multiple of 100)+(the last two digits).N = (\text{a multiple of } 100) + (\text{the last two digits}).N=(a multiple of 100)+(the last two digits).

That part is a multiple of 100100100, and therefore of 444, so it contributes no remainder. So divisibility by 444 depends only on the number made by the last two digits.

The very same reasoning, pushed out one more column, gives the rule for 888. Here 100010001000 is a multiple of 888, because 1000=8×1251000 = 8 \times 1251000=8×125, so everything from the thousands column up is a multiple of 888. That is why divisibility by 888 depends only on the last three digits.

The reasoning above works for any whole number, because the columns above the hundreds (or the thousands) always count a whole number of hundreds (or thousands). You can read that argument written for any whole number in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Worked example 3 Is 53,61653{,}61653,616 divisible by 444? By 888?

16=4×4,16 = 4 \times 4,16=4×4,

so 53,61653{,}61653,616 is divisible by 444.

Divisibility by 6: combine 2 and 3

Some numbers carry no rule of their own. Instead you build a test from the rules for their factors. The tidiest example is 666.

Watch it happen on 424242. The last digit is even, so 222 divides it, and 42=2×2142 = 2 \times 2142=2×21. The digit sum is 4+2=64 + 2 = 64+2=6, so 333 divides 424242 as well. Now look at the 212121 sitting beside that 222. It is 3×73 \times 73×7, so the factor of 333 was waiting inside the other half all along. Collecting the pieces gives 42=2×3×7=6×742 = 2 \times 3 \times 7 = 6 \times 742=2×3×7=6×7.

Because 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3, a number is divisible by 666 exactly when it is divisible by both 222 and 333. You already own both tests, so you just run them together. Confirm that the last digit is even and that the digit sum is a multiple of 333.

Here is why passing both tests is always enough, without any letters. Look at the multiples of 333 in order, starting from 000: 0,3,6,9,12,15,18,21,24,…0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, \ldots0,3,6,9,12,15,18,21,24,…. They alternate even, odd, even, odd, forever, because adding 333 to an even number gives an odd one and adding 333 to an odd number gives an even one. So exactly every other multiple of 333 is even, and those are exactly the numbers passing both the 222 test and the 333 test at once. But the even multiples of 333 are exactly the multiples of 666: 0,6,12,18,24,…0, 6, 12, 18, 24, \ldots0,6,12,18,24,…. Passing both tests always lands you on one of those.

The alternating pattern above is the whole reason, said with numbers. The same idea also has an algebraic form, working directly from what “divisible by 333” and “divisible by 222” mean, which you can read in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the full proof

This combining trick works whenever the two factors share no common factor bigger than 111, as 222 and 333 do not. It does not work to build a test for 444 by running the 222 test twice over, because there the two factors are both 222 rather than different numbers, and passing the 222 test once already guarantees passing it again. For 666 the split into a 222 and a 333 is clean, so the combined test is exactly right.

Why does passing both the 222 test and the 333 test guarantee divisibility by 666, rather than just make it likely?

Answer choices

Passing both tests is not a coincidence, it is forced.

0,3,6,9,12,15,18,21,24,…0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, \ldots0,3,6,9,12,15,18,21,24,…

Adding 333 to an even number gives an odd one, and adding 333 to an odd number gives an even one, so the multiples of 333 alternate even and odd forever. Exactly every other one is even, and those even multiples of 333 are exactly 0,6,12,18,24,…0, 6, 12, 18, 24, \ldots0,6,12,18,24,…, the multiples of 666. So any number that clears both tests has to be one of them. There is no operation that 'adds' two divisibility tests together; plenty of multiples of 333 are odd, like 999 and 151515, so being divisible by 333 never by itself guarantees being divisible by 222; and 666 being the larger number is just arithmetic, not a reason the combined test is exact.

Worked example 4 Is 2,5142{,}5142,514 divisible by 666?

Run the two tests that make up 666.

First, divisibility by 222: the last digit is 444, which is even, so 2,5142{,}5142,514 passes.

Next, divisibility by 333: the digit sum is

2+5+1+4=12,2 + 5 + 1 + 4 = 12,2+5+1+4=12,

and 12=3×412 = 3 \times 412=3×4 is a multiple of 333, so 2,5142{,}5142,514 passes that test too.

It clears both halves, so 2,5142{,}5142,514 is divisible by 666. As a check, 2,514=6×4192{,}514 = 6 \times 4192,514=6×419.

Worked example 5 Which of 2,3,4,5,6,8,9,102, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 102,3,4,5,6,8,9,10 divide 7,3807{,}3807,380?

Run each test on 7,3807{,}3807,380 in turn.

The last digit is 000, so the number is even and divisible by 222, by 555, and by 101010 all at once.

The digit sum is 7+3+8+0=187 + 3 + 8 + 0 = 187+3+8+0=18, which is 9×29 \times 29×2, so the number is divisible by 333 and by 999.

Because it passes the 222 test and the 333 test, it is divisible by 666.

The last two digits form 808080, and 80=4×2080 = 4 \times 2080=4×20, so it is divisible by 444.

The last three digits form 380380380, and 380=(8×47)+4380 = (8 \times 47) + 4380=(8×47)+4, a remainder of 444, so 7,3807{,}3807,380 is not divisible by 888.

So 7,3807{,}3807,380 is divisible by every one of 2,3,4,5,6,9,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9,2,3,4,5,6,9, and 101010, but not by 888. Notice how one digit sum (181818) settled both the 333 and the 999 questions, the single trailing zero settled three tests by itself, and the two block tests for 444 and 888 gave different verdicts even though they look alike. The rules share their work, and each one still has to be checked on its own terms.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading the last digit for 333 or 999. The last-digit shortcut works only for 222, 555, and 101010. For 333 and 999 you must add all the digits; 323232 ends in an even digit yet is not divisible by 333.
  • Forgetting the remainder is the whole point. “Divisible” means the remainder is 000. Since 23÷523 \div 523÷5 gives 444 with 333 left over, 232323 is not divisible by 555, even though the division “almost” works.
  • Thinking “even” is enough for 444. A number can be even without being divisible by 444: both 141414 and 181818 are even, yet neither is divisible by 444. Check the last two digits as one number, using 100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25. (Dividing by 222 twice, with nothing left over each time, works too, but it is slower to do by hand on a large number.)
  • Combining rules that share a factor. “Divisible by 666” splits cleanly into 222 and 333 because they share no factor above 111. You cannot test 888 as ”222 and 444” or 121212 as ”222 and 666”; the shared factor gets counted twice. When unsure, fall back on the dedicated rule.
  • Stopping the digit sum too soon. If the digit sum is still large, like 484848, fold it again (4+8=124 + 8 = 124+8=12, then 1+2=31 + 2 = 31+2=3) until the answer is clear. A multiple of 333 stays a multiple of 333 at every fold.

Key takeaways

  • A number is divisible by ddd when the division leaves remainder 000. The same fact says that ddd is a factor of the number and that the number is a multiple of ddd.
  • Most rules come from splitting the number using place value: peel off a part that is obviously a multiple of ddd, and test only the small leftover. The rule for 666 instead combines two other rules.
  • The last digit decides 222 (even), 555 (000 or 555), and 101010 (000), because every column above the ones is a multiple of 101010.
  • The digit sum decides 333 and 999, because each of 10,100,1000,…10, 100, 1000, \ldots10,100,1000,… is one more than a multiple of 999. Each of those place values therefore leaves one copy of its own digit behind.
  • The last two digits decide 444 (100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25), and the last three digits decide 888 (1000=8×1251000 = 8 \times 1251000=8×125).
  • For 666, combine the 222 and 333 tests, which is valid because 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3 and those factors share nothing above 111.

Why the last digit is enough for 222, 555, and 101010

The lesson showed the reasoning on 836836836: everything above the ones column is a multiple of 101010, so it cannot change the verdict for 222, 555, or 101010. Here is that same argument run again on 574574574, and then written so it covers every whole number at once.

Why only the last digit matters for 222, 555, and 101010#

Run the argument on 574574574 before running it on letters. Peel the last digit away and you have 574=570+4574 = 570 + 4574=570+4. That 570570570 is 575757 tens, so it equals 10×5710 \times 5710×57. And 101010 is 2×52 \times 52×5, so the same piece is a multiple of 222 and a multiple of 555. All three divisions therefore leave nothing over on the 570570570, and each verdict rests on the 444 alone. The 444 is even, so 574574574 is divisible by 222, and it is neither 000 nor 555, so the test for 555 fails. It is not 000 either, so 574574574 is not a multiple of 101010.

Nothing there used the particular digits of 574574574. Write any whole number as N=T+uN = T + uN=T+u, where uuu is its ones digit and TTT is everything else. That leftover TTT is built from the tens column and up, so it counts a whole number of tens: T=10×kT = 10 \times kT=10×k for some whole number kkk.

Since 10=2×510 = 2 \times 510=2×5, that piece is a multiple of 101010, of 555 and of 222 at once. So it leaves remainder 000 in all three tests. By the splitting idea from the last section, N=T+uN = T + uN=T+u passes one of those tests exactly when the leftover digit uuu passes it:

  • Divisible by 101010: the ones digit must be 000, the only single digit that is a multiple of 101010.
  • Divisible by 555: the ones digit must be 000 or 555, the single-digit multiples of 555.
  • Divisible by 222: the ones digit must be 0,2,4,6,0, 2, 4, 6,0,2,4,6, or 888, an even digit.

Any other last digit leaves a nonzero remainder, so the number fails.

∎

Why the digit-sum trick works for every whole number

The lesson showed the reasoning on 531531531: every place value is one more than a multiple of 999, so each column hands over a multiple of 999 and keeps only its own digit. Here is that same argument run again on 347434743474, and then carried all the way to a number of any length.

Why the digit sum decides divisibility by 999 (and by 333)#

Work it on 347434743474 first. Written out column by column, that number is

3474=(3×1000)+(4×100)+(7×10)+4.3474 = (3 \times 1000) + (4 \times 100) + (7 \times 10) + 4.3474=(3×1000)+(4×100)+(7×10)+4.

Swap each place value for a multiple of 999 plus 111, using 1000=999+11000 = 999 + 11000=999+1, 100=99+1100 = 99 + 1100=99+1 and 10=9+110 = 9 + 110=9+1:

3474=3×(999+1)+4×(99+1)+7×(9+1)+4.3474 = 3 \times (999 + 1) + 4 \times (99 + 1) + 7 \times (9 + 1) + 4.3474=3×(999+1)+4×(99+1)+7×(9+1)+4.

Multiply out, then sort the pieces into the multiples of 999 and the bare digits:

3474=(3×999)+(4×99)+(7×9)⏟a multiple of 9+3+4+7+4⏟the digit sum.3474 = \underbrace{(3 \times 999) + (4 \times 99) + (7 \times 9)}_{\text{a multiple of } 9} + \underbrace{3 + 4 + 7 + 4}_{\text{the digit sum}}.3474=a multiple of 9(3×999)+(4×99)+(7×9)​​+the digit sum3+4+7+4​​.

Every column has handed over a multiple of 999 and kept only its own digit. The first group comes to 2997+396+63=34562997 + 396 + 63 = 34562997+396+63=3456, which is 9×3849 \times 3849×384, so it leaves remainder 000. The question lands on the leftover 3+4+7+4=183 + 4 + 7 + 4 = 183+4+7+4=18, which is 9×29 \times 29×2. So 347434743474 is divisible by 999, and dividing confirms it: 3474=9×3863474 = 9 \times 3863474=9×386.

Nothing there depended on the digits being 3,4,73, 4, 73,4,7 and 444, so now use letters in their place. Take a four-digit number with digits aaa, bbb, ccc and ddd, from the thousands column down. Its value is

N=(a×1000)+(b×100)+(c×10)+d.N = (a \times 1000) + (b \times 100) + (c \times 10) + d.N=(a×1000)+(b×100)+(c×10)+d.

Swap each place value for “a multiple of 999, plus 111”, exactly as before:

N=a×(999+1)+b×(99+1)+c×(9+1)+d.N = a \times (999 + 1) + b \times (99 + 1) + c \times (9 + 1) + d.N=a×(999+1)+b×(99+1)+c×(9+1)+d.

Multiply out, then sort the multiples of 999 to one side and the bare digits to the other:

N=(a×999)+(b×99)+(c×9)⏟a multiple of 9+(a+b+c+d)⏟the digit sum.N = \underbrace{(a \times 999) + (b \times 99) + (c \times 9)}_{\text{a multiple of } 9} + \underbrace{(a + b + c + d)}_{\text{the digit sum}}.N=a multiple of 9(a×999)+(b×99)+(c×9)​​+the digit sum(a+b+c+d)​​.

The first group is a multiple of 999 whatever the digits are, so it leaves remainder 000. By the splitting idea, NNN is divisible by 999 exactly when the leftover a+b+c+da + b + c + da+b+c+d, its digit sum, is.

For 333, note that 9=3×39 = 3 \times 39=3×3, so each of those 999s, 999999s and 999999999s is a multiple of 333 as well. The same argument then shows that NNN is divisible by 333 exactly when its digit sum is. A shorter number simply drops terms and a longer one adds more, namely 9,9999{,}9999,999 and so on. Each is still a multiple of 999, so the rule holds at any length.

∎

Why 100100100 and 100010001000 do the work for 444 and 888

The lesson showed the reasoning on 1,7321{,}7321,732: the hundreds column and up always counts a whole number of hundreds, which is why only the last two (or three) digits matter. Here is that same argument run again on 7,3167{,}3167,316, and then written for any whole number.

Why the last two digits decide divisibility by 444#

Take 7,3167{,}3167,316 first. Splitting off the last two digits gives 7,316=7,300+167{,}316 = 7{,}300 + 167,316=7,300+16. That 7,3007{,}3007,300 is 737373 hundreds, and a hundred is 4×254 \times 254×25, so the piece equals 4×1,8254 \times 1{,}8254×1,825 and leaves nothing over. Only the 161616 is left to judge, and 16=4×416 = 4 \times 416=4×4, so 7,3167{,}3167,316 is divisible by 444. Dividing confirms it: 7,316=4×1,8297{,}316 = 4 \times 1{,}8297,316=4×1,829.

Now cut the same number one column further out, for 888. That gives 7,316=7,000+3167{,}316 = 7{,}000 + 3167,316=7,000+316, and the 7,0007{,}0007,000 is 777 thousands, each of them 8×1258 \times 1258×125, so that piece is 8×8758 \times 8758×875 and again leaves nothing. Everything rests on the 316316316, and 316=8×39316 = 8 \times 39316=8×39 with 444 left over. So 7,3167{,}3167,316 is not divisible by 888, even though it passed for 444.

The 737373, the 161616 and the 316316316 played no special part, so run it again with letters.

Write N=H+LN = H + LN=H+L, where LLL is the number formed by the tens and ones digits, the last two. The other piece HHH is everything from the hundreds column up. Because HHH is built only from hundreds and higher columns, it counts a whole number of hundreds. So H=100×mH = 100 \times mH=100×m for some whole number mmm.

Since 100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25, regroup it as H=100×m=4×(25×m)H = 100 \times m = 4 \times (25 \times m)H=100×m=4×(25×m). So HHH is a multiple of 444 whatever mmm is, and it leaves remainder 000. By the splitting idea, NNN is divisible by 444 exactly when the two-digit leftover LLL is. Testing LLL is fast, because it never exceeds 999999.

Stepping one column further out proves the rule for 888 in exactly the same way. Now 1000=8×1251000 = 8 \times 1251000=8×125, so the part of NNN from the thousands column up is a multiple of 888. Only the last three digits remain to be checked.

∎

Why passing the 222 and 333 tests always gives 666

The lesson already gave the reason using the alternating pattern of multiples of 333. Here is the same fact reached a second way, working directly from what “divisible by 333” and “divisible by 222” mean, first on 787878 and then for any whole number that passes both tests.

Why passing both the 222 test and the 333 test means divisible by 666#

Watch it happen on 787878, which passes both tests: it ends in 888, and its digits add to 151515. Passing the 333 test means 78=3×2678 = 3 \times 2678=3×26. Now look at the partner 262626. Since 787878 is even and the multiplier 333 is odd, that evenness has to be coming from the 262626, and indeed 26=2×1326 = 2 \times 1326=2×13. Substituting it back gives 78=3×(2×13)=6×1378 = 3 \times (2 \times 13) = 6 \times 1378=3×(2×13)=6×13.

Now the same steps in general. Suppose a number NNN is divisible by 333. Then N=3×qN = 3 \times qN=3×q for some whole number qqq. Suppose as well that NNN is divisible by 222, so NNN is even.

Look again at N=3×qN = 3 \times qN=3×q. The multiplier 333 is odd, so the only way that product comes out even is for qqq to be even. That means q=2×rq = 2 \times rq=2×r for some whole number rrr, and substituting gives

N=3×q=3×(2×r)=6×r,N = 3 \times q = 3 \times (2 \times r) = 6 \times r,N=3×q=3×(2×r)=6×r,

a multiple of 666. The argument leaned on 222 and 333 sharing no common factor above 111. That independence is what forced the extra factor of 222 to hide inside qqq, rather than overlapping the 333.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

A merchant adds a long column of figures. How does he know the total is right?

For centuries the cheap answer was this lesson’s digit sum. The trick was called casting out nines. You replace each number by the sum of its digits, throwing away any nines as you go. Then you redo the small sum on those leftovers alone. If the two results disagree, the total is definitely wrong.

Luca Pacioli, a friar who taught arithmetic in Venice, printed the method in 1494. His book is remembered for something else: it gave Europe its first printed account of double-entry bookkeeping. So the same volume that taught merchants to keep accounts also taught them to check accounts.

The check is not perfect. Two wrong answers can agree by luck, and it stays silent then. But it costs seconds, and a disagreement is proof of a slip. It works for one reason: a number and its digit sum leave the same remainder under nine, the fact you proved here.

    1. Reading the last digit . Foundational, 11 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    Some divisibility tests read one digit and stop, while others need every digit in the numeral. The parts below cut a numeral apart at the ones column and ask how much of the work that single cut can be trusted to do.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write 8,2568{,}2568,256 as a whole number of tens plus its ones digit, giving both pieces. Then say, for each of 222, 555 and 101010, whether that divisor goes into 8,2568{,}2568,256 with nothing left over.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A four-digit number is written 2,75□2{,}75\square2,75□, where the blank holds a single digit. List every digit that makes it divisible by 222, then every digit that makes it divisible by 555, and then every digit that does both at once.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why a test for 222, for 555 or for 101010 can safely ignore every digit except the last, and then explain why the same argument gives no last-digit test for 333.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every column to the left of the ones is made of tens, so ask first what a whole number of tens is automatically a multiple of, before you look at any digit at all.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Build the two lists separately, one test at a time, and only then look for the digits that turn up on both of them.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Try running the same opening cut with a divisor of 333 and watch which step refuses to work. A pair of numbers ending in the same digit will show you what has gone missing.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    8,256=(825×10)+68{,}256 = (825 \times 10) + 68,256=(825×10)+6. It is divisible by 222, and not by 555 or by 101010.

    Part B

    Divisible by 222: 0,2,4,6,80, 2, 4, 6, 80,2,4,6,8. Divisible by 555: 000 or 555. Both at once: only 000, which gives 2,7502{,}7502,750.

    Part C

    Every column above the ones holds a whole number of tens, and 101010 is a multiple of 222, of 555 and of itself, so that part leaves remainder 000 and only the ones digit can decide. The argument fails at its first step for 333, since 101010 is not a multiple of 333.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Everything above the ones column counts a whole number of tens. In 8,2568{,}2568,256 that part is 8,2508{,}2508,250, which is 825825825 tens, so the cut gives

    8,256=(825×10)+6.8{,}256 = (825 \times 10) + 6.8,256=(825×10)+6.

    Because 10=2×510 = 2 \times 510=2×5, the piece 825×10825 \times 10825×10 is a multiple of 101010, a multiple of 555 and a multiple of 222 all at once, so in each of those three tests it has already passed and contributes a remainder of 000. Only the leftover 666 can still decide anything.

    The digit 666 is even, so 8,2568{,}2568,256 is divisible by 222. It is neither 000 nor 555, so 8,2568{,}2568,256 is not divisible by 555. It is not 000, so 8,2568{,}2568,256 is not divisible by 101010.

    Checking by division: 8,256÷2=4,1288{,}256 \div 2 = 4{,}1288,256÷2=4,128 exactly, while 8,256÷58{,}256 \div 58,256÷5 gives 1,6511{,}6511,651 with a remainder of 111. That leftover 111 is what the last digit predicted, and it is the whole reason the 555 verdict is a no.

    Part B

    Call the missing digit uuu. The three digits in front of it make 275275275 tens, so the number is

    (275×10)+u.(275 \times 10) + u.(275×10)+u.

    That first piece is a whole number of tens, hence a multiple of 222, of 555 and of 101010 at the same time, and it contributes remainder 000 to all three tests. So uuu alone settles every one of them, and the digits 222, 777 and 555 never enter the question.

    For divisibility by 222 the leftover digit must be even, so uuu is one of 0,2,4,6,80, 2, 4, 6, 80,2,4,6,8.

    For divisibility by 555 the leftover digit must itself be a multiple of 555, and the only single digits that are, are 000 and 555.

    A digit that does both jobs has to appear on both lists. Of 000 and 555, only 000 is even, so u=0u = 0u=0 is the single answer, and the number it builds is 2,750=2×1,375=5×5502{,}750 = 2 \times 1{,}375 = 5 \times 5502,750=2×1,375=5×550. Notice that this is also the only digit that passes the 101010 test, which is another way of saying that a number divisible by both 222 and 555 is divisible by 101010.

    Part C

    Write any whole number as N=T+uN = T + uN=T+u, where uuu is the ones digit and TTT is everything to the left of it. Because TTT is built only from the tens, hundreds, thousands and higher columns, it counts a whole number of tens:

    T=10×kfor some whole number k.T = 10 \times k \quad \text{for some whole number } k.T=10×kfor some whole number k.

    Now use 10=2×510 = 2 \times 510=2×5. That one equation makes TTT a multiple of 101010, a multiple of 555 (it is 5×2k5 \times 2k5×2k) and a multiple of 222 (it is 2×5k2 \times 5k2×5k), whatever kkk happens to be. So in all three tests the entire piece TTT has already passed, contributing a remainder of 000, and the only thing that can still move a verdict is uuu. That is why the test reads one digit, and why the length of the number makes no difference to how long the test takes.

    Run the same opening move for 333 and it stalls at once. We would need T=10×kT = 10 \times kT=10×k to be a multiple of 333 for every kkk, and it is not, because 101010 is not a multiple of 333. The tens part keeps a leftover of its own, and how big that leftover is depends on kkk, which is to say on the digits we were trying to ignore.

    One pair of numbers shows the damage. Both 212121 and 414141 end in the same digit, yet

    21=3×7,41=(3×13)+2.21 = 3 \times 7, \qquad 41 = (3 \times 13) + 2.21=3×7,41=(3×13)+2.

    The first is divisible by 333 and the second is not. A rule that looks only at the final digit cannot possibly separate them, so no last-digit test for 333 can exist. The tens have to be dealt with rather than discarded, and dealing with them is exactly what the digit-sum rule does.

    In one line

    8,256=(825×10)+68{,}256 = (825 \times 10) + 68,256=(825×10)+6, so it is divisible by 222 but not by 555 or by 101010; the blank in 2,75□2{,}75\square2,75□ accepts 0,2,4,6,80, 2, 4, 6, 80,2,4,6,8 for the 222 test and 000 or 555 for the 555 test, so only 000 passes both; and the last digit settles 222, 555 and 101010 because everything above it is a multiple of 10=2×510 = 2 \times 510=2×5, which is precisely the step that fails for 333.

    2. Adding the digits . Foundational, 13 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 13 pts

    For 333 and 999 the final digit tells you nothing, so the test collects a contribution from every column instead. Adding the digits is that collection, and the small number it produces is then tested in place of the large one.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Compute the digit sum of 8,7698{,}7698,769. Then use that sum, and nothing else, to decide whether 8,7698{,}7698,769 is divisible by 333 and whether it is divisible by 999.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      The four-digit number 4,□154{,}\square 154,□15 has one digit missing. Find every digit that can fill the blank so that the number is divisible by 999, and every digit that fills it so that the number is divisible by 333.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A student says: "If a number passes the 999 test then it passes the 333 test, and if it passes the 333 test then it passes the 999 test, because both tests read the very same digit sum." Decide whether each half of that statement holds, and justify each decision from what the digit sum is being tested against.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      A digit sum is not the answer to anything, it is a smaller number to interrogate. Whatever you would have asked of the original number, ask it of the sum instead.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Name the missing digit and write the sum as an expression in it. A single digit can only be 000 through 999, which pins that sum inside a stretch of ten consecutive values you can search by hand.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Two divisors where one is a multiple of the other do not behave symmetrically. Try building a digit sum that clears the lower bar and misses the higher one, then find any number carrying that sum.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The digit sum is 303030. It is a multiple of 333 but not of 999, so 8,7698{,}7698,769 is divisible by 333 and not by 999.

    Part B

    Only 888 makes it divisible by 999, giving 4,8154{,}8154,815. For divisibility by 333 the blank can be 222, 555 or 888.

    Part C

    The first half holds and the second does not. The two tests do read one sum, but they test it against different divisors, and a sum can be a multiple of 333 without being a multiple of 999.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Add every digit, not just the last one:

    8+7+6+9=30.8 + 7 + 6 + 9 = 30.8+7+6+9=30.

    Now put the sum itself on trial. It is a multiple of 333, since 30=3×1030 = 3 \times 1030=3×10, so 8,7698{,}7698,769 is divisible by 333. It is not a multiple of 999: the multiples of 999 run 272727, then 363636, stepping straight over 303030. So 8,7698{,}7698,769 is not divisible by 999.

    If 303030 is still awkward to read, fold it once more, which is allowed because the rule applies to the sum exactly as it applied to the original: 3+0=33 + 0 = 33+0=3, a multiple of 333 and not of 999, the same pair of verdicts.

    Checking by division, 8,769=3×2,9238{,}769 = 3 \times 2{,}9238,769=3×2,923, while dividing by 999 leaves a remainder of 333. That remainder is the folded digit sum, which is not a coincidence and is the subject of the last question in this set.

    Part B

    Call the missing digit bbb. The three known digits add to

    4+1+5=10,4 + 1 + 5 = 10,4+1+5=10,

    so the digit sum of the whole number is 10+b10 + b10+b. Writing it that way, with the unknown still in it, is what makes the search finite: a single digit runs from 000 to 999, so 10+b10 + b10+b can only land somewhere between 101010 and 191919.

    For the 999 test, 10+b10 + b10+b must be a multiple of 999. The only multiple of 999 between 101010 and 191919 is 181818, so

    10+b=18  ⇒  b=8.10 + b = 18 \;\Rightarrow\; b = 8.10+b=18⇒b=8.

    That builds 4,8154{,}8154,815, and indeed 4,815=9×5354{,}815 = 9 \times 5354,815=9×535.

    For the 333 test, 10+b10 + b10+b must be a multiple of 333. Between 101010 and 191919 those are 121212, 151515 and 181818, which ask for b=2b = 2b=2, b=5b = 5b=5 and b=8b = 8b=8 in turn.

    Three digits clear the 333 test where one clears the 999 test, and the spacing explains it: multiples of 333 sit three apart and multiples of 999 nine apart, so a run of ten consecutive sums meets three or four of the first and one or two of the second.

    Part C

    The student's premise is correct: both tests start from the same digit sum. What the argument misses is that the sum is then compared against two different divisors, and those two divisors do not sit symmetrically.

    The first half. Suppose the digit sum is a multiple of 999, so it is 9×m9 \times m9×m for some whole number mmm. Since 9=3×39 = 3 \times 39=3×3,

    9×m=3×(3m),9 \times m = 3 \times (3m),9×m=3×(3m),

    which is a multiple of 333. So a digit sum that clears the 999 test clears the 333 test automatically, and a number that passes the 999 test passes the 333 test. That half holds for every whole number, with no exception to look for.

    The second half. One number retires it. The digits of 1,5271{,}5271,527 add to

    1+5+2+7=15,1 + 5 + 2 + 7 = 15,1+5+2+7=15,

    which is a multiple of 333, so 1,5271{,}5271,527 passes the 333 test, and 1,527=3×5091{,}527 = 3 \times 5091,527=3×509. But 151515 is not a multiple of 999, so 1,5271{,}5271,527 fails the 999 test: dividing it by 999 leaves a remainder of 666, which is exactly the remainder 151515 itself leaves.

    So the implication runs one way only. Passing the harder test guarantees the easier one, because 999 is itself a multiple of 333. Passing the easier one guarantees nothing about the harder, because 333 is not a multiple of 999.

    In one line

    The digit sum of 8,7698{,}7698,769 is 303030, so the number is divisible by 333 and not by 999; the blank of 4,□154{,}\square 154,□15 must be 888 for divisibility by 999, while 222, 555 or 888 all give divisibility by 333; and only the first half of the student's statement holds, since 999 is a multiple of 333 but 333 is not a multiple of 999, as 1,5271{,}5271,527 shows.

    3. Chairs in equal rows . Application, 13 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 13 pts

    A conference hall keeps 5,9765{,}9765,976 folding chairs in storage. The crew sets them out in identical rows, and a row size only works if every chair ends up in a full row with none left standing.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Decide whether rows of 444 work and whether rows of 888 work, testing only the block of digits each rule actually needs. Say which block you used in each case, and how many rows any size that works would give.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      The crew also considers rows of 555, rows of 666 and rows of 999. Test all three and report which of those sizes would leave chairs over and which would not.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Suppose some count of chairs passes both the 888 test and the 999 test. Explain what that pair of results tells you about setting those chairs out in rows of 727272, and explain why the same style of argument would not settle rows of 888 for you by running the 222 test and the 444 test.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Each divisor here has a rule attached to one particular slice of the numeral: a single digit, the last two, the last three, or all of them added together. Decide which slice a rule needs before computing anything.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      One of the three sizes has no rule of its own. Write it as a product of two smaller numbers you do have rules for, and check that those two share no factor above 111 before you trust the pair.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Write the count as the first divisor times something, then ask whether the second divisor's factor is forced to live in that something. Try the same sentence on the second pair and watch the force disappear.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Both work. The last two digits give 76=4×1976 = 4 \times 1976=4×19, and the last three give 976=8×122976 = 8 \times 122976=8×122, so there would be 1,4941{,}4941,494 rows of 444 or 747747747 rows of 888.

    Part B

    Rows of 555 would leave a chair over; rows of 666 and rows of 999 would not. The count ends in 666, which is neither 000 nor 555, and its digit sum is 272727.

    Part C

    Since 72=8×972 = 8 \times 972=8×9 and 888 and 999 share no factor above 111, passing both tests forces the count to be a multiple of 727272, so rows of 727272 come out even. The pair 222 and 444 is different: they share the factor 222, so the 222 test is passed automatically and forces nothing.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    For 444, cut the count into the hundreds and above plus the last two digits:

    5,976=(59×100)+76.5{,}976 = (59 \times 100) + 76.5,976=(59×100)+76.

    Since 100=4×25100 = 4 \times 25100=4×25, the piece 59×10059 \times 10059×100 is a multiple of 444 and leaves nothing over, so only 767676 is still on trial. And 76=4×1976 = 4 \times 1976=4×19, so rows of 444 work. There are 5,976÷4=1,4945{,}976 \div 4 = 1{,}4945,976÷4=1,494 of them.

    For 888, cut one column further out:

    5,976=(5×1000)+976.5{,}976 = (5 \times 1000) + 976.5,976=(5×1000)+976.

    Since 1000=8×1251000 = 8 \times 1251000=8×125, the piece 5×10005 \times 10005×1000 is a multiple of 888, so only the last three digits matter. Testing 976976976 is far quicker than testing 5,9765{,}9765,976: take 8×100=8008 \times 100 = 8008×100=800, which leaves 176176176, and 8×22=1768 \times 22 = 1768×22=176, so 976=8×122976 = 8 \times 122976=8×122. Rows of 888 work too, and there are 5,976÷8=7475{,}976 \div 8 = 7475,976÷8=747 of them.

    Notice what each cut bought. The 444 rule threw away two of the four digits of the count and the 888 rule threw away one, and in both cases the thrown-away part was a multiple of the divisor for reasons that had nothing to do with this particular count.

    Part B

    Rows of 555. The rule reads the final digit, which is 666. It is neither 000 nor 555, so 5,9765{,}9765,976 is not divisible by 555 and some chairs would be left standing. Dividing confirms it: 5,976=(5×1,195)+15{,}976 = (5 \times 1{,}195) + 15,976=(5×1,195)+1, so exactly one chair is stranded.

    Rows of 999. Add the digits.

    5+9+7+6=27,5 + 9 + 7 + 6 = 27,5+9+7+6=27,

    and 27=9×327 = 9 \times 327=9×3, so 5,9765{,}9765,976 is divisible by 999. That gives 5,976÷9=6645{,}976 \div 9 = 6645,976÷9=664 rows and nothing left over.

    Rows of 666. There is no rule of its own to run for 666, so run the tests for 222 and for 333 instead. That substitution is legitimate because 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3 and those two share no common factor above 111. The last digit 666 is even, so the 222 test passes. The digit sum 272727 is 3×93 \times 93×9, so the 333 test passes. Both halves clear, so 5,9765{,}9765,976 is divisible by 666, giving 996996996 rows.

    One digit sum settled the 999 question and half of the 666 question at once. That reuse is why chaining tests beats chaining divisions: the work overlaps, and the divisions would not.

    Part C

    Start from what the two passes actually give you. The count is a multiple of 888, so

    N=8×qfor some whole number q,N = 8 \times q \quad \text{for some whole number } q,N=8×qfor some whole number q,

    and it is a multiple of 999 as well.

    Now ask where that factor of 999 can be hiding. It cannot come out of the 888. Any whole number above 111 that divided both 888 and 999 would have to be a divisor of 888 other than 111, so it would be 222, 444 or 888, all even, and no even number divides the odd number 999. So 888 and 999 share no factor above 111, and the whole factor of 999 has to sit inside qqq. That is, q=9×rq = 9 \times rq=9×r, and

    N=8×(9×r)=72×r.N = 8 \times (9 \times r) = 72 \times r.N=8×(9×r)=72×r.

    The count is a multiple of 727272, so rows of 727272 come out even, with rrr of them.

    Now try the same move on 222 and 444. Suppose a count passes the 444 test, so N=4×sN = 4 \times sN=4×s, and we want the 222 test to force a factor of 222 into sss. It cannot: 444 is itself even, so 4×s4 \times s4×s is even for every whole number sss, and the 222 test is passed for free by anything that passed the 444 test.

Factors and Multiples: Chapter Review

A rapid review before the test: the chapter's vocabulary and notation, every formula with the conditions to use it, the standard problem types step by step, and the traps that cost points.

Vocabulary and notation

Divisible, factor, divisor, multiple
For d≠0d \neq 0d=0, nnn is divisible by ddd when n÷dn \div dn÷d leaves remainder 000. Said three ways: ddd is a factor (or divisor) of nnn, and nnn is a multiple of ddd.
Digit sum
The total of all the digits, folded again if still awkward to read: 9,996→33→69{,}996 \to 33 \to 69,996→33→6.
Even, odd
Even means the last digit is 0,2,4,6,0, 2, 4, 6,0,2,4,6, or 888, the same property as divisible by 222. Every other whole number is odd.
Prime
A whole number greater than 111 with exactly two distinct factors, 111 and itself. The list starts 2,3,5,7,11,132, 3, 5, 7, 11, 132,3,5,7,11,13; 222 is the only even entry.
Composite
A whole number greater than 111 with more than two distinct factors. Every whole number greater than 111 is exactly one of prime or composite; 111 is neither, having only the factor 111.
Prime factorization
A whole number greater than 111 written as a product of primes only: 84=2×2×3×784 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 784=2×2×3×7. A number already prime is its own factorization.
Prime-power form
The factorization with equal primes collected into powers, smallest prime first: 84=22×3×784 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 784=22×3×7. An exponent of 111 is left unwritten.
Common factor, GCF⁡(a,b,…)\operatorname{GCF}(a, b, \ldots)GCF(a,b,…)
A common factor divides all the numbers; the largest is the greatest common factor. Any list shares 111, so a GCF always exists.
Common multiple, LCM⁡(a,b,…)\operatorname{LCM}(a, b, \ldots)LCM(a,b,…)
A multiple of all the numbers; the smallest other than 000 is the least common multiple. Common multiples never run out.

Formulas and theorems

  • The divisibility tests

    Use when Whole numbers in base ten. These are the digit tests; for 777 or 111111, divide directly.

  • Multiples of ddd add and subtract

    (d×m)±(d×n)=d×(m±n)(d \times m) \pm (d \times n) = d \times (m \pm n)(d×m)±(d×n)=d×(m±n)

    Use when Whole numbers, with m≥nm \ge nm≥n so the difference stays whole. A sum or difference of two multiples of ddd is again one, so split off a part plainly divisible by ddd and test only the leftover.

    e.g. 714=700+14714 = 700 + 14714=700+14, and 777 divides both pieces, so 777 divides 714714714.

  • A factor of a factor is a factor

    e divides d and d divides n⟹ e divides n\begin{gathered} e \text{ divides } d \text{ and } d \text{ divides } n\\ \Longrightarrow\ e \text{ divides } n \end{gathered}e divides d and d divides n⟹ e divides n​

    Use when Whole numbers, one direction only. Divisible by 999 forces divisible by 333, and by 101010 forces divisible by 222 and 555; divisible by 333 forces nothing about 999.

  • Remainder from the digit sum

    n and its digit sum leavethe same remainder ondivision by 9, and by 3\begin{gathered} n \text{ and its digit sum leave}\\ \text{the same remainder on}\\ \text{division by } 9, \text{ and by } 3 \end{gathered}n and its digit sum leavethe same remainder ondivision by 9, and by 3​

    Use when Base ten whole numbers. Fold as often as needed; a fold landing on 999 means remainder 000, not 999.

    e.g. 4,2714{,}2714,271 has digit sum 141414, which leaves 555 on division by 999, so 4,2714{,}2714,271 leaves 555.

  • Combined test for a composite divisor

    n is divisible by a×b  ⟺  n is divisible by a and by b\begin{gathered} n \text{ is divisible by } a \times b\\ \iff n \text{ is divisible by } a \text{ and by } b \end{gathered}n is divisible by a×b⟺n is divisible by a and by b​

    Use when Requires GCF⁡(a,b)=1\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) = 1GCF(a,b)=1. Workable splits: 12=4×312 = 4 \times 312=4×3, 18=2×918 = 2 \times 918=2×9, 20=4×520 = 4 \times 520=4×5, 24=8×324 = 8 \times 324=8×3, 36=4×936 = 4 \times 936=4×9, 45=9×545 = 9 \times 545=9×5, 72=8×972 = 8 \times 972=8×9. Left to right holds unconditionally; only right to left needs the check.

  • Stopping rule for trial division

    test d=2,3,5,7,11,…only while d×d≤n\begin{gathered} \text{test } d = 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, \ldots\\ \text{only while } d \times d \le n \end{gathered}test d=2,3,5,7,11,…only while d×d≤n​

    Use when nnn greater than 111. The bound is not strict: at n=49n = 49n=49 you still test d=7d = 7d=7. Any divisor found makes nnn composite; none found in range makes it prime.

    e.g. For 127127127 the largest ddd with d×d≤127d \times d \le 127d×d≤127 is 111111, so 2,3,5,7,112, 3, 5, 7, 112,3,5,7,11 settle it.

  • Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

    n’s prime factorizationis unique, apart from order\begin{gathered} n\text{'s prime factorization}\\ \text{is unique, apart from order} \end{gathered}n’s prime factorizationis unique, apart from order​

    Use when Every whole number greater than 111 has exactly one prime factorization. A factor tree's starting split never changes it, and 111, not being prime, never appears.

    e.g. 8×98 \times 98×9 and 6×126 \times 126×12 both finish at 72=23×3272 = 2^3 \times 3^272=23×32.

  • Reading factors off a factorization

    d is a factor of n  ⟺  d uses only primes of n,each to at most its exponent in n\begin{gathered} d \text{ is a factor of } n\\ \iff d \text{ uses only primes of } n,\\ \text{each to at most its exponent in } n \end{gathered}d is a factor of n⟺d uses only primes of n,each to at most its exponent in n​

    Use when nnn greater than 111 in prime-power form; d=1d = 1d=1 qualifies, using no primes. A prime absent from the factorization can never divide nnn.

    e.g. 210=2×3×5×7210 = 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7210=2×3×5×7: 35=5×735 = 5 \times 735=5×7 is a factor, 111111 is not.

  • GCF and LCM from prime factorizations

    GCF⁡:lowest powerof each shared primeLCM⁡:highest powerof every prime that appears\begin{gathered} \operatorname{GCF}: \text{lowest power}\\ \text{of each shared prime}\\ \operatorname{LCM}: \text{highest power}\\ \text{of every prime that appears} \end{gathered}GCF:lowest powerof each shared primeLCM:highest powerof every prime that appears​

    Use when Whole numbers greater than 111 in prime-power form. A prime present in only some adds nothing to the GCF and its full power to the LCM. Both rules extend to three or more numbers.

  • GCF times LCM

    GCF⁡(a,b)×LCM⁡(a,b)=a×bLCM⁡(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF⁡(a,b)\begin{gathered} \operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b\\ \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = (a \times b) \div \operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \end{gathered}GCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×bLCM(a,b)=(a×b)÷GCF(a,b)​

    Use when Exactly two positive whole numbers, and no rule of this shape for three: GCF⁡(2,3,4)×LCM⁡(2,3,4)=12\operatorname{GCF}(2, 3, 4) \times \operatorname{LCM}(2, 3, 4) = 12GCF(2,3,4)×LCM(2,3,4)=12, not 242424.

    e.g. 181818 and 303030: 6×90=5406 \times 90 = 5406×90=540, and 18×30=54018 \times 30 = 54018×30=540.

  • Size check on a GCF or an LCM

    GCF⁡(a,b)≤a,b≤LCM⁡(a,b)\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) \le a, b \le \operatorname{LCM}(a, b)GCF(a,b)≤a,b≤LCM(a,b)

    Use when Positive whole numbers. Both become equalities exactly when the smaller divides the larger: 777 and 141414 give GCF 777, LCM 141414. And for two numbers, GCF⁡(a,b)=1\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) = 1GCF(a,b)=1 exactly when LCM⁡(a,b)=a×b\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = a \times bLCM(a,b)=a×b.

Problem types, step by step

Sweep a number through all eight divisibility tests

  1. Last digit: even clears 222, a 000 or 555 clears 555, a 000 clears 101010.
  2. Digit sum: a multiple of 333 clears 333, a multiple of 999 clears 999.
  3. Last two digits as a number for 444, last three for 888.
  4. Passing both 222 and 333 clears 666.

e.g. 5,9405{,}9405,940 ends in 000, digit sum 181818, last two digits 404040: clears 2,3,4,5,6,9,102, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 102,3,4,5,6,9,10, but 940÷8940 \div 8940÷8 leaves 444.

Fill in a missing digit so the number is divisible by ddd

  1. Write down what the test for ddd demands.
  2. 222, 555, 101010: a blank in the last place is forced to an allowed digit; elsewhere it is free.
  3. 333 or 999: add the known digits and pick the blank to reach the next multiple, discarding values above 999.
  4. 444 or 888: try 000 through 999 in the last two or three places and keep those that divide.
  5. Substitute back and rerun the test.

e.g. 7□47\square47□4 divisible by 999: 7+4=117 + 4 = 117+4=11, so the blank is 777, giving 774=9×86774 = 9 \times 86774=9×86.

Test divisibility by a composite divisor

  1. Split the divisor into two factors whose GCF is 111: 36=4×936 = 4 \times 936=4×9, never 6×66 \times 66×6.
  2. Run each factor's test separately.
  3. Both pass means divisible; either failing means not.

e.g. 2,7722{,}7722,772 ends 727272 (clears 444) and has digit sum 181818 (clears 999), so 363636 divides it: 2,772=36×772{,}772 = 36 \times 772,772=36×77.

Decide whether a number is prime or composite

  1. Set 111 aside as neither; settle 222 and 333 on sight.
  2. Run the 222, 333, 555 tests; a hit makes nnn composite unless nnn is that divisor itself.
  3. Trial-divide by 7,11,13,…7, 11, 13, \ldots7,11,13,…, testing ddd only while d×d≤nd \times d \le nd×d≤n.
  4. Divisor found: composite. Range exhausted with none: prime.

e.g. 149149149: 2,3,5,7,112, 3, 5, 7, 112,3,5,7,11 all fail and 13×13=169>14913 \times 13 = 169 > 14913×13=169>149, so 149149149 is prime.

List every prime up to a limit (sieve of Eratosthenes)

  1. Write 222 through the limit; never list 111.
  2. Circle the smallest uncrossed number; it is prime.
  3. Cross out every larger multiple of it.
  4. Repeat until the circled prime times itself passes the limit; everything left standing is prime.

e.g. Up to 202020: cross multiples of 222, then of 333, and stop since 5×5>205 \times 5 > 205×5>20, leaving 2,3,5,7,11,13,17,192, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 192,3,5,7,11,13,17,19.

Find a prime factorization

  1. Factor tree: split into any two factors above 111, splitting each composite branch until every leaf is prime.
  2. Or divide repeatedly by the smallest prime that fits, using the divisibility tests, until the quotient is 111.
  3. Collect into prime-power form, smallest prime first.
  4. Multiply back out to confirm the original number.

e.g. 468÷2=234468 \div 2 = 234468÷2=234, ÷2=117\div 2 = 117÷2=117, ÷3=39\div 3 = 39÷3=39, ÷3=13\div 3 = 13÷3=13, so 468=22×32×13468 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 13468=22×32×13.

Find the GCF and the LCM from prime factorizations

  1. Write every number in prime-power form; list every prime appearing anywhere.
  2. GCF: lowest power of each prime present in all of them, dropping any prime missing from even one.
  3. LCM: highest power of each prime appearing anywhere.
  4. Check: the GCF divides every number, and every number divides the LCM.
  5. Listing method, for small numbers: take the largest factor shared by every list, or the first value all the multiple lists share.

e.g. 90=2×32×590 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 590=2×32×5 and 84=22×3×784 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 784=22×3×7 give GCF⁡=6\operatorname{GCF} = 6GCF=6 and LCM⁡=22×32×5×7=1,260\operatorname{LCM} = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 = 1{,}260LCM=22×32×5×7=1,260.

Decide whether a word problem wants the GCF or the LCM

  1. Largest equal groups, biggest piece fitting several totals, most identical bundles from fixed supplies: GCF of the totals.
  2. Repeating cycles next coinciding, or the smallest amount several fixed sizes all reach exactly: LCM of the cycle lengths.
  3. Compute it, then answer what was asked: often a per-group count a÷GCF⁡a \div \operatorname{GCF}a÷GCF, not the GCF.

e.g. 545454 red and 363636 blue pens into identical largest packs: GCF⁡(54,36)=18\operatorname{GCF}(54, 36) = 18GCF(54,36)=18 packs, each of 333 red and 222 blue.

Exam traps

  • Trap Reading a digit sum that is a multiple of 333 as proof of divisibility by 999.

    Fix Only a digit sum that is a multiple of 999 clears 999. The digit sum of 888888888 is 242424, a multiple of 333 but not 999: 888÷3=296888 \div 3 = 296888÷3=296, but 888÷9888 \div 9888÷9 leaves 666.

  • Trap Splitting a composite divisor into factors that share a factor, testing 121212 as "222 and 666" or 444 as "222, twice".

    Fix The split needs GCF⁡=1\operatorname{GCF} = 1GCF=1. 181818 passes the 222 and 666 tests yet 18÷1218 \div 1218÷12 is not whole; 12=4×312 = 4 \times 312=4×3 correctly fails it.

  • Trap Quitting trial division after the first few primes fail.

    Fix Keep going while d×d≤nd \times d \le nd×d≤n. Stopping on 391391391 after 131313 misses 391=17×23391 = 17 \times 23391=17×23, since 19×19=361<39119 \times 19 = 361 < 39119×19=361<391.

  • Trap Treating 111 as prime, or assuming every prime is odd and every odd number prime.

    Fix 111 has a single factor, so it is neither. 222 is prime and even, while 999, 151515, 212121, 272727 are odd and composite.

  • Trap Handing in a partial split as a prime factorization, as 24=22×624 = 2^2 \times 624=22×6.

    Fix Every factor written must be prime. Split the 666 and finish at 24=23×324 = 2^3 \times 324=23×3.

  • Trap Dropping an unshared prime from the LCM, or swapping the lowest-power and highest-power rules.

    Fix A prime in only one number joins the LCM at full power. For 8=238 = 2^38=23 and 9=329 = 3^29=32: LCM⁡=72\operatorname{LCM} = 72LCM=72, GCF⁡=1\operatorname{GCF} = 1GCF=1, never LCM⁡=1\operatorname{LCM} = 1LCM=1.

  • Trap Handing in a×ba \times ba×b as the LCM.

    Fix That is a common multiple, least only when GCF⁡(a,b)=1\operatorname{GCF}(a, b) = 1GCF(a,b)=1. For 666 and 101010 the product 606060 is not the LCM 303030; divide the product by the GCF.

  • Trap Carrying GCF⁡×LCM⁡=a×b\operatorname{GCF} \times \operatorname{LCM} = a \times bGCF×LCM=a×b over to three numbers.

    Fix Guaranteed for a pair, no rule for three: GCF⁡(2,3,4)=1\operatorname{GCF}(2, 3, 4) = 1GCF(2,3,4)=1 and LCM⁡(2,3,4)=12\operatorname{LCM}(2, 3, 4) = 12LCM(2,3,4)=12 multiply to 121212, not 242424. Use the highest powers for a three-number LCM.

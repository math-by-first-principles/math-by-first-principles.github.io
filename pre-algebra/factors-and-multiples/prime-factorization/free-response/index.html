    1. Down the branches of a tree . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    A factor tree records the splitting. Write the number at the top, split it into two factors, draw a branch down to each, and keep working down each branch for as long as there is anything left to split. What ends up at the ends of the branches is what the tree was built to find.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Build a factor tree for 228228228, taking 228=4×57228 = 4 \times 57228=4×57 as the first split. Carry every branch as far as it will go, then list the numbers standing at the ends of the branches and write the number as a plain product of them.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Write that same factorization of 228228228 in prime-power form, with the primes running from smallest to largest, and check your form by multiplying the powers back out.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Now take 350350350. Build a factor tree for it, choosing the first split yourself, and give its factorization in prime-power form. Then say how you knew that each branch of your tree had gone as far as it could.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Ask of every number on the tree whether it still has a factor pair other than 111 and itself. If it does, that branch is not finished yet.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Count how many times each prime turned up at the ends of the branches. That count is exactly what an exponent records.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Any genuine split of the new number is allowed, so pull off a factor you can see at once and then deal with whatever is left beside it.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The ends of the branches hold 222, 222, 333 and 191919, so the product is 2×2×3×192 \times 2 \times 3 \times 192×2×3×19.

    Part B

    22×3×192^2 \times 3 \times 1922×3×19, and multiplying the powers back out gives 4×3×19=2284 \times 3 \times 19 = 2284×3×19=228.

    Part C

    350=2×52×7350 = 2 \times 5^2 \times 7350=2×52×7. A branch has gone as far as it can when the number standing on it is prime, because a prime has no factor pair besides 111 and itself for the branch to split into.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The first split is handed to you, so the tree starts with two branches:

    228=4×57.228 = 4 \times 57.228=4×57.

    Neither branch is finished. 444 is composite, and so is 575757: its digit sum is 5+7=125 + 7 = 125+7=12, a multiple of 333, so 333 divides it. Split each one:

    4=2×2,57=3×19.4 = 2 \times 2, \qquad 57 = 3 \times 19.4=2×2,57=3×19.

    Now look at the four numbers left at the ends: 222, 222, 333 and 191919. None of them has a factor pair other than 111 and itself, so no branch can be split again and the tree is complete. Reading the ends from left to right,

    228=2×2×3×19.228 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 19.228=2×2×3×19.

    Multiplying them back is the check: 2×2=42 \times 2 = 42×2=4, then 4×3=124 \times 3 = 124×3=12, then 12×19=22812 \times 19 = 22812×19=228.

    Part B

    Prime-power form collects the equal primes together. The tree produced two copies of 222, one 333 and one 191919, and an exponent is nothing more than a count of copies:

    2×2=22.2 \times 2 = 2^2.2×2=22.

    The 333 and the 191919 each appear once, and a prime that appears once is written with no exponent at all, since one copy is what that means. Listing the primes from smallest to largest,

    228=22×3×19.228 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 19.228=22×3×19.

    Expanding the power back out checks the bookkeeping: 22=42^2 = 422=4, and 4×3×19=2284 \times 3 \times 19 = 2284×3×19=228, the number the tree started from. Nothing was gained or lost in the rewrite; prime-power form is the same product written more briefly.

    Part C

    Any genuine split will do, so start with one you can see. Taking 350=35×10350 = 35 \times 10350=35×10, both branches are composite and both split once more:

    35=5×7,10=2×5.35 = 5 \times 7, \qquad 10 = 2 \times 5.35=5×7,10=2×5.

    Every number at the end of a branch is now prime, so the tree is finished. The ends are 555, 777, 222 and 555, and collecting the two copies of 555 into a power gives

    350=2×52×7.350 = 2 \times 5^2 \times 7.350=2×52×7.

    What tells you a branch is finished is the number sitting on it, not the shape of the tree. A composite number has a factor pair with both factors bigger than 111, so it can always be split again; a prime has no such pair, so it cannot. Every number on a tree is bigger than 111, because 1×n1 \times n1×n is not a genuine split, so each branch ends exactly when its number is prime.

    In one line

    228=2×2×3×19=22×3×19228 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 19 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 19228=2×2×3×19=22×3×19, and 350=2×52×7350 = 2 \times 5^2 \times 7350=2×52×7. A branch of a tree is finished exactly when the number on it is prime, since only a composite still has a factor pair to split into.

    Another way: Peel the smallest prime off first

    Instead of hunting for a split, always take the smallest prime that divides the current number and put the quotient on the other branch. For the first number that gives 228=2×114228 = 2 \times 114228=2×114, then 114=2×57114 = 2 \times 57114=2×57, then 57=3×1957 = 3 \times 1957=3×19:

    228=2×2×3×19.228 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 19.228=2×2×3×19.

    The tree comes out one-sided, a long staircase rather than a bush, and the collection at the ends is unchanged.

    When it is worth it When no split jumps out at you, or when the number is large enough that a wrong guess would cost more than a division. It is also the shape that turns into repeated division, where the same work is kept in a single column.

    2. One column, one prime at a time . Foundational, 13 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 13 pts

    Repeated division keeps the whole calculation in a single column. Divide by the smallest prime that fits, divide the new quotient the same way, and carry on until the quotient reaches 111. The divisibility tests are what make each choice of divisor quick, and the stopping rule from the previous lesson is what limits how many primes you ever have to try.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find the prime factorization of 2,2502{,}2502,250 by repeated division. Take the smallest prime that fits at every step, keep going until the quotient reaches 111, name the test that told you each divisor would work, and give the answer in prime-power form.

    2. B

    3. C
      Part C.

      A student hands in this work: "702=2×27×13702 = 2 \times 27 \times 13702=2×27×13, and multiplying that out gives 702702702 again, so it is the prime factorization of 702702702."     2,250=2×32×532{,}250 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 5^32,250=2×32×53.

    Part C

    Multiplying back to the number is only half of what is required: 272727 is composite, so the product is not built from primes only. Splitting that factor further gives 702=2×33×13702 = 2 \times 3^3 \times 13702=2×33×13.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The number ends in 000, so it is even and 222 divides it:

    2,250÷2=1,125.2{,}250 \div 2 = 1{,}125.2,250÷2=1,125.

    Now 1,1251{,}1251,125 is odd, so 222 is finished. Its digit sum is 1+1+2+5=91 + 1 + 2 + 5 = 91+1+2+5=9, a multiple of 333, so 333 divides it, and the digit sum of the quotient, 3+7+5=153 + 7 + 5 = 153+7+5=15, says that 333 divides that too:

    1,125÷3=375,375÷3=125.1{,}125 \div 3 = 375, \qquad 375 \div 3 = 125.1,125÷3=375,375÷3=125.

    The digit sum of 125125125 is 888, not a multiple of 333, so 333 is finished. It ends in 555, so 555 divides it, and it does so three times over:

    125÷5=25,25÷5=5,5÷5=1.125 \div 5 = 25, \qquad 25 \div 5 = 5, \qquad 5 \div 5 = 1.125÷5=25,25÷5=5,5÷5=1.

    The quotient has reached 111, so the column is closed. The divisors used, in the order they were used, were 222, 333, 333, 555, 555 and 555, so collecting the repeats,

    2,250=2×32×53.2{,}250 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 5^3.2,250=2×32×53.

    As a check, 32=93^2 = 932=9 and 53=1255^3 = 12553=125, so 2×9=182 \times 9 = 182×9=18 and 18×125=2,25018 \times 125 = 2{,}25018×125=2,250.

    Part B

    Rule out the small primes with the digit tests rather than by dividing. The number is odd, so 222 is out. Its digit sum is 3+9+1=133 + 9 + 1 = 133+9+1=13, not a multiple of 333, so 333 is out. It ends in neither 000 nor 555, so 555 is out.

    That leaves the primes above 555, tried in turn. 7×55=3857 \times 55 = 3857×55=385 and 7×56=3927 \times 56 = 3927×56=392, so 777 misses. 11×35=38511 \times 35 = 38511×35=385 and 11×36=39611 \times 36 = 39611×36=396, so 111111 misses. 13×30=39013 \times 30 = 39013×30=390, one short, so 131313 misses. Then comes 171717:

    391÷17=23.391 \div 17 = 23.391÷17=23.

    Now factor the quotient. Is 232323 prime? 4×4=164 \times 4 = 164×4=16 is under it and 5×5=255 \times 5 = 255×5=25 is past it, so only 222 and 333 need testing, and neither divides it. Both factors are prime, so the column is done:

    391=17×23.391 = 17 \times 23.391=17×23.

    The stopping rule bounds how much work this could ever have been. A prime ppp is worth testing only while p×pp \times pp×p is no bigger than the number: 19×19=36119 \times 19 = 36119×19=361 is under 391391391, while 23×23=52923 \times 23 = 52923×23=529 is past it. So 191919 was the last candidate, and if none of 222, 333, 555, 777, 111111, 131313, 171717, 191919 had divided the number, it would have been prime itself.

    Part C

    The arithmetic is sound: 2×27=542 \times 27 = 542×27=54 and 54×13=70254 \times 13 = 70254×13=702. But multiplying back to the right number is only one of the two things a prime factorization has to do. The other is that every factor written down is prime, and 272727 is not: it is 3×93 \times 93×9, and the 999 splits again.

    Carry that branch down:

    27=3×3×3=33.27 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 3^3.27=3×3×3=33.

    So the repair is to replace the composite factor by its own primes and leave everything else alone:

    702=2×33×13.702 = 2 \times 3^3 \times 13.702=2×33×13.

    Repeated division arrives at the same place from the other end. The number is even, so 702÷2=351702 \div 2 = 351702÷2=351; the digit sum of 351351351 is 999, so 351÷3=117351 \div 3 = 117351÷3=117, then 117÷3=39117 \div 3 = 39117÷3=39, then 39÷3=1339 \div 3 = 1339÷3=13, which is prime.

    The general point is worth keeping. Any product that multiplies back to the number is a factorization of it, and a number has many of those. Only a product built entirely from primes is the prime factorization, and that is the one the theorem promises there is just one of, apart from the order in which the factors are written.

    In one line

    2,250=2×32×532{,}250 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 5^32,250=2×32×53 and 391=17×23391 = 17 \times 23391=17×23, found after testing the primes as far as 171717. The student's product is correct arithmetic but not a prime factorization, since 272727 is composite; the prime factorization is 702=2×33×13702 = 2 \times 3^3 \times 13702=2×33×13.

    3. Cookies by the pallet . Application, 11 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 11 pts

    A bakery stacks its cookies in nested containers: a box holds 141414 cookies, a crate holds 151515 boxes, and a pallet holds 666 crates. Each container count multiplies the one below it, so the totals in this warehouse arrive already written as products.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Give the prime factorization of the number of cookies on one full pallet, in prime-power form, without multiplying the three container counts together first. Then give the number of cookies on the pallet.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A supermarket orders four full pallets of the same kind. Give the prime factorization of the total number of cookies in that order, and the total itself.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why factoring each container count separately and pooling the primes has to give the same answer as multiplying the three counts together first and then factoring that single number.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      A product is already a partly built factor tree: the numbers being multiplied are its first branches, and each one that is composite still has to come apart.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      The multiplier here is not prime, so ask which primes it brings with it and where they join the pile you already have.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Two calculations that both end in a product of primes for the same number cannot disagree, and there is a theorem that says exactly why.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    A full pallet holds 1,2601{,}2601,260 cookies, and 1,260=22×32×5×71{,}260 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 71,260=22×32×5×7.

    Part B

    24×32×5×72^4 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 724×32×5×7, which is 5,0405{,}0405,040 cookies.

    Part C

    Pooling the primes is a factor tree whose first splits happen to be the container counts, and by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic a whole number greater than 111 has only one prime factorization apart from order, so no route down the tree can end in different primes.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    A pallet is 666 crates of 151515 boxes of 141414 cookies, so the count is the product 6×15×146 \times 15 \times 146×15×14. That product is a factor tree already in progress: its first splits have been handed to you, and each of the three counts is composite, so each splits once more:

    6=2×3,15=3×5,14=2×7.6 = 2 \times 3, \qquad 15 = 3 \times 5, \qquad 14 = 2 \times 7.6=2×3,15=3×5,14=2×7.

    Every branch now ends in a prime. Pooling the six of them and collecting the repeats,

    6×15×14=2×3×3×5×2×7=22×32×5×7.6 \times 15 \times 14 = 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 2 \times 7 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7.6×15×14=2×3×3×5×2×7=22×32×5×7.

    For the count itself, multiply the powers out: 4×9=364 \times 9 = 364×9=36, then 36×5=18036 \times 5 = 18036×5=180, then 180×7=1,260180 \times 7 = 1{,}260180×7=1,260 cookies on a pallet. Multiplying the container counts directly agrees: 15×14=21015 \times 14 = 21015×14=210 and 6×210=1,2606 \times 210 = 1{,}2606×210=1,260.

    Part B

    Four pallets hold four times as many cookies as one, so the total is 444 multiplied by a pallet's worth. The factorization does not have to be started again: 444 is not prime, and its own factorization is

    4=2×2.4 = 2 \times 2.4=2×2.

    So the order adds two more copies of 222 to the primes a single pallet already contributed, and touches nothing else:

    24×32×5×7.2^4 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7.24×32×5×7.

    The exponent on the 222 grows because an exponent counts copies, and two more copies have joined the pile. Multiplying out, 16×9=14416 \times 9 = 14416×9=144, then 144×5=720144 \times 5 = 720144×5=720, then 720×7=5,040720 \times 7 = 5{,}040720×7=5,040 cookies in the order.

    Part C

    The two calculations are the same tree entered at different points.

    The product of the three counts is the number of cookies on a pallet, so writing that number as 6×15×146 \times 15 \times 146×15×14 is a legitimate way to start its factor tree. Three branches instead of two, but every one of them still has to be carried down. Factoring each count and pooling what falls out is nothing more than finishing those branches:

    6×15×14=(2×3)×(3×5)×(2×7).6 \times 15 \times 14 = (2 \times 3) \times (3 \times 5) \times (2 \times 7).6×15×14=(2×3)×(3×5)×(2×7).

    Multiplying first and then factoring starts the same number from the top with some other first split, and it also stops only when every branch holds a prime.

    That the two agree is not luck, and it is not something to be checked case by case. The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic says that a whole number greater than 111 has exactly one prime factorization, apart from the order in which the factors are written. Both calculations end in a product of primes equal to the same number, so they must end in the same collection of primes, differing at most in the order they were produced.

    In one line

    One pallet holds 1,260=22×32×5×71{,}260 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 71,260=22×32×5×7 cookies, and four pallets hold 24×32×5×7=5,0402^4 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 = 5{,}04024×32×5×7=5,040 cookies. Factoring the container counts and pooling the primes is one factor tree among many for the same number, and uniqueness is what guarantees every route ends in the same primes.

    Another way: Multiply first, then divide by primes

    Multiply the container counts to get the pallet total, then run repeated division on that single number:

    1,260÷2=630,630÷2=315,315÷3=105,105÷3=35,35÷5=7,7÷7=1.1{,}260 \div 2 = 630, \quad 630 \div 2 = 315, \quad 315 \div 3 = 105, \quad 105 \div 3 = 35, \quad 35 \div 5 = 7, \quad 7 \div 7 = 1.1,260÷2=630,630÷2=315,315÷3=105,105÷3=35,35÷5=7,7÷7=1.

    The divisors in order are 222, 222, 333, 333, 555, 777, the same collection the pooled tree produced.

    When it is worth it When the numbers being multiplied are small enough to combine in your head, or when you want a second, independent route to the same factorization as a check on the first.

    The answer

    Part A

    Every whole number greater than 111 has exactly one prime factorization, apart from the order of the factors.     Worked solution

    Part A

    Two phrases are missing, and each is missing for its own reason.

    Which numbers it covers. Every prime is at least 222, so any product of primes is at least 222 as well. No product of primes can therefore equal 111 or 000, and the sentence has to be restricted to whole numbers greater than 111. The number 111 is the case that bites in practice: it has no prime factor at all, because every prime is larger than 111 itself, so there is nothing for a factorization of it to be built from.

    In what sense it is unique. Take 110110110. It can be written

    110=2×5×11and110=11×5×2,110 = 2 \times 5 \times 11 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 110 = 11 \times 5 \times 2,110=2×5×11and110=11×5×2,

    and a reader who counts those as two different prime factorizations has just contradicted the uncorrected sentence. They are the same collection of primes written in a different order, so what the theorem claims is uniqueness apart from order. That is also why prime-power form conventionally lists the primes from smallest to largest: fixing the order makes the one answer look like one answer.

    Put together: every whole number greater than 111 has exactly one prime factorization, apart from the order in which the factors are written.

    Part B

    The number is even, so 34=2×1734 = 2 \times 1734=2×17, and 171717 is prime. That is the one factorization the theorem promises.

    Now suppose 111 counted as a prime. Multiplying by 111 changes nothing, so every one of

    34=1×2×17=1×1×2×17=1×1×1×2×1734 = 1 \times 2 \times 17 = 1 \times 1 \times 2 \times 17 = 1 \times 1 \times 1 \times 2 \times 1734=1×2×17=1×1×2×17=1×1×1×2×17

    would be a product of "primes" equal to 343434, and the list never ends, because another copy of 111 can always be pushed in. Uniqueness would not merely be dented in an odd case; it would fail for every number at once, and "the" prime factorization would be a phrase with no meaning behind it.

    Keeping 111 out costs nothing, since 111 contributes nothing to a product, and it buys back the single answer the theorem is about. That is the bookkeeping reason mathematicians do not count 111 as prime, and it is why a factorization is built only out of numbers that are at least 222.

    Part C

    The first claim. Suppose two numbers have the same prime factorization. A factorization is a product, and a product of one fixed collection of numbers has one value, so each of the two numbers equals that value and they are equal to each other. Notice that this direction does not even need the theorem; it is ordinary multiplication.

    The theorem is what makes the reverse direction work: one number cannot present two genuinely different prime factorizations, because apart from the order of the factors there is only one to have. Taken together, the two directions are what let a prime factorization be called a fingerprint. It names one number and no other.

    The weakened version. Now only the primes that appear are compared, and how many times each appears is thrown away. That is not enough to pin a number down:

    44=22×11and242=2×112.44 = 2^2 \times 11 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 242 = 2 \times 11^2.44=22×11and242=2×112.

    Both use the primes 222 and 111111 and no others, and the two numbers are plainly different. One such pair is all it takes to retire the weakened claim.

    The gap between the two claims is exactly what an exponent records. A prime factorization says which primes appear and how many copies of each; a bare list of the primes keeps only the first half, and it is the second half that tells these two numbers apart.

    In one line

    The statement needs both "greater than 111" and "apart from the order of the factors". Admitting 111 as a prime would let any factorization be padded endlessly, which is why 111 is kept out. And while the same prime factorization does force two numbers to be equal, the same primes alone do not, as 44=22×1144 = 2^2 \times 1144=22×11 and 242=2×112242 = 2 \times 11^2242=2×112 show.

Prime Factorization

Intuition

Take 121212. It is composite, so it is built from smaller numbers: 12=3×412 = 3 \times 412=3×4. Keep going and break 444 apart too: 12=3×2×212 = 3 \times 2 \times 212=3×2×2. Every piece left is now prime, so the splitting stops. That fully broken-down form is the prime factorization of a number, and it is the closest thing arithmetic has to a fingerprint: every whole number greater than 111 has one, and no two different numbers share the same one. Finding that factorization turns hard questions about factors and multiples into easy ones. This lesson shows you how to find it and why it always comes out the same.

Learning goals

  • Build a factor tree until every leaf is prime
  • Divide repeatedly by the smallest prime that fits, using the divisibility rules to move fast
  • Collect repeated primes into prime-power form
  • Explain why the starting split never changes the final list of primes

What a prime factorization is

A prime factorization of a whole number greater than 111 is a way of writing it as a product of prime numbers only. For example,

12=2×2×3,30=2×3×5,7=7.12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3, \qquad 30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5, \qquad 7 = 7.12=2×2×3,30=2×3×5,7=7.

Every factor on the right is prime. A number like 121212 that is composite breaks into several primes. A number that is already prime, like 777, is its own prime factorization, a product of a single prime. The point is to keep splitting until you cannot split any further, which happens exactly when every remaining factor is prime. The splitting stops there because a prime is the kind of number that has no smaller factors to break off.

This is different from just listing factors. The factors of 121212 are 1,2,3,4,6,121, 2, 3, 4, 6, 121,2,3,4,6,12, a mixed bag of primes and composites. The prime factorization picks out only the prime building blocks and shows how many of each you multiply together to rebuild the number. It is the recipe, not the inventory.

Every number breaks down into primes

Before learning a method, settle the basic question: can every composite number really be written this way? The answer is yes.

Watch 606060 come apart. Start anywhere you like, say 60=6×1060 = 6 \times 1060=6×10. Neither piece is prime, so split each one again: 6=2×36 = 2 \times 36=2×3 and 10=2×510 = 2 \times 510=2×5. Every piece is now prime, and 60=2×3×2×560 = 2 \times 3 \times 2 \times 560=2×3×2×5.

Each step traded a number for pieces smaller than it, and no piece is allowed below 222. There is only so much room between 222 and 606060, so the splitting runs out of places to go. The same reasoning works for any whole number greater than 111: every split trades a number for smaller pieces, the pieces can never sink below 222, so the splitting always has to stop, and it stops exactly when only primes are left.

The reasoning above works for 606060, but nothing about 606060 was special. The Go deeper section carries the same argument through a second number and then through a general whole number nnn, to show it never fails.

Whatever number you start with, the splitting ends in a pile of primes that multiply back to it. The question left is whether two people who split the same number in two different ways could end up with two different piles.

The factor-tree method

The most common way to carry out the splitting is a factor tree (a diagram that branches a number down into its primes). Write the number at the top. Find any two factors whose product is that number (not 111 and the number itself, but a genuine split). Then draw a branch down to each. Whenever a number is composite, split it again. Whenever a number is prime, stop that branch and circle it. The factorization is the collection of primes at the ends of the branches, the leaves of the tree.

Take 363636. One natural split is 36=4×936 = 4 \times 936=4×9. Neither 444 nor 999 is prime, so each splits again: 4=2×24 = 2 \times 24=2×2 and 9=3×39 = 3 \times 39=3×3. Now every leaf is prime, so we are finished.

Reading the leaves from left to right gives

36=2×2×3×3.36 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3.36=2×2×3×3.

It does not matter which factor you write first or which branch you split first. As long as you keep going until every leaf is prime, the leaves you collect are the prime factorization.

Now try building one yourself. Start 484848 as 48=6×848 = 6 \times 848=6×8, and split each branch until every leaf is prime.

Repeated multiplication in exponent form

When the same prime shows up more than once, writing it out in full gets clumsy. The factorization of 363636 has two 222s and two 333s, and a larger number can have a prime repeated many times. There is a compact shorthand: 2×22 \times 22×2 can be written 222^222, and 2×2×22 \times 2 \times 22×2×2 can be written 232^323. The small raised number, the exponent, just counts how many copies of the prime are multiplied together. It is nothing more than shorthand for repeated multiplication:

23=2×2×2=8,32=3×3=9.2^3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8, \qquad 3^2 = 3 \times 3 = 9.23=2×2×2=8,32=3×3=9.

Using this, the factorization of 363636 becomes

36=2×2×3×3=22×32.36 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 2^2 \times 3^2.36=2×2×3×3=22×32.

This compact form is called prime-power form: each distinct prime written once, with an exponent telling how many times it appears. When you write a factorization, group the equal primes together and collect them into powers, and by convention list the primes from smallest to largest. You can always expand the shorthand back into a plain product whenever you want to see every factor spelled out.

The other choices stop too early: 666, 444, and 444 are all still composite, so those are not prime factorizations.

Repeated division by primes

A factor tree can branch in many shapes, which is handy but a little untidy for bookkeeping. A second method keeps everything in a single column: repeated division by primes. Divide the number by the smallest prime that goes into it, then divide the quotient by the smallest prime that goes into it. Keep going until the quotient reaches 111. The divisors you used, in order, are the prime factorization.

Run this on 360360360. The smallest prime dividing it is 222, and you keep dividing by 222 as long as the result stays even:

360÷2=180,180÷2=90,90÷2=45.360 \div 2 = 180, \quad 180 \div 2 = 90, \quad 90 \div 2 = 45.360÷2=180,180÷2=90,90÷2=45.

Now 454545 is odd, so 222 is finished. The smallest prime that divides 454545 is 333:

45÷3=15,15÷3=5.45 \div 3 = 15, \quad 15 \div 3 = 5.45÷3=15,15÷3=5.

Finally 555 is prime and divides itself: 5÷5=15 \div 5 = 15÷5=1. We have reached 111, so we stop. The divisors, in the order we used them, were 2,2,2,3,3,52, 2, 2, 3, 3, 52,2,2,3,3,5, so

360=23×32×5.360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5.360=23×32×5.

The divisibility rules from earlier in this chapter make each step quick. They tell you at a glance whether 222, 333, or 555 divides the current number, so you rarely have to guess.

This is where the prime factorization starts paying off. Regroup the primes of 360360360 any way you like and you get a genuine factor pair for free: (2×32)×(22×5)=18×20(2 \times 3^2) \times (2^2 \times 5) = 18 \times 20(2×32)×(22×5)=18×20, so 360=18×20360 = 18 \times 20360=18×20. Spotting that pair by guessing would take longer than reading it off the factorization. The next lesson uses this same idea to find the greatest common factor and least common multiple of two numbers.

Worked example 1 Find the prime factorization of 848484

Use repeated division, always dividing by the smallest prime that fits.

The number 848484 is even, so start with 222:

84÷2=42,42÷2=21.84 \div 2 = 42, \qquad 42 \div 2 = 21.84÷2=42,42÷2=21.

Now 212121 is odd, so 222 is done. Its digit sum is 2+1=32 + 1 = 32+1=3, a multiple of 333, so divide by 333:

21÷3=7.21 \div 3 = 7.21÷3=7.

The quotient 777 is prime, so the last step is 7÷7=17 \div 7 = 17÷7=1. Collecting the divisors 2,2,3,72, 2, 3, 72,2,3,7 and grouping the repeats,

84=2×2×3×7=22×3×7.84 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 7 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7.84=2×2×3×7=22×3×7.

A prime that appears only once, like the 333 and the 777 here, is written with no exponent (an exponent of 111 is understood). As a check, multiply the factorization back out: 22×3×7=4×3×7=842^2 \times 3 \times 7 = 4 \times 3 \times 7 = 8422×3×7=4×3×7=84, the number we started with.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Here is the result that makes prime factorization so powerful. We saw that 363636 could be started as 4×94 \times 94×9. But you could just as well start it as 6×66 \times 66×6, or as 2×182 \times 182×18. Do those other starting splits change the final answer?

Both trees end in the same four primes: two 222s and two 333s. The order of the leaves changes, but the collection does not. This is not a lucky coincidence of 363636. It is a law that holds for every whole number, important enough to have a name.

Apart from the order in which you write the factors, every whole number greater than 111 has exactly one prime factorization. That statement is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. The order may change, but the same primes show up the same number of times, no matter how you start the tree. That uniqueness is also why we can speak of the prime factorization of a number rather than a prime factorization.

Why is the answer always the same? It comes down to one special fact about primes: if a prime divides a product of two numbers, it must divide at least one of them. Take 333 and 2×6=122 \times 6 = 122×6=12: the 333 shows up in the 666. Every prime has this guarantee. A composite number need not: 444 divides 2×6=122 \times 6 = 122×6=12, but 444 divides neither 222 nor 666.

That is what ties two prime lists together. Every prime in one list has to match a prime in the other, so neither list can hold anything the other is missing.

So a prime factorization is a fact about the number, not a choice you make.

This argument shows that no whole number has two different prime factorizations, using the special fact about primes stated above. Whichever splits you choose on the way down, the primes you finish with are always the same ones, in the same amounts. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Larger numbers, same method

Nothing changes for bigger numbers; you just take more steps. Peel off the small primes first using the divisibility rules, which shrinks the number fast and keeps the arithmetic light, as in the 360360360 example above. But sometimes none of the small primes fit at all, and you have to work upward.

Worked example 2 Find the prime factorization of 539539539

This one has no small even or five factor to give it away. So lean on the divisibility rules to rule out the easy primes, then test the next ones in turn.

The number 539539539 is odd, so 222 is out. Its digit sum is 5+3+9=175 + 3 + 9 = 175+3+9=17, not a multiple of 333, so 333 is out. It does not end in 000 or 555, so 555 is out. The next prime is 777:

539÷7=77.539 \div 7 = 77.539÷7=77.

So 777 works, and now factor the quotient 777777. It is 7×117 \times 117×11:

77÷7=11,11÷11=1.77 \div 7 = 11, \quad 11 \div 11 = 1.77÷7=11,11÷11=1.

Both 777 and 111111 are prime, so the factorization is

539=7×7×11=72×11.539 = 7 \times 7 \times 11 = 7^2 \times 11.539=7×7×11=72×11.

When the small primes all fail, work upward through 7,11,13,…7, 11, 13, \ldots7,11,13,…, and remember the stopping rule from the last lesson: keep testing primes only while the prime times itself is at most the number you are testing. Once every prime up to that point has failed, what remains is itself prime.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Leaving a composite factor in the final answer. Writing 24=4×624 = 4 \times 624=4×6, 36=6×636 = 6 \times 636=6×6, or 22×62^2 \times 622×6 is a start, not a finished prime factorization, because 444 and 666 are still composite. Every factor written down must be prime; keep splitting until none are left.
  • Miscounting the exponent. The exponent counts how many times the prime is multiplied, so 2×2×22 \times 2 \times 22×2×2 is 232^323, not 222^222. When in doubt, expand the power back out and count the copies.
  • Thinking the starting split changes the answer. By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, every route down the factor tree ends in the same primes. If two of your trees disagree, check each one: either a branch was not carried all the way down to primes, a split used a wrong factor pair, or a repeated prime was miscounted.
  • Listing 111 as a prime factor. 111 is not prime, and multiplying by 111 never changes the product, so it never belongs in a factorization. A factorization is built from primes, which start at 222.

Key takeaways

  • A prime factorization writes a whole number greater than 111 as a product of primes only, for example 360=23×32×5360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5360=23×32×5. By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, every number has exactly one, apart from the order you write it in, so the starting split never changes the final primes.
  • Find it with a factor tree (split until every leaf is prime), or by repeated division by the smallest prime that fits until the quotient reaches 111. The divisibility rules for 222, 333, and 555 make both methods fast.
  • Collect repeated primes into prime-power form, where the exponent is shorthand for repeated multiplication: 2×2×2=232 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^32×2×2=23.
  • Regrouping the primes exposes factor pairs for free, like 360=18×20360 = 18 \times 20360=18×20, which the next lesson uses to find the greatest common factor and least common multiple.

Why the splitting always finishes

The lesson showed the reasoning on 606060: every split trades a number for smaller pieces, and the pieces can never sink below 222, so the splitting has to stop. Here is that same argument run on a second number, and then written so it covers every whole number at once.

Why every whole number greater than 111 is a product of primes#

Follow 126126126 through the argument. It is composite, so it splits, say 126=2×63126 = 2 \times 63126=2×63, and both factors are smaller than 126126126. The 222 is prime, so that branch is finished. The 636363 is composite, so put the same question to it: 63=7×963 = 7 \times 963=7×9, and the 999 splits once more into 3×33 \times 33×3. Every piece is prime now, and 126=2×7×3×3126 = 2 \times 7 \times 3 \times 3126=2×7×3×3. Each split lowered the numbers in play, and they can never sink below 222, which is why the splitting ran out of moves.

Now the general version, with a letter in place of the 126126126.

Take any whole number nnn greater than 111. Either it is prime or it is composite.

If nnn is prime, you are already done: nnn by itself is a product of primes.

If nnn is composite, it has a factor other than 111 and itself. So you can write n=a×bn = a \times bn=a×b with both aaa and bbb greater than 111, which makes both smaller than nnn. Ask the same question of each one: leave it alone if it is prime, split it again if it is composite, and keep repeating.

This has to end, because every split replaces a number with strictly smaller factors. There are only finitely many whole numbers between 222 and nnn to descend through. It ends exactly when every factor is prime, since a prime is the only number that refuses to split. That leaves nnn written as a product of primes.

∎

Why the answer cannot depend on how you split the number

The lesson gives the reason uniqueness holds: a prime lands whole inside one factor of a product. This is that reason carried all the way to a finish, for anyone who wants to see the pairing done rather than described.

The idea behind uniqueness#

One fact about primes carries the whole argument: if a prime divides a product of two numbers, it divides at least one of them. For instance 333 divides 2×6=122 \times 6 = 122×6=12, and sure enough 333 divides the 666. This is the special property, seen earlier in the lesson, that composite numbers do not share.

Watch the pairing work on 606060, whose prime list is 2,2,3,52, 2, 3, 52,2,3,5. Suppose someone claims a second and different list for it. Take the 555 from the first list. It divides 606060, so it divides the product of the claimed primes, and by the fact above it has to divide one of them. That forces the prime it divides to be 555 itself. Cross out a 555 from each list and 121212 is left on both sides, ready for the same move with the 333, and then with the two 222s. The lists empty together, so the second was the first all along.

Now the same argument with letters. Suppose some number had two genuinely different prime factorizations, and pick a prime ppp from the first. That number also equals the product of the second list, and ppp divides the number, so ppp divides that product. By the fact above, ppp divides one of those primes, and one prime divides another only when they are equal. So ppp matches a prime in the second list exactly. Cancel the matching pair from both lists and repeat on what remains. Every prime in the first list is paired off this way, and none can be stranded in the second. When the first list runs out, what is left of it multiplies to 111. So what is left of the second must multiply to 111 too, and no prime can do that. The two lists were the same all along, in a different order.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

Why would anyone bother to prove the theorem in this lesson? That a number breaks into primes in just one way looks too obvious to argue about.

Then people began inventing new kinds of number. In the 1840s a German mathematician named Ernst Kummer was working inside one such system. It had its own primes and its own factor lists, and one alarming feature: a number in it could be built from two genuinely different sets of building blocks. Uniqueness simply failed.

That is why our version earns a proof and a grand name. Ordinary whole numbers behave, but they were never obliged to. That good behavior rests on one small fact about primes. A prime dividing a product must land whole inside one of the two factors.

So when your factor tree and a classmate’s tree end in the same primes, that is not luck. It is built into the number, and it had to be earned.

    1. Counting a number's distinct factors . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    The definition of prime and composite is a counting rule and nothing more: it asks how many distinct factors a whole number greater than 111 has, and then reads the classification off that count. So the count is where the work is.

    1. A
      Part A.

      List every factor of 686868, and then list every factor of 626262. For each of the two numbers, say how many distinct factors it has and classify it by the definition.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Decide whether 129129129 has a factor other than 111 and 129129129. If it does, exhibit one factor pair 129=a×b129 = a \times b129=a×b in which neither aaa nor bbb is 111 or 129129129, and name the divisibility rule that found it. Then say what your finding establishes about 129129129, and why a single pair would be enough to establish it.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A single factor pair, once one has been found, settles a number's classification on the spot, while a long run of divisions that all leave a remainder settles nothing on its own. Explain what each of the two definitions is asking for, and say what has to be added to a run of failed divisions before it settles anything at all.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Everything here runs off one counting rule: a whole number greater than 111 is prime when its distinct factors number exactly two, and composite when they number more than two. Settle the count, and the classification follows.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

    That is every hint for this question.

    The answer

    Part A

    686868 has the six factors 1,2,4,17,34,681, 2, 4, 17, 34, 681,2,4,17,34,68 and 626262 has the four factors 1,2,31,621, 2, 31, 621,2,31,62. Both counts are more than two, so both numbers are composite.

    Part B

    129=3×43129 = 3 \times 43129=3×43, found by the digit-sum rule for 333 (since 1+2+9=121 + 2 + 9 = 121+2+9=12). That one pair exhibits a factor beyond 111 and 129129129, which is all the definition of composite asks for.

    Part C

    Composite asks only that at least one factor beyond 111 and nnn exists, so a single pair proves it. Prime asks that no such factor exists anywhere, a claim about every candidate at once, so failed divisions settle it only once a stopping rule accounts for the candidates never tested.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    A factor of a number divides it with remainder 000, and factors come in pairs: each one you find hands you its partner, since the two multiply back to the number. So the whole list can be built by testing only the small candidates and writing down both members of every pair as it appears.

    For 686868, test candidates while the candidate multiplied by itself has not passed 686868:

    8×8=64≤68,9×9=81>68,8 \times 8 = 64 \le 68, \qquad 9 \times 9 = 81 > 68,8×8=64≤68,9×9=81>68,

    so 111 through 888 is the entire search. Of those, 111 divides (partner 686868), 222 divides because the number is even (partner 343434), and 444 divides (partner 171717). The candidate 333 fails the digit-sum test, since 6+8=146 + 8 = 146+8=14, and 555, 666, 777 and 888 each leave a remainder. Collecting both members of every pair,

    68=1×68=2×34=4×17,68 = 1 \times 68 = 2 \times 34 = 4 \times 17,68=1×68=2×34=4×17,

    which gives the six distinct factors 1,2,4,17,34,681, 2, 4, 17, 34, 681,2,4,17,34,68.

    For 626262 the boundary sits lower:

    7×7=49≤62,8×8=64>62,7 \times 7 = 49 \le 62, \qquad 8 \times 8 = 64 > 62,7×7=49≤62,8×8=64>62,

    so the search is 111 through 777. Only 111 (partner 626262) and 222 (partner 313131) divide, giving

    62=1×62=2×31,62 = 1 \times 62 = 2 \times 31,62=1×62=2×31,

    and the four distinct factors 1,2,31,621, 2, 31, 621,2,31,62.

    Now read the counts against the definition. Six is more than two and four is more than two, so both numbers land in the same group. The two counts are quite different and the definition does not care: it asks only whether the count is exactly two or more than two, never how much more.

    Part B

    Run the quick tests in order before dividing anything. The number is odd, so 222 is out, and it does not end in 000 or 555, so 555 is out. That leaves the digit-sum rule for 333:

    1+2+9=12,1 + 2 + 9 = 12,1+2+9=12,

    and 121212 is a multiple of 333, so 333 divides 129129129. One division hands over the partner:

    129÷3=43,so129=3×43.129 \div 3 = 43, \qquad \text{so} \qquad 129 = 3 \times 43.129÷3=43,so129=3×43.

    Neither 333 nor 434343 is 111 or 129129129, so the number has a factor beyond the two that every whole number greater than 111 is forced to have. Its factor list therefore holds at least 111, 333, 434343 and 129129129, which is more than two distinct factors, and more than two is the definition of composite.

    That is why a single pair finishes the job. The definition does not ask how many extra factors there are or what they all are; it asks only whether the count gets past two. As soon as one extra factor is on the table the count is past two, so no further candidate has to be divided and no further factor has to be listed.

    Part C

    Compare what the two words are actually asking for.

    Composite asks for more than two distinct factors, which is a claim that something exists: somewhere among the whole numbers there is a factor of the number that is neither 111 nor the number itself. A claim that something exists is settled by producing it, once. In part B the pair

    129=3×43129 = 3 \times 43129=3×43

    does exactly that, and the search can stop there. Whether the number turns out to have four factors or forty makes no difference to the classification.

    Prime asks for exactly two distinct factors, which is a claim that something does not exist: there is no factor besides 111 and the number itself, anywhere. A claim that nothing of a certain kind exists is not settled by failed searches, however many. The next candidate you have not tried could still be the one that divides, so ten failed divisions and a hundred failed divisions carry the same weight, which is none, for as long as untested candidates remain.

    What repairs it is a reason to stop: an argument that the candidates you did not test could not have worked. That is what the stopping rule supplies. Once a candidate multiplied by itself has passed the number, every remaining candidate is too large to be the smaller member of a factor pair, so the failed divisions turn into a finished search instead of an abandoned one. Only then do they establish that the number is prime.

    In one line

    686868 has six factors (1,2,4,17,34,681, 2, 4, 17, 34, 681,2,4,17,34,68) and 626262 has four (1,2,31,621, 2, 31, 621,2,31,62), so both are composite. So is 129129129, since the digit-sum rule for 333 gives 129=3×43129 = 3 \times 43129=3×43. One factor pair settles composite because that definition asks only that some extra factor exist, while prime asks that none exist, which failed divisions establish only once the stopping rule accounts for the candidates that were never tested.

    Two whole numbers get sorted wrongly more often than all the others together: 111 and 222. Neither is a special case to be memorised, and each part below settles one of them from the counting rule itself. The last part then tests a rule that a student has built on top of the result about 222.

    Answer and solution

    The answer

    Part A

    The complete factor list of 111 is the single number 111. Prime requires exactly two distinct factors and composite requires more than two; one factor is neither exactly two nor more than two, so 111 meets neither definition. The count forces the outcome.

    Part B

    Being even means 222 divides nnn, so 222 is a factor. It is not 111, and it is not nnn either, because nnn was taken greater than 222. So nnn has at least the three distinct factors 111, 222 and nnn, which is more than two, and no even number past 222 can be prime.

    Part C

    The student has swapped a true statement for its converse, which does not follow: that every prime past 222 is odd says nothing about whether every odd number is prime. Take 119=7×17119 = 7 \times 17119=7×17, odd and with more than two factors. Odd removes 222 from the candidate divisors and nothing else.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Start where both definitions start, with the factor list. A factor of 111 is a whole number that divides 111 with remainder 000. Only 111 does: 1÷1=11 \div 1 = 11÷1=1 exactly, and every whole number larger than 111 is already bigger than 111, so it must leave a remainder. The complete list is therefore

    {1},\{1\},{1},

    a list with one member.

    Now hold that count against each definition in turn.

    Prime requires exactly two distinct factors, 111 and the number itself. For the number 111, those two descriptions name the same number, so they collect into a single entry rather than two. One is not two, so 111 is not prime.

    Composite requires more than two distinct factors. One is fewer than two, not more, so 111 is not composite either.

    Nothing was chosen anywhere in that. Both definitions are conditions on a single count, the count is 111, and 111 satisfies neither condition. So 111 sits outside both groups, and that is exactly why the classification is stated for whole numbers greater than 111. There is a payoff further on as well: a building block that could be glued onto a product any number of times without changing it would not be much of a building block.

    Part B

    Take nnn even and greater than 222, with nothing else assumed about it.

    Being even is precisely what the divisibility rule for 222 detects: the last digit is 000, 222, 444, 666 or 888, and 222 divides the number. So 222 is a factor of nnn.

    Every whole number greater than 111 already carries 111 and itself on its factor list, so the list of nnn contains 111, nnn and 222. The question is whether that is genuinely three entries, or whether 222 has been counted twice under another name.

    It has not. The factor 222 is not 111, since 2≠12 \ne 12=1. And 222 is not nnn, since nnn was taken greater than 222. The three entries are distinct, so

    count of distinct factors of n≥3.\text{count of distinct factors of } n \ge 3.count of distinct factors of n≥3.

    More than two distinct factors is the definition of composite, so every even nnn greater than 222 is composite and none of them is prime. Since 222 itself has exactly the two factors 111 and 222, it is prime, and it is the only even number that can be.

    Notice what the argument never did. It never used the size of nnn, never carried out a division, and never looked at a particular number, so it settles every even number past 222 in one stroke: 949494, 488488488 and an even number a hundred digits long are all covered by the same three lines. That is the difference between checking examples and establishing a statement about all of them.

    Part C

    The student's first sentence is true, and part B is the reason: every even number past 222 is composite, so the primes past 222 all have to be odd. The trouble is in the second sentence, where that statement gets read backwards.

    "Every prime greater than 222 is odd" and "every odd number greater than 111 is prime" are different claims. The first says the primes sit inside the odd numbers. The second says the odd numbers sit inside the primes. Turning a statement around like this produces its converse, and a converse is a new claim that has to be tested on its own account; it never comes free with the original.

    So test it. Take 119119119. It is odd, so the student's rule calls it prime. But

    119=7×17,119 = 7 \times 17,119=7×17,

    so besides 111 and 119119119 the factor list also holds 777 and 171717. That is more than two distinct factors, which makes 119119119 composite, and one number like this is enough to retire the rule for good.

    What does being odd actually buy, then? Exactly one thing: an odd number has no factor of 222. That clears a single candidate divisor, the very first one, and says nothing whatever about 333, 555, 777, 111111 and the rest, which is why the testing carries on past 222 instead of stopping there. Odd is a filter, not a verdict.

    In one line

    The number 111 has a single factor, so it meets neither definition: prime asks for exactly two and composite for more than two. Any even nnn greater than 222 has 111, 222 and nnn as three distinct factors and is therefore composite, which leaves 222 as the only even prime. The student's rule is the converse of a true statement and fails on any odd composite, for instance 119=7×17119 = 7 \times 17119=7×17: being odd removes 222 from the candidate divisors and does nothing else.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    An arrangement of rrr rows holding ccc seedlings each uses r×cr \times cr×c seedlings altogether, so the grower is asking for a factor pair of 319319319 in which neither member is 111. That is the same search as looking for a factor other than 111 and the batch size.

    Part C

    A claim of the form "exactly when" is two claims, and both have to be argued. Checking one and assuming the other is how a plausible rule turns out to be half right.

    First direction: composite gives a tray. Let nnn be composite. By definition it has more than two distinct factors, so beyond 111 and nnn there is some factor ddd with

    Both directions hold, so the claim is correct as stated, and the two batches above are the two cases in action. The word "exactly" is what makes the second argument necessary. Had only the first direction been checked, the rule would have looked fine on every example anyone tried, and it would still have been an unproved guess in precisely the direction the grower cares about: the direction that says when a batch cannot be arranged.

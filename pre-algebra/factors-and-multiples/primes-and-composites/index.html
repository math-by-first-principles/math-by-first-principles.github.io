Primes and Composites

Intuition

You already know how to find the factors of a number: the whole numbers that divide it with no remainder. Some numbers, like 121212, have plenty of factors. Others, like 131313, have only the two that every number is forced to have: 111 and the number itself. That second kind is special. Numbers with exactly two factors are the primes, and every composite number is built by multiplying primes together. This lesson defines primes and their opposite, the composites, and gives you a reliable way to decide which kind any number is.

Learning goals

  • Define prime and composite by counting a number's factors
  • Explain why 111 is neither prime nor composite and why 222 is the only even prime
  • Test a number by trial division, stopping once the divisor times itself passes it
  • Run the Sieve of Eratosthenes to list primes up to a limit

Factors, the starting point

A quick reminder from the last lesson. A factor (or divisor) of a whole number nnn is a whole number greater than 000 that divides nnn leaving remainder 000. To list the factors of nnn, you ask of each candidate “does it divide nnn evenly?” The factors of 121212 are

1,2,3,4,6,12,1, \quad 2, \quad 3, \quad 4, \quad 6, \quad 12,1,2,3,4,6,12,

because each of these divides 121212 with nothing left over, and no other whole number does. Two of those factors come free for every whole number greater than 111. Every such number is always divisible by 111 (one group containing everything) and by itself (one group the size of the whole number). So the real question, the one that sorts numbers into two camps, comes down to this. Does a number have only those two forced factors, or does it have more?

Defining prime and composite

This is the whole idea, stated carefully. We count only the distinct factors, and we work with whole numbers greater than 111.

A whole number greater than 111 is prime when it has exactly two distinct factors: 111 and itself, and nothing in between. The number 777 is prime because the only whole numbers that divide it are 111 and 777. Try anything else, say 222, 333, 444, 555, or 666, and a remainder appears.

A whole number greater than 111 is composite when it has more than two distinct factors. The number 121212 is composite because, beyond 111 and 121212, the numbers 2,3,4,2, 3, 4,2,3,4, and 666 also divide it. Having even one extra factor is enough to make a number composite.

Both of those claims are things you can check rather than take on trust. Below you choose how many dots there are and how many go in each row. When every row comes out full, the row width is a factor. When the last row is short, it is not, and the leftover dots are drawn hollow so you can count them.

Set the count to 121212 and walk the row width from 222 up to 666. Widths 222, 333, 444 and 666 all come out full, and only 555 leaves anything over, so 121212 has factors to spare and is composite. Now set the count to 777 and walk the same widths again. Every single one leaves a short row. Nothing between 111 and 777 divides it, which is exactly what makes 777 prime, and you have now checked it rather than been told it.

Every whole number greater than 111 falls into exactly one of these two groups, never both and never neither. Either its only factors are 111 and itself (prime), or it has at least one more (composite). Here is the start of the list, sorted:

Why 1 is neither prime nor composite

The number 111 looks like it might be prime, since nothing divides it except 111. But run it through the definition exactly. A prime needs two distinct factors. The number 111 has only one factor, namely 111 itself: the "111" and the “itself” are the same number, so they do not count twice.

The exclusion of 111 is not just a bookkeeping rule. It keeps every whole number’s breakdown into primes unique: if 111 counted as prime, you could glue extra copies of it onto any breakdown (6=2×3=1×2×3=1×1×2×36 = 2 \times 3 = 1 \times 2 \times 3 = 1 \times 1 \times 2 \times 36=2×3=1×2×3=1×1×2×3) and never land on one settled answer. The next lesson, on prime factorization, depends on that uniqueness, which is why 111 is kept out.

Why 2 is the only even prime

Look at the prime list again: 2,3,5,7,11,13,…2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, \ldots2,3,5,7,11,13,…. After 222, every entry is odd. That is no accident.

Try to find an even number past 222 that survives the definition. Take 141414. Being even hands it 222 as a factor for free, on top of the 111 and the 141414 that every number is forced to have. Its factor list is 1,2,7,141, 2, 7, 141,2,7,14, which is four factors, and a prime is allowed exactly two.

The same thing happens for any even number bigger than 222, not just 141414: being even hands it 222 as an extra factor, on top of the 111 and itself it already has. That is three distinct factors, one more than a prime is allowed, so the number is composite. Every even number past 222 fails the same way, which leaves 222 as the only even prime.

This gives you a fast first filter. If a number bigger than 222 ends in 0,2,4,6,0, 2, 4, 6,0,2,4,6, or 888, you can declare it composite on sight, no division needed. The candidates for “prime” past 222 are only the odd numbers, and even among those, plenty (like 999, 151515, 212121) turn out to be composite.

Testing a number by trial division

To decide whether a number nnn is prime, you hunt for a factor other than 111 and nnn. Find even one and nnn is composite; find none and nnn is prime. The direct method is trial division: try the candidate divisors in turn and watch for a clean division. You do not need to test every number below nnn, and you do not need long division either. That is because the divisibility rules from the last lesson do most of the work.

Test the candidates in increasing order, 2,3,5,7,…2, 3, 5, 7, \ldots2,3,5,7,…, and lean on the rules you already know:

  • Try 222: is the last digit even? If yes (and n>2n > 2n>2), then nnn is composite.
  • Try 333: does the digit sum come out a multiple of 333? If yes (and n>3n > 3n>3), then nnn is composite.
  • Try 555: does it end in 000 or 555? If yes (and n>5n > 5n>5), then nnn is composite.
  • Try 777, then the next candidates, dividing to check, until either a factor appears or you reach the stopping point in the next section.

You can skip every even candidate after 222, since an even divisor would make nnn even and you have already checked 222. You can also skip multiples of 333 after 333, so in practice you test 2,3,5,7,11,13,…2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, \ldots2,3,5,7,11,13,…, the primes themselves. A few small divisors are enough to settle even a fairly large number. The explanation is the stopping rule, which is the heart of the method.

The stopping rule: when can you quit?

Testing divisors one by one could go on a long time. The key shortcut is that you can stop early: once a candidate divisor ddd grows large enough that d×dd \times dd×d passes nnn, you are done. If nothing has divided nnn by the time you reach that point, nnn is prime. The reason is a fact about how factors come in pairs.

Watch the factors of 363636 do it. They arrive two at a time: 111 with 363636, 222 with 181818, 333 with 121212, 444 with 999, and 666 with 666. Every one of those pairs has a partner of 666 or less, and 666 is the number whose square is 363636. Those six numbers, 111 through 666, account for every factor pair 363636 has. A divisor above 666 could only be the larger partner of a pair you already caught by its smaller one.

In plain terms: keep testing divisors d=2,3,5,7,…d = 2, 3, 5, 7, \ldotsd=2,3,5,7,… only while d×d≤nd \times d \le nd×d≤n. As soon as d×dd \times dd×d passes nnn, stop.

The pairing that 363636 showed you is the whole reason, and it holds for every number. Whenever a number divides nnn, it arrives with a partner: multiply the two and you get nnn back. One of that pair is never larger than the value whose square is nnn, because two partners both above it would already multiply past nnn. So a search that tries every candidate up to that point, without finding one that divides nnn, has already ruled out every possible pair, and nnn must be prime.

The pairing argument above is the real reason, stated for every number at once, with a full worked example. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See the general proof

Worked example 1 Is 515151 prime or composite?

The number 515151 is odd and does not end in 000 or 555, so 222 and 555 are out. Try the next candidate, 333, with the digit-sum rule:

5+1=6=3×2,5 + 1 = 6 = 3 \times 2,5+1=6=3×2,

so 333 divides 515151. That is a third factor besides 111 and 515151, which settles it:

51=3×17.51 = 3 \times 17.51=3×17.

So 515151 is composite. A number can look prime at a glance and still hide a factor; the digit-sum test for 333 catches many that the eye misses.

Worked example 2 Is 838383 prime or composite?

Test divisors in order, stopping once a divisor times itself passes 838383.

It is odd, so 222 fails. Its digit sum is 8+3=118 + 3 = 118+3=11, not a multiple of 333, so 333 fails. It does not end in 000 or 555, so 555 fails. Try 777:

83÷7=11 remainder 6,83 \div 7 = 11 \text{ remainder } 6,83÷7=11 remainder 6,

so 777 fails too. The next candidate is 111111, but check the stopping rule first:

11×11=121>83,11 \times 11 = 121 > 83,11×11=121>83,

so we have passed the stopping point. No divisor up to here worked, so there is no factor pair to find. Therefore 838383 is prime. Notice we only had to try 2,3,5,2, 3, 5,2,3,5, and 777.

The Sieve of Eratosthenes

To find all the primes up to some limit at once, there is a method faster than testing each number on its own. That method has been known since antiquity as the Sieve of Eratosthenes (a way to find every prime up to a limit by crossing out multiples). The idea is to start with every number and strain out the composites, leaving the primes behind. A sieve does the same, straining out what you do not want.

Write the whole numbers from 222 up to your limit. Then repeat one move:

  1. Circle the smallest number not yet crossed out. It is prime (nothing below it divided it, or it would already be crossed out).
  2. Cross out every larger multiple of that number. They are all composite, since they have it as a factor.

The first prime is 222, so circle it and cross out 4,6,8,10,…4, 6, 8, 10, \ldots4,6,8,10,…. The next surviving number is 333, so circle it and cross out 6,9,12,…6, 9, 12, \ldots6,9,12,…. Then 555, then 777, and so on. By the stopping rule, once the circled prime times itself passes your limit, everything still standing is prime and you are finished.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Calling 111 prime. A prime needs two distinct factors; 111 has only one factor (itself), so it is neither prime nor composite.
  • Thinking primes must be odd. “Odd” and “prime” are different ideas. 222 is prime and even, while 9,15,9, 15,9,15, and 212121 are odd and composite. Odd is only a first filter, not a guarantee.
  • Testing too many divisors. You never need to test past the point where the divisor times itself exceeds nnn. For n=97n = 97n=97 you stop after 777, because 11×11=121>9711 \times 11 = 121 > 9711×11=121>97. Going further wastes effort.
  • Forgetting to use the divisibility rules. Trial division does not mean long division. Check 222, 333, and 555 by their quick tests first; they eliminate most composites in seconds.
  • Stopping a sieve too late, or marking 111. The sieve lists numbers from 222, not 111, and once the current prime times itself passes the limit, everything left is already prime.

Key takeaways

  • A prime is a whole number greater than 111 with exactly two distinct factors, 111 and itself. A composite is a whole number greater than 111 with more than two factors.
  • The number 111 is neither, because it has only one factor, and every whole number greater than 111 is exactly one of prime or composite.
  • 222 is the only even prime: any larger even number has 222 as a third factor, so it is composite.
  • To test nnn, use trial division with the divisibility rules, trying 2,3,5,7,…2, 3, 5, 7, \ldots2,3,5,7,… in turn.
  • You may stop once the divisor ddd satisfies d×d>nd \times d > nd×d>n, because every composite has a factor no larger than that. If no factor has appeared by then, nnn is prime.
  • The Sieve of Eratosthenes finds all primes up to a limit by circling the next survivor and crossing out its multiples.

The full stopping-rule argument, with letters

The factor-pair picture (36 folding around 6) is the whole reason the stopping rule works. Here is that same reasoning made precise for every number at once.

Why you may stop once the divisor times itself passes nnn#

Try it on 919191 with the divisor 777. The division gives 91÷7=1391 \div 7 = 1391÷7=13, so the 777 arrives holding a partner, and 91=7×1391 = 7 \times 1391=7×13. Those two partners fall either side of the value whose square is 919191, which sits between 999 and 101010. They are forced to: two partners both above it would multiply past 919191, since even 10×10=10010 \times 10 = 10010×10=100 overshoots. So the smaller partner 777 is the one a search meets first, and it is met while 7×7=497 \times 7 = 497×7=49 is still under 919191.

Now the same reasoning with letters. Whenever a number ddd divides nnn, the division produces a partner. If n÷d=en \div d = en÷d=e, then

n=d×e,n = d \times e,n=d×e,

so ddd and eee are a pair of factors whose product is nnn. They always come together: find one and you have found the other.

Now compare the pair against the value where a number times itself equals nnn. The two partners cannot both be larger than it, or their product would overshoot nnn. So whichever of ddd and eee is the smaller, call it sss, satisfies s×s≤ns \times s \le ns×s≤n. The larger partner has no such bound: for 91=7×1391 = 7 \times 1391=7×13 the smaller is 777, and 13×1313 \times 1313×13 is far past 919191.

Every composite number therefore has a factor sss small enough to meet before your trial divisor reaches the point where it times itself passes nnn. Suppose you have tested every candidate up to there and none divided nnn. Then no factor pair is left to find, so nnn has no factor besides 111 and nnn, and nnn is prime. Any factor past that point would drag in a partner you already tried and ruled out.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

Eratosthenes was a Greek scholar who ran the great library at Alexandria in Egypt in the third century BCE. Testing numbers for primality one at a time is slow work, so he found a way to skip it: write every number down, then cross out the multiples of two, then of three, then of five. Whatever survives is prime, and you never divided anything.

That is the sieve you ran in this lesson, circling a survivor and striking out its multiples. It is over two thousand years old, and computers still run a version of it today.

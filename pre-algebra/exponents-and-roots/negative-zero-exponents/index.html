Negative and Zero Exponents

▾

Progress can't be saved in this browser, so this lesson's status can't be set.

Intuition

The quotient 23/252^3/2^523/25 is an ordinary fraction: 8/32=1/48/32 = 1/48/32=1/4. But subtracting its exponents gives 2−22^{-2}2−2. What should a negative exponent mean? We can extend our definition of powers so that the quotient rule still works. Zero and negative exponents then fit the same pattern as positive ones, provided the base is not zero.

Learning goals

  • Show why the quotient rule forces a0a^0a0 to equal one
  • Read a−na^{-n}a−n as the reciprocal of the matching positive power
  • Evaluate a zero or negative power to a definite number
  • Separate the sign of the exponent from the sign of the base
  • Extend the exponent laws to every integer exponent for a nonzero base

Zero as an exponent

Our definition so far counts a positive whole number of factors. It has not yet assigned a value to zero or negative exponents.

The last lesson proved am/an=am−na^m/a^n = a^{m-n}am/an=am−n for positive whole numbers m>nm>nm>n and a≠0a\ne0a=0. Now consider equal exponents. Direct calculation gives

2323=88=1.\frac{2^3}{2^3} = \frac88 = 1.2323​=88​=1.

If subtracting exponents is to work here too, the same quotient must be 23−3=202^{3-3} = 2^023−3=20. So we define 20=12^0 = 120=1. This is not a guess from one example: the same requirement determines the zero power of every nonzero base.

Why a0=1a^0 = 1a0=1 for every base a≠0a \neq 0a=0#

Take another nonzero base:

9292=8181=1.\frac{9^2}{9^2}=\frac{81}{81}=1.9292​=8181​=1.

If subtracting exponents is to work for equal exponents too, it must describe this quotient as 92−2=909^{2-2}=9^092−2=90. Consistency therefore requires 90=19^0=190=1. The choice of 999 or 222 is not essential: every nonzero power divided by itself is 111.

Let a≠0a\ne0a=0 and let nnn be a positive whole number. A nonzero number divided by itself is 111:

anan=1.\frac{a^n}{a^n} = 1.anan​=1.

Extending the quotient rule to equal exponents would write that quotient as an−n=a0a^{n-n} = a^0an−n=a0. To preserve the rule, we therefore define

a0=1(a≠0).a^0 = 1 \qquad (a\ne0).a0=1(a=0).

The condition matters: 0/00/00/0 is undefined, so this reasoning does not assign a value to 000^000. We leave 000^000 undefined in this course.

∎

You can also follow descending powers. For a nonzero base, lowering a positive exponent by one removes one factor of the base, so it divides the value by that base. Each step from 24=162^4=1624=16 to 23=82^3=823=8, then 22=42^2=422=4, then 21=22^1=221=2, divides by 222. One more step gives 20=12^0=120=1.

A zero exponent is not a zero value

A zero exponent gives 111 for any nonzero base: 50=15^0 = 150=1, 1000=1100^0 = 11000=1, and (37)0=1(\tfrac37)^0 = 1(73​)0=1. It does not multiply the base by zero. For a nonzero base, picture starting at 111 and multiplying by the base zero times: the value stays 111.

Answer choices

The quotient is 49/49=149/49=149/49=1. To keep the same-base quotient rule, its other form is 72−2=707^{2-2}=7^072−2=70, so 70=17^0=170=1. This argument needs a nonzero base; it does not use multiplication by zero or subtraction of powers.

Negative exponents

Now let the denominator have more factors. Cancel three matching factors from 23/252^3/2^523/25:

2325=2×2×22×2×2×2×2=12×2=14.\begin{aligned} \frac{2^3}{2^5} &= \frac{2 \times 2 \times 2}{2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2} \\ &= \frac{1}{2 \times 2} = \frac14. \end{aligned}2523​​=2×2×2×2×22×2×2​=2×21​=41​.​

Extending the quotient rule gives 23−5=2−22^{3-5} = 2^{-2}23−5=2−2. To keep the two values equal, define 2−2=1/22=1/42^{-2} = 1/2^2 = 1/42−2=1/22=1/4.

A negative exponent asks for the reciprocal of the matching positive power: 111 divided by that power. It does not ask for a negative value.

Why a−n=1ana^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}a−n=an1​ for every base a≠0a \neq 0a=0#

In 6265\frac{6^2}{6^5}6562​, cancel the two numerator factors against two of the five denominator factors:

6265=6×66×6×6×6×6=16×6×6=163.\begin{aligned} \frac{6^2}{6^5}&=\frac{6\times6}{6\times6\times6\times6\times6}\\ &=\frac1{6\times6\times6}=\frac1{6^3}. \end{aligned}6562​​=6×6×6×6×66×6​=6×6×61​=631​.​

Extending exponent subtraction would name the same quotient 62−5=6−36^{2-5}=6^{-3}62−5=6−3. To keep that rule, define 6−3=1/636^{-3}=1/6^36−3=1/63. Any nonzero base works the same way: the uncanceled denominator factors become the reciprocal power.

Let a≠0a\ne0a=0, and let mmm and nnn be positive whole numbers. In am/am+na^m/a^{m+n}am/am+n, cancel the mmm numerator factors against mmm of the denominator factors. The remaining nnn factors stay below the fraction bar:

amam+n=1a⋯a⏟n=1an.\frac{a^m}{a^{m+n}} = \frac{1}{\underbrace{a\cdots a}_{n}} = \frac1{a^n}.am+nam​=na⋯a​​1​=an1​.

Subtracting exponents would give am−(m+n)=a−na^{m-(m+n)}=a^{-n}am−(m+n)=a−n. To preserve the quotient rule, define

a−n=1an(a≠0).a^{-n} = \frac1{a^n} \qquad (a\ne0).a−n=an1​(a=0).

The nonzero condition permits both cancellation and the reciprocal. Reading the equation backward lets us write a reciprocal power as a negative power too.

∎

The descending-powers pattern agrees. Starting at 20=12^0=120=1, keep dividing by 222: 2−1=1/22^{-1}=1/22−1=1/2, 2−2=1/42^{-2}=1/42−2=1/4, and 2−3=1/82^{-3}=1/82−3=1/8. Only the exponent becomes negative; these values stay positive.

Answer choices

3−2=132=193^{-2} = \frac{1}{3^2} = \frac{1}{9}3−2=321​=91​

The value is positive: the minus sign moves the power to the denominator, it does not make the number negative, so 3−23^{-2}3−2 is not −9-9−9.

Evaluating zero and negative powers

For a negative exponent, write the reciprocal first, then evaluate the positive power.

Worked example 1 Evaluate 2−32^{-3}2−3, 505^050, and 10−210^{-2}10−2

For 2−32^{-3}2−3, the negative exponent flips it to a reciprocal, then evaluate the cube:

2−3=123=18.2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}.2−3=231​=81​.

For 505^050, a zero exponent on a nonzero base is 111 outright:

50=1.5^0 = 1.50=1.

For 10−210^{-2}10−2, take the reciprocal of 10210^2102:

10−2=1102=1100=0.01.10^{-2} = \frac{1}{10^2} = \frac{1}{100} = 0.01.10−2=1021​=1001​=0.01.

If the base is a fraction, the reciprocal turns it upside down.

Worked example 2 Evaluate (12)−1\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}(21​)−1 and (23)−2\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{-2}(32​)−2

A power of −1-1−1 asks for one reciprocal and nothing more, so it just flips the base over.

For (12)−1\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}(21​)−1, take the reciprocal of 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​:

(12)−1=1(12)1=112=2.\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{1}} = \frac{1}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = 2.(21​)−1=(21​)11​=21​1​=2.

Dividing 111 by 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ gives 222, so a positive base smaller than 111 raised to a negative power comes out larger than 111. For (23)−2\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{-2}(32​)−2, flip the fraction first, then square it:

(23)−2=(32)2=3222=94.\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{-2} = \left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)^{2} = \frac{3^2}{2^2} = \frac{9}{4}.(32​)−2=(23​)2=2232​=49​.

Flipping the base turns the −2-2−2 into a +2+2+2, which is the quickest route for a fraction raised to a negative power.

Check your understanding

Evaluate (34)−2\left(\frac34\right)^{-2}(43​)−2.

Answer choices

Take the reciprocal, then square:

(34)−2=(43)2=4232=169.\left(\frac34\right)^{-2}=\left(\frac43\right)^2=\frac{4^2}{3^2}=\frac{16}{9}.(43​)−2=(34​)2=3242​=916​.

A negative exponent need not give a number below 111: the original base here is between 000 and 111.

The sign of the base is a separate question

  • Exponent’s minus sign: take a reciprocal.
  • Base’s minus sign: count whether the number of negative factors is even or odd.

For example,

(−2)−2=1(−2)2=14,(−2)−3=1(−2)3=−18.(-2)^{-2} = \frac1{(-2)^2} = \frac14, \qquad (-2)^{-3} = \frac1{(-2)^3} = -\frac18.(−2)−2=(−2)21​=41​,(−2)−3=(−2)31​=−81​.

The first value is positive because two negative factors make a positive product. The second is negative because three leave one negative factor unpaired. Taking a reciprocal preserves the sign of that product.

Parentheses still matter

Without parentheses, the leading minus stays outside the power: −2−2=−(2−2)=−1/4-2^{-2} = -(2^{-2}) = -1/4−2−2=−(2−2)=−1/4. With parentheses, (−2)−2=1/4(-2)^{-2}=1/4(−2)−2=1/4.

Check your understanding

Evaluate (−3)−2(-3)^{-2}(−3)−2.

Answer choices

Answer two separate questions. The exponent's sign is negative, so take a reciprocal of (−3)2(-3)^2(−3)2. The exponent's size, 222, is even, so squaring the negative base cancels its sign.

(−3)−2=1(−3)2=19(-3)^{-2} = \frac{1}{(-3)^2} = \frac{1}{9}(−3)−2=(−3)21​=91​

The result is positive even though both the base and the exponent carry a minus sign: the exponent's sign only decides whether to reciprocate, and the base's sign only survives an odd exponent.

The exponent laws still work

With these definitions, the laws extend to all integer exponents when the bases are nonzero. Here is the product rule with a negative exponent, checked against ordinary fraction arithmetic.

Worked example 3 Show 23×2−5=142^3 \times 2^{-5} = \tfrac{1}{4}23×2−5=41​ two ways

First use the product rule, adding the exponents straight through, including the negative one:

23×2−5=23+(−5)=2−2=122=14.\begin{aligned} 2^3 \times 2^{-5} &= 2^{3+(-5)} = 2^{-2} \\ &= \frac1{2^2} = \frac14. \end{aligned}23×2−5​=23+(−5)=2−2=221​=41​.​

Now check using the reciprocal definition and ordinary fraction arithmetic, without applying the product rule. Rewrite 2−52^{-5}2−5 as its reciprocal first, then multiply the fractions:

23×2−5=8×125=832=14.2^3 \times 2^{-5} = 8 \times \frac{1}{2^5} = \frac{8}{32} = \frac{1}{4}.23×2−5=8×251​=328​=41​.

Both routes land on 14\tfrac{1}{4}41​. The product rule still says “add the exponents,” and adding 3+(−5)=−23 + (-5) = -23+(−5)=−2 gives the same answer as grinding through the arithmetic.

The example illustrates the rule, but does not prove every case. The general reason is that negative powers can be rewritten as reciprocals. For positive whole numbers m,nm,nm,n,

ama−n=aman,a−ma−n=1aman=1am+n.a^m a^{-n}=\frac{a^m}{a^n}, \qquad a^{-m}a^{-n}=\frac1{a^m a^n}=\frac1{a^{m+n}}.ama−n=anam​,a−ma−n=aman1​=am+n1​.

In the first expression, cancellation leaves factors above, no factors, or factors below, depending on whether m>nm>nm>n, m=nm=nm=n, or m<nm<nm<n. These give am−na^{m-n}am−n in all three cases. The second expression is a−(m+n)a^{-(m+n)}a−(m+n), again the sum of its original exponents. Zero powers multiply by 111.

The other laws extend in the same way, for nonzero bases and integer exponents. For a quotient with a negative exponent below, try

727−1=49÷17=49×7=343=73.\begin{aligned} \frac{7^2}{7^{-1}}&=49\div\frac17\\ &=49\times7=343=7^3. \end{aligned}7−172​​=49÷71​=49×7=343=73.​

Dividing by 17\tfrac1771​ is multiplying by 777, so the exponent became 2−(−1)=32-(-1)=32−(−1)=3. In general, dividing by any power multiplies by its reciprocal, which reverses the exponent’s sign. The product rule then gives subtraction of exponents.

For a power of a power, a positive outer exponent repeats the inner power:

(5−1)2=15×15=125=5−2.(5^{-1})^2=\frac15\times\frac15=\frac1{25}=5^{-2}.(5−1)2=51​×51​=251​=5−2.

A negative outer exponent takes a reciprocal as well. For example,

(3−2)−2=(19)−2=92=81=34.\begin{aligned} (3^{-2})^{-2}&=\left(\frac19\right)^{-2}\\ &=9^2=81=3^4. \end{aligned}(3−2)−2​=(91​)−2=92=81=34.​

The exponent product is (−2)(−2)=4(-2)(-2)=4(−2)(−2)=4. Generally, repeating a reciprocal power repeats its denominator factors; taking a reciprocal flips the exponent’s sign. In both cases, the exponent counts multiply. A zero outer exponent gives 111, while a zero inner exponent makes the base of the outer power 111, so those cases agree too.

Powers of products and quotients also follow ordinary fraction arithmetic:

(2×7)−1=12×7=12×17.(2\times7)^{-1}=\frac1{2\times7}=\frac12\times\frac17.(2×7)−1=2×71​=21​×71​.

The two factors on the right are 2−12^{-1}2−1 and 7−17^{-1}7−1. For a quotient,

(47)−2=(74)2=4916,4−27−2=1/161/49=4916.\begin{aligned} \left(\frac47\right)^{-2}&=\left(\frac74\right)^2=\frac{49}{16},\\ \frac{4^{-2}}{7^{-2}}&=\frac{1/16}{1/49}=\frac{49}{16}. \end{aligned}(74​)−27−24−2​​=(47​)2=1649​,=1/491/16​=1649​.​

The numbers illustrate the general reason: reciprocating a product reciprocates each factor, and reciprocating a quotient reverses numerator and denominator. Positive powers then apply to each factor by the laws already proved. For exponent zero, both sides are 111. All bases and denominators in these extensions must be nonzero.

Worked example 4 Simplify a2a5\dfrac{a^2}{a^5}a5a2​ using only a positive exponent (with a≠0a \neq 0a=0)

The base is shared, so subtract the exponents, even though the bottom one is larger:

a2a5=a2−5=a−3.\frac{a^2}{a^5} = a^{2-5} = a^{-3}.a5a2​=a2−5=a−3.

That is already a single power, but a negative exponent is usually rewritten with a positive one by moving the power to the denominator:

a−3=1a3.a^{-3} = \frac{1}{a^3}.a−3=a31​.

So a2a5=1a3\dfrac{a^2}{a^5} = \dfrac{1}{a^3}a5a2​=a31​. Reading it directly agrees: two factors of aaa over five factors cancel two pairs, leaving three factors of aaa on the bottom.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Reading a zero exponent as zero. 90=19^0=190=1, not 000. The base must be nonzero.
  • Reading a negative exponent as a negative value. 2−3=1/82^{-3}=1/82−3=1/8, not −8-8−8.
  • Changing the exponent into a factor. 2−3=1/232^{-3}=1/2^32−3=1/23, not 1/(2×3)1/(2\times3)1/(2×3).
  • Ignoring the base’s sign or parentheses. (−2)−2=1/4(-2)^{-2}=1/4(−2)−2=1/4, while −2−2=−1/4-2^{-2}=-1/4−2−2=−1/4.
  • Reversing subtraction. 32/36=32−6=3−43^2/3^6=3^{2-6}=3^{-4}32/36=32−6=3−4, not 343^434.

Key takeaways

  • Zero exponent: a0=1a^0=1a0=1 for a≠0a\ne0a=0, so the quotient rule works for equal exponents. Here 000^000 stays undefined.
  • Negative exponent: a−n=1/ana^{-n}=1/a^na−n=1/an for a≠0a\ne0a=0 and positive whole-number nnn.
  • Fraction base: (a/b)−n=(b/a)n(a/b)^{-n}=(b/a)^n(a/b)−n=(b/a)n when a,b≠0a,b\ne0a,b=0.
  • Signs: a negative exponent asks for a reciprocal; it does not switch the sign of the corresponding positive power. Base sign and exponent sign have different jobs.
  • Laws: exponent laws extend to integer exponents for nonzero bases; the quotient rule no longer needs m>nm>nm>n.

A bit of history (optional)

In 1484, Nicolas Chuquet completed a handwritten arithmetic book in France. He experimented with superscript numbers to mark powers of an unknown quantity, including zero and negative powers. His symbols did not mean exactly what our numeric-base powers mean today, but he was already calculating beyond positive exponents.

Chuquet described himself as a Bachelor of Medicine. That degree did not qualify him to practice as a doctor; surviving records also describe him as a copyist and a calculator. His mathematical work had an unusually long wait for readers: the manuscript remained unpublished until 1880, almost four centuries after he wrote it.

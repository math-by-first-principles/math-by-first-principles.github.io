    1. The row does not stop at one . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    Powers of a single base can be written out in a row with the exponent dropping by one at each step to the right. While the exponents stay positive every entry can be multiplied out from the definition of a power, so the left-hand part of such a row can always be checked directly. What happens at the right-hand end, where the exponents reach zero and then go below it, is what this question is about.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Work out 636^{3}63, 626^{2}62 and 616^{1}61 as plain numbers and write them in a row in that order. Then state the single operation that carries each entry to the next one on its right, and use that operation once more to say what the entry after 616^{1}61, which is 606^{0}60, has to be.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Take the row two steps further, to the entries under the exponents −1-1−1 and −2-2−2, using the same operation at each new step. Give both entries as fractions, and describe what the entries are doing as the row carries on to the right.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A classmate agrees the row looks tidier carried on this way, but says the entries below 616^{1}61 are still a matter of choice, since nothing there has been multiplied out to check. Respond to the objection: say what one step to the right does to the exponent and to the value at every stage of the row, and settle whether those entries are a choice.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Ask what happens to an entry when its exponent drops by one. A factor of the base is being taken away, and taking a factor away is an operation you can perform on any number, not only on a whole-number power.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Nothing new is permitted at the two extra steps. Whatever carried 626^{2}62 to 616^{1}61 has to carry 616^{1}61 to the entry after it, and then do the same job once again.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Count the rules in play under each option. One rule that runs the whole way, against a rule that runs part of the way plus a separate decision for the rest.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    The row is 216216216, 363636, 666. Each step to the right divides by 666, so the next entry is 6÷6=16 \div 6 = 16÷6=1, giving 60=16^{0} = 160=1.

    Part B

    6−1=166^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}6−1=61​ and 6−2=1366^{-2} = \frac{1}{36}6−2=361​. The entries stay positive and keep shrinking, closing in on zero without reaching it.

    Part C

    They are not a choice. One step right always lowers the exponent by 111 and divides the value by 666, a single rule already verified on the part of the row that can be multiplied out. Any other entry below 616^{1}61 would need a second rule, starting exactly where the checking stops.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Each power comes straight from the definition, as a string of factors of the base:

    63=6×6×6=216,62=6×6=36,61=6.6^{3} = 6 \times 6 \times 6 = 216, \qquad 6^{2} = 6 \times 6 = 36, \qquad 6^{1} = 6.63=6×6×6=216,62=6×6=36,61=6.

    With the exponent falling, the row reads 216216216, 363636, 666. Now compare each entry with the one on its right. From 216216216 to 363636 the entry has been divided by 666, and from 363636 to 666 it has been divided by 666 again.

    That is no coincidence. Dropping the exponent by one removes one factor of the base, and removing a factor is exactly dividing by it. So one step to the right means one division by 666, and the entry after 616^{1}61 is found the same way as every entry before it:

    6÷6=1,so60=1.6 \div 6 = 1, \qquad \text{so} \qquad 6^{0} = 1.6÷6=1,so60=1.

    Nothing had to be assumed about what a zero exponent means. The row was simply continued by the operation it was already running on.

    Part B

    The operation does not change at the new steps, so keep dividing by 666:

    1÷6=16,16÷6=136.1 \div 6 = \frac{1}{6}, \qquad \frac{1}{6} \div 6 = \frac{1}{36}.1÷6=61​,61​÷6=361​.

    Those two entries sit under the exponents −1-1−1 and −2-2−2:

    6−1=16,6−2=136.6^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}, \qquad 6^{-2} = \frac{1}{36}.6−1=61​,6−2=361​.

    The row now reads 216216216, 363636, 666, 111, 16\tfrac{1}{6}61​, 136\tfrac{1}{36}361​. Every entry is positive. Dividing a positive number by 666 makes it smaller, but it can never carry the result down past zero, so the entries close in on zero from above and never cross it. A negative exponent is producing a small positive fraction here, not a negative number, and it is the divide step that guarantees it.

    Part C

    The row runs on one rule, and that rule has two halves which always move together: one step to the right lowers the exponent by 111, and one step to the right divides the value by 666.

    6n÷6=6n−1.6^{n} \div 6 = 6^{n-1}.6n÷6=6n−1.

    On the left-hand part of the row this is not a guess. Each of 216216216, 363636 and 666 can be multiplied out straight from the definition of a power, and the divide-by-666 step is then observed to hold between them. So the rule is not being invented for the new entries; it is already in force where it can be tested.

    Now suppose someone hands 606^{0}60 a value other than 111. The exponent half of the rule still applies, since the exponent does drop from 111 to 000. It is the value half that would have to be abandoned, at exactly that step, and abandoned again at every step after it. The cost is a second rule, taking over precisely where the multiplying out runs out, with nothing to recommend it except that the checking stopped there.

    So the classmate has the situation backwards. Keeping one rule for the whole row leaves exactly one entry available at each new step, and those entries are 111, then 16\tfrac{1}{6}61​, then 136\tfrac{1}{36}361​. What the classmate is right about is that nothing below 616^{1}61 can be multiplied out. That is the point: there is nothing there to multiply out, which is why the rule has to settle the value instead.

    In one line

    The row reads 216216216, 363636, 666, 111, 16\tfrac{1}{6}61​, 136\tfrac{1}{36}361​ for the exponents 333 down to −2-2−2, because one step to the right always divides by 666. The entries below 616^{1}61 are forced by that single rule rather than chosen: they stay positive and shrink toward zero, so 60=16^{0} = 160=1, 6−1=166^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{6}6−1=61​ and 6−2=1366^{-2} = \tfrac{1}{36}6−2=361​.

    Another way: Read the row from right to left instead

    Every step to the right divides by 666, so every step to the LEFT multiplies by 666. Start at the far right of the finished row and walk back up it:

    136×6=16,16×6=1,1×6=6.\frac{1}{36} \times 6 = \frac{1}{6}, \qquad \frac{1}{6} \times 6 = 1, \qquad 1 \times 6 = 6.361​×6=61​,61​×6=1,1×6=6.

    The last of those is the check worth noticing: walking left from the entry under 606^{0}60 has to land on 61=66^{1} = 661=6, and only the entry 111 does that, since 111 is the one number that 666 multiplies up to itself.

    When it is worth it When you want the new entries tested against a power you can already multiply out, rather than derived from one. It is also the quickest way to see that a wrong entry at 606^{0}60 would break the row in both directions, not just going right.

    2. One quotient, two readings . Foundational, 11 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 11 pts

    The quotient rule says that aman=am−n\dfrac{a^{m}}{a^{n}} = a^{m-n}anam​=am−n, and it was proved by cancelling shared factors while the exponent on top was the larger one. A quotient does not have to be built that way. When the two exponents are equal, or when the larger one is underneath, the rule still produces something, and the quotient is still an ordinary fraction that can be worked out on its own.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Work out 5454\dfrac{5^{4}}{5^{4}}5454​ twice: once as an ordinary fraction, using no exponent rule at all, and once by subtracting the exponents. Then write down what the two readings together tell you about 505^{0}50.

    2. B

Then read the same quotient by subtracting the exponents and set the two results side by side.

    3. C

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

    The answer

    Part A

    As a fraction it is 625625=1\frac{625}{625} = 1625625​=1; by subtracting exponents it is 54−4=505^{4-4} = 5^{0}54−4=50. One quotient cannot have two values, so 50=15^{0} = 150=1.

    Part B

    Cancelling leaves 153=1125\frac{1}{5^{3}} = \frac{1}{125}531​=1251​, while subtracting exponents gives 53−6=5−35^{3-6} = 5^{-3}53−6=5−3. Side by side they say 5−3=11255^{-3} = \frac{1}{125}5−3=1251​.

    Part C

    A quotient is one number, so two correct readings of it cannot disagree. The fraction reading pins the value because it never uses the rule being extended. At base 000 the step that fails is dividing a number by itself to get 111, since 00\tfrac{0}{0}00​ is not 111 and is not any other number either.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    First the ordinary fraction. Since 54=6255^{4} = 62554=625, the top and the bottom are the same number, and a nonzero number divided by itself is 111:

    5454=625625=1.\frac{5^{4}}{5^{4}} = \frac{625}{625} = 1.5454​=625625​=1.

    Notice that this reading uses nothing from this lesson or the last one. It is division, and it would have been available long before powers were introduced.

    Now the same quotient through the rule, which subtracts the exponent underneath from the exponent on top:

    5454=54−4=50.\frac{5^{4}}{5^{4}} = 5^{4-4} = 5^{0}.5454​=54−4=50.

    The two readings start from identical left-hand sides, so their right-hand sides describe the same number:

    50=1.5^{0} = 1.50=1.

    Part B

    There are three factors of 555 on top and six underneath, so three pairs cancel:

    5356=5×5×55×5×5×5×5×5=15×5×5=153=1125.\frac{5^{3}}{5^{6}} = \frac{5 \times 5 \times 5}{5 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5} = \frac{1}{5 \times 5 \times 5} = \frac{1}{5^{3}} = \frac{1}{125}.5653​=5×5×5×5×5×55×5×5​=5×5×51​=531​=1251​.

    When every factor on top has been cancelled, what is left there is 111, not nothing: cancelling replaces each pair by 111, and the top is a product of those.

    The rule reads the very same quotient by subtracting the exponents, and this time the subtraction runs past zero:

    5356=53−6=5−3.\frac{5^{3}}{5^{6}} = 5^{3-6} = 5^{-3}.5653​=53−6=5−3.

    Setting the two results beside each other, and remembering they are two descriptions of one quotient,

    5−3=153=1125.5^{-3} = \frac{1}{5^{3}} = \frac{1}{125}.5−3=531​=1251​.

    So the negative exponent has turned out to mean the reciprocal of the matching positive power. It was not decided; it was read off.

    Part C

    Why the shape of the argument is binding. Each part wrote down one expression, 5454\dfrac{5^{4}}{5^{4}}5454​ or 5356\dfrac{5^{3}}{5^{6}}5653​, and then described it twice. An expression like that stands for a single number, so two correct descriptions of it cannot name different numbers. That is what turns a pair of readings into an equation:

    5356=1125and5356=5−3.\frac{5^{3}}{5^{6}} = \frac{1}{125} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{5^{3}}{5^{6}} = 5^{-3}.5653​=1251​and5653​=5−3.

    Which reading does the pinning. Not both equally. The fraction reading is entitled to settle the matter because it uses only cancelling and ordinary division, neither of which mentions a zero or negative exponent. It was available before the question was even asked. The rule's reading is the one on trial: it produces a symbol nobody has yet given a meaning to. So the fraction supplies the value and the rule supplies the name, and the definition is what pins the name to the value. Had the fraction reading itself relied on the meaning of 5−35^{-3}5−3, the argument would have been circular and would have proved nothing.

    Where a base of 000 breaks it. In part A the fraction reading turns on the fact that a number divided by itself is 111. With base 000 that step reads

    0404=00,\frac{0^{4}}{0^{4}} = \frac{0}{0},0404​=00​,

    and 00\tfrac{0}{0}00​ is not 111. It is not any number at all: it would have to be a number that multiplies 000 back up to 000, and every number does that, so nothing picks one out. With nothing definite standing on the left, there is nothing to pin the name to, which is exactly why 000^{0}00 is left undefined while every other base gets a value.

    In one line

    5454\dfrac{5^{4}}{5^{4}}5454​ is 111 and is also 505^{0}50, so 50=15^{0} = 150=1. 5356\dfrac{5^{3}}{5^{6}}5653​ is 1125\dfrac{1}{125}1251​ and is also 5−35^{-3}5−3, so 5−3=153=11255^{-3} = \dfrac{1}{5^{3}} = \dfrac{1}{125}5−3=531​=1251​. Each value is forced, because one quotient cannot have two values and the fraction reading never uses the rule being extended. The base must be nonzero, since 00\tfrac{0}{0}00​ is not 111.

    Answer and solution

    The answer

    Part A

    149\frac{1}{49}491​, 149\frac{1}{49}491​ and −149-\frac{1}{49}−491​, in that order. The parentheses make −7-7−7 the base; without them the exponent belongs to the 777 alone and the minus sign stays outside the power.

    Part B

    Positive, positive, negative, negative, in that order. A minus sign in the exponent only calls for a reciprocal and never sets the sign. The sign comes from the base: a power of a negative base is positive when the exponent is even and negative when it is odd, and a minus sign outside a power negates whatever that power came to.

    Part C

    It fails: 7−2=1497^{-2} = \tfrac{1}{49}7−2=491​ carries a minus sign and is positive, and (−7)−2=149(-7)^{-2} = \tfrac{1}{49}(−7)−2=491​ carries two and is still positive.     Worked solution

    Part A

    Handle each expression by asking first what the base is, then doing the reciprocal and the power as separate steps.

    In 7−27^{-2}7−2 the base is 777. The negative exponent calls for the reciprocal of the matching positive power:

    7−2=172=149.7^{-2} = \frac{1}{7^{2}} = \frac{1}{49}.7−2=721​=491​.

    In (−7)−2(-7)^{-2}(−7)−2 the parentheses put the minus sign inside the base, so the base is −7-7−7 and the whole of it is used twice. Two negative factors multiply to a positive:

    (−7)−2=1(−7)2=1(−7)×(−7)=149.(-7)^{-2} = \frac{1}{(-7)^{2}} = \frac{1}{(-7) \times (-7)} = \frac{1}{49}.(−7)−2=(−7)21​=(−7)×(−7)1​=491​.

    In −7−2-7^{-2}−7−2 there are no parentheses, so the exponent is attached to the 777 alone and the leading minus sign waits outside for the power to be finished:

    −7−2=−(7−2)=−149.-7^{-2} = -\left(7^{-2}\right) = -\frac{1}{49}.−7−2=−(7−2)=−491​.

    So the three values are 149\tfrac{1}{49}491​, 149\tfrac{1}{49}491​ and −149-\tfrac{1}{49}−491​. The only thing that changed between the second and the third was a pair of parentheses, and it changed which number was being raised to the power.

    Part B

    Take the four in turn, deciding on sight.

    7−27^{-2}7−2: the base is 777, a positive number, so every factor is positive and so is the reciprocal of the result. Positive.

    (−7)−2(-7)^{-2}(−7)−2: the base is −7-7−7 and the exponent is even. Negative factors pair off, and each pair multiplies to a positive:

    (−7)×(−7)=49,(−7)×(−7)×(−7)=−343.(-7) \times (-7) = 49, \qquad (-7) \times (-7) \times (-7) = -343.(−7)×(−7)=49,(−7)×(−7)×(−7)=−343.

    So an even count of negative factors leaves nothing negative behind. Taking the reciprocal of a positive number keeps it positive. Positive.

    −7−2-7^{-2}−7−2: the power itself is 7−27^{-2}7−2, which is positive, and the minus sign outside then negates it. Negative.

    (−7)−3(-7)^{-3}(−7)−3: the base is −7-7−7 and the exponent is odd, so one minus sign is left over after the pairing, as the second display above shows. The reciprocal of a negative number is negative, since the number and its reciprocal multiply to a positive 111. Negative.

    Comparing the two kinds of minus sign, they do completely different jobs. The one in the exponent decides only whether the power is flipped over into a reciprocal, and flipping a number over never changes its sign. The one belonging to the base decides whether the factors being multiplied are negative, and then the parity of the exponent decides whether those minus signs pair off. A minus sign left outside the power is a third thing again: it is not part of the power at all, and it simply reverses the sign of whatever the power comes to.

    Part C

    First counterexample: the minus sign is only in the exponent. Take 7−27^{-2}7−2. A minus sign certainly appears in it, so the rule predicts a negative value. But

    7−2=172=149,7^{-2} = \frac{1}{7^{2}} = \frac{1}{49},7−2=721​=491​,

    which is positive. One counterexample already retires the rule.

    Second counterexample, of a different kind: the base carries a minus sign too. Take (−7)−2(-7)^{-2}(−7)−2. Now there are two minus signs, so on the revision sheet's reading the case ought to be even clearer. Yet

    (−7)−2=1(−7)2=149,(-7)^{-2} = \frac{1}{(-7)^{2}} = \frac{1}{49},(−7)−2=(−7)21​=491​,

    also positive. This one matters because it blocks the obvious patch. Somebody might try to save the rule by saying a minus sign counts only when it is on the base; this case has one there, and the value is still positive.

    A corrected rule. What actually decides the sign is which number is being multiplied and how many times, so state it that way. For a nonzero base written in parentheses, the power is negative exactly when the base is negative and the exponent is odd.

  • States a corrected rule and then tests its own two counterexamples against it, rather than asserting that the new rule holds. 3

  • Turns each instruction into a power of 444 with the correct sign on the exponent. 2
  • Evaluates both, giving the shrinking multiplier as a fraction. 1
  • Says what each multiplier means for a line on the screen, in the language of the drawing rather than as a bare number. 1

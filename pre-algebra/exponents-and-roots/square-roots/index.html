Square Roots

Intuition

You already know how to square a number: 52=255^2 = 2552=25. A square root runs that machine in reverse. It starts from the answer and asks for the input: “what number, multiplied by itself, gives 252525?” The answer is 555, so the square root of 252525 is 555. Many operations in arithmetic come paired this way, each one undoing another: addition undoes subtraction, multiplication undoes division, and squaring undoes square roots, at least when you start from a number that is zero or positive (more on that soon). The picture to keep in mind is a square tile: if a square holds 252525 unit tiles, its side is 555. So taking a square root is finding the side length of a square from its area.

Learning goals

  • Undo squaring to evaluate square roots of perfect squares, including the side of a square from its area
  • Explain why the radical sign returns only the non-negative root
  • Pull a perfect-square factor out with ab=ab\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}ab​=a​b​ for non-negative aaa and bbb
  • Trap a non-perfect-square root between neighboring perfect squares
  • Recognize why 2\sqrt{2}2​ is irrational, and why an exact radical is not the same as a rounded decimal

Squaring, and undoing it

Squaring a number means multiplying it by itself. The results, starting from 111, are the perfect squares:

12=1,22=4,32=9,42=16,52=25,62=36,…1^2 = 1, \quad 2^2 = 4, \quad 3^2 = 9, \quad 4^2 = 16, \quad 5^2 = 25, \quad 6^2 = 36, \quad \ldots12=1,22=4,32=9,42=16,52=25,62=36,…

A perfect square is any whole number you can reach by squaring a whole number, so 0,1,4,9,16,25,36,49,64,81,1000, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 1000,1,4,9,16,25,36,49,64,81,100 are the first eleven of them (02=00^2 = 002=0 counts, even though it takes no work to see). The square root of a number asks the reverse question. Given a number, what do you square to get it? The square root of 363636 is 666, because 62=366^2 = 3662=36. The square root of 100100100 is 101010, because 102=10010^2 = 100102=100.

We write a square root with the radical sign x\sqrt{\phantom{x}}x​. The number tucked under it is called the radicand. So

25=5means52=25,\sqrt{25} = 5 \quad\text{means}\quad 5^2 = 25,25​=5means52=25,

read aloud as “the square root of twenty-five is five.” Squaring and square-rooting are inverse operations: on numbers that are zero or more, each one undoes the other. Square 555 to get 252525, then take the square root of 252525 and you are back to 555. Starting from a negative number the round trip does not bring it back. Squaring −5-5−5 gives 252525, and the square root of 252525 is 555, not −5-5−5. In general the square root of x2x^2x2 is the size of xxx, which means xxx with any minus sign dropped.

Because the radical undoes the square, the two cancel when you stack them. Start from 72\sqrt{7^2}72​: the radical hands back the number that was squared, so 72=7\sqrt{7^2} = 772​=7. You never need the middle step of working out that 727^272 is 494949 and then rooting 494949 instead. Stacking them in the other order lands in the same place, since (49)2=72=49\left(\sqrt{49}\right)^2 = 7^2 = 49(49​)2=72=49. So for any non-negative number aaa,

a2=aand(a)2=a.\sqrt{a^2} = a \qquad\text{and}\qquad \left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2 = a.a2​=aand(a​)2=a.

Why the root is the non-negative one

There is a subtlety hiding in the question “what squares to 252525?” The obvious answer is 555. But −5-5−5 also works, because a negative times a negative is positive:

52=25and(−5)2=(−5)×(−5)=25.5^2 = 25 \qquad\text{and}\qquad (-5)^2 = (-5)\times(-5) = 25.52=25and(−5)2=(−5)×(−5)=25.

So two different numbers, 555 and −5-5−5, both square to 252525: the equation x2=25x^2 = 25x2=25 has both x=5x = 5x=5 and x=−5x = -5x=−5 as solutions. If the radical sign returned both, then 25\sqrt{25}25​ would not name a single number, and you could not safely write it inside a calculation. To keep the square root a single, well-defined value, mathematicians make a choice: the radical sign always returns the non-negative root. This chosen value is called the principal square root, so 25=5\sqrt{25} = 525​=5 names one number even though the equation x2=25x^2 = 25x2=25 has two solutions. The same choice works for any positive number, and the one radicand that needs no choice at all is 000 itself, since 02=00^2 = 002=0 gives 0=0\sqrt{0} = 00​=0.

The reasoning above works for 252525, but nothing about 252525 was special. The Go deeper section checks it again on 363636, then carries the same argument through any positive number aaa.

Roots of negative numbers

What about −9\sqrt{-9}−9​? No real number squares to a negative, because squaring any real number, positive or negative, gives a result that is zero or positive. So −9\sqrt{-9}−9​ is not a real number at all, and it is outside the scope of this lesson. Square roots here always have a radicand that is zero or positive.

The radical sign returns the principal (non-negative) root, so the answer is 999 alone, not −9-9−9 and not ±9\pm 9±9. And it is certainly not 81÷2=40.581 \div 2 = 40.581÷2=40.5: a square root is not half the number.

Roots of perfect squares

When the radicand is a perfect square, the root is a whole number, and you find it by recognizing which number was squared. It pays to know the first several perfect squares by sight, because then their roots come for free:

nnn111222333444555666777888999101010111111121212
n2n^2n2111444999161616252525363636494949646464818181100100100121121121144144144

Reading the table from the bottom row up gives you the square root directly. Since 144144144 sits under 121212, you know 144=12\sqrt{144} = 12144​=12. Since 494949 sits under 777, you know 49=7\sqrt{49} = 749​=7.

In the figure below you set the two sides of a rectangle, and the readout counts the unit squares it holds.

Make the width and the height equal and you have built a square, so its area is one side multiplied by itself. Step that square up from 222 by 222 and you will generate the start of the table above (444, then 999, 161616, 252525, 363636). In each of those squares, the side length is the root of the area. Then break the equality on purpose: set the rectangle 999 wide and 333 tall, and its area of 272727 lands between two perfect squares. Those squares are 252525 and 363636, and that trapping is exactly how this lesson later estimates a root that is not a whole number. It is only the square’s side, not just any rectangle’s, that is the square root of the area: an unequal rectangle can land on a perfect-square count too, like 222 by 888 giving 161616, without either side being 16\sqrt{16}16​.

Worked example 1 Evaluate 64\sqrt{64}64​ and 121\sqrt{121}121​

For each one, find the whole number that squares to the radicand.

For 64\sqrt{64}64​, search the perfect squares for 646464. Since 8×8=648 \times 8 = 648×8=64,

64=8.\sqrt{64} = 8.64​=8.

For 121\sqrt{121}121​, try it yourself before reading on: which whole number times itself gives 121121121? Since 11×11=12111 \times 11 = 12111×11=121,

121=11.\sqrt{121} = 11.121​=11.

Squaring each answer lands back on its radicand: 82=648^2 = 6482=64 and 112=12111^2 = 121112=121, both correct.

The product of two perfect squares is itself a perfect square, and its root is the product of the roots. For instance 400=20\sqrt{400} = 20400​=20 because 400=4×100400 = 4 \times 100400=4×100 and 4×100=2×10=20\sqrt{4} \times \sqrt{100} = 2 \times 10 = 204​×100​=2×10=20.

The product rule for square roots

Square roots of a product can be split apart. For non-negative numbers aaa and bbb,

a×b=a×b.\sqrt{a \times b} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}.a×b​=a​×b​.

This is true because squaring the right-hand side returns a×ba \times ba×b. So the right-hand side is the non-negative number whose square is a×ba \times ba×b, which is exactly what a×b\sqrt{a \times b}a×b​ means. Check it on 999 and 494949: their roots are 333 and 777, and squaring that product puts both radicands back, (3×7)2=3×3×7×7=9×49(3\times7)^2 = 3\times3\times7\times7 = 9\times49(3×7)2=3×3×7×7=9×49, so 9×49=9×49\sqrt{9\times49} = \sqrt{9}\times\sqrt{49}9×49​=9​×49​.

The check above works for any radicands, not just 999 and 494949. The Go deeper section carries the same argument through any non-negative aaa and bbb.

See the full proof

Division splits the same way: ab=ab\sqrt{\tfrac{a}{b}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}ba​​=b​a​​, provided aaa is zero or more and bbb is greater than zero. The same squaring argument carries it, since (ab)2=ab\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}\right)^2 = \dfrac{a}{b}(b​a​​)2=ba​. So a quotient of square roots combines or splits just like a product.

This rule lets you simplify a root by pulling out any perfect-square factor hiding inside the radicand. The trick is to split the radicand into a perfect square times whatever is left.

Worked example 2 Simplify 12\sqrt{12}12​

The number 121212 is not a perfect square, but it contains the perfect-square factor 444, since 12=4×312 = 4 \times 312=4×3. Split the radical with the product rule:

12=4×3=4×3.\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4 \times 3} = \sqrt{4}\times\sqrt{3}.12​=4×3​=4​×3​.

Now 4=2\sqrt{4} = 24​=2 is a whole number, while 3\sqrt{3}3​ has no perfect-square factor greater than 111, so it stays as it is:

12=23.\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}.12​=23​.

Here 232\sqrt{3}23​ means 2×32 \times \sqrt{3}2×3​, written without the times sign the way 2x2x2x means 2×x2 \times x2×x. The form is considered simpler because the largest perfect square has been taken out from under the radical. Pulling out the largest perfect-square factor finishes the job in one step; had you used 12=1×1212 = 1 \times 1212=1×12 you would make no progress, since 111 is a perfect square but removing it changes nothing.

Estimating roots that are not whole numbers

Most whole-number radicands are not perfect squares, so their roots are not whole numbers. You can still pin a root down closely by trapping it between the two perfect squares it falls between. Take 10\sqrt{10}10​. The perfect squares on either side of 101010 are 999 and 161616:

9<10<16.9 < 10 < 16.9<10<16.

Taking the square root of all three keeps the order, because a bigger number has a bigger root:

9<10<16,that is3<10<4.\sqrt{9} < \sqrt{10} < \sqrt{16}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad 3 < \sqrt{10} < 4.9​<10​<16​,that is3<10​<4.

You can say more: since 101010 is much closer to 999 than to 161616, its root sits close to 333, just a little above it. A quick check confirms that estimate: 3.22=10.243.2^2 = 10.243.22=10.24 is already a bit over 101010, so 10\sqrt{10}10​ is a little under 3.23.23.2. (A calculator gives 10≈3.16\sqrt{10} \approx 3.1610​≈3.16.)

The method is general: to estimate the square root of any whole number that is not a perfect square, find the nearest perfect square below the radicand and the nearest one above it. The root lies between their two whole-number roots, and it leans toward whichever perfect square the radicand is closer to.

Worked example 3 Between which two whole numbers does 50\sqrt{50}50​ lie?

Hunt for the perfect squares that bracket 505050. Counting up the squares, 49=7249 = 7^249=72 sits just below 505050, and 64=8264 = 8^264=82 is the next one above:

49<50<64.49 < 50 < 64.49<50<64.

Take the square root across the inequality, which preserves the order:

49<50<64,so7<50<8.\sqrt{49} < \sqrt{50} < \sqrt{64}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad 7 < \sqrt{50} < 8.49​<50​<64​,so7<50​<8.

So 50\sqrt{50}50​ lies between 777 and 888. Because 505050 is only just past 494949, the root is very close to 777 (in fact about 7.077.077.07).

Why most square roots are irrational

When you estimate 10\sqrt{10}10​ you can keep refining: 3.13.13.1, then 3.163.163.16, then 3.1623.1623.162, and the decimal never settles and never falls into a repeating pattern. A square root of a whole number that is not a perfect square is an irrational number. Its decimal expansion runs on forever without ever repeating, so it can never be written exactly as a fraction or as a terminating decimal.

You met this idea when fractions and decimals were compared. A fraction always turns into a decimal that either stops or repeats, while an irrational number does neither. Numbers like 2=1.41421356…\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots2​=1.41421356… and 3=1.7320508…\sqrt{3} = 1.7320508\ldots3​=1.7320508… are the classic examples. The impossibility can be proved, as the Greeks first did for 2\sqrt{2}2​ (the history below tells that story). This is why we usually leave such a root in its exact radical form, writing 2\sqrt{2}2​ rather than a rounded decimal.

Exact versus approximate

There is nothing wrong with a rounded value like 2≈1.41\sqrt{2}\approx 1.412​≈1.41 when you need a number to measure or compute with. Just keep the two ideas separate: 2\sqrt{2}2​ is the exact root. The number 1.411.411.41 is a convenient approximation that is slightly off, since 1.412=1.98811.41^2 = 1.98811.412=1.9881, not quite 222.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Halving instead of rooting. 16\sqrt{16}16​ is 444, not 16÷2=816 \div 2 = 816÷2=8. A square root asks what number squared gives the radicand; it is not half of it. Test your answer by squaring it back.
  • Giving the negative root. 16=4\sqrt{16} = 416​=4 only. The radical returns the principal (non-negative) root, so it is not −4-4−4 and not ±4\pm 4±4, even though (−4)2=16(-4)^2 = 16(−4)2=16 is also true.
  • Splitting a root across a sum. a+b\sqrt{a + b}a+b​ is not a+b\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}a​+b​. For example 9+16=25=5\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 59+16​=25​=5, but 9+16=3+4=7\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 79​+16​=3+4=7. The product rule splits a product, never a sum.
  • Calling an irrational root a fraction. 2\sqrt{2}2​ cannot be written exactly as a fraction or a terminating decimal. Its decimal runs on forever without repeating, so 1.411.411.41 is only an approximation, never the exact value.

Key takeaways

  • The square root a\sqrt{a}a​ is the inverse of squaring: it is the non-negative number whose square is aaa. Squaring and rooting undo each other, so a2=a\sqrt{a^2} = aa2​=a for non-negative aaa.
  • The radical sign returns the principal (non-negative) root, so 25=5\sqrt{25} = 525​=5, never −5-5−5 or ±5\pm 5±5. That is because both 555 and −5-5−5 square to 252525, and we pick the non-negative one.
  • A perfect square has a whole-number root you can read off: 49=7\sqrt{49} = 749​=7, 100=10\sqrt{100} = 10100​=10, 144=12\sqrt{144} = 12144​=12.
  • The product rule ab=a b\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}ab​=a​b​ holds when aaa and bbb are both zero or more. It lets you pull a perfect-square factor out of a radical, so 12=4 3=23\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4}\,\sqrt{3} = 2\sqrt{3}12​=4​3​=23​.
  • Estimate a non-perfect-square root by trapping it between neighboring perfect squares: since 9<10<169 < 10 < 169<10<16, you get 3<10<43 < \sqrt{10} < 43<10​<4.
  • The square root of a non-perfect-square whole number is irrational: its decimal never ends and never repeats. That is why 2\sqrt{2}2​ is best left in exact radical form.
  • −9\sqrt{-9}−9​ is not a real number, since no real number squares to a negative.

Why a\sqrt{a}a​ is defined as the non-negative root, in general

The lesson gives the reason for 252525: since 555 and −5-5−5 both square to 252525, the radical sign has to choose one of them, and it chooses the non-negative one. Here is that same choice checked on a second number, and then written so it covers every positive number at once.

Why a\sqrt{a}a​ is defined as the non-negative root#

Start with 363636 and ask which numbers square to give it. One of them is 666, since 62=366^2 = 3662=36, and its opposite works just as well:

(−6)2=(−6)×(−6)=36.(-6)^2 = (-6)\times(-6) = 36.(−6)2=(−6)×(−6)=36.

So 363636 has two square roots, 666 and −6-6−6, one positive and one negative. That is a problem for notation: a symbol standing for 666 and −6-6−6 at the same time could not be used in an equation. You would never know which value was meant. The fix is to single out one of the pair by a fixed rule. So 36\sqrt{36}36​ is defined to mean 666, the member that is not negative. The other root is still available, written separately as −36=−6-\sqrt{36} = -6−36​=−6.

Nothing in that argument depended on the number being 363636. Take any positive number aaa. If some number xxx satisfies x2=ax^2 = ax2=a, then its opposite also works, because

(−x)2=(−x)×(−x)=x2=a.(-x)^2 = (-x)\times(-x) = x^2 = a.(−x)2=(−x)×(−x)=x2=a.

The solutions again come in a matched pair, so we define a\sqrt{a}a​ to be the member that is zero or positive. With that rule the symbol names exactly one number every time, and the negative solution is written as −a-\sqrt{a}−a​ when it is wanted.

∎

Why ab=a b\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}ab​=a​b​ for non-negative aaa and bbb

The lesson checks the product rule on 999 and 494949. This is that same check carried through any non-negative aaa and bbb, for anyone who wants to see the general argument done in full.

Why ab=a b\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}ab​=a​b​ for non-negative aaa and bbb#

The core lesson checks this on 999 and 494949: their roots are 333 and 777, and squaring the product 212121 puts both radicands back, 212=9×49=44121^2 = 9\times49 = 441212=9×49=441. Nothing in that check used the particular radicands, only the squaring, so the same argument carries through any non-negative aaa and bbb. Let aaa and bbb be zero or positive, so that a\sqrt{a}a​ and b\sqrt{b}b​ both exist and are non-negative. Look at the product a×b\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}a​×b​ and square it. Multiplication can be reordered freely, so

(a×b)2=a×a×b×b=(a)2×(b)2=a×b.\left(\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}\right)^2 = \sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}\times\sqrt{b} = \left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2 \times \left(\sqrt{b}\right)^2 = a \times b.(a​×b​)2=a​×a​×b​×b​=(a​)2×(b​)2=a×b.

So a×b\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}a​×b​ is a number that squares to a×ba \times ba×b. It is also non-negative, because a product of two non-negative numbers is non-negative. But the non-negative number whose square is a×ba \times ba×b is precisely what the symbol a×b\sqrt{a \times b}a×b​ stands for. Two non-negative numbers with the same square must be equal, so

a×b=a×b.\sqrt{a \times b} = \sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}.a×b​=a​×b​.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

Draw a square one unit wide and rule in its diagonal. You can see that line and measure it, so it clearly has a length. Writing that length as a fraction turns out to be impossible. So does writing it as a decimal that ends.

The school of Pythagoras, a Greek teacher from twenty-five centuries ago, held that every length was a ratio of two whole numbers. One of them proved the diagonal is not. The trouble is not sloppy measuring. No fraction at all equals 2\sqrt{2}2​, and no finer ruler will change that.

The discovery is said to have shaken the school badly. Legend has the man who told it drowned at sea. The story is almost surely made up. The unease behind it was real, because an ordinary line had escaped their arithmetic.

That is why a root is left standing under the radical sign. The decimal 1.411.411.41 is a shade too small, so it is never quite the number you want. The exact length of that diagonal is 2\sqrt{2}2​, and the radical sign is how you write it without rounding.

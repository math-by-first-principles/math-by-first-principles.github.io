    1. Undoing a square, and the sign that gets lost . Foundational, 12 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 12 pts

    Squaring and taking a square root are partners: each is supposed to undo the other. Most partnerships in arithmetic run both ways without comment, and this one does not quite. This question evaluates three roots, then examines what the radical sign has to promise before it can be written inside a calculation at all, and how far the undoing can be trusted.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Evaluate 441\sqrt{441}441​ by splitting the radicand into a product of two perfect squares. Then evaluate 232\sqrt{23^2}232​ and (38)2\left(\sqrt{38}\right)^2(38​)2 without working out either square, and state the check that confirms the first of the three.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      A classmate objects that a square root question never has one single answer, because two different whole numbers square to 484484484. Write down both of those numbers. Say which one the symbol 484\sqrt{484}484​ stands for, explain what would go wrong with the notation if the radical were allowed to name both, and show how the other number is written when it is the one you want.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Riya writes down a rule of her own: "squaring and rooting undo each other, so x2=x\sqrt{x^2} = xx2​=x whatever number xxx is." Decide whether that rule holds for every number. If it does, say what makes it safe everywhere; if it does not, give a value of xxx that shows it, work out what each side comes to there, and repair it so that what you end with is true for every number.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing in this question needs a calculator. One of the three values in part A falls out of the product rule once you spot the perfect-square factors hiding in the radicand, the other two need no arithmetic at all, and everything after that turns on what the radical sign is permitted to hand back.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Ask what would happen to a piece of working that contained a symbol standing for two numbers at once. Try adding one to it, and see whether what you get is still a number.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      A claim about every number is only as strong as its worst case. The values that come to mind first are the ones that were never in any danger, so go looking for the ones that were.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Take the first one by hunting for perfect-square factors. The radicand is odd, so 444 and 100100100 are no use, but its digits add to 999, so it is divisible by 999, and 441÷9=49441 \div 9 = 49441÷9=49. Both factors are perfect squares, so the product rule finishes the job:

    441=9×49=9×49=3×7=21.\sqrt{441} = \sqrt{9 \times 49} = \sqrt{9}\times\sqrt{49} = 3 \times 7 = 21.441​=9×49​=9​×49​=3×7=21.

    The check is to square the answer and see whether the radicand comes back, and it does: 212=44121^2 = 441212=441.

    The other two need no arithmetic at all, because the square and the radical are inverse operations and cancel whenever the number underneath is not negative:

    232=23and(38)2=38.\sqrt{23^2} = 23 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \left(\sqrt{38}\right)^2 = 38.232​=23and(38​)2=38.

    Working out 232=52923^2 = 529232=529 and then hunting for the root of 529529529 would reach the same place after far more work, and the second one cannot be done that way at all, since 38\sqrt{38}38​ cannot be evaluated as a whole number or as a decimal that stops. The radical form records it exactly, but there is nothing to compute before squaring it. That the middle step can be skipped is what calling the two operations inverse actually buys you.

    Part B

    First find the pair. Since 484=4×121484 = 4 \times 121484=4×121 and both factors are perfect squares,

    484=4×121=2×11=22.\sqrt{484} = \sqrt{4}\times\sqrt{121} = 2 \times 11 = 22.484​=4​×121​=2×11=22.

    The classmate is right that 222222 is not alone in squaring to 484484484, because a negative times a negative is positive:

    222=484and(−22)2=(−22)×(−22)=484.22^2 = 484 \qquad\text{and}\qquad (-22)^2 = (-22)\times(-22) = 484.222=484and(−22)2=(−22)×(−22)=484.

    So the question "what squares to 484484484?" genuinely has two answers. What cannot have two answers is the symbol. If 484\sqrt{484}484​ named both numbers, then 484+1\sqrt{484} + 1484​+1 would be 232323 and −21-21−21 at the same time, and no line of working containing a radical could be checked, or even read. A piece of notation that names two numbers is not a number, and only a number can be carried through a calculation.

    The agreement that fixes this is to hand back the non-negative member of the pair, the principal square root, so 484=22\sqrt{484} = 22484​=22 and nothing else. The negative solution has not been lost, only moved out of the symbol and onto the page in front of it:

    −484=−22.-\sqrt{484} = -22.−484​=−22.

    Both facts then live side by side without contradicting each other: two numbers square to 484484484, and the radical names one of them.

    Part C

    Test the rule where nobody checked it. The lesson states this cancelling for a number that is zero or positive, and Riya has quietly dropped that condition, so try a negative value. Take x=−17x = -17x=−17:

    (−17)2=(−17)×(−17)=289,289=17.(-17)^2 = (-17)\times(-17) = 289, \qquad \sqrt{289} = 17.(−17)2=(−17)×(−17)=289,289​=17.

    Her left side is 171717 and her right side is −17-17−17, so the rule is false as written. One counterexample is enough to bring down a claim about every number, and this one is not a freak case: the same thing happens for every negative xxx, because squaring destroys the sign and the radical is required to come back on the non-negative side.

    There are two honest repairs.

    The narrow one puts the missing condition back: x2=x\sqrt{x^2} = xx2​=x whenever xxx is zero or positive. That is the form the lesson states, and it is true.

    The wider one keeps every number in play by asking what x2\sqrt{x^2}x2​ actually returns. It returns the size of xxx with the sign discarded, and that is exactly what absolute value means:

    x2=∣x∣.\sqrt{x^2} = |x|.x2​=∣x∣.

    Check it on both signs. For the counterexample, (−17)2=17=∣−17∣\sqrt{(-17)^2} = 17 = |-17|(−17)2​=17=∣−17∣, and going the other way 172=17=∣17∣\sqrt{17^2} = 17 = |17|172​=17=∣17∣. Riya's version is the special case of this one in which xxx was already non-negative, which is why it looked right on every example she had tried.

    2. What a radical may be split across . Foundational, 13 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 13 pts

    The product rule lets a radical be taken apart: the root of a product is the product of the roots. It is tempting to read that as general permission to take a radical apart, and a sum is where that reading goes wrong. This question simplifies two radicals, measures the damage a sum does, and then traces both outcomes back to the definition of the principal square root.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write 63\sqrt{63}63​ and 180\sqrt{180}180​ in simplest radical form, pulling out the largest perfect-square factor each time. For the second one, say what would have been left unfinished had you pulled out 444 instead.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Evaluate 9×144\sqrt{9 \times 144}9×144​ by splitting the radicand, giving a whole number. Then take the sum: trap 9+144\sqrt{9 + 144}9+144​ between two consecutive whole numbers, naming the perfect squares that trap it, work out 9+144\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{144}9​+144​ exactly, and report how far apart those two results are.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why the product rule is true, starting from what the symbol x\sqrt{\phantom{x}}x​ is defined to return and using the fact that squaring undoes it. Then run that same argument on a+b\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}a​+b​ for non-negative aaa and bbb, say exactly what appears that spoils it, and state for which non-negative aaa and bbb the sum version would hold after all.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 6 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Any question about a radical can be turned into a question about a square, because the symbol names the non-negative number whose square is the radicand. Square whatever you are testing and see what comes back.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part A

      Run through the perfect squares in order and keep the largest one that divides the radicand. If the number left underneath is still divisible by a perfect square, you stopped too early.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Multiplying a sum by itself is not the same as multiplying its two pieces by themselves. Write both brackets out and multiply every term of one by every term of the other, then count what you are left holding.

    That is every hint for this question.

    The answer

    Part A

    63=37\sqrt{63} = 3\sqrt{7}63​=37​ and 180=65\sqrt{180} = 6\sqrt{5}180​=65​. Pulling out 444 gives 2452\sqrt{45}245​, whose radicand still contains the perfect square 999, so that form is not finished.

    Part B

    9×144=36\sqrt{9 \times 144} = 369×144​=36. For the sum, 12<153<1312 < \sqrt{153} < 1312<153​<13, while 9+144=15\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{144} = 159​+144​=15, so splitting across the sum overshoots the true root by more than 222.

    Part C

    Squaring a×b\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}a​×b​ returns a×ba \times ba×b, and the product is non-negative, so it is the principal root of a×ba \times ba×b. Squaring a+b\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}a​+b​ leaves two extra cross terms, so it exceeds a+ba + ba+b unless aaa or bbb is zero.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Simplifying a radical means finding the largest perfect square that divides the radicand.

    For 636363, run through the perfect squares. It is odd, so 444 is out; 999 divides it, since 63=9×763 = 9 \times 763=9×7; and nothing larger fits. Split it there:

    63=9×7=9×7=37.\sqrt{63} = \sqrt{9 \times 7} = \sqrt{9}\times\sqrt{7} = 3\sqrt{7}.63​=9×7​=9​×7​=37​.

    The 777 stays under the radical, because 777 is prime and so has no perfect-square factor except 111.

    For 180180180, several perfect squares divide it: 444, 999 and 363636. The largest is 363636, since 180=36×5180 = 36 \times 5180=36×5:

    180=36×5=36×5=65.\sqrt{180} = \sqrt{36 \times 5} = \sqrt{36}\times\sqrt{5} = 6\sqrt{5}.180​=36×5​=36​×5​=65​.

    Pulling out 444 instead is not wrong, only unfinished:

    180=4×45=245.\sqrt{180} = \sqrt{4 \times 45} = 2\sqrt{45}.180​=4×45​=245​.

    The radicand 454545 still holds the perfect square 999, so the same rule has to be applied a second time: 245=2×35=652\sqrt{45} = 2 \times 3\sqrt{5} = 6\sqrt{5}245​=2×35​=65​. Taking the largest perfect square first arrives in one step instead of two, and it is the only way to be sure at a glance that nothing is left inside.

    Part B

    The product first, where the rule applies. Both factors are perfect squares, so the radical comes apart cleanly:

    9×144=9×144=3×12=36.\sqrt{9 \times 144} = \sqrt{9}\times\sqrt{144} = 3 \times 12 = 36.9×144​=9​×144​=3×12=36.

    A check confirms it: 9×144=12969 \times 144 = 12969×144=1296, and 362=129636^2 = 1296362=1296.

    Now the sum, where it does not apply. The radicand is 9+144=1539 + 144 = 1539+144=153, which is not a perfect square, so trap it between the perfect squares on either side. Counting up, 144=122144 = 12^2144=122 sits just below it and 169=132169 = 13^2169=132 is the next one above:

    144<153<169,so12<153<13.144 < 153 < 169, \qquad\text{so}\qquad 12 < \sqrt{153} < 13.144<153<169,so12<153​<13.

    Splitting the radical across the sum instead produces a number that is not even inside that range:

    9+144=3+12=15.\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{144} = 3 + 12 = 15.9​+144​=3+12=15.

    The true root is below 131313 and the split answer is 151515, so the split overshoots by more than 222. That is not a rounding slip. The two expressions are simply different quantities, and part C says what the difference is made of.

    Part C

    Everything here follows from one description of the symbol: n\sqrt{n}n​ is the non-negative number whose square is nnn. So to test whether some expression is n\sqrt{n}n​, square it, and check as well that it is not negative.

    Take the product. Squaring a×b\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}a​×b​ means multiplying it by itself, and multiplication may be reordered freely:

    (a×b)2=a×a×b×b=a×b.\left(\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}\right)^2 = \sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}\times\sqrt{b} = a \times b.(a​×b​)2=a​×a​×b​×b​=a×b.

    It is also non-negative, being a product of two non-negative numbers. It squares to a×ba \times ba×b and it is not negative, which is the entire description of a×b\sqrt{a \times b}a×b​, so the two are the same number.

    Now run the identical test on the sum. Squaring a+b\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}a​+b​ means multiplying that sum by itself, and each term of the first bracket multiplies each term of the second:

    (a+b)2=aa+ab+ba+bb.\left(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}\right)^2 = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} + \sqrt{b}\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}\sqrt{b}.(a​+b​)2=a​a​+a​b​+b​a​+b​b​.

    The first and last of those are aaa and bbb, and the middle two are equal to each other, so

    (a+b)2=a+b+2ab.\left(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}\right)^2 = a + b + 2\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}.(a​+b​)2=a+b+2a​b​.

    There is the spoiler: two cross terms that the product version never produced. Squaring a+b\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}a​+b​ does not give a+ba + ba+b, it gives a+ba + ba+b plus something more, and unless one of aaa and bbb is zero that extra piece is genuinely positive, so a+b\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}a​+b​ is the root of something larger than a+ba + ba+b rather than of a+ba + ba+b itself.

    That extra piece also says exactly when the sum version would be safe. Both roots are non-negative, so 2ab2\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}2a​b​ is zero exactly when one of them is zero, which happens exactly when aaa or bbb is zero. That is the only case, and it is a useless one: with b=0b = 0b=0 the claim reads a+0=a+0\sqrt{a + 0} = \sqrt{a} + 0a+0​=a​+0. For every other pair of non-negative numbers the cross terms are genuinely present, and part B already measured them. With a=9a = 9a=9 and b=144b = 144b=144 they add 2×3×12=722 \times 3 \times 12 = 722×3×12=72, and 153+72=225153 + 72 = 225153+72=225, which is why the split answer of 151515 is the root of 225225225 rather than of 153153153.

    63=37\sqrt{63} = 3\sqrt{7}63​=37​ and 180=65\sqrt{180} = 6\sqrt{5}180​=65​, where pulling out 444 instead would leave 2452\sqrt{45}245​ with the perfect square 999 still inside.     Another way: Simplify from the prime factorization

    When the largest perfect-square factor is not obvious, factor the radicand into primes and pair them off. Each pair of equal primes is a perfect square, so it leaves the radical as a single copy of that prime:

    180=22×32×5,180=2×3×5=65.180 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5, \qquad \sqrt{180} = 2 \times 3 \times \sqrt{5} = 6\sqrt{5}.180=22×32×5,180​=2×3×5​=65​.

    Anything left unpaired stays underneath, which is why the 555 does not come out. The method never has to guess, and it also proves there is nothing further to extract: an unpaired prime cannot be part of any perfect-square factor.

    When it is worth it On a large radicand, or whenever you are unsure whether the form you have reached is really the simplest one.

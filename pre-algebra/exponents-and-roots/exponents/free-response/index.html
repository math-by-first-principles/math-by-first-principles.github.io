    1. From an instruction in words to a number . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    A set of building instructions ends with this line: take the number 666 and multiply it by itself until 666 has appeared as a factor five times. The line never writes the resulting number down, and it does not have to: it already fixes exactly one number. This question turns that instruction into notation, then into a number, and then asks how such a number is said aloud.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write the number the instructions describe as a power, and write that same power out in full as a product with nothing abbreviated. Name which number is the base and which is the exponent, and say what role each of the two plays.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Evaluate that power, bringing one new copy of the base into a running product at each step and showing every step. Then work out the base multiplied by the exponent, and report both numbers side by side.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Two of the powers of 666 are read aloud with names of their own that come from geometry. Say how 626^262 and 636^363 are each read, say what shape each name refers to and what the power measures about that shape, and say how 656^565 is read.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      An exponent is a count of factors, not a factor. Settle first how many copies of the base the instruction calls for, and write those copies out before any multiplying starts.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Keep a running product so that you never hold more than two numbers at once: multiply the first two copies together, then bring the next copy into the result you are already holding.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      The two special names are borrowed from shapes. Ask what a second power measures about a square and what a third power measures about a cube, then ask whether a fifth power has any shape to borrow from.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    656^565, which written out in full is 6×6×6×6×66 \times 6 \times 6 \times 6 \times 66×6×6×6×6. The base is 666, the number being used as a factor, and the exponent is 555, which counts the copies.

    Part B

    65=77766^5 = 777665=7776, while the base multiplied by the exponent is 6×5=306 \times 5 = 306×5=30.

    Part C

    626^262 is read "six squared" and 636^363 is read "six cubed", after the area of a square of side 666 and the volume of a cube of edge 666. Past the third power there is no special word, so 656^565 is read "six to the fifth power".

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    A power records two separate things, and the instruction supplies both of them. The number being used as a factor is 666, and the number of times it is used is five.

    Written as a power, that is

    65.6^5.65.

    Written out with nothing abbreviated, it is five copies of 666 multiplied together:

    65=6×6×6×6×6.6^5 = 6 \times 6 \times 6 \times 6 \times 6.65=6×6×6×6×6.

    The base is the 666, which is the number being multiplied. The exponent is the 555, and it counts the copies.

    Notice what does not appear in the full product: the 555 itself. It is nowhere among the factors, because it was never a factor. It is a count of how many factors there are, and keeping that straight is the single most useful thing about this notation.

    Part B

    Take one factor at a time and carry the running product forward, so that no step ever asks you to hold more than two numbers at once.

    6×6=366 \times 6 = 366×6=36

    36×6=21636 \times 6 = 21636×6=216

    216×6=1296216 \times 6 = 1296216×6=1296

    1296×6=77761296 \times 6 = 77761296×6=7776

    Five copies of the base needed four multiplications, because the first step uses two copies at once and every later step brings in one more. So

    65=7776.6^5 = 7776.65=7776.

    The other calculation is finished in a single step:

    6×5=30.6 \times 5 = 30.6×5=30.

    Put the two side by side and they are 777677767776 and 303030. Multiplying the base by the exponent does not evaluate the power at all; it works out a different and far smaller quantity, and the distance between 303030 and 777677767776 is the size of that mistake after only five factors.

    Part C

    The two names are not decorations. Each one records a measurement.

    626^262 is read "six squared". A square with a side of 666 units is made of six rows of six unit squares, and counting them is exactly this multiplication:

    62=6×6=36.6^2 = 6 \times 6 = 36.62=6×6=36.

    So a second power measures the area of a square built on that side, and that is where the word comes from.

    636^363 is read "six cubed". A cube with an edge of 666 units is built from six layers, each layer holding the 363636 unit cubes of a six by six square:

    63=6×6×6=216.6^3 = 6 \times 6 \times 6 = 216.63=6×6×6=216.

    So a third power measures the volume of a cube built on that edge.

    There is no everyday fourth or fifth shape to borrow a word from, so past the third power the reading is plain: 656^565 is read "six to the fifth power", or simply "six to the fifth". The pattern of names stops, but the meaning of the notation does not change one bit.

    In one line

    The instruction describes 65=6×6×6×6×66^5 = 6 \times 6 \times 6 \times 6 \times 665=6×6×6×6×6, whose base 666 is the number used as a factor and whose exponent 555 counts the copies. Bringing one copy in at a time gives 363636, then 216216216, then 129612961296, and finally 65=77766^5 = 777665=7776, against 6×5=306 \times 5 = 306×5=30 for the base times the exponent. Aloud, 626^262 is "six squared" after the area of a square of side 666, 636^363 is "six cubed" after the volume of a cube of edge 666, and 656^565 is simply "six to the fifth power".

    Another way: Keep the ladder you climbed

    The running product passes through every lower power of 666 on its way up, so writing the whole ladder down costs nothing extra:

    61=6,62=36,63=2166^1 = 6, \qquad 6^2 = 36, \qquad 6^3 = 21661=6,62=36,63=216

    64=1296,65=77766^4 = 1296, \qquad 6^5 = 777664=1296,65=7776

    Each line is the line above it with one more copy of the base multiplied in, which is the definition of a power doing all the work.

    When it is worth it When a question asks for several powers of one base, and as a check on your own arithmetic: every entry must be six times the entry above it.

    2. A rule that agreed the first time . Reasoning, 11 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Reasoning 11 pts

    A student decides that a power can be worked out by multiplying the base by the exponent. They try the idea on 222^222, find nothing wrong with it, and adopt it for good. This question puts the same rule to work on a second power, then asks what a single agreement is actually worth.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Work out 222^222 and 363^636 properly, by expanding each one into its factors, and work out what the student's rule gives for each. Report all four values, and say for each of the two powers whether the two calculations agreed.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Multiplication is itself an abbreviation for something simpler. Using that, explain what quantity each of the two calculations on 363^636 is really asking for, and say precisely what the student's rule has put in place of what.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The student objects that the rule cannot be wrong, since it agreed on the very power they first tried it on. Say what one agreeing case establishes and what one disagreeing case establishes, give a whole family of powers on which the rule agrees, and give your verdict on the rule.

      Justify your claim State the claim, then give the reason it has to be true. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing here is settled by quoting a rule or by asserting that one is wrong. Carry the student's rule out in full on each power, alongside the definition, and let the numbers you get do the deciding.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Multiplication was itself a shortcut once, for repeated addition. Write out what each of the two calculations is a shortcut for, and the difference between them stops being a matter of opinion.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Ask what a single agreeing case can prove, then ask what a single disagreeing case can prove. The two are not the same, and one of them is far stronger than the other.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    22=42^2 = 422=4 and the rule also gives 444, so those agree. 36=7293^6 = 72936=729 while the rule gives 3×6=183 \times 6 = 183×6=18, so those do not.

    Part B

    They are different operations. 3×63 \times 63×6 adds six copies of 333 and reaches 181818, while 363^636 multiplies six copies of 333 and reaches 729729729. The rule hands back a repeated addition where the notation asked for a repeated multiplication.

    Part C

    The rule is false. A case where it agrees establishes only that instance, not the rule, while the single case where it disagrees is enough to reject a claim made about every power. It also agrees at every first power, since one copy of the base is the base itself, so agreements can be produced without the rule being true.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Take each power twice over, once from the definition and once from the rule.

    For 222^222 the definition asks for two copies of 222:

    22=2×2=4.2^2 = 2 \times 2 = 4.22=2×2=4.

    The rule asks for the base multiplied by the exponent, which here is also 2×22 \times 22×2:

    2×2=4.2 \times 2 = 4.2×2=4.

    The two agree, and it is worth seeing exactly why. With a base of 222 and an exponent of 222, the two recipes are written with the same two numbers joined by the same sign, so they could hardly have come out differently.

    For 363^636 the definition asks for six copies of 333, brought in one at a time:

    3×3=9,9×3=27,27×3=813 \times 3 = 9, \qquad 9 \times 3 = 27, \qquad 27 \times 3 = 813×3=9,9×3=27,27×3=81

    81×3=243,243×3=729.81 \times 3 = 243, \qquad 243 \times 3 = 729.81×3=243,243×3=729.

    The rule finishes in one step:

    3×6=18.3 \times 6 = 18.3×6=18.

    Here the two values are 729729729 and 181818, which are not a near miss: the power is more than forty times what the rule reports.

    Part B

    Multiplication began as a shortcut for repeated addition, and 3×63 \times 63×6 is three copies of 666 added together. Since the order of two factors does not change a product, it is equally six copies of 333 added together, and that is the form worth keeping here:

    3×6=3+3+3+3+3+3=18.3 \times 6 = 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 18.3×6=3+3+3+3+3+3=18.

    An exponent is the next shortcut along the same line, and it abbreviates repeated multiplication instead. Written out in full, that is

    36=3×3×3×3×3×3=729.3^6 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 729.36=3×3×3×3×3×3=729.

    Compare the two lines. Both start from six copies of 333, and every copy is present in both. The only difference is what joins the copies together: plus signs on the first line, times signs on the second.

    So the student's rule is not mis-evaluating the power. It is evaluating a different quantity altogether. It returns the repeated addition of the copies when the notation asked for their repeated multiplication.

    That also accounts for the size of the gap in part A. Adding one more copy of 333 moves a total up by three, while multiplying by one more copy of 333 makes a total three times as large. Six steps of those two habits finish a very long way apart, and every further step widens the gap faster than the one before it.

    Part C

    The two kinds of case do not carry the same weight, and that difference is the whole question.

    A rule of this sort is a claim about every power. One case where it agrees is consistent with the claim, but it is equally consistent with the claim being false, so on its own it establishes only that one instance and not the rule. One case where it disagrees is decisive, because the claim said every power: part A produced a power where the rule gives 181818 and the true value is 729729729, and that single case is enough to reject it.

    Agreeing cases are not even rare. Every first power is one, since a first power is a single copy of the base while the rule multiplies the base by 111:

    81=8,8×1=8.8^1 = 8, \qquad 8 \times 1 = 8.81=8,8×1=8.

    The same happens for 919^191, for 57157^1571, and for any base whatever. A base of 000 agrees at every positive whole-number exponent as well, since multiplying copies of 000 and multiplying 000 by the exponent both give 000. So a supply of agreements can be manufactured without the rule holding anywhere else.

    The verdict is that the rule is false, and the case that first persuaded the student was one of those accidents: with a base of 222 and an exponent of 222, the two recipes are the same multiplication written twice. What the rule is worth is as a reminder of what an exponent is not. It counts the factors; it is not one of them.

    In one line

    22=42^2 = 422=4 and the rule gives 444, so they agree; but 36=7293^6 = 72936=729 while the rule gives 181818, so they do not. Written out, 3×63 \times 63×6 adds six copies of 333 and 363^636 multiplies six copies of 333, so the rule returns a repeated addition where the notation asked for a repeated multiplication. One agreeing case establishes only that instance and not the rule, while the single disagreement is enough to reject a claim made about every power, and agreements are cheap: every first power is one, since 81=88^1 = 881=8 and 8×1=88 \times 1 = 88×1=8. The rule is false.

    Mark your own paper against this. Give yourself the points for each element you actually wrote down, not the ones you meant to.

    2

    Part B 4 points

    3

    Writes out what each of the two calculations abbreviates, in terms of the copies each notation collects and what joins them. . Worth 3 points. needs an explanation, not just an answer

    1

    Says what the rule has substituted for what, rather than only comparing the two values. . Worth 1 point.

    Part C 4 points

    2

    Separates what a case where the rule agrees can establish from what a case where it disagrees can establish. . Worth 2 points. needs an explanation, not just an answer

    2

    Produces a whole family of powers on which the rule agrees, with the reason it agrees there, rather than one further example. . Worth 2 points.

    Try a similar problem (Optional)

    Same idea, different numbers. Work it on paper, then check yourself the same way.

    A classmate says the rule only breaks down when the exponent is bigger than the base, and offers 444^444 as a case where the two are equal, so it should agree just as 222^222 did. Work out both calculations for 444^444, and give a verdict on the classmate's reasoning.

    The answer

    44=2564^4 = 25644=256 while the rule gives 161616, so the classmate is wrong. Making the base equal to the exponent does not rescue the rule: it only ever returns that number multiplied by itself, and 222^222 agreed because that one pair of numbers makes the definition and the rule the very same multiplication.

    Evaluate the power from the definition, four copies of 444:

    4×4=16,16×4=64,64×4=256.4 \times 4 = 16, \qquad 16 \times 4 = 64, \qquad 64 \times 4 = 256.4×4=16,16×4=64,64×4=256.

    The rule gives

    4×4=16.4 \times 4 = 16.4×4=16.

    So 44=2564^4 = 25644=256 against the rule's 161616, and the classmate's case fails.

    The reasoning behind it fails too. When the base and the exponent are equal, the rule returns that number multiplied by itself, whatever the power was asking for. Matching the base to the exponent therefore rescues nothing. What made 222^222 agree was a coincidence between those two particular numbers: the definition asks for 2×22 \times 22×2, and the rule asks for 2×22 \times 22×2 as well. A coincidence is not a method, and 444^444 is what happens when you try to build on one.

    3. Counting seeds by the tens . Application, 11 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 11 pts

    A seed supplier packs everything in tens. Ten seeds fill a sachet, ten sachets fill a packet, ten packets fill a box, ten boxes fill a case, and ten cases fill a pallet. Nobody in the warehouse ever counts individual seeds. They count packages, and then work out what that comes to in seeds.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write the number of seeds on one full pallet as a power of ten and as the product of tens that power stands for, and give its value. Then say how many digits that value has, and say what the exponent counts in the packing chain.

      Model the situation Name your unknown first, then write every other quantity in terms of that one letter. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      The supplier's annual report gives a grand total of 100,000,000100{,}000{,}000100,000,000 seeds, without saying how that would be written as a power. Write the total as a power of ten, and say how you fixed the exponent without multiplying anything out.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A clerk writing the report says that any seed count with nine digits must be 10910^9109. Work out what 10910^9109 actually is, decide whether the clerk's statement holds, and state how the exponent on a power of ten is related to the number of digits in its value.

      Find and correct the error Say which line first goes wrong, why it is wrong, and then do it correctly. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every step of the packing chain multiplies by ten, and you already know from place value what multiplying by ten does to the digits of a number. Follow the digits rather than grinding out the arithmetic.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Read the reported total from the left and count what follows the leading digit. That count is the thing the exponent records, so no multiplying is needed.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Write the clerk's power out in full and count its digits before deciding anything. Then ask a second question: does a count of that size have to be a power of ten in the first place?

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    105=10×10×10×10×10=100,00010^5 = 10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 100{,}000105=10×10×10×10×10=100,000 seeds. The value has six digits, and the exponent counts the five packing steps between a single seed and a pallet.

    Part B

    10810^8108. The total is a 111 followed by eight zeros, and the number of zeros is the number of tens multiplied together.

    Part C

    The statement fails. 10910^9109 is 1,000,000,0001{,}000{,}000{,}0001,000,000,000, which has ten digits. A power of ten built from nnn factors carries nnn zeros and so n+1n + 1n+1 digits, so the nine-digit power of ten is 10810^8108, and in any case most nine-digit counts are not powers of ten at all.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Follow the chain one step at a time. Each step multiplies the running count of seeds by ten.

    A sachet holds 101010 seeds. A packet holds ten sachets, so a packet holds

    10×10=10010 \times 10 = 10010×10=100

    seeds. A box holds ten packets, a case holds ten boxes, and a pallet holds ten cases, which brings in three more factors of ten:

    10×10×10×10×10=100,000.10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 100{,}000.10×10×10×10×10=100,000.

    Five tens are multiplied together, and that is exactly what the notation 10510^5105 records, so a pallet holds

    105=100,00010^5 = 100{,}000105=100,000

    seeds.

    Now count the digits of that value. There are six: a leading 111 and then five zeros. The five zeros are the five packing steps, one for every factor of ten, and the leading 111 is the single seed the chain started from. Keep those two counts apart, because the exponent matches the zeros rather than the digits.

    Part B

    Every factor of ten in a power of ten appends one zero, because multiplying a whole number by ten shifts each of its digits one place up and writes a 000 into the ones place. So the zeros can be counted instead of the multiplications being done.

    The total is written

    100,000,000,100{,}000{,}000,100,000,000,

    which is a 111 followed by eight zeros. Eight zeros mean eight factors of ten:

    108=100,000,000.10^8 = 100{,}000{,}000.108=100,000,000.

    No multiplication was carried out anywhere in that. The journey back is just as quick: to turn a power of ten into its value, write a 111 and then as many zeros as the exponent names.

    Part C

    Write the clerk's own number out before judging anything. Nine factors of ten give a 111 followed by nine zeros:

    109=1,000,000,000.10^9 = 1{,}000{,}000{,}000.109=1,000,000,000.

    Count its digits: the leading 111 and then nine zeros make ten digits, not nine. So the clerk's example does not even have the property they claimed for it.

    The relationship is off by one all the way along. For a whole-number exponent nnn of at least 111, the value of 10n10^n10n is a 111 followed by nnn zeros, so it carries nnn zeros and n+1n + 1n+1 digits. The nine-digit power of ten is therefore the one with eight zeros:

    108=100,000,000,10^8 = 100{,}000{,}000,108=100,000,000,

    which is the annual total from part B.

    There is a second thing wrong with the statement, and it is the larger of the two. Nine-digit counts run all the way from 100,000,000100{,}000{,}000100,000,000 to 999,999,999999{,}999{,}999999,999,999, and almost none of them are powers of ten: a count of 384,512,907384{,}512{,}907384,512,907 seeds has nine digits and is no power of ten. Knowing the digit count of a number tells you roughly how big it is. It does not turn it into a power of ten.

    In one line

    A pallet holds 105=100,00010^5 = 100{,}000105=100,000 seeds, a six-digit value whose five zeros are the five packing steps. The annual total 100,000,000100{,}000{,}000100,000,000 is a 111 followed by eight zeros, so it is 10810^8108, read straight off without multiplying. The clerk is wrong: 109=1,000,000,00010^9 = 1{,}000{,}000{,}000109=1,000,000,000 has ten digits, because a power of ten built from nnn factors carries nnn zeros and n+1n + 1n+1 digits. The nine-digit power of ten is 10810^8108, and most nine-digit counts are not powers of ten at all.

    Six factors of ten give a 111 followed by six zeros:

    106=1,000,000.10^6 = 1{,}000{,}000.106=1,000,000.

    That is seven digits, one more than the exponent.

    The catalogue's claim inherits the same off-by-one. Twelve factors of ten give a 111 followed by twelve zeros:

    1012=1,000,000,000,000,10^{12} = 1{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}000,1012=1,000,000,000,000,

    which has thirteen digits. The power of ten with twelve digits is the one carrying eleven zeros, namely 101110^{11}1011.

    And even that is only about powers of ten. A twelve-digit stock figure need not be a power of ten at all, and almost none of them are.

    4. Paving the courtyard around the fountain . Application, 12 points. Question 4 of 5.

    Application 12 pts

    A community centre is repaving a square courtyard that is laid out as a grid of square slabs, twelve slabs along each side. A square fountain sits inside it and covers a patch five slabs by five slabs; those slabs stay where they are and are not repaved.     Text description of this figure

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    8×(122−52)8 \times (12^2 - 5^2)8×(122−52) dollars, with the bracket holding the number of slabs that are actually repaved.

    Part B

    952952952 dollars, which is the price of 119119119 slabs.

    Part C

    It comes to 392392392 dollars. Squaring the difference prices one square patch seven slabs on a side, which is 494949 slabs, in place of the 119119119 that are left once the fountain's patch is removed.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Build the count of slabs first, and price it afterwards.

    The courtyard is a square grid with twelve slabs along each side, so it holds twelve rows of twelve slabs, which is a second power:

    122=12×12.12^2 = 12 \times 12.122=12×12.

    The fountain covers a square patch five slabs along each side, which is five rows of five:

    52=5×5.5^2 = 5 \times 5.52=5×5.

    Those slabs are not repaved, so the number that is repaved is the first count with the second taken away, and the price of 888 dollars applies to that whole difference:

    8×(122−52).8 \times (12^2 - 5^2).8×(122−52).

    The bracket is doing real work. Each power is settled before the subtraction in any case, since exponents outrank subtraction, but without the bracket the multiplication by 888 would also come before the subtraction, and only the first count would be priced.

    Part B

    Taking the expression as it stands, nothing outside the bracket may act until the bracket has become a single number, so start inside it. (Rewriting the expression before evaluating it is a different route, and the alternate method takes it.) Inside, the powers sit on the higher tier and are settled first:

    122−52=144−5212^2 - 5^2 = 144 - 5^2122−52=144−52

    144−52=144−25144 - 5^2 = 144 - 25144−52=144−25

    Now the subtraction, which is the only operation left inside the bracket:

    144−25=119.144 - 25 = 119.144−25=119.

    That 119119119 is the number of slabs being repaved. The whole expression has become a single multiplication:

    8×119=952.8 \times 119 = 952.8×119=952.

    The repaving costs 952952952 dollars.

    Part C

    Evaluate the line exactly as it is written. The bracket is settled first, then the power, then the multiplication:

    8×(12−5)2=8×728 \times (12 - 5)^2 = 8 \times 7^28×(12−5)2=8×72

    8×72=8×49=392.8 \times 7^2 = 8 \times 49 = 392.8×72=8×49=392.

    So that line asks for 392392392 dollars, against the 952952952 dollars the job actually costs, leaving the contractor 560560560 dollars short.

    The 494949 is easy to picture, and picturing it is the quickest way to see what went wrong. It is a square patch seven slabs on a side, the square you would get by shortening both sides of the courtyard by the width of the fountain and throwing the rest away. The courtyard with the fountain patch removed is not that shape at all: it is a twelve by twelve grid with a five by five hole in the middle of it, and it holds 119119119 slabs.

    The difference is entirely about what the exponent is attached to. In 122−5212^2 - 5^2122−52 each exponent is attached to a single side length, so two square counts are formed and one is subtracted from the other. In (12−5)2(12 - 5)^2(12−5)2 the exponent is attached to the difference of the two side lengths, and it squares a length that no side of the repaved region has. Parentheses decide what a power applies to, and moving them here moved the shape being counted.

    In one line

    The bill is 8×(122−52)8 \times (12^2 - 5^2)8×(122−52), which comes to 8×(144−25)=8×119=9528 \times (144 - 25) = 8 \times 119 = 9528×(144−25)=8×119=952 dollars for 119119119 slabs. The contractor's 8×(12−5)28 \times (12 - 5)^28×(12−5)2 comes to 8×49=3928 \times 49 = 3928×49=392 dollars, because squaring the difference prices a single square seven slabs on a side instead of a twelve by twelve grid with a five by five patch missing, which leaves the job 560560560 dollars short.

    Another way: Price the whole grid, then take the fountain off the bill

    Every slab costs the same, so the two counts can be priced separately and the bill adjusted:

    8×144=1152,8×25=2008 \times 144 = 1152, \qquad 8 \times 25 = 2008×144=1152,8×25=200

    1152−200=952.1152 - 200 = 952.1152−200=952.

    This is the distributive property read in reverse, and it agrees with the grouped calculation exactly, as it has to.

    When it is worth it When a full price is already known and a deduction has to be justified line by line, or as a check on a grouped expression: two honest routes must land on the same number.

    5. What the minus sign is attached to . Reasoning, 11 points. Question 5 of 5.

    Reasoning 11 pts

    The expressions (−7)2(-7)^2(−7)2 and −72-7^2−72 are written with the same digits and differ only by a pair of parentheses. A classmate says that a pair of parentheses around a negative base always changes the value, so that two expressions like these can never come out the same.     Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      In each expression, decide which number the exponent is attached to before you multiply anything at all. The parentheses are the only thing on the page that can answer that.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      You now hold one exponent where the two expressions come out differently and one where they come out the same. A claim that says always has to survive both of them.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    (−7)2=49(-7)^2 = 49(−7)2=49, where the base is −7-7−7 and both factors carry the sign. −72=−49-7^2 = -49−72=−49, where the exponent is attached to the 777 alone and the minus sign is applied after the squaring.

    Part B

    (−7)3=−343(-7)^3 = -343(−7)3=−343 and −73=−343-7^3 = -343−73=−343. At this exponent the two agree.

    Part C

    The claim fails. For a positive base and a positive whole-number exponent, the two arrangements agree exactly when the exponent is odd and differ when it is even, because the parentheses put a minus sign on every factor and an even count of them cancels, while the version without parentheses stays negative throughout.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The parentheses are the only thing in either expression that says what the exponent is attached to, so settle that before multiplying anything.

    In (−7)2(-7)^2(−7)2 the parentheses bundle the sign into the base, so the base is the single number −7-7−7, and squaring it means multiplying it by itself:

    (−7)2=(−7)×(−7)=49.(-7)^2 = (-7) \times (-7) = 49.(−7)2=(−7)×(−7)=49.

    There are two negative factors, and a negative times a negative is positive, so the result is positive.

    In −72-7^2−72 there are no parentheses, so by the order of operations the exponent binds to the 777 alone, ahead of the minus sign standing in front. Read it as −(72)-(7^2)−(72), which is to square first and negate afterwards:

    −72=−(7×7)=−49.-7^2 = -(7 \times 7) = -49.−72=−(7×7)=−49.

    Only one minus sign is in play here and it never enters the multiplication at all, so the result stays negative. Same digits, same exponent, opposite signs, and the parentheses are the whole of the difference.

    Part B

    Take the parenthesised one first. The base is −7-7−7 and the exponent calls for three copies of it:

    (−7)3=(−7)×(−7)×(−7).(-7)^3 = (-7) \times (-7) \times (-7).(−7)3=(−7)×(−7)×(−7).

    The first two factors multiply to 494949, and the third brings the sign back in:

    49×(−7)=−343.49 \times (-7) = -343.49×(−7)=−343.

    Now the one without parentheses. The exponent binds to the 777 alone and the minus sign waits its turn:

    −73=−(7×7×7)=−343.-7^3 = -(7 \times 7 \times 7) = -343.−73=−(7×7×7)=−343.

    Both expressions come to −343-343−343, so at this exponent they agree. They got there by different routes: in the first, three negative factors leave one minus sign uncancelled, while in the second a positive cube is negated at the end. This time the two routes land on the same number.

    Part C

    The claim says always, so a single exponent where the two agree is enough to reject it, and part B supplied one: at the third power both expressions came to −343-343−343.

    What is really going on is a count of minus signs. Take a positive base and a positive whole-number exponent.

    In the parenthesised version the whole negative number is the base, so every factor in the product carries a minus sign. Negative factors cancel in pairs, so an even count of them leaves the product positive, while an odd count leaves exactly one minus sign uncancelled:

    (−7)2=49,(−7)3=−343.(-7)^2 = 49, \qquad (-7)^3 = -343.(−7)2=49,(−7)3=−343.

    In the version without parentheses there is exactly one minus sign however large the exponent, and it is applied after the power has been worked out, so the result is negative every time:

    −72=−49,−73=−343.-7^2 = -49, \qquad -7^3 = -343.−72=−49,−73=−343.

    Set the two lines beside each other. When the exponent is even, the first value is positive and the second is negative, so they differ. When the exponent is odd, the first value is negative and the same size as the second, so they agree. For a positive base and a positive whole-number exponent that is the whole story: the two arrangements agree exactly when the exponent is odd.

    So the parentheses are neither decoration nor always decisive. They decide whether the sign sits inside the multiplication or outside it, and that choice only shows up in the answer when an even count of negative factors would have cancelled.

    In one line

    (−7)2=(−7)×(−7)=49(-7)^2 = (-7) \times (-7) = 49(−7)2=(−7)×(−7)=49 while −72=−(7×7)=−49-7^2 = -(7 \times 7) = -49−72=−(7×7)=−49, since the parentheses decide whether the sign belongs to the base. At the third power, though, (−7)3(-7)^3(−7)3 and −73-7^3−73 both come to −343-343−343. So the claim fails: for a positive base and a positive whole-number exponent, the two arrangements agree exactly when the exponent is odd and differ when it is even, because the parenthesised version puts a minus sign on every factor and an even count of them cancels, while the other version carries exactly one minus sign, applied after the power.

Introduction to Exponents

Intuition

Multiplication began as a shortcut for repeated addition: rather than writing 5+5+5+55 + 5 + 5 + 55+5+5+5, you write 4×54 \times 54×5. Exponents are the very next shortcut, this time for repeated multiplication. Rather than writing 2×2×2×2×22 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 22×2×2×2×2, you write 252^525. Repeated multiplication turns up everywhere (areas, volumes, place value, doubling), and when the base is bigger than 111, it grows breathtakingly fast.

Learning goals

  • Read ana^nan as nnn copies of the base multiplied together
  • Evaluate a power step by step, so 343^434 is 818181
  • Name a2a^2a2 squared and a3a^3a3 cubed, and tie each to a shape
  • Count the zeros in 10n10^n10n to read a number's size
  • Apply the order of operations to exponents, including how parentheses decide what a negative sign covers

What an exponent means

An exponent records how many times a number is used as a factor. In the power

25=2×2×2×2×2⏟five 2’s=32,2^5 = \underbrace{2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2}_{\text{five 2's}} = 32,25=five 2’s2×2×2×2×2​​=32,

the large number 222 is the base (the number being multiplied). The small raised number 555 is the exponent (it counts the copies of the base). The whole expression 252^525 is called a power of 222. You read it as “two to the fifth power,” or just “two to the fifth.”

In general, for a base aaa and a whole-number exponent n≥1n \ge 1n≥1,

an=a×a×⋯×a⏟n copies of a.a^n = \underbrace{a \times a \times \cdots \times a}_{n \text{ copies of } a}.an=n copies of aa×a×⋯×a​​.

ana^nan is the result of starting at aaa and multiplying by aaa again and again until aaa has appeared as a factor nnn times. The base aaa can be any number you have met so far: a whole number, an integer, a fraction, or a decimal. (A negative base needs parentheses, as in (−3)4(-3)^4(−3)4; you’ll see why later in this lesson.) The exponent nnn here is a whole number, and it is at least 111.

The exponent is a count, not a thing you multiply by. So 252^525 is not 2×5=102 \times 5 = 102×5=10. The 555 tells you how many 222‘s to multiply, and five 222‘s multiply to 323232. Read the exponent as an instruction (“use the base as a factor this many times”), never as a factor in its own right.

Reading powers aloud: squared and cubed

Most powers are read with the word “power,” as in ”777 to the fourth power.” Two exponents come up so often that they have their own names, and both names come from geometry.

A second power is read “squared.” A 444 by 444 square holds 4×4=164 \times 4 = 164×4=16 unit squares, so “four squared is sixteen.” The name comes from that picture: a2a^2a2 is the area of a square whose side has length aaa.

A third power is read “cubed.” A 222 by 222 by 222 cube is built from 2×2×2=82 \times 2 \times 2 = 82×2×2=8 unit cubes, so “two cubed is eight.” Again the name comes from the shape: a3a^3a3 is the volume of a cube whose edge has length aaa. Past the third power there are no special words; you simply say “to the fourth,” “to the fifth,” and so on.

The first power

Any number to the first power is just the number itself: a1=aa^1 = aa1=a. The exponent 111 asks for a single copy of the base, and one copy multiplied by nothing else is the base alone. So 81=88^1 = 881=8 and 11=11^1 = 111=1. Writing 888 or 818^181 gives the same value.

Evaluating powers

To evaluate a power means to work out the single number it equals. Write the base out the right number of times, then multiply step by step. Keep a running product as you go, so you never juggle more than two numbers at once.

Worked example 1 Evaluate 343^434

The exponent 444 calls for four factors of 333:

34=3×3×3×3.3^4 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3.34=3×3×3×3.

Multiply left to right, carrying the running product forward each time:

3×3=9,9×3=27,27×3=81.3 \times 3 = 9, \qquad 9 \times 3 = 27, \qquad 27 \times 3 = 81.3×3=9,9×3=27,27×3=81.

So 34=813^4 = 8134=81. Notice this is much larger than 3×4=123 \times 4 = 123×4=12, which is the trap to avoid.

Worked example 2 Evaluate 525^252, 232^323, and 10310^3103

A square is two factors:

52=5×5=25.5^2 = 5 \times 5 = 25.52=5×5=25.

A cube is three factors:

23=2×2×2=8.2^3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8.23=2×2×2=8.

And a power of ten, again three factors:

103=10×10×10=1000.10^3 = 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1000.103=10×10×10=1000.

The last answer, 100010001000, is a 111 followed by three zeros, and that three is the exponent.

The same procedure works when the base is a fraction or a decimal, because a power is still just repeated multiplication. To square a fraction you multiply it by itself, so (12)2=12×12=14\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}(21​)2=21​×21​=41​, and to square a decimal you do the same, so 0.12=0.1×0.1=0.010.1^2 = 0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.010.12=0.1×0.1=0.01.

Check your understanding

What is 242^424?

Answer choices

The exponent 444 means four factors of 222, multiplied step by step.

24=2×2×2×2=162^4 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 1624=2×2×2×2=16

It is not 2×4=82 \times 4 = 82×4=8. The exponent counts the copies; it is not a factor itself.

Powers of ten and the digit-count pattern

Powers of ten deserve a closer look, because they are the backbone of place value and of writing large numbers. Watch what each one equals:

101=10,102=100,103=1000,104=10,000.10^1 = 10, \quad 10^2 = 100, \quad 10^3 = 1000, \quad 10^4 = 10{,}000.101=10,102=100,103=1000,104=10,000.

Every time the exponent goes up by one, the value gains one more zero.

Why 10n10^n10n is a 111 followed by nnn zeros#

Start from the meaning of the power: 10n10^n10n is nnn factors of 101010 multiplied together. Multiplying any whole number by 101010 shifts every digit one place to the left and writes a 000 in the new ones place, which is the same as tacking one more zero onto the right-hand end.

Begin with 111 and multiply by 101010, again and again:

1  →  10  →  100  →  1000  →  ⋯1 \;\to\; 10 \;\to\; 100 \;\to\; 1000 \;\to\; \cdots1→10→100→1000→⋯

Each arrow tacks on exactly one more zero. After nnn multiplications there have been nnn arrows, so the starting 111 ends up carrying exactly nnn zeros on its right. That is why 10n10^n10n is written as a 111 followed by nnn zeros.

∎

This is why 10610^6106 is a million (111 followed by six zeros) and 10910^9109 is a billion, without writing out a single multiplication.

Zeros are not the same as digits. 105=100,00010^5 = 100{,}000105=100,000 has five zeros, but six digits in total: the leading 111 plus the five zeros. In general, 10n10^n10n has nnn zeros and n+1n + 1n+1 digits. A number can have six digits without being a power of ten at all, like 300,000300{,}000300,000; only a 111 followed entirely by zeros counts.

Check your understanding

How many zeros are in the value of 10510^5105?

Answer choices

For a power of ten, the exponent is exactly the number of zeros.

105=100,00010^5 = 100{,}000105=100,000

That is a 111 followed by five zeros, matching the exponent 555.

How fast does a power grow?

The reason exponents matter so much is the speed, but only when the base is bigger than 111. Repeated addition climbs in even steps, while repeated multiplication by a number bigger than 111 accelerates: each step multiplies by that same number again, so the jumps keep getting bigger. Watch what happens as the exponent on 222 climbs by one each time:

nnn2n2^n2n
111222
222444
333888

555323232

Each row doubles the one above it. By 2102^{10}210 you have already passed a thousand (210=10242^{10} = 1024210=1024), starting from a single 222. That runaway growth shows up anywhere something doubles: a sheet of paper folded again and again, a savings balance that doubles each year, or a rumor that spreads to two new people at each step.

Not every base grows this way. A base of 111 stays at 111 no matter the exponent (1100=11^{100} = 11100=1), and a positive fraction less than 111 actually shrinks: (12)2=14\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}(21​)2=41​ is smaller than 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​.

Order of operations with exponents

When a power sits inside a longer expression, where does it fall in the order of operations? Evaluate the power first, then multiply or divide, and finally add or subtract.

Worked example 3 Evaluate 2+322 + 3^22+32 and 5×235 \times 2^35×23

In 2+322 + 3^22+32, square the 333 first, then add:

2+32=2+9=11.2 + 3^2 = 2 + 9 = 11.2+32=2+9=11.

It is not 52=255^2 = 2552=25; the addition waits its turn. In 5×235 \times 2^35×23, cube the 222 first, then multiply:

5×23=5×8=40.5 \times 2^3 = 5 \times 8 = 40.5×23=5×8=40.

It is not (5×2)3=103=1000(5 \times 2)^3 = 10^3 = 1000(5×2)3=103=1000; without parentheses the exponent applies only to the 222, not to the product.

So powers are evaluated after parentheses but before multiplication, division, addition, and subtraction. This is the “E” in the mnemonic PEMDAS.

Parentheses override this, because you work inside them first of all. So (2+3)2(2 + 3)^2(2+3)2 adds before squaring, giving 52=255^2 = 2552=25, a different number from 2+32=112 + 3^2 = 112+32=11. Whenever you want the exponent to apply to a whole sum or product, you must wrap that sum or product in parentheses.

Check your understanding

Evaluate 1+2×321 + 2 \times 3^21+2×32.

Answer choices

Evaluate the power first, then the multiplication, then the addition.

32=9,2×9=18,1+18=193^2 = 9, \quad 2 \times 9 = 18, \quad 1 + 18 = 1932=9,2×9=18,1+18=19

The exponent applies only to the 333, ahead of the ×\times× and the +++.

The crucial case: a minus sign and a square

The place this matters most, and the place almost everyone slips at least once, is a minus sign next to a power. Compare these two expressions carefully; they look almost identical but give opposite signs:

(−2)2versus−22.(-2)^2 \qquad \text{versus} \qquad -2^2.(−2)2versus−22.

In (−2)2(-2)^2(−2)2, the parentheses make −2-2−2 the base, so the whole negative number is squared. In −22-2^2−22, there are no parentheses, so the exponent applies only to the 222, and the minus sign applies afterward.

Why (−2)2=4(-2)^2 = 4(−2)2=4 but −22=−4-2^2 = -4−22=−4#

Start with (−2)2(-2)^2(−2)2. The parentheses bundle the sign into the base, so the base is the single number −2-2−2 and squaring means multiplying it by itself:

(−2)2=(−2)×(−2)=4,(-2)^2 = (-2) \times (-2) = 4,(−2)2=(−2)×(−2)=4,

since a negative times a negative is positive.

Now −22-2^2−22. Here there are no parentheses, so by the order of operations the exponent applies only to the 222, ahead of the minus sign. Read it as −(22)-(2^2)−(22): first square, then negate.

−22=−(2×2)=−(4)=−4.-2^2 = -(2 \times 2) = -(4) = -4.−22=−(2×2)=−(4)=−4.

Only one minus sign is in play, and it is applied after the squaring, so the result stays negative.

∎

The same logic settles odd powers, where the cancelling works out differently. In (−2)3(-2)^3(−2)3 you multiply three negative factors, and three minus signs leave the result negative: (−2)3=(−2)(−2)(−2)=−8(-2)^3 = (-2)(-2)(-2) = -8(−2)3=(−2)(−2)(−2)=−8. Compare −23-2^3−23: with no parentheses the exponent applies only to 222, so −23=−(2×2×2)=−8-2^3 = -(2 \times 2 \times 2) = -8−23=−(2×2×2)=−8. Here the two forms happen to agree, but only because the exponent is odd. For an even exponent, like the square above, they disagree: (−2)2=4(-2)^2 = 4(−2)2=4 but −22=−4-2^2 = -4−22=−4. Count the negative factors of a nonzero base: an even count cancels to a positive, an odd count stays negative.

Check your understanding

Evaluate −42-4^2−42.

Answer choices

With no parentheses, the exponent applies only to the 444, and the minus sign applies afterward.

−42=−(4×4)=−16-4^2 = -(4 \times 4) = -16−42=−(4×4)=−16

Compare (−4)2=(−4)(−4)=16(-4)^2 = (-4)(-4) = 16(−4)2=(−4)(−4)=16, where the parentheses make the whole −4-4−4 the base.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying the base by the exponent. 252^525 is 2×2×2×2×2=322 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 322×2×2×2×2=32, not 2×5=102 \times 5 = 102×5=10. The exponent counts copies of the base; it is never one of the factors.
  • Forgetting that a1=aa^1 = aa1=a. A first power is one copy of the base, so 91=99^1 = 991=9, not 111. A number with no visible exponent already has an exponent of 111.
  • Doing addition or multiplication before the power. In 2+322 + 3^22+32 the power goes first: 2+9=112 + 9 = 112+9=11, not 52=255^2 = 2552=25. Exponents come before +++, −-−, ×\times×, and ÷\div÷.
  • Dropping parentheses around a negative base. (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9 (the negatives cancel), but −32=−9-3^2 = -9−32=−9 (square first, then negate). The parentheses decide what the exponent applies to.
  • Confusing aba^bab with bab^aba. Order matters: 23=82^3 = 823=8 but 32=93^2 = 932=9. They are usually different numbers.

Key takeaways

  • ana^nan means nnn copies of aaa multiplied together. Here aaa is the base, nnn is the exponent (a count of factors, not a factor itself), and the complete expression ana^nan is called a power.
  • a1=aa^1 = aa1=a; a2a^2a2 is read ”aaa squared” (a square’s area), and a3a^3a3 is read ”aaa cubed” (a cube’s volume).
  • To evaluate a power, multiply the copies of the base step by step: 34=813^4 = 8134=81, 23=82^3 = 823=8.
  • 10n10^n10n is a 111 followed by nnn zeros, so the exponent on 101010 is the zero count (one less than the digit count).
  • In order of operations, powers come after parentheses but before ×\times×, ÷\div÷, +++, and −-−: 2+32=112 + 3^2 = 112+32=11.
  • Parentheses decide what the exponent applies to: (−2)2=4(-2)^2 = 4(−2)2=4, but −22=−4-2^2 = -4−22=−4.
  • Repeated multiplication can grow extremely fast when the base is bigger than 111, which is one reason the notation is worth having.

A bit of history (optional)

Try describing “two used as a factor twenty times” in words alone. It is slow, and it is easy to lose count. That was the ordinary difficulty for a very long time. Early writers had no small raised digit to lean on.

They named the powers instead. A second power was a square, since it measured a square. A third power was a cube. Past the cube, writers kept coining names rather than running out of them (square-square for a fourth power, sursolid for a fifth, and more), but the names multiplied faster than they helped: every new power needed its own word, and different writers did not agree on them. A higher power still often meant falling back on a whole sentence.

In 1544 a German writer named Michael Stifel gave the little number a name of its own. He called it an exponent, from a Latin word meaning to set out. For the whole-number exponents in this lesson, the word is exact. An exponent sets out how many factors of the base to take. Here, it does nothing else.

Almost five hundred years later, that whole-number job has not changed. Later in this chapter you will meet exponents that are zero or negative. The notation stretches to cover those cases too. But every exponent in this lesson still names a plain count of factors.

    1. Counting the copies . Foundational, 9 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 9 pts

    Every law in this lesson is read off a single fact: a power is a count of equal factors, so ana^nan stands for nnn copies of aaa multiplied together. These parts take powers apart into their factors, put them back together, and watch what happens to the count. Write the factors out in full, because the counting is the point here and the arithmetic is not.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write 73×757^3 \times 7^573×75 out as one long product of 777's, count the factors standing in that product, and give the result as a single power of 777.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Now take 117113\dfrac{11^7}{11^3}113117​. Write the top and the bottom out as factors, cancel every pair you can, and give what survives as a single power of 111111. Report how many factors cancelled and how many were left standing.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      A classmate objects: "You are multiplying, so the sevens should multiply too, and the base ought to grow to 494949." Answer the objection in terms of copies of the base: say what multiplying two powers does to the number of factors, and what it does to the factor being counted. Then test the classmate's rule on the smallest product of two powers of 777 you can write, and report what each rule gives there.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 3 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Nothing here has to be recalled. Replace each power by the run of equal factors it stands for, and then count what is in front of you.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Every factor on the bottom kills one factor on the top, so ask how many pairs disappear and how many are left over instead of reaching for a rule.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Shrink the situation until you can evaluate it exactly, then see which of the two competing rules the plain arithmetic agrees with.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    787^878, which stands for a run of eight factors of 777.

    Part B

    11411^4114: three pairs cancel and four factors of 111111 are left on top.

    Part C

    The number of factors grows, but the factor being counted does not, so the base stays 777. On 71×717^1 \times 7^171×71 the counting gives 494949, while the classmate's rule would give 492=2,40149^2 = 2{,}401492=2,401.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The first power is three factors of 777 and the second is five of them. Multiplying sets the two runs side by side, and since multiplication puts nothing between them, the inner grouping can be dropped:

    73×75=(7×7×7)×(7×7×7×7×7).7^3 \times 7^5 = (7 \times 7 \times 7) \times (7 \times 7 \times 7 \times 7 \times 7).73×75=(7×7×7)×(7×7×7×7×7).

    Now count what is in the row. Three factors came from the first group and five from the second, so eight factors of 777 stand there in all, and eight factors of 777 is exactly what 787^878 means:

    73×75=73+5=78.7^3 \times 7^5 = 7^{3+5} = 7^8.73×75=73+5=78.

    The only thing that changed is how many factors there are. Every one of them is still a 777, which is why the answer is a power of 777 and not of anything else.

    Part B

    The top is seven factors of 111111 and the bottom is three:

    117113=11×11×11×11×11×11×1111×11×11.\frac{11^7}{11^3} = \frac{11 \times 11 \times 11 \times 11 \times 11 \times 11 \times 11}{11 \times 11 \times 11}.113117​=11×11×1111×11×11×11×11×11×11​.

    Each factor below cancels one factor above, because 1111=1\tfrac{11}{11} = 11111​=1. There are three below, so three of the seven above are cancelled and 7−3=47 - 3 = 47−3=4 of them survive:

    117113=11×11×11×11=114.\frac{11^7}{11^3} = 11 \times 11 \times 11 \times 11 = 11^4.113117​=11×11×11×11=114.

    Cancelling is subtraction done to a count. The bottom removes as many factors as it has, which is why the exponent of the answer is the top count with the bottom count taken away, and why the base is untouched by the whole operation.

    Part C

    Go back to what the symbols stand for. A power is a pile of equal factors, and multiplying two such piles pours them into one pile. Pouring changes how many things are in the pile; it does not change what the things are. So the count of factors grows, while the factor itself, the base, is carried through untouched.

    The smallest product of two powers of 777 is one factor times one factor:

    71×71=7×7=49=72.7^1 \times 7^1 = 7 \times 7 = 49 = 7^2.71×71=7×7=49=72.

    The counting rule predicts 727^272, and 727^272 really is 494949, so the arithmetic agrees with it. The classmate's rule predicts a base of 494949 with the same exponent, which is

    492=2,401,49^2 = 2{,}401,492=2,401,

    not 494949. One small case is enough to show the two rules disagree and which of them the arithmetic sides with.

    It is worth seeing why the classmate's rule overshoots by so much. Turning the base 777 into 494949 is itself squaring, since 49=7249 = 7^249=72, so every factor in the pile is quietly doubled up. That is a second multiplication nobody asked for, and it is exactly what makes 492=7449^2 = 7^4492=74 instead of 727^272.

    In one line

    73×75=787^3 \times 7^5 = 7^873×75=78 and 117113=114\dfrac{11^7}{11^3} = 11^4113117​=114. Multiplying joins two runs of factors, so the counts add; dividing cancels them in pairs, so the counts subtract. Either way every factor is still the same number, so the base comes through unchanged: on the smallest case, 71×71=49=727^1 \times 7^1 = 49 = 7^271×71=49=72, not 492=2,40149^2 = 2{,}401492=2,401.

    2. Side by side, or one inside the other . Foundational, 13 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Foundational 13 pts

    Two setups look alike on the page and behave completely differently. In 63×656^3 \times 6^563×65 the two powers stand side by side. In (63)5(6^3)^5(63)5 one power is raised to the other. The same base and the same pair of small numbers appear in both, so the only way to tell the results apart is to ask what each setup is instructing you to multiply.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write each of 63×656^3 \times 6^563×65 and (63)5(6^3)^5(63)5 as a single power of 666. For each one, say in a sentence what you were counting: how many groups of factors there were, and how many factors of 666 sat in each group.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 4 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Simplify (25)423×29\dfrac{(2^5)^4}{2^3 \times 2^9}23×29(25)4​ to a single power of 222, working from the inside out and naming the law you use at each step.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Ravi says: "Both of those setups pair a 333 with a 555 and neither one moves the base, so 63×656^3 \times 6^563×65 and (63)5(6^3)^5(63)5 have to come out the same." Compare the two setups by what each one asks to be multiplied, and give Ravi a test he can run on any printed expression to see which operation on the exponents it is calling for.

      Compare the two methods Say what each one costs you, and when you would reach for it. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      These are two different questions about the same numbers. Ask each one what it wants multiplied before you touch either exponent.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      The order of operations fixes the running order: whatever sits inside a bracket has to be settled before that bracket can take part in anything larger.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Count how many factors of the base each setup finally holds, and notice that one of those counts arrives from an addition and the other from a multiplication.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    63×65=686^3 \times 6^5 = 6^863×65=68 and (63)5=615(6^3)^5 = 6^{15}(63)5=615.

    Part B

    282^828.

    Part C

    They differ: two powers of the same base standing side by side pool their factors, so the counts add, while an outer exponent makes that many copies of a whole power, so the counts multiply. The test is where the second exponent sits: on a power of its own, or outside a bracket that already holds one.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Side by side. Three factors of 666 next to five factors of 666 make one run of eight, so the counts add:

    63×65=63+5=68.6^3 \times 6^5 = 6^{3+5} = 6^8.63×65=63+5=68.

    There is one group here, formed by pouring two runs together.

    One inside the other. The outer exponent says to use 636^363 as a factor five times over, so there are five groups and each group holds three factors of 666:

    (63)5=63×63×63×63×63.(6^3)^5 = 6^3 \times 6^3 \times 6^3 \times 6^3 \times 6^3.(63)5=63×63×63×63×63.

    Five groups of three factors is fifteen factors in all, and repeated addition of equal groups is multiplication:

    (63)5=63×5=615.(6^3)^5 = 6^{3 \times 5} = 6^{15}.(63)5=63×5=615.

    So the same two numbers meet an addition in one setup and a multiplication in the other, 3+5=83 + 5 = 83+5=8 against 3×5=153 \times 5 = 153×5=15, and the second answer carries almost twice as many factors as the first.

    Part B

    Work outward from the innermost bracket, as the order of operations directs. On top sits a power of a power, four groups of five factors, so the exponents multiply:

    (25)4=25×4=220.(2^5)^4 = 2^{5 \times 4} = 2^{20}.(25)4=25×4=220.

    Underneath sit two powers of the same base side by side, so their counts add:

    23×29=23+9=212.2^3 \times 2^9 = 2^{3+9} = 2^{12}.23×29=23+9=212.

    The expression is now one power over another with the same base and the larger count on top, so cancel in pairs, which subtracts the counts:

    220212=220−12=28.\frac{2^{20}}{2^{12}} = 2^{20-12} = 2^8.212220​=220−12=28.

    Each step used exactly one law, and which law it was depended only on the shape the expression had at that moment. That is the whole method: look at the shape, apply the matching rule, look again.

    Part C

    Ravi is right that the base does not move, and that is the only thing the two setups share.

    What each asks to be multiplied. In 63×656^3 \times 6^563×65 the two powers are separate quantities being multiplied together, so their factors simply pool: three copies of 666 joining five copies of 666 give a run of eight, and the counts add. In (63)5(6^3)^5(63)5 there is only one power, and the outer exponent is an instruction about it: use the whole of 636^363 as a factor five times. That builds five equal groups of three factors, and equal groups multiply:

    63+5=68against63×5=615.6^{3+5} = 6^8 \qquad \text{against} \qquad 6^{3 \times 5} = 6^{15}.63+5=68against63×5=615.

    Seven extra factors of 666 is not a small discrepancy. Since 615=68×676^{15} = 6^8 \times 6^7615=68×67, the second result is 676^767 times the first, so these are nowhere near each other.

    A test for any expression. Look at where the second exponent is written. If it sits on a base of its own, with a multiplication sign between the two powers, the powers are separate quantities, and when they share a base their exponents add; when the bases differ, the product rule does not apply at all and nothing is added. If the second exponent sits outside a bracket that already contains a power, it is being applied to that whole power, and the exponents multiply, whatever the base happens to be. One reliable phrasing: ask whether the second exponent is counting copies of the base, or counting copies of the first power.

    In one line

    63×65=686^3 \times 6^5 = 6^863×65=68 while (63)5=615(6^3)^5 = 6^{15}(63)5=615, and (25)423×29=28\dfrac{(2^5)^4}{2^3 \times 2^9} = 2^823×29(25)4​=28. Powers of the same base standing side by side pool their factors, so the exponents add; an outer exponent copies a whole power, so the exponents multiply. The test is whether the second exponent counts copies of the base or copies of the first power.

    Another way: Settle the two shapes on a case you can evaluate

    If the two setups ever blur, shrink them until the arithmetic is easy and compare plain numbers. Pick two different exponents, and not both 222: with both exponents equal to 222 the two shapes genuinely coincide, since 22×22=(22)2=242^2 \times 2^2 = (2^2)^2 = 2^422×22=(22)2=24, and a test that cannot tell them apart settles nothing. Take base 222 with exponents 222 and 444:

    22×24=4×16=64,(22)4=4×4×4×4=256.2^2 \times 2^4 = 4 \times 16 = 64, \qquad (2^2)^4 = 4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 256.22×24=4×16=64,(22)4=4×4×4×4=256.

    The first is 262^626 and the second is 282^828, so the two shapes have already parted company at the smallest sizes worth trying, and the gap only widens as the exponents grow.

    When it is worth it When you cannot recall which of adding and multiplying belongs to which shape and want a check that takes ten seconds. It is also the fastest way to test any exponent rule someone hands you.

    3. Pixels, tiles, and a picture that doubles . Application, 13 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 13 pts

    A photo app stores a square picture as a grid of pixels: 272^727 pixels across and 272^727 pixels down. It draws that picture on screen in square tiles, each one 232^323 pixels across and 232^323 pixels down, laid edge to edge with none overlapping and none left over. Every number below is a count of pixels or a count of tiles, so keep the powers of 222 whole and let the laws do the arithmetic.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      How many tiles fit across one side of the picture, and how many tiles cover the whole picture? Give each count as a single power of 222 and as a plain number, and keep it clear which of your numbers count tiles and which count pixels.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 5 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      The app has a "double size" setting that doubles the picture along each side, keeping the same tiles. By what factor does the pixel count grow, and by what factor does the tile count grow? Explain, using the laws, why doubling each side does not simply double either count.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every measurement here is already a power of 222, so resist evaluating early: combine the powers with the laws first, and turn the single power into a plain number only at the end.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      Fitting one length inside another is a division. Do it on the sides, where the numbers are small, before going anywhere near the whole picture.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      The word "double" is applied to the width and then again to the height, so ask what one doubling contributes to a count of factors, and what two of them do together.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    2142^{14}214 pixels, which is 16,38416{,}38416,384 pixels.

    Part B

    24=162^4 = 1624=16 tiles across a side, and 24×24=28=2562^4 \times 2^4 = 2^8 = 25624×24=28=256 tiles covering the picture.

    Part C

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The grid has 272^727 rows, and each row holds 272^727 pixels, so the total is a product of two powers of the same base. That is the product rule, and the counts add:

    27×27=27+7=214 pixels.2^7 \times 2^7 = 2^{7+7} = 2^{14} \text{ pixels}.27×27=27+7=214 pixels.

    Multiplying it out, 214=16,3842^{14} = 16{,}384214=16,384 pixels.

    The exponent is not counting pixels. It counts factors of 222: 2142^{14}214 means fourteen 222's multiplied together, and the number that product comes to is the pixel count. Keeping that straight is what lets the rest of the question stay in powers instead of in five-digit numbers.

    The same total can be reached a second way, because the picture is square. Its side is 272^727 pixels and that side is used twice, which is a power of a power:

    (27)2=27×2=214 pixels.(2^7)^2 = 2^{7 \times 2} = 2^{14} \text{ pixels}.(27)2=27×2=214 pixels.

    Adding 7+77 + 77+7 and multiplying 7×27 \times 27×2 agree here for the same reason that doubling a number and adding it to itself agree.

    Part B

    Across one side, the question is how many runs of 232^323 pixels fit inside 272^727 pixels, which is a division of two powers of 222 with the larger count on top:

    2723=27−3=24=16 tiles across.\frac{2^7}{2^3} = 2^{7-3} = 2^4 = 16 \text{ tiles across}.2327​=27−3=24=16 tiles across.

    The tiles are laid in a square grid, 242^424 of them across and 242^424 of them down, so the number of tiles is a product of powers of the same base:

    24×24=24+4=28=256 tiles.2^4 \times 2^4 = 2^{4+4} = 2^8 = 256 \text{ tiles}.24×24=24+4=28=256 tiles.

    It is worth pausing on what each number counts. 242^424 is tiles across a side, 282^828 is tiles over the whole picture, and neither is a count of pixels. One tile carries 23×23=26=642^3 \times 2^3 = 2^6 = 6423×23=26=64 pixels of its own, so 256256256 tiles of 646464 pixels each is

    26×28=214 pixels,2^6 \times 2^8 = 2^{14} \text{ pixels},26×28=214 pixels,

    which is the picture's own pixel count. The two routes agree, which is a real check on both.

    Part C

    Doubling a side multiplies its pixel count by 222, and 222 is 212^121, so the product rule gives the new side:

    2×27=21×27=28 pixels.2 \times 2^7 = 2^1 \times 2^7 = 2^8 \text{ pixels}.2×27=21×27=28 pixels.

    The enlarged picture is 282^828 across and 282^828 down, so its pixel count is

    28×28=216 pixels.2^8 \times 2^8 = 2^{16} \text{ pixels}.28×28=216 pixels.

    Compare that with the old count by dividing, which subtracts the counts of factors:

    216214=216−14=22=4.\frac{2^{16}}{2^{14}} = 2^{16-14} = 2^2 = 4.214216​=216−14=22=4.

    Four times as many pixels, not twice as many. The tiles behave the same way: each enlarged side holds 2823=25=32\tfrac{2^8}{2^3} = 2^5 = 322328​=25=32 tiles, so the picture needs 25×25=210=1,0242^5 \times 2^5 = 2^{10} = 1{,}02425×25=210=1,024 tiles, and 21028=22=4\tfrac{2^{10}}{2^8} = 2^2 = 428210​=22=4 again.

    The reason is that the doubling is applied twice, once in each direction. Each doubling contributes one extra factor of 222 to the total, and two extra factors of 222 are multiplied together, not added, so they come to 22=42^2 = 422=4. Any count of equal cells filling the whole rectangle grows this way: stretch both directions by the same amount and the count grows by that amount used twice. Not everything on a grid does, though. Count the lines of the grid, or the corner points where they cross, and the pattern is different, because those are counted along the directions rather than over the region.

    In one line

    The picture holds 27×27=214=16,3842^7 \times 2^7 = 2^{14} = 16{,}38427×27=214=16,384 pixels. It is 2723=24=16\tfrac{2^7}{2^3} = 2^4 = 162327​=24=16 tiles across, so 24×24=28=2562^4 \times 2^4 = 2^8 = 25624×24=28=256 tiles cover it. Doubling each side puts one extra factor of 222 into each direction, and 2×2=42 \times 2 = 42×2=4, so both counts grow four times over, to 2162^{16}216 pixels and 2102^{10}210 tiles.

    Another way: Count the tiles through their pixels

    Instead of measuring the picture in tiles across a side, divide the whole picture's pixels by one tile's pixels. A tile is 232^323 across and 232^323 down, so it holds

    23×23=26=64 pixels,2^3 \times 2^3 = 2^6 = 64 \text{ pixels},23×23=26=64 pixels,

    and the number of tiles is then

    21426=28=256 tiles,\frac{2^{14}}{2^6} = 2^8 = 256 \text{ tiles},26214​=28=256 tiles,

    the same count the side-by-side route produced.

    When it is worth it When the picture is not square, so there is no single side to work from, or when you already have a total and want a second route to the tile count as a check. The two routes lean on different laws, so their agreement is real evidence.

    4. Three lines and two claims . Reasoning, 14 points. Question 4 of 5.

    Reasoning 14 pts

    A page of homework comes back with the answers covered up, so only the working shows. A step can go wrong in more than one way: the base can be tampered with, the wrong operation can be done to the exponents, or a law can be used where no law applies at all. Marking a line honestly means naming which of those happened, not merely noticing that the result is wrong.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Here are three lines from the page:

      (i) 94×93=8179^4 \times 9^3 = 81^794×93=817

      (ii) (43)2=45(4^3)^2 = 4^5(43)2=45

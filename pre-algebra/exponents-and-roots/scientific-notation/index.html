Scientific Notation

Intuition

The distance to the Sun is about 150,000,000150{,}000{,}000150,000,000 kilometers, and a single water molecule is about 0.00000000030.00000000030.0000000003 meters across. Writing numbers like these in full is slow, hard to read, and easy to get wrong. Miscount the zeros by one and you are off by a factor of ten. Scientific notation is the fix. It splits every positive number into two clean pieces, the meaningful digits and the size. It does that by writing the number as a coefficient times a power of ten. The Sun’s distance becomes 1.5×1081.5 \times 10^81.5×108 kilometers and the molecule becomes 3×10−103 \times 10^{-10}3×10−10 meters.

Learning goals

  • Write a positive number as a×10na \times 10^{n}a×10n with 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10
  • Explain why the coefficient range makes each positive number's form unique
  • Convert between a number and its scientific notation, moving the point left or right as needed
  • Recognize that a negative power-of-ten exponent gives a small positive value, never a negative one
  • Compare magnitudes by exponent first, coefficient only to break a tie
  • Multiply and divide by combining coefficients and exponents, then renormalize

What scientific notation is

A positive number is in scientific notation when it is written in the form

a×10n,a \times 10^{n},a×10n,

where the coefficient aaa satisfies 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10 and the exponent nnn is an integer (it may be positive, zero, or negative). The two pieces do two separate jobs. The coefficient aaa carries the number’s digits, written in order. The power 10n10^n10n carries the magnitude, the size, telling you where the decimal point really sits. Pulling these apart is the whole point: 4.2×1094.2 \times 10^94.2×109 and 4.2×10−94.2 \times 10^{-9}4.2×10−9 share the same digits (4.24.24.2) and differ only in size.

A power of ten with a positive exponent is a 111 followed by that many zeros, so 103=100010^3 = 1000103=1000. A power of ten with a negative exponent is the reciprocal of the matching positive power, 10−n=110n10^{-n} = \frac{1}{10^n}10−n=10n1​, so 10−3=1103=11000=0.00110^{-3} = \frac{1}{10^3} = \frac{1}{1000} = 0.00110−3=1031​=10001​=0.001.

A single digit sitting in a place-value chart is already a number in this form. In the chart below you choose the digit and the column it occupies. The readout then names that digit’s value as the digit multiplied by a power of ten, which is the shape scientific notation uses.

Pick the digit 333 and walk it from the ones column out to the thousands. The value climbs 333, 303030, 300300300, 300030003000, which is 3×1003 \times 10^03×100, then 3×1013 \times 10^13×101, 3×1023 \times 10^23×102, 3×1033 \times 10^33×103. The digit is the coefficient and the column supplies the power of ten. Now change the digit without moving it, and watch the power of ten hold still while the value changes. A coefficient such as 1.51.51.5 or 4.834.834.83 does the same job, with more digits to carry.

Why the coefficient stays between 1 and 10

The rule 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10 looks fussy, but it is there for a real reason: without it, the same number could be written many different ways. Take 300030003000. Every line below equals 300030003000:

3000=3×103=30×102=0.3×104=300×101.3000 = 3 \times 10^3 = 30 \times 10^2 = 0.3 \times 10^4 = 300 \times 10^1.3000=3×103=30×102=0.3×104=300×101.

All four are true, yet only the first has a coefficient in the range 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10. Restricting the coefficient to a single nonzero digit before the decimal point means aaa is at least 111 but less than 101010. Requiring that range picks out exactly one of these forms as the official one. So two people who convert 300030003000 correctly always get the identical expression, 3×1033 \times 10^33×103.

Here is the reason there is only one such form. Take 620062006200 and divide by 101010 again and again: 6200,  620,  62,  6.2,  0.62, …6200,\; 620,\; 62,\; 6.2,\; 0.62,\, \ldots6200,620,62,6.2,0.62,…, each one a tenth of the number before it. Walking down that list, 626262 is still too big and 0.620.620.62 has already dropped under 111. Only 6.26.26.2 lands in between, at or above 111 but below 101010. Reaching 6.26.26.2 from 620062006200 took three divisions by 101010, so 6200=6.2×1036200 = 6.2 \times 10^36200=6.2×103.

Nothing about that walk was special to 620062006200. Dividing by 101010 always shrinks a number tenfold, and multiplying by 101010 always grows it tenfold. So the candidates step past 1,10,100,…1, 10, 100, \ldots1,10,100,… one at a time and land between 111 and 101010 exactly once.

The walk above works for 620062006200, but the same reasoning applies to any positive number. Dividing or multiplying by 101010 moves the coefficient through the same list of candidates, and exactly one of them lands between 111 and 101010. You can read that argument written for any positive number in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

So 30×10230 \times 10^230×102 is a perfectly correct equation, and it does equal 300030003000. But 30×10230 \times 10^230×102 is not proper scientific notation, because the coefficient 303030 is not less than 101010. The same goes for 0.3×1040.3 \times 10^40.3×104, whose coefficient 0.30.30.3 is less than 111.

Large numbers: a positive exponent

The decimal point has to end up with exactly one nonzero digit in front of it. The exponent then comes from counting how far the point moved to reach that spot.

Start with 150,000,000150{,}000{,}000150,000,000. Its decimal point is at the far right (a whole number’s point sits after the last digit). Slide it left until just one digit, the leading 111, stands before it:

150,000,000.  ⟶  1.5×10?150{,}000{,}000. \;\longrightarrow\; 1.5 \times 10^{?}150,000,000.⟶1.5×10?

Counting the hops, the point moves 888 places to the left to get from after the final zero to between the 111 and the 555. Each leftward hop is a division by 101010, and there were 888 of them. So to keep the value unchanged you must multiply back by 101010 eight times, that is by 10810^8108:

150,000,000=1.5×108.150{,}000{,}000 = 1.5 \times 10^{8}.150,000,000=1.5×108.

The exponent is positive because the original number is large (at least 101010), and it equals the number of places the point moved left. The check is quick: 10810^8108 is 111 followed by eight zeros, and 1.5×100,000,000=150,000,0001.5 \times 100{,}000{,}000 = 150{,}000{,}0001.5×100,000,000=150,000,000, the number you started with.

Worked example 1 Write 48,30048{,}30048,300 in scientific notation

The decimal point starts after the final 000, as the form 48,300.48{,}300.48,300. shows. Move the point left until a single nonzero digit sits in front, which means placing it just after the 444:

48,300.  ⟶  4.830048{,}300. \;\longrightarrow\; 4.830048,300.⟶4.8300

Count the hops the point made: from after the last 000 to between the 444 and the 888 is 444 places. Trailing zeros that fall after the last nonzero digit are dropped from the coefficient, since 4.83004.83004.8300 and 4.834.834.83 are the same number. So the coefficient is 4.834.834.83. The point moved 444 places left, so the exponent is 444:

48,300=4.83×104.48{,}300 = 4.83 \times 10^{4}.48,300=4.83×104.

The coefficient 4.834.834.83 is between 111 and 101010, as required, and 4.83×10,000=48,3004.83 \times 10{,}000 = 48{,}3004.83×10,000=48,300 checks out.

Small numbers: a negative exponent

Numbers smaller than 111 work the same way. But now the decimal point moves the other direction, to the right, and the exponent comes out negative.

Take 0.000420.000420.00042. To get one nonzero digit in front of the point, slide the point right until it sits just after the 444:

0.00042  ⟶  4.2×10?0.00042 \;\longrightarrow\; 4.2 \times 10^{?}0.00042⟶4.2×10?

The point moves 444 places to the right to travel from its start to between the 444 and the 222. Each rightward hop is a multiplication by 101010, and there were 444 of them. So to leave the value unchanged you must divide back by 101010 four times, that is multiply by 10−410^{-4}10−4:

0.00042=4.2×10−4.0.00042 = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}.0.00042=4.2×10−4.

The negative exponent is not a sign that the number itself is negative. The number 0.000420.000420.00042 is positive; it is simply small, between 000 and 111. The minus sign on the exponent means “reciprocal”: 10−4=1104=110,000=0.000110^{-4} = \frac{1}{10^4} = \frac{1}{10{,}000} = 0.000110−4=1041​=10,0001​=0.0001, and 4.2×0.0001=0.000424.2 \times 0.0001 = 0.000424.2×0.0001=0.00042. In scientific notation, a negative exponent on the power of ten always marks a positive number between 000 and 111, never a number below zero.

Worked example 2 Write 0.03070.03070.0307 in scientific notation

The number is between 000 and 111, so expect a negative exponent. Slide the decimal point right until one nonzero digit stands in front of it, which means placing it just after the 333:

0.0307  ⟶  3.070.0307 \;\longrightarrow\; 3.070.0307⟶3.07

The leading zeros before the 333 are placeholders, and they vanish once the point is repositioned. The zero between the 333 and the 777 stays, because it is part of the number’s value, not a placeholder. Count the hops: the point moves 222 places to the right to get from the start of 0.03070.03070.0307 to just after the 333. Moving right gives a negative exponent of that size:

0.0307=3.07×10−2.0.0307 = 3.07 \times 10^{-2}.0.0307=3.07×10−2.

Check it: 10−2=1100=0.0110^{-2} = \frac{1}{100} = 0.0110−2=1001​=0.01, and 3.07×0.01=0.03073.07 \times 0.01 = 0.03073.07×0.01=0.0307.

Converting back to standard form

Going the other way, from scientific notation to an ordinary number, the point moves in the opposite direction from before. You already have the coefficient, and you read the exponent as a direction and a distance to move its decimal point.

Take 6.02×1056.02 \times 10^{5}6.02×105. Multiplying by 10510^{5}105 makes the number a hundred thousand times bigger, so the point travels 555 places right. Starting from 6.026.026.02 there are only two digits after the point, so three extra zeros fill the remaining places:

6.02×105=602,000.6.02 \times 10^{5} = 602{,}000.6.02×105=602,000.

Now take 7.4×10−37.4 \times 10^{-3}7.4×10−3. Here 10−3=0.00110^{-3} = 0.00110−3=0.001 is tiny, so multiplying by it must shrink the coefficient, and the point travels 333 places left, padding with zeros:

7.4×10−3=0.0074.7.4 \times 10^{-3} = 0.0074.7.4×10−3=0.0074.

In both cases the direction followed the size. A positive exponent grows the number, so move the point right that many places, filling empty places with zeros. A negative exponent shrinks it, so move the point left that many places.

Worked example 3 Write 9.1×1069.1 \times 10^{6}9.1×106 and 2.5×10−42.5 \times 10^{-4}2.5×10−4 as ordinary numbers

For 9.1×1069.1 \times 10^{6}9.1×106, the +6+6+6 moves the point 666 places right. After the 9.19.19.1 there is one digit past the point, so five zeros fill the rest:

9.1×106=9,100,000.9.1 \times 10^{6} = 9{,}100{,}000.9.1×106=9,100,000.

2.5×10−4=0.00025.2.5 \times 10^{-4} = 0.00025.2.5×10−4=0.00025.

Comparing magnitudes at a glance

Compare 8.1×1048.1 \times 10^{4}8.1×104 and 3.5×1063.5 \times 10^{6}3.5×106. The exponents are 444 and 666, and 6>46 > 46>4, so 3.5×1063.5 \times 10^{6}3.5×106 is the larger number, even though its coefficient 3.53.53.5 is the smaller of the two. The coefficient 8.18.18.1 cannot close that gap. It is only about twice 3.53.53.5, while 10610^{6}106 is a hundred times 10410^{4}104.

The power of ten carries the magnitude. So as long as both coefficients sit in the standard range 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10, the number with the larger exponent is larger. You only look at the coefficients to break a tie when the exponents are equal.

Here is why. Compare 1.2×1031.2 \times 10^{3}1.2×103 with 9.6×1029.6 \times 10^{2}9.6×102, where the smaller exponent carries the bigger coefficient and the exponents are as close as they can get. Since 1.21.21.2 is at least 111, the first number is at least 1×103=10001 \times 10^{3} = 10001×103=1000. Since 9.69.69.6 is below 101010, the second is under 10×102=100010 \times 10^{2} = 100010×102=1000. The two bounds meet exactly at 100010001000, so 9.6×102<1000≤1.2×1039.6 \times 10^{2} < 1000 \le 1.2 \times 10^{3}9.6×102<1000≤1.2×103: by hand, 960<1200960 < 1200960<1200.

That gap can never close, whatever numbers you pick. Swapping coefficients changes a number by less than a factor of 101010, since every coefficient stays below 101010. Raising the exponent by even 111 always multiplies the number by a full factor of 101010, which no coefficient swap can match. That is why a larger exponent always wins once both coefficients are normalized to 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10.

The boundary case above is the whole reason, said with numbers. The same idea also has a general form, comparing a×10ma \times 10^ma×10m with b×10nb \times 10^nb×10n for any coefficients with 1≤a,b<101 \le a, b < 101≤a,b<10. You can read that general form in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

Multiplying and dividing in scientific notation

Scientific notation also makes multiplication and division of awkward numbers manageable, because the laws of exponents do the heavy lifting. After either operation, the new coefficient might fall outside 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10, so you renormalize. If it is 101010 or more, shift one factor of ten back into the power, raising the exponent by 111. If it is below 111, borrow one factor of ten, lowering the exponent by 111.

Multiplying

Take (2×103)×(4×102)(2 \times 10^{3}) \times (4 \times 10^{2})(2×103)×(4×102). Reordering the factors puts the coefficients side by side and the powers of ten side by side: 2×4=82 \times 4 = 82×4=8, and 103×102=10510^{3} \times 10^{2} = 10^{5}103×102=105, so the product is 8×1058 \times 10^{5}8×105. The exponents added because the first factor carried three tens and the second carried two, which is five tens in all. To multiply, multiply the coefficients and add the exponents, since 10m×10n=10m+n10^{m} \times 10^{n} = 10^{m+n}10m×10n=10m+n by the product rule.

Worked example 5 Compute (5×106)×(4×103)(5 \times 10^{6}) \times (4 \times 10^{3})(5×106)×(4×103) and fix the coefficient

Multiply the coefficients and add the exponents:

(5×106)×(4×103)=(5×4)×106+3=20×109.(5 \times 10^{6}) \times (4 \times 10^{3}) = (5 \times 4) \times 10^{6+3} = 20 \times 10^{9}.(5×106)×(4×103)=(5×4)×106+3=20×109.

The coefficient 202020 is not less than 101010, so 20×10920 \times 10^{9}20×109 is not yet proper scientific notation. Rewrite 202020 as 2×102 \times 102×10, then merge that extra factor of ten into the power using the product rule:

20×109=(2×101)×109=2×101+9=2×1010.20 \times 10^{9} = (2 \times 10^{1}) \times 10^{9} = 2 \times 10^{1+9} = 2 \times 10^{10}.20×109=(2×101)×109=2×101+9=2×1010.

Now the coefficient 222 is in range, so the final answer is 2×10102 \times 10^{10}2×1010.

Dividing

To divide, divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents, since 10m10n=10m−n\frac{10^{m}}{10^{n}} = 10^{m-n}10n10m​=10m−n by the quotient rule. Dividing can also push the coefficient below 111, which renormalizes the other way.

=0.3×107−3=0.3×104.= 0.3 \times 10^{7-3} = 0.3 \times 10^{4}.=0.3×107−3=0.3×104.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Letting the coefficient leave the range from 1 to 10. Proper scientific notation needs 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10, so 30×10430 \times 10^{4}30×104 and 0.5×1030.5 \times 10^{3}0.5×103 are not in scientific notation, even though they are valid numbers. Shift a factor of ten until the coefficient has exactly one nonzero digit before the point.
  • Reading a negative exponent as a negative number. 4.2×10−4=0.000424.2 \times 10^{-4} = 0.000424.2×10−4=0.00042 is a small positive number. In scientific notation, the minus sign on the power-of-ten exponent marks a value between 000 and 111 (it means a reciprocal power). The exponent’s minus sign never makes the number itself negative.
  • Moving the decimal point the wrong way. A positive exponent grows the number, so the point moves right; a negative exponent shrinks it, so the point moves left. Mixing these up turns 6.02×1056.02 \times 10^{5}6.02×105 into a tiny decimal instead of 602,000602{,}000602,000.
  • Miscounting the places. The exponent equals the number of places the point moves, so count carefully, including the zeros you pass. In 0.000420.000420.00042 the point moves 444 places, not 333.
  • Forgetting to renormalize after multiplying or dividing. 20×10920 \times 10^{9}20×109 is an intermediate result, not a final answer; rewrite it as 2×10102 \times 10^{10}2×1010 so the coefficient is back in range.
  • Confusing the power 10n10^{n}10n with the exponent nnn. In 7×1037 \times 10^{3}7×103 the exponent is 333, while 103=100010^{3} = 1000103=1000 is the power itself. The exponent is the small count; the power is the big number it stands for.

Key takeaways

  • Scientific notation writes a positive number as a×10na \times 10^{n}a×10n with the coefficient 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10 and the exponent nnn an integer. The coefficient holds the digits and the power of ten holds the size.
  • Keeping 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10 makes the form unique, so each positive number has exactly one scientific-notation expression.
  • For a large number (at least 101010) the exponent is positive and equals how many places the decimal point moves left. For a small number (between 000 and 111) the exponent is negative and equals how many places the decimal point moves right.
  • A negative exponent on the power of ten marks a small positive number, using 10−n=110n10^{-n} = \frac{1}{10^{n}}10−n=10n1​; it never signals a negative value.
  • To compare, the larger exponent wins (coefficients break a tie).
  • To multiply, multiply coefficients and add exponents. To divide, divide coefficients and subtract exponents. Either way, renormalize afterward so 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10.

Why every positive number has exactly one scientific-notation form

The lesson showed the reasoning on 620062006200: dividing again and again by 101010 steps past every power of ten exactly once. Exactly one of those steps lands the coefficient between 111 and 101010. Here it is again, run first on a different number, and then written so it covers every positive number at once.

Why exactly one power of ten makes the coefficient land between 1 and 10#

Run the walk on 47,00047{,}00047,000 first. Dividing by 101010 repeatedly gives 47,000,  4700,  470,  47,  4.7,  0.47, …47{,}000,\; 4700,\; 470,\; 47,\; 4.7,\; 0.47,\, \ldots47,000,4700,470,47,4.7,0.47,…, each a tenth of the one before. 474747 is still ten or more, and 0.470.470.47 has already dropped under 111. Only 4.74.74.7 sits at or above 111 while staying below 101010, reached after four divisions by 101010, so 47,000=4.7×10447{,}000 = 4.7 \times 10^{4}47,000=4.7×104.

Now take any positive number and ask which powers of ten you could divide or multiply it by. Dividing by 101010 moves the decimal point one place left and shrinks the number tenfold; multiplying by 101010 moves it one place right and grows the number tenfold. So the candidate coefficients you could reach, …,  a×10,  a,  a÷10,  …\ldots, \; a \times 10,\; a,\; a \div 10, \; \ldots…,a×10,a,a÷10,…, each differ from the next by a single factor of 101010.

The interval from 111 up to (but not including) 101010 is exactly one factor of ten wide, since its far end, 101010, is ten times its near end, 111. Because each candidate is ten times the one below it, the candidates step past 1,10,100,…1, 10, 100, \ldots1,10,100,… one at a time. So exactly one of them lands at or above 111 while staying below 101010. Land lower and the value is under 111; land higher and it is 101010 or more.

That single landing spot is the coefficient aaa, and the number of factors of 101010 moved to reach it is the exponent nnn. Since there is exactly one landing spot, every positive number has exactly one way to be written as a×10na \times 10^{n}a×10n with 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10.

∎

Why a larger exponent always wins, for any two normalized numbers

The lesson showed the reasoning using 1.2×1031.2 \times 10^{3}1.2×103 and 9.6×1029.6 \times 10^{2}9.6×102, the closest a smaller exponent can get to catching up. Here it is again, checked first on a different pair, and then written for any two normalized numbers.

Why a larger exponent beats any normalized coefficient#

Check it again on 3.1×1043.1 \times 10^{4}3.1×104 versus 9.4×1039.4 \times 10^{3}9.4×103. Since 3.1≥13.1 \ge 13.1≥1, the first number is at least 1×104=10,0001 \times 10^{4} = 10{,}0001×104=10,000. Since 9.4<109.4 < 109.4<10, the second is under 10×103=10,00010 \times 10^{3} = 10{,}00010×103=10,000. So 9.4×103<10,000≤3.1×1049.4 \times 10^{3} < 10{,}000 \le 3.1 \times 10^{4}9.4×103<10,000≤3.1×104, which checks by hand: 9400<31,0009400 < 31{,}0009400<31,000.

Now compare a×10ma \times 10^{m}a×10m and b×10nb \times 10^{n}b×10n for any coefficients with 1≤a<101 \le a < 101≤a<10 and 1≤b<101 \le b < 101≤b<10, and suppose m>nm > nm>n, so mmm is at least n+1n + 1n+1.

The first number is at least its smallest possible value, when a=1a = 1a=1: a×10m≥1×10m=10ma \times 10^{m} \ge 1 \times 10^{m} = 10^{m}a×10m≥1×10m=10m.

The second number is below its largest possible value: since b<10b < 10b<10, b×10n<10×10n=10n+1b \times 10^{n} < 10 \times 10^{n} = 10^{n+1}b×10n<10×10n=10n+1. And because m≥n+1m \ge n + 1m≥n+1, that upper bound 10n+110^{n+1}10n+1 is at most 10m10^{m}10m.

Chaining these together, the second number is strictly less than 10n+1≤10m10^{n+1} \le 10^{m}10n+1≤10m, while the first number is at least 10m10^{m}10m:

b×10n<10n+1≤10m≤a×10m.b \times 10^{n} < 10^{n+1} \le 10^{m} \le a \times 10^{m}.b×10n<10n+1≤10m≤a×10m.

So b×10n<a×10mb \times 10^{n} < a \times 10^{m}b×10n<a×10m: whenever m>nm > nm>n, the number with the larger exponent is larger, whatever the coefficients are. Nothing in that chain used the values 1.21.21.2, 9.69.69.6, 3.13.13.1, or 9.49.49.4: it needed only the two range conditions and m>nm > nm>n. When the exponents are equal, the powers of ten match, and the comparison falls back to the coefficients alone.

∎

A bit of history (optional)

Suppose you want to name a number far larger than anything you meet in everyday life. Picture a one with a hundred zeros behind it. Written out in full it swallows a whole line and tells a reader almost nothing about its size. So what do you call it?

Edward Kasner was a mathematician who enjoyed explaining his subject to ordinary readers. On a walk around 1920 he asked his nine-year-old nephew for a word. The boy, Milton Sirotta, said such a number should be called a googol. The name has stuck to it ever since.

Now look at what the notation does to the same monster. A googol is 1×101001 \times 10^{100}1×10100. The coefficient holds one digit and the exponent holds the magnitude. The whole quantity fits in a few marks on the page.

Once you pull those two pieces apart, no number is too enormous to compare with another.

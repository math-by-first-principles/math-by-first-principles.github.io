Order of Operations

Intuition

Read the expression 2+3×42 + 3 \times 42+3×4 and try to find its value. If you add first, you get 5×4=205 \times 4 = 205×4=20. If you multiply first, you get 2+12=142 + 12 = 142+12=14. Same five symbols, two different answers. Nothing in the symbols says which operation to do first. Two careful readers can pick different first steps, and both will feel sure they are right. The order of operations is the shared rule that removes the choice. Once everyone reads a line the same way, it has one reading, and here that reading works out to 141414.

Learning goals

  • Explain why a written expression needs one agreed reading before it names a number
  • Evaluate an expression in four tiers, from grouping down to addition and subtraction
  • Say why ranking multiplication first lets a product be written without parentheses
  • Work left to right within a tier, so 8÷4×28 \div 4 \times 28÷4×2 is 444
  • Settle grouping symbols from the innermost outward, and read a fraction bar as grouping

Why we need an agreed order at all

The symbols cannot settle it, so we agree on one order and everybody uses it. An agreement like that is called a convention. Nobody proved it, and a different choice would have worked just as well, as long as everyone made the same one. That is what it buys: a written line means the same thing to its writer and its reader.

The tiers, and why they are ranked this way

The agreed order sorts operations into tiers and works through the tiers from the top down. From first to last:

  1. Grouping. Anything inside parentheses (  )(\;)(), brackets [  ][\;][], or braces {  }\{\;\}{} is evaluated first, working from the innermost group outward.
  2. Exponents. Powers are evaluated next.
  3. Multiplication and division. These two share one tier and are evaluated together, left to right.
  4. Addition and subtraction. These also share one tier, evaluated together, left to right.

Why this ranking and not some other? Nothing in arithmetic forces it. But one choice saves far more writing than the rest, and you can see which by comparing the two readings of 5+3×45 + 3 \times 45+3×4.

You met in an earlier lesson that multiplication is a short way of writing repeated addition, so 3×43 \times 43×4 is a short way of writing 4+4+44 + 4 + 44+4+4. Take the multiplication first, and the line counts three 444s with a lone 555 beside them:

5+3×4=5+(4+4+4)=5+12=17.5 + 3 \times 4 = 5 + (4 + 4 + 4) = 5 + 12 = 17.5+3×4=5+(4+4+4)=5+12=17.

Take the addition first, as (5+3)×4=32(5 + 3) \times 4 = 32(5+3)×4=32, and the line counts eight 444s with nothing standing beside them. The 555 has stopped being a quantity of its own and has become part of how many copies there are. The two readings describe different collections, so the symbols alone cannot pick between them. Only the agreement can.

It picks the first one, and the reason is practical. The shape we write most often is a few products added together, the way a shopping bill is. Ranking multiplication above addition lets every one of those products be written bare. We get to write 5+3×45 + 3 \times 45+3×4 and never have to write 5+(3×4)5 + (3 \times 4)5+(3×4). The other ranking would work perfectly well if everyone used it, but then almost every bill would need parentheses.

Exponents sit above multiplication for the same practical reason. A power is a short way of writing repeated multiplication. In 232^323 the small raised 333 counts how many copies of 222 are multiplied together:

23=2×2×2=8.2^3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8.23=2×2×2=8.

Read 323^232 the same way. It is 3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9, not 3×23 \times 23×2. Ranking powers above multiplication lets a power be written bare too, so 5×235 \times 2^35×23 means 5×8=405 \times 8 = 405×8=40.

Grouping sits above all of them for a different reason. A pair of parentheses is the one instruction you write by hand. It says “treat what is inside me as one quantity,” and that overrides every ranking below it.

Why same-tier operations go left to right

Multiplication and division sit on the same tier, and so do addition and subtraction. When two operations from one tier appear together, the rule is to work strictly left to right. Without a rule of that kind, some chains of subtractions would give different values depending on how a reader grouped them.

Why a left-to-right convention is needed for 10−3−210 - 3 - 210−3−2#

Unlike addition, subtraction cares how you group it: the grouping changes the answer. Take 10−3−210 - 3 - 210−3−2 and group it the two ways:

(10−3)−2=7−2=5,10−(3−2)=10−1=9.(10 - 3) - 2 = 7 - 2 = 5, \qquad 10 - (3 - 2) = 10 - 1 = 9.(10−3)−2=7−2=5,10−(3−2)=10−1=9.

The two groupings give 555 and 999, which are different numbers. So the symbols on their own do not pick a value here, and the agreed reading has to. That reading is left to right, so each operation acts on the running total built so far. Take 101010, subtract 333 to get the running total 777, then subtract 222 from that to land on 555.

The same reading settles a mixed line like 8−3+28 - 3 + 28−3+2. Left to right, subtract first to get the running total 555, then add 222 for 777. Reading it as 8−(3+2)=8−5=38 - (3 + 2) = 8 - 5 = 38−(3+2)=8−5=3 adds a pair of parentheses the expression never wrote. The agreed reading exists so that nobody gets to add them.

∎

The same reasoning settles division. The expression 16÷4÷216 \div 4 \div 216÷4÷2 means (16÷4)÷2=4÷2=2(16 \div 4) \div 2 = 4 \div 2 = 2(16÷4)÷2=4÷2=2, not 16÷(4÷2)=16÷2=816 \div (4 \div 2) = 16 \div 2 = 816÷(4÷2)=16÷2=8. Read same-tier operations strictly from left to right, and you will never have to guess.

Check your understanding

Evaluate 2+3×42 + 3 \times 42+3×4.

Answer choices

Multiplication is on a higher tier than addition, so it goes first.

2+3×4=2+12=142 + 3 \times 4 = 2 + 12 = 142+3×4=2+12=14

Adding first would give the incorrect 202020, because it would treat 2+32 + 32+3 as one quantity that the expression never grouped.

Check your understanding

Evaluate 20−6−420 - 6 - 420−6−4.

Answer choices

Subtraction shares a tier with itself, so work left to right.

20−6−4=14−4=1020 - 6 - 4 = 14 - 4 = 1020−6−4=14−4=10

Doing 6−46 - 46−4 first would wrongly give 181818, which silently groups the last two numbers.

What a grouping symbol actually tells you

Grouping symbols are the one place where you, the writer, get to override the ranking of the tiers. A pair of parentheses says “treat everything inside me as one quantity, and settle it before any operation outside is allowed to use it.”

A grouping symbol does not mean “do everything inside first, then ignore the order of operations for the rest of the expression.” Once a group is settled down to a single number, the operations outside it still obey the same tiers as always. Look at 4+2×(5−1)4 + 2 \times (5 - 1)4+2×(5−1). The parentheses tell you to settle 5−1=45 - 1 = 45−1=4 first, and that group then becomes the single number 444. But from that moment the rest of the line is back to ordinary rules, so the multiplication still happens before the addition.

Worked example 1 Evaluate 4+2×(5−1)4 + 2 \times (5 - 1)4+2×(5−1), one operation per line

Settle the group first, then return to the normal tiers for what remains. Take one step at a time:

4+2×(5−1)=4+2×44 + 2 \times (5 - 1) = 4 + 2 \times 44+2×(5−1)=4+2×4

The group is now the single number 444. It does not get to “go first” again; the leftover expression is just 4+2×44 + 2 \times 44+2×4, where multiplication outranks addition:

4+2×4=4+84 + 2 \times 4 = 4 + 84+2×4=4+84+8=124 + 8 = 124+8=12

So the value is 121212. The mistake to avoid is finishing the parentheses and then sweeping left to right, 4+2=64 + 2 = 64+2=6 then 6×4=246 \times 4 = 246×4=24. The parentheses gave the subtraction an early turn; they did not switch off the tiers for everything else.

When you do want an operation like addition to happen before a multiplication, parentheses are exactly the tool. Writing (4+2)×5(4 + 2) \times 5(4+2)×5 forces the sum first and gives 6×5=306 \times 5 = 306×5=30, a different number from the un-grouped 4+2×5=144 + 2 \times 5 = 144+2×5=14. Grouping is how you bend the order on purpose.

Nested groups: innermost first

When a group sits inside another group, settle the innermost one first and work outward. Brackets and braces behave exactly like parentheses; the different shapes are only there to make each matching pair easy to spot at a glance.

Worked example 2 Evaluate 3×[ 4+(10−2×3) ]3 \times [\,4 + (10 - 2 \times 3)\,]3×[4+(10−2×3)], one operation per line

Start at the innermost group. Even inside parentheses the tiers still apply, so the multiplication 2×32 \times 32×3 goes before the subtraction:

3×[ 4+(10−2×3) ]=3×[ 4+(10−6) ]3 \times [\,4 + (10 - 2 \times 3)\,] = 3 \times [\,4 + (10 - 6)\,]3×[4+(10−2×3)]=3×[4+(10−6)]

Finish the inner group:

3×[ 4+(10−6) ]=3×[ 4+4 ]3 \times [\,4 + (10 - 6)\,] = 3 \times [\,4 + 4\,]3×[4+(10−6)]=3×[4+4]

Now settle the outer bracket:

3×[ 4+4 ]=3×83 \times [\,4 + 4\,] = 3 \times 83×[4+4]=3×83×8=243 \times 8 = 243×8=24

Working from the inside out keeps you from ever holding more than one small step in your head at a time.

The fraction bar is an invisible grouping symbol

A horizontal fraction bar quietly groups everything above it and everything below it, as though the numerator and the denominator each wore their own parentheses. You evaluate the entire top and the entire bottom first, then divide. This is why writing a quotient as a stacked fraction is so much safer than squeezing it onto one line.

6+42+3=105=2.\frac{6 + 4}{2 + 3} = \frac{10}{5} = 2.2+36+4​=510​=2.

Written on one line, that fraction is (6+4)÷(2+3)(6 + 4) \div (2 + 3)(6+4)÷(2+3), with both pairs of parentheses put in by hand. It is not 6+4÷2+36 + 4 \div 2 + 36+4÷2+3, which comes to 6+2+3=116 + 2 + 3 = 116+2+3=11 under the tiers. Dropping the parentheses changes which numbers are divided, and that is what changes the value from 222 to 111111. Whenever a stacked fraction has to go onto one line, write those parentheses in.

Worked example 3 Evaluate 23+46−2+1\dfrac{2^3 + 4}{6 - 2} + 16−223+4​+1, one operation per line

The bar groups the numerator and the denominator separately, so settle each fully before dividing. Inside the numerator the exponent goes first:

23+46−2+1=8+46−2+1\frac{2^3 + 4}{6 - 2} + 1 = \frac{8 + 4}{6 - 2} + 16−223+4​+1=6−28+4​+1

Finish the numerator:

8+46−2+1=126−2+1\frac{8 + 4}{6 - 2} + 1 = \frac{12}{6 - 2} + 16−28+4​+1=6−212​+1

Finish the denominator:

126−2+1=124+1\frac{12}{6 - 2} + 1 = \frac{12}{4} + 16−212​+1=412​+1

Both are single numbers now, so the bar can act as a division:

124+1=3+1\frac{12}{4} + 1 = 3 + 1412​+1=3+13+1=43 + 1 = 43+1=4

So the value is 444. The +1+1+1 sits outside the fraction, so it is never part of the numerator.

Check your understanding

Evaluate 16÷4×216 \div 4 \times 216÷4×2.

Answer choices

Multiplication and division share one tier, so work left to right.

16÷4×2=4×2=816 \div 4 \times 2 = 4 \times 2 = 816÷4×2=4×2=8

Doing 4×24 \times 24×2 first would wrongly give 222. Division does not have to wait for the multiplication.

Check your understanding

Evaluate 30−2×(3+4)30 - 2 \times (3 + 4)30−2×(3+4).

Answer choices

Settle the group, then return to the normal tiers; the group does not cancel them.

30−2×(3+4)=30−2×7=30−14=1630 - 2 \times (3 + 4) = 30 - 2 \times 7 = 30 - 14 = 1630−2×(3+4)=30−2×7=30−14=16

Finishing the parentheses and then sweeping left to right, 30−2=2830 - 2 = 2830−2=28 then 28×728 \times 728×7, ignores that multiplication still outranks subtraction.

A larger expression, start to finish

The reliable habit is to scan the whole expression, find the highest tier present, do one operation from it, rewrite the line, and repeat. Writing one step at a time makes each step easier to check.

Worked example 4 Evaluate 5+2×32−45 + 2 \times 3^2 - 45+2×32−4, one operation per line

The highest tier present is exponents, so square the 333 first:

5+2×32−4=5+2×9−45 + 2 \times 3^2 - 4 = 5 + 2 \times 9 - 45+2×32−4=5+2×9−4

Multiplication is now the highest tier, so do it next:

5+2×9−4=5+18−45 + 2 \times 9 - 4 = 5 + 18 - 45+2×9−4=5+18−4

Only addition and subtraction remain. They share a tier, so go left to right:

5+18−4=23−45 + 18 - 4 = 23 - 45+18−4=23−423−4=1923 - 4 = 1923−4=19

So 5+2×32−4=195 + 2 \times 3^2 - 4 = 195+2×32−4=19. Notice the addition and subtraction genuinely waited their turn behind the exponent and the multiplication, then ran in reading order.

Check your understanding

A student evaluated 8−2×38 - 2 \times 38−2×3 as 181818. What is the correct value?

Answer choices

Multiplication is on a higher tier than subtraction, so it goes first.

8−2×3=8−6=28 - 2 \times 3 = 8 - 6 = 28−2×3=8−6=2

The student subtracted 8−2=68 - 2 = 68−2=6 first and then multiplied by 333, sweeping left to right across two different tiers.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Thinking multiplication always comes before division. They share one tier, so position decides: 16÷4×2=816 \div 4 \times 2 = 816÷4×2=8, not 222. The same goes for addition and subtraction, where 10−4+1=710 - 4 + 1 = 710−4+1=7, not 555.
  • Treating a grouping symbol as “do everything inside, then ignore the tiers.” Once a group is a single number, the tiers outside it are still in force: 30−2×(3+4)=1630 - 2 \times (3 + 4) = 1630−2×(3+4)=16, not 196196196.
  • Sweeping left to right across different tiers. 2+3×4=142 + 3 \times 4 = 142+3×4=14, not 202020. The multiplication goes first no matter where it sits in the line.
  • Forgetting that a fraction bar groups. Settle the whole top and the whole bottom before dividing, not just the terms touching the bar.

Key takeaways

  • The order of operations is a convention, a shared agreement about how to read a written line. Under it, an expression built from the operation signs in this lesson has exactly one reading. It then has one value, provided every operation in it can be carried out.
  • Evaluate in tiers, top down: grouping, then exponents, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.
  • Multiplication is ranked above addition because that lets a product sit inside a sum with no parentheses around it. The ranking is useful rather than forced.
  • Multiplication and division share one tier; addition and subtraction share one tier. Within a tier, work strictly left to right. Multiplication is not automatically before division.
  • A grouping symbol settles its contents early, but once that group is a single number the rest of the expression still follows the tiers. With groups inside groups, start innermost.
  • A fraction bar is a grouping symbol: settle the whole numerator and the whole denominator before dividing.

You can now read any grouped calculation and get the one value it names. The last lesson of the chapter asks a different question about that same expression. When a number multiplies a group, the calculation can be rewritten in a second form entirely, and the value stays put.

A bit of history (optional)

Some rules are kept by everyone and set down by no one. The order you just learned is one of them: no vote fixed it, and no proof made it true.

In 1917 that gap caused a small public argument. A teacher named Nels Lennes wrote to a journal to complain about his own textbooks. Handed one printed line, two books would give two answers, and each was certain it was correct.

Lennes wanted an official ruling, and none ever came. What settled it was slower and duller than that. Writers copied writers, and one habit spread until its rivals vanished.

So this rule is not like the others in the lesson. It is not a truth about numbers; it is an agreement we keep. It works while we all keep it, and not one moment longer.

A line like 6÷2(1+2)6 \div 2(1+2)6÷2(1+2) still splits careful readers. The 222 and the bracket beside it are written with no times sign between them, which is a shorthand this lesson has not used. Treat it as an ordinary multiplication and the four tiers settle the line at 999. The argument is over whether a product written with no sign should outrank the division to its left. Either way the remedy is yours: write the grouping in, and nobody has to choose.

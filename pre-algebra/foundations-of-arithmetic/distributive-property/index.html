The Distributive Property

Intuition

Suppose you buy 666 gift bags, and each bag holds a toy worth 101010 dollars and a treat worth 333 dollars. You can total it two ways. Count each bag as 10+3=1310 + 3 = 1310+3=13 dollars and buy 666 of those. Or buy all 666 toys for 6×10=606 \times 10 = 606×10=60 dollars and all 666 treats for 6×3=186 \times 3 = 186×3=18 dollars, then add. Both give 787878 dollars, because they describe the very same pile of stuff. That is the distributive property. Multiplying a group by 666 is the same as multiplying each part of the group by 666 and adding the results.

Learning goals

  • Multiply a sum or a difference by sending the number outside the parentheses to every number inside
  • Explain why the rule works, using a rectangle whose width is cut in two
  • Split an awkward number around a round one to multiply in your head
  • Run the rule backward to pull out the largest number that divides both parts
  • Tell a sum inside the parentheses from a product, where the rule does not apply

The statement

Write the gift-bag total the two ways you counted it:

6(10+3)=6×10+6×3.6(10 + 3) = 6 \times 10 + 6 \times 3.6(10+3)=6×10+6×3.

Two words from the properties lesson make this easier to talk about. The numbers being added inside the parentheses, 101010 and 333, are the terms of that sum. The 666 multiplying them is a factor, and since it stands outside the parentheses we can call it the outside factor. The rule is that the outside factor multiplies every term, and then you add the products.

Here is the same sentence in letters, so that you can put your own numbers in:

a(b+c)=ab+ac.a(b + c) = ab + ac.a(b+c)=ab+ac.

In it, a(b+c)a(b + c)a(b+c) means aaa times the quantity (b+c)(b + c)(b+c), and ababab is shorthand for a×ba \times ba×b. The outside factor aaa is distributed across the terms bbb and ccc, which is where the property gets its name. In this lesson the letters stand for whole numbers, and later on the same rule will hold for fractions, decimals, and negative numbers too.

Why it is true: the area of a split rectangle

The reason the rule holds is a picture you can measure. One word first. The area of a shape is the number of squares of side 111 needed to tile it, with no gaps and no overlaps. Tile a rectangle 333 tall and 999 wide that way and it holds 333 rows with 999 squares in each row. That is why its area is the height times the width, 3×9=273 \times 9 = 273×9=27. Counting rows of squares is all the geometry this argument needs; a later chapter takes area up in its own right.

Now cut that rectangle’s width into 444 and 555. Nothing has been added or taken away, so the area is still 272727. It now sits in two pieces: a 333 by 444 piece of area 121212, and a 333 by 555 piece of area 151515. And 12+15=2712 + 15 = 2712+15=27.

Nothing in that argument depended on the numbers 333, 444, and 555. Put the same picture in letters. A rectangle aaa tall and b+cb + cb+c wide has area a(b+c)a(b + c)a(b+c). One vertical cut splits the width into a piece of length bbb and a piece of length ccc. That gives an aaa by bbb piece of area ababab, and an aaa by ccc piece of area acacac. The whole is still the sum of its parts:

a(b+c)=ab+ac.a(b + c) = ab + ac.a(b+c)=ab+ac.

A rectangle’s area does not care where you chop its width, so multiplying the whole width at once must equal multiplying the pieces and adding.

Check your understanding

A rectangle is 555 tall and 888 wide. One cut splits its width into 333 and 555. What are the two piece areas, and what do they add to?

Answer choices

A cut from top to bottom changes only the widths, so both pieces are still 555 tall.

5×3=15,5×5=25,15+25=405 \times 3 = 15, \qquad 5 \times 5 = 25, \qquad 15 + 25 = 405×3=15,5×5=25,15+25=40

That total is the whole rectangle's area, 5×8=405 \times 8 = 405×8=40, because the cut moved no space.

More than two terms is no different. Cut the width twice instead of once, and the outside factor reaches all three pieces:

3(10+4+2)=3×10+3×4+3×2=30+12+6=48.3(10 + 4 + 2) = 3 \times 10 + 3 \times 4 + 3 \times 2 = 30 + 12 + 6 = 48.3(10+4+2)=3×10+3×4+3×2=30+12+6=48.

Checking straight through, 3×16=483 \times 16 = 483×16=48 as well.

In the picture above the cut is drawn for you. In the rectangle below you set the width and the height, and the readout does the cutting. It splits the width down the middle and reports both piece areas beside the whole.

Set the height to 444 and the width to 777. Then change the width to 999, and after that raise the height. Each time, add the two piece areas and compare that total with the area of the whole rectangle. They match every time, because a(b+c)a(b + c)a(b+c) and ab+acab + acab+ac count the same unit squares.

Distributing over subtraction

The rule works the same way when the parentheses hold a difference instead of a sum:

a(b−c)=ab−ac.a(b - c) = ab - ac.a(b−c)=ab−ac.

For now, keep the first number at least as large as the one being subtracted, so that the difference inside is not negative.

Picture a rectangle of width bbb with a strip of width ccc removed from the end. The full aaa by bbb area is ababab, the removed aaa by ccc strip is acacac, and what is left is ab−acab - acab−ac. For example, with a=4a = 4a=4, b=8b = 8b=8, and c=3c = 3c=3:

4(8−3)=4×5=20,4×8−4×3=32−12=20.4(8 - 3) = 4 \times 5 = 20, \qquad 4 \times 8 - 4 \times 3 = 32 - 12 = 20.4(8−3)=4×5=20,4×8−4×3=32−12=20.

Again both routes agree. The outside factor still reaches every term inside; only the sign joining the two products changes from a plus to a minus.

Mental math: distribution is the trick behind it

Distribution turns one hard multiplication into two easy ones. Split a number that is awkward to multiply into a friendly sum or difference, usually around a round number like 101010 or 100100100, then distribute.

Take 6×136 \times 136×13. The number 131313 is just 10+310 + 310+3, and multiplying by 101010 or by 333 is easy:

6×13=6(10+3)=6×10+6×3=60+18=78.6 \times 13 = 6(10 + 3) = 6 \times 10 + 6 \times 3 = 60 + 18 = 78.6×13=6(10+3)=6×10+6×3=60+18=78.

That is the same 787878 from the opening. This split is exactly how people multiply in their heads without realizing they are using a named property. When a number sits just below a round one, split it as a difference instead:

7×98=7(100−2)=700−14=686.7 \times 98 = 7(100 - 2) = 700 - 14 = 686.7×98=7(100−2)=700−14=686.

Every split gives the right answer, so choose the one that leaves you the easiest work. You want two products you can do at a glance, and an addition or subtraction you can finish in your head.

Worked example 1 Choose the easier split for 8×638 \times 638×63

The round number below 636363 is 606060, and the round number above it is 707070. Both splits are legal, so try each one. Splitting downward:

8×63=8(60+3)=480+24=504.8 \times 63 = 8(60 + 3) = 480 + 24 = 504.8×63=8(60+3)=480+24=504.

Splitting upward:

8×63=8(70−7)=560−56=504.8 \times 63 = 8(70 - 7) = 560 - 56 = 504.8×63=8(70−7)=560−56=504.

Same answer, different amounts of work. The first leaves 480+24480 + 24480+24, which you can finish at a glance; the second leaves 560−56560 - 56560−56, which takes longer. Splitting at the nearest round number usually wins, but look at the two products before you commit.

Factoring: running the rule backward

Read the rule from right to left and it says ab+ac=a(b+c)ab + ac = a(b + c)ab+ac=a(b+c). Start with two products that plainly share a factor:

4×7+4×3=4(7+3)=4×10=40.4 \times 7 + 4 \times 3 = 4(7 + 3) = 4 \times 10 = 40.4×7+4×3=4(7+3)=4×10=40.

The shared 444 has moved outside a single pair of parentheses. Pulling out a shared factor like that is called factoring. It is the distributive property traveled in the opposite direction, so each move undoes the other.

Usually the shared factor is hidden, because the terms are written as plain numbers. To factor 12+1812 + 1812+18, look for the largest number that divides both terms. That number is called their greatest common factor.

Finding it does not take a special technique yet. When both terms are above zero, no shared factor can be bigger than the smaller term. So start at that term and count downward, stopping at the first number that divides both. For 12+1812 + 1812+18 the smaller term is 121212, and 121212 itself does not divide 181818, nor does 111111, 101010, 999, 888 or 777. Then 666 divides 121212 and divides 181818, so the search stops there and 666 is the greatest common factor. Counting down works because it reaches the largest candidate first. For numbers too big to search this way, the Factors and Multiples chapter later gives a faster method.

Both 121212 and 181818 are divisible by 666, and nothing larger divides both, so rewrite each term and take the 666 out front:

12+18=6×2+6×3=6(2+3).12 + 18 = 6 \times 2 + 6 \times 3 = 6(2 + 3).12+18=6×2+6×3=6(2+3).

You can always check a factoring by distributing back: 6(2+3)=12+186(2 + 3) = 12 + 186(2+3)=12+18, which matches. Pull out the greatest common factor, not just any common factor. Taking out only 333 would give 3(4+6)3(4 + 6)3(4+6), which is true but not finished, because 4+64 + 64+6 still share a factor of 222.

Worked example 2 Factor 18+2418 + 2418+24 completely

Count down from the smaller term, 181818, and stop at the first number dividing both. Nothing from 181818 down to 777 divides both terms. For instance 999 divides 181818 but not 242424, while 888 and 121212 divide 242424 but not 181818. Then 666 divides 181818 and divides 242424, so 666 is their greatest common factor. Since 18=6×318 = 6 \times 318=6×3 and 24=6×424 = 6 \times 424=6×4,

18+24=6×3+6×4=6(3+4).18 + 24 = 6 \times 3 + 6 \times 4 = 6(3 + 4).18+24=6×3+6×4=6(3+4).

Check by distributing: 6(3+4)=18+246(3 + 4) = 18 + 246(3+4)=18+24. The leftover 3+43 + 43+4 share no common factor above 111, so the factoring is complete.

20+30=10×2+10×3=10(2+3)20 + 30 = 10 \times 2 + 10 \times 3 = 10(2 + 3)20+30=10×2+10×3=10(2+3)

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying only some of the terms. 6(10+3)6(10 + 3)6(10+3) is 60+18=7860 + 18 = 7860+18=78, not 60+3=6360 + 3 = 6360+3=63. The outside factor must reach every term inside, however many there are, so 5(2+3+4)=10+15+20=455(2 + 3 + 4) = 10 + 15 + 20 = 455(2+3+4)=10+15+20=45, with none skipped.
  • Trying to distribute over a product. Distribution works over addition and subtraction only. a(b×c)a(b \times c)a(b×c) is not ab×acab \times acab×ac. To find 4(2×5)4(2 \times 5)4(2×5) you just multiply inside first: 4×10=404 \times 10 = 404×10=40. Distributing it would wrongly give 8×20=1608 \times 20 = 1608×20=160.
  • Stopping a factoring too early. 12+18=3(4+6)12 + 18 = 3(4 + 6)12+18=3(4+6) is a true equation but not fully factored, because 4+64 + 64+6 still share a factor of 222. Always pull out the greatest common factor: 6(2+3)6(2 + 3)6(2+3).

Key takeaways

  • The rule: a(b+c)=ab+aca(b + c) = ab + aca(b+c)=ab+ac, and the same with a minus sign, a(b−c)=ab−aca(b - c) = ab - aca(b−c)=ab−ac. The outside factor multiplies every term inside the parentheses, then you add or subtract the products.
  • Why it holds: cutting a rectangle’s width changes the pieces, not the area. So the whole, a(b+c)a(b + c)a(b+c), must equal the two pieces, ab+acab + acab+ac.
  • Both directions: forward, it splits an awkward factor for mental math, as in 6×13=6(10+3)=786 \times 13 = 6(10 + 3) = 786×13=6(10+3)=78. Backward, it pulls out the greatest common factor, as in 12+18=6(2+3)12 + 18 = 6(2 + 3)12+18=6(2+3).

The counting reason for the distributive property

The rectangle explains the rule with area. Here is the same rule from the meaning of multiplication, for a whole number of copies.

Why a(b+c)=ab+aca(b + c) = ab + aca(b+c)=ab+ac#

Try it on 4(5+2)4(5 + 2)4(5+2). Multiplying by 444 means taking four copies and adding them:

4(5+2)=(5+2)+(5+2)+(5+2)+(5+2).4(5 + 2) = (5 + 2) + (5 + 2) + (5 + 2) + (5 + 2).4(5+2)=(5+2)+(5+2)+(5+2)+(5+2).

A sum can be reordered and regrouped freely, so gather the four 555s together and the four 222s together:

=(5+5+5+5)+(2+2+2+2)=20+8=28.= (5 + 5 + 5 + 5) + (2 + 2 + 2 + 2) = 20 + 8 = 28.=(5+5+5+5)+(2+2+2+2)=20+8=28.

That is 4×54 \times 54×5 plus 4×24 \times 24×2. Reading 4(5+2)4(5 + 2)4(5+2) straight through gives 4×7=284 \times 7 = 284×7=28 as well.

Now let aaa be any whole number. By the meaning of multiplication, a(b+c)a(b + c)a(b+c) is aaa copies of (b+c)(b + c)(b+c) added together:

a(b+c)=(b+c)+(b+c)+⋯+(b+c)⏟a copies.a(b + c) = \underbrace{(b + c) + (b + c) + \cdots + (b + c)}_{a \text{ copies}}.a(b+c)=a copies(b+c)+(b+c)+⋯+(b+c)​​.

Regroup as before, all the bbb terms into one group and all the ccc terms into another:

=(b+b+⋯+b)⏟a copies+(c+c+⋯+c)⏟a copies=ab+ac.= \underbrace{(b + b + \cdots + b)}_{a \text{ copies}} + \underbrace{(c + c + \cdots + c)}_{a \text{ copies}} = ab + ac.=a copies(b+b+⋯+b)​​+a copies(c+c+⋯+c)​​=ab+ac.

The only moves were reordering and regrouping the addends, and no step used the numbers 444, 555, and 222. It matches the rectangle exactly: the aaa rows are the aaa copies, and sorting the bbb part of every row from the ccc part is the single vertical cut.

∎

What happens if you split both factors

In 6×13=6(10+3)6 \times 13 = 6(10 + 3)6×13=6(10+3), only the 131313 is broken up. The outside 666 stays whole so that it reaches both pieces. What if you split it as well, writing 6×136 \times 136×13 as (3+3)(10+3)(3 + 3)(10 + 3)(3+3)(10+3)?

Then every piece of the first factor has to meet every piece of the second, which is four products rather than two:

(3+3)(10+3)=3×10+3×3+3×10+3×3=30+9+30+9=78.\begin{aligned} (3 + 3)(10 + 3) &= 3 \times 10 + 3 \times 3 + 3 \times 10 + 3 \times 3\\ &= 30 + 9 + 30 + 9 = 78. \end{aligned}(3+3)(10+3)​=3×10+3×3+3×10+3×3=30+9+30+9=78.​

The total is still 787878, but the bookkeeping is worse, and it is easy to stop after 3×10+3×3=393 \times 10 + 3 \times 3 = 393×10+3×3=39, which is exactly half the answer. Multiplying two sums together is a later topic. For now, split one factor and leave the other whole.

A bit of history (optional)

Mathematicians had been listing the rules of arithmetic for a very long time. Over the early twentieth century, what those lists were used for gradually shifted.

Rather than starting with numbers and noting the rules they follow, you can start with a short list of rules. Then you ask what else follows from that list. Anything obeying it inherits every consequence of it, whether or not it is made of numbers. Emmy Noether and Emil Artin both helped shape that way of working. Their lectures were written up by Bartel van der Waerden in a textbook called Moderne Algebra in 1930. That book helped the approach spread, and something close to it is still how algebra is taught to university students.

One of those short lists defines a structure called a ring, and the rule you learned today is on it. Most rules in arithmetic are about adding on its own, or about multiplying on its own. This one says how the two fit together. You read it off a rectangle cut in two, and it now does a great deal more work than a rectangle.

    1. One cut across a rectangular strip . Foundational, 10 points. Question 1 of 5.

    Foundational 10 pts

    A rectangular strip of card is 666 units tall and 171717 units wide. One straight cut runs from its top edge to its bottom edge, leaving a piece 121212 units wide and a piece 555 units wide. Nothing is thrown away.

    Text description of this figure

    A rectangular strip is drawn six units tall and seventeen units wide, with one vertical cut running from its top edge to its bottom edge. The cut leaves a piece twelve units wide on the left and a piece five units wide on the right. Only the height and the three widths are labeled; no areas are written in.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Find the area of the whole strip before the cut, using its height and its full width.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 2 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Find the area of each of the two pieces separately. Then add the two areas.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why the two answers had to come out equal. Give that reason without doing any arithmetic. Then say whether your reason would still hold if the cut fell somewhere else along the width.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 5 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Every rectangle in the picture shares one measurement, its height, so each area you need is a single multiplication of that height by whichever width you are looking at.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      A cut from top to bottom changes only the widths. Use the height twice, once with each of the two smaller widths, and keep the pieces separate until the very end.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      A cut does not add card and does not remove card. If the surface is unchanged, the two totals have to match.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    102102102 square units.

    Part B

    727272 square units and 303030 square units, which add to 102102102 square units.

    Part C

    The cut neither adds nor removes surface, so the pieces together cover exactly what the uncut strip covered; multiplying the full width at once therefore has to equal multiplying each piece's width and adding. The position of the cut is irrelevant, since no position can create or destroy area.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    The area of a rectangle is its height times its width, and the strip is 666 units tall and 171717 units wide:

    6×17=102.6 \times 17 = 102.6×17=102.

    The strip covers 102102102 square units.

    Part B

    Neither piece lost any height: the cut ran from the top edge straight to the bottom edge, so both pieces are still 666 units tall. Only the widths differ, 121212 for the left piece and 555 for the right.

    6×12=72,6×5=30.6 \times 12 = 72, \qquad 6 \times 5 = 30.6×12=72,6×5=30.

    Adding the two pieces accounts for every part of the card:

    72+30=102.72 + 30 = 102.72+30=102.

    Part C

    Cutting is not the same as removing. The blade moves no card and leaves no gap, so the two pieces taken together are exactly the original strip. Whatever number of square units the strip covered, the pieces cover that same number, and this is settled before any multiplying begins.

    Now read each route as a measurement of that one piece of card. Multiplying the height by the full width measures the strip in one go. Multiplying the height by 121212, then by 555, and adding measures the same card in two goes, the left part and then the right. Both must report the same surface:

    6×17=6×12+6×5.6 \times 17 = 6 \times 12 + 6 \times 5.6×17=6×12+6×5.

    That is the distributive property, with the width 171717 written as 12+512 + 512+5. Nothing in the reason used the numbers 121212 and 555. A cut anywhere along the width splits it into two widths that add back to the full width, and the areas still have to total the same, so the rule holds for every split. The height is just as free: a taller strip only repeats the argument with a different first factor.

    In one line

    The uncut strip covers 102102102 square units. The pieces cover 727272 and 303030 square units, which total 102102102. The two routes must agree because a cut creates no new card and destroys none, wherever along the width it falls, so both routes are measuring the same card.

    2. When splitting is allowed, and when it is not . Reasoning, 13 points. Question 2 of 5.

    Reasoning 13 pts

    The distributive property lets you split a factor across a sum. Does the same move work when the two numbers inside the parentheses are multiplied instead of added? Both parts below use the numbers 555, 444, and 333. Only the sign between the 444 and the 333 changes.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Work out 5×(4+3)5 \times (4 + 3)5×(4+3) by adding inside the parentheses first. Then work it out again by multiplying the 555 into the 444 and into the 333, and adding those two results. Report both totals.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Now change the +++ to a ×\times×. Work out 5×(4×3)5 \times (4 \times 3)5×(4×3) by multiplying inside the parentheses first. Then try the same splitting move: multiply the 555 into the 444, multiply the 555 into the 333, and multiply those two results together. Report both totals.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      Explain why the splitting move is allowed in part A but not in part B. Say what the rule actually asks for. Then say what goes wrong with the 555 in part B.

      Explain why it works A sentence or two. Reasons, not steps. 6 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      Work out what is inside the parentheses first, before you try any splitting. That gives you the answer each route has to match.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      You have multiplied by 555 twice over. Count how many 555s appear in your second route, then count how many the question asked for.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Look again at what the rule needs inside the parentheses. Then ask whether part B gives you one.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    Both routes give 353535: 5×7=355 \times 7 = 355×7=35, and 5×4+5×3=20+15=355 \times 4 + 5 \times 3 = 20 + 15 = 355×4+5×3=20+15=35.

    Part B

    The two routes disagree: 5×12=605 \times 12 = 605×12=60, but (5×4)×(5×3)=20×15=300(5 \times 4) \times (5 \times 3) = 20 \times 15 = 300(5×4)×(5×3)=20×15=300.

    Part C

    The rule splits a factor across a sum, the way a cut across the width of a rectangle splits its area into two pieces that add back. Part B has no sum inside the parentheses, so there is nothing to split. Multiplying both the 444 and the 333 by 555 puts the 555 into the product twice, which is why 300300300 is five times too big.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Adding inside the parentheses first:

    5×(4+3)=5×7=35.5 \times (4 + 3) = 5 \times 7 = 35.5×(4+3)=5×7=35.

    Splitting the 555 across the sum instead:

    5×4+5×3=20+15=35.5 \times 4 + 5 \times 3 = 20 + 15 = 35.5×4+5×3=20+15=35.

    The two routes agree. That agreement is the distributive property.

    Part B

    Multiplying inside the parentheses first:

    5×(4×3)=5×12=60.5 \times (4 \times 3) = 5 \times 12 = 60.5×(4×3)=5×12=60.

    Splitting the 555 into both factors instead:

    (5×4)×(5×3)=20×15=300.(5 \times 4) \times (5 \times 3) = 20 \times 15 = 300.(5×4)×(5×3)=20×15=300.

    These do not agree, and 300300300 is exactly 555 times 606060. Splitting is not allowed here.

    Part C

    Part A matches the rule exactly. A rectangle 555 tall and 4+34 + 34+3 wide can be cut across its width into a 555 by 444 piece and a 555 by 333 piece, and the two areas add back to the whole. That is why 5×4+5×35 \times 4 + 5 \times 35×4+5×3 returns the same 353535.

    Part B has no sum inside the parentheses. The rule needs two numbers that are added, and 4×34 \times 34×3 is a single product, not two pieces adding to something, so the rule does not apply. Multiplying the 444 by 555 and the 333 by 555 puts the 555 in twice:

    (5×4)×(5×3)=5×5×4×3.(5 \times 4) \times (5 \times 3) = 5 \times 5 \times 4 \times 3.(5×4)×(5×3)=5×5×4×3.

    That is 5×605 \times 605×60, not 606060, which is exactly the factor of five between 300300300 and the correct 606060.

    In one line

    In part A both routes give 353535. In part B they disagree: 5×12=605 \times 12 = 605×12=60, while (5×4)×(5×3)=20×15=300(5 \times 4) \times (5 \times 3) = 20 \times 15 = 300(5×4)×(5×3)=20×15=300. Splitting works over a sum, which is a width a rectangle can be cut across, and fails over a product because applying the 555 to both factors puts it into the answer twice.

    3. Damaged notebooks in every crate . Application, 11 points. Question 3 of 5.

    Application 11 pts

    A shop receives 888 identical crates of notebooks. Each crate holds 252525 notebooks, and in each crate exactly 444 notebooks arrive water-damaged and cannot be sold.

    1. A
      Part A.

      Write a single expression for the number of sellable notebooks. Build it from three numbers: the crates, the notebooks in one crate, and the damaged notebooks in that crate. Then say what each of those three numbers counts.

      Write the expression An equation or an expression is enough here. Show how you built it. 3 points

    2. B
      Part B.

      Evaluate that expression two ways. First work out the parentheses. Then send the number of crates to both numbers inside the parentheses instead. Report the count each time.

      Carry your own answer forward Work from the expression you wrote in part A, whatever form it took. If you would now write it differently, say so and carry on with the version you prefer; the marks here are for the two routes of evaluation, not for part A a second time.

      Solve and show your work Write each step out, and end with the value and its units. 4 points

    3. C
      Part C.

      What does 8×258 \times 258×25 count in the shop? What does 8×48 \times 48×4 count? Explain why subtracting the second count from the first gives the same number of sellable notebooks as removing the 444 damaged ones from each crate first.

      Explain what it means Words, not just symbols. Say what the number is telling you about the situation. 4 points

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 3

      There are two ways to count. First subtract the damaged notebooks in one crate, then multiply by the number of crates. Or count every notebook that arrived, then subtract all the damaged ones. Both must give the same number.

    2. Hint 2 of 3 · Part B

      One route needs a subtraction and then a multiplication. The other needs two multiplications and then a subtraction, and the sign joining the two products is the same sign that sat inside the parentheses.

    3. Hint 3 of 3 · Part C

      Say what each product counts in the shop, as a number of notebooks of one kind. Then say what the subtraction removes.

    That is every hint for this question.

    Answer and solution

    Check your answer first. If it is wrong, go back to your paper: the worked solution will still be here.

    The answer

    Part A

    8×(25−4)8 \times (25 - 4)8×(25−4), where 888 counts the crates, 252525 counts the notebooks in one crate, and 444 counts the damaged notebooks in that same crate.

    Part B

    8×21=1688 \times 21 = 1688×21=168 notebooks, and 8×25−8×4=200−32=1688 \times 25 - 8 \times 4 = 200 - 32 = 1688×25−8×4=200−32=168 notebooks.

    Part C

    The first product counts every notebook delivered, damaged ones included; the second counts all the damaged notebooks across the eight crates. Removing that whole amount at the end discards exactly the notebooks the crate-by-crate route discards 444 at a time, so the two counts agree.

    Not what you got? Look for the slip on your own paper before you open the solution. Finding it yourself is worth more than reading it.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Work one crate at a time first. A crate arrives holding 252525 notebooks and 444 of them cannot be sold, so a single crate yields 25−425 - 425−4 sellable notebooks. Every crate is identical, so there are 888 such yields:

    8×(25−4).8 \times (25 - 4).8×(25−4).

    The parentheses carry the meaning here. They say that the subtraction is a fact about one crate, and that all 888 crates deliver that same yield.

    Part B

    Taking the parentheses first, one crate yields 25−4=2125 - 4 = 2125−4=21 sellable notebooks, and eight crates yield

    8×21=168.8 \times 21 = 168.8×21=168.

    Distributing instead multiplies both numbers inside by 888, and the two products are joined by the same minus sign that sat inside the parentheses:

    8×25−8×4=200−32=168.8 \times 25 - 8 \times 4 = 200 - 32 = 168.8×25−8×4=200−32=168.

    Both routes report 168168168 sellable notebooks.

    Part C

    Read each product back into the shop. The product 8×25=2008 \times 25 = 2008×25=200 counts every notebook that came off the truck, sellable or not, as though nothing had been damaged. The product 8×4=328 \times 4 = 328×4=32 counts the damaged notebooks, 444 from each of the 888 crates, pooled into one pile.

    200−32=168.200 - 32 = 168.200−32=168.

    The subtraction removes that pile all at once. The parentheses route removes the same notebooks 444 at a time, one crate at a time, before any total is worked out. Both routes remove the same notebooks and keep the same notebooks, so they cannot report different counts.

    Distributing the 888 does not change the shop. It changes only the order of the counting, from crate by crate to all notebooks first and all damage second.

    In one line

    The shop can sell 8×(25−4)=8×21=1688 \times (25 - 4) = 8 \times 21 = 1688×(25−4)=8×21=168 notebooks, and distributing gives the same 168168168 from 200−32200 - 32200−32. The 200200200 counts every notebook delivered and the 323232 counts every damaged one, so removing the damage in one pile keeps exactly the notebooks that removing 444 per crate keeps.

    4. Multiplying in your head by splitting a factor . Application, 12 points. Question 4 of 5.

    Application 12 pts

    Distribution turns one awkward multiplication into two easy ones: replace a factor by a round number and a small gap, then send the other factor to both parts of the split.

    1. A
      Part A.

    Hints

    One at a time, each one a step further than the last. Take only as many as you need.

    1. Hint 1 of 4

    3. Hint 3 of 4 · Part B

    The answer

    Part A

    7×48=3367 \times 48 = 3367×48=336.

    Part B

    8×63=5048 \times 63 = 5048×63=504, using the split 63=60+363 = 60 + 363=60+3. Any split that recovers 636363, whether by adding or by subtracting the gap, gives the same product.

    Part C

    The third expression is the first wrong one: the 777 reached the 505050 but not the 222, so 350−2350 - 2350−2 should read 350−14350 - 14350−14. The corrected value is 336336336.

    5. Running the rule backward . Reasoning, 14 points. Question 5 of 5.

      The rule reads correctly in both directions. Left to right it spreads a factor across a sum; right to left it collects a repeated factor back into one multiplication.

    2. Hint 2 of 4 · Part A

      The two products in front of you begin with the same number. That number can stand outside a single pair of parentheses while the two numbers it was multiplying add inside.

    3. Hint 3 of 4 · Part B

      List the numbers that divide each term, then take the largest one appearing on both lists. A smaller shared number is true but leaves work undone.

    4. Hint 4 of 4 · Part C

      Test the two claims separately. Multiply the right side out to settle the equation, then look only at what the two numbers inside the parentheses still have in common.

    Worked solution

    Part A

    Both products are built on the same factor 777, so the rule read from right to left takes that 777 out in front and leaves the two other numbers inside one pair of parentheses:

    7×13+7×7=7×(13+7).7 \times 13 + 7 \times 7 = 7 \times (13 + 7).7×13+7×7=7×(13+7).

    The inside now adds to a round number:

    7×20=140.7 \times 20 = 140.7×20=140.

    The collected form is less work: one multiplication by a round number instead of two awkward products and an addition. Multiplying out the long way gives 91+49=14091 + 49 = 14091+49=140 as well, so that route is a check rather than a shortcut.

    Part B

    Look for the largest number that divides both terms. The numbers dividing 454545 are 1,3,5,9,15,451, 3, 5, 9, 15, 451,3,5,9,15,45, and those dividing 727272 are 1,2,3,4,6,8,9,12,18,24,36,721, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 721,2,3,4,6,8,9,12,18,24,36,72. The largest they share is 999, and 45=9×545 = 9 \times 545=9×5 while 72=9×872 = 9 \times 872=9×8, so the shared 999 comes out in front:

    45+72=9×5+9×8=9×(5+8).45 + 72 = 9 \times 5 + 9 \times 8 = 9 \times (5 + 8).45+72=9×5+9×8=9×(5+8).

    Multiplying back out is the check: 9×5=459 \times 5 = 459×5=45 and 9×8=729 \times 8 = 729×8=72, which is the sum we began with. The factoring is finished, because 555 and 888 share no factor above 111, so there is nothing left inside to pull out. If you want the total as well, either side comes to 117117117.

    Part C

    There are two separate checks here. Distribute back to test whether the equation is true. Then look at the two numbers inside the parentheses to test whether the factoring is finished. An equation can pass the first check and fail the second.

    Is the equation true? Multiply the right side back out. 2×15=302 \times 15 = 302×15=30 and 2×21=422 \times 21 = 422×21=42, so the right side is 30+4230 + 4230+42, exactly the left side. Both sides come to 727272. There is nothing wrong with the equation.

    Is the factoring finished? Look at what the two numbers inside the parentheses still share. Both 151515 and 212121 are divisible by 333, so a common factor is still sitting inside. Taking that out as well collects a 666 in front:

    30+42=6×(5+7).30 + 42 = 6 \times (5 + 7).30+42=6×(5+7).

    Now 555 and 777 share no factor above 111, so nothing remains inside and the factoring is complete. Checking, 6×5=306 \times 5 = 306×5=30 and 6×7=426 \times 7 = 426×7=42. Notice that the classmate's 222 and the extra 333 multiply to that 666, which is why the two answers agree rather than contradict each other. The classmate started correctly and stopped one step early.

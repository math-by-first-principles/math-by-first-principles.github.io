Properties of Addition and Multiplication

Intuition

You have already used the rules in this lesson, you just have not named them. You add 28+47+228 + 47 + 228+47+2 by pairing 282828 and 222 into a round 303030 first, or work out 4×17×254 \times 17 \times 254×17×25 by multiplying 4×25=1004 \times 25 = 1004×25=100 before touching the 171717. Either way, you are quietly reordering and regrouping the numbers to reach an easier path. This lesson is about why those rearrangements never change a sum or a product. It is also about where they quietly fail, because −-− and ÷\div÷ do not allow them.

Learning goals

  • Tell the commutative property (order) apart from the associative property (grouping)
  • Explain why reordering or regrouping cannot change a sum or a product
  • Identify 000 and 111 as the identities, and say why multiplying by 000 gives 000
  • Reorder and regroup a long sum or product to reach the easiest path
  • Give a counterexample for each way subtraction and division fail these two rules

What a “property” is claiming

A property of an operation is a statement that holds for every choice of numbers, not just a lucky few. So a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a is not the single fact ”3+53 + 53+5 equals 888.” It is the promise that the swap works no matter what aaa and bbb are. The letters aaa, bbb, and ccc below stand for numbers you pick.

The pictures here use whole numbers, because those are the easiest to draw, so the reasons below are given for whole numbers. Each rule keeps working for the fractions and negative numbers you meet later. An example shows the pattern once; a general reason explains why the pattern keeps working, and every rule below comes with one.

Three words save a lot of repetition. Any calculation written out in symbols, such as 3+53 + 53+5 or 10−4−210 - 4 - 210−4−2, is an expression: it names a value without yet being worked out. The numbers being added in a sum are its terms, so 333 and 555 are the terms of 3+53 + 53+5. The numbers being multiplied in a product are its factors, so 666 and 999 are the factors of 6×96 \times 96×9.

The commutative property: order does not matter

Swapping the two inputs of an addition or a multiplication leaves the result unchanged. Check that on a pair of numbers: 3+8=113 + 8 = 113+8=11 and 8+3=118 + 3 = 118+3=11, while 6×9=546 \times 9 = 546×9=54 and 9×6=549 \times 6 = 549×6=54. An operation that behaves this way is called commutative, and addition and multiplication both are:

a+b=b+a,a×b=b×a.a + b = b + a, \qquad a \times b = b \times a.a+b=b+a,a×b=b×a.

The name comes from “commute,” to move from place to place: the numbers may move past each other freely.

Why a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a#

Lay a stick of length 444 end to end with a stick of length 999, and the row measures 131313. Now swap the two pieces, so the 999-stick comes first and the 444-stick second. Nothing was stretched or cut, so it is the same row and it still measures 131313. That is why 4+94 + 94+9 and 9+49 + 49+4 both come to 131313.

Nothing in that argument used the lengths 444 and 999. Call the two lengths aaa and bbb instead, and every step reads the same. Laying them the other way round gives the same row, measured from the other end. So a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a for any two lengths.

∎

For multiplication the same idea becomes an array of dots that you can count two ways.

Why a×b=b×aa \times b = b \times aa×b=b×a#

Take the grid drawn above, 333 rows with 555 dots in each row. Counting row by row gives 3×5=153 \times 5 = 153×5=15 dots. Now read the very same grid by columns: it has 555 columns with 333 dots in each, which counts to 5×3=155 \times 3 = 155×3=15. Not one dot moved between the two counts, so both had to land on 151515.

The grid did not have to be 333 by 555. Call it aaa rows of bbb dots, and reading the same grid by columns gives bbb columns of aaa dots. Tilting your view adds no dots and removes none, so a×b=b×aa \times b = b \times aa×b=b×a for any whole numbers aaa and bbb.

∎

Those dots settle one pair of numbers, and a property claims something about every pair. So go hunting for a pair that breaks it. In the rectangle below you set the width and the height, and the readout counts the unit squares inside. Look for a width and a height whose swap changes that count.

No pair breaks it, and the reason is the one the dots gave. A swap stands the rectangle on its end without moving a single square, so the count cannot change.

The associative property: grouping does not matter

Commutativity is about the order of two numbers. The next property is about something different. When you combine three numbers, you can only do one +++ (or one ×\times×) at a time. So you must choose which pair to combine first. The parentheses say “do this pair first.” Below, both choices are tried on 222, 777, and 333. The numbers hold their places; only the pair you combine first changes.

Two different routes, the same total. An operation is associative when that choice of first pair never changes the result:

(a+b)+c=a+(b+c),(a×b)×c=a×(b×c).(a + b) + c = a + (b + c), \qquad (a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c).(a+b)+c=a+(b+c),(a×b)×c=a×(b×c).

Why regrouping is safe

Why (a+b)+c=a+(b+c)(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)#

Lay three sticks in a row: one of length 222, then one of length 777, then one of length 333. Fusing the first pair gives (2+7)+3=9+3=12(2 + 7) + 3 = 9 + 3 = 12(2+7)+3=9+3=12. Fusing the last pair instead gives 2+(7+3)=2+10=122 + (7 + 3) = 2 + 10 = 122+(7+3)=2+10=12. The sticks never moved, so the row was 121212 long before either fusing began.

Nothing there depended on 222, 777, and 333. Lay sticks of length aaa, bbb, and ccc in that order. The row has a total length already, before you decide how to add. Fusing aaa to bbb first, or bbb to ccc first, glues the same three pieces at the same two joints. Gluing joints in a different order cannot lengthen or shorten the row, so

(a+b)+c=a+(b+c).(a + b) + c = a + (b + c).(a+b)+c=a+(b+c).

∎

Multiplication regroups for the same reason, with equal groups in place of a row of sticks. The figure below holds three bags, two boxes to a bag, and five marbles to a box.

Why (a×b)×c=a×(b×c)(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)(a×b)×c=a×(b×c)#

Both routes in the figure count one fixed pile of marbles, so (3×2)×5=3×(2×5)(3 \times 2) \times 5 = 3 \times (2 \times 5)(3×2)×5=3×(2×5), with 303030 at the end of each.

Nothing there depended on 333, 222, and 555. Take aaa bags, bbb boxes to a bag, and ccc marbles to a box. Grouping aaa with bbb counts the boxes first, and grouping bbb with ccc counts one bag first. The marbles are in the bags before you pick a route, so neither route can reach a different total.

∎

Because the grouping never matters, we are allowed to drop the parentheses entirely and write a+b+ca + b + ca+b+c or a×b×ca \times b \times ca×b×c. Every way of putting them back gives the same value, so none of them is needed.

Order versus grouping, kept straight

The two words name different freedoms, and the root word is the giveaway. Commute means move, so the commutative property moves numbers past each other: it changes their order. Associate means group, so the associative property changes which numbers are grouped first: it changes where the parentheses go. Commutativity needs two numbers to say anything; associativity needs three.

Check your understanding

Which of these equations shows the associative property?

Answer choices

In 7×5=5×77 \times 5 = 5 \times 77×5=5×7 the two factors trade places, so that one is commutative.

(7×5)×2=70=7×(5×2)(7 \times 5) \times 2 = 70 = 7 \times (5 \times 2)(7×5)×2=70=7×(5×2)

Here the three factors stay in the same order and only the parentheses move, which is the associative property. Order is one freedom, grouping is the other.

Reshuffling a calculation

Used together, the two rules give you permission. You may reorder a sum or a product and then regroup it into whatever form is easiest, and the value will not change.

Worked example 1 Compute 4×17×254 \times 17 \times 254×17×25 the easy way

Multiplying in the printed order gives 4×17=684 \times 17 = 684×17=68, then the clumsy 68×2568 \times 2568×25. Instead, move the 252525 next to the 444 and group that pair first:

4×17×25=4×25×17(commutative: swap the order)=(4×25)×17(associative: group the easy pair)=100×17=1700.\begin{aligned} 4 \times 17 \times 25 &= 4 \times 25 \times 17 &&\text{(commutative: swap the order)}\\ &= (4 \times 25) \times 17 &&\text{(associative: group the easy pair)}\\ &= 100 \times 17\\ &= 1700. \end{aligned}4×17×25​=4×25×17=(4×25)×17=100×17=1700.​(commutative: swap the order)(associative: group the easy pair)

The two properties are what guarantee this rearranged product equals the original one, so nothing is lost by choosing the convenient route.

Worked example 2 Add 28+47+228 + 47 + 228+47+2 by making a round number

The numbers 282828 and 222 pair into a clean 303030, so bring them together first:

28+47+2=28+2+47(commutative property of addition)=(28+2)+47(associative property of addition)=30+47=77.\begin{aligned} 28 + 47 + 2 &= 28 + 2 + 47 &&\text{(commutative property of addition)}\\ &= (28 + 2) + 47 &&\text{(associative property of addition)}\\ &= 30 + 47\\ &= 77. \end{aligned}28+47+2​=28+2+47=(28+2)+47=30+47=77.​(commutative property of addition)(associative property of addition)

Reordering to create the round 303030 first turns an awkward sum into one you can finish in your head.

Every route reaches 858585, because that is what these two properties guarantee. This one is the easiest to hold in your head, since 40+4540 + 4540+45 needs no carrying.

The identity elements: the numbers that change nothing

Adding nothing to a quantity leaves it as it was, so 17+0=1717 + 0 = 1717+0=17. Taking exactly one copy of a quantity is the quantity itself, so 17×1=1717 \times 1 = 1717×1=17. A number you can combine with any number and change nothing is called an identity element for that operation. The rule saying so is the identity property. Addition and multiplication each have one identity:

a+0=a,a×1=a.a + 0 = a, \qquad a \times 1 = a.a+0=a,a×1=a.

Zero is the additive identity and one is the multiplicative identity.

The zero property of multiplication

Multiplying any number by 000 collapses it to 000. Keep the reading from the dot array: the first factor counts the groups, and the second counts what sits in each group. So 7×07 \times 07×0 is seven groups with nothing in them, and seven helpings of nothing is nothing. Turn it round: 0×70 \times 70×7 is zero groups of seven. You never pick up a group at all, so again you have nothing. The same reading works for every number:

a×0=0.a \times 0 = 0.a×0=0.

This is a separate fact from the identity property, and it is easy to mix them up. The identity 111 leaves aaa untouched; the number 000 wipes aaa out.

Check your understanding

Which equation shows the multiplicative identity?

Answer choices

The multiplicative identity is the number that leaves a value unchanged under multiplication, and that number is 111.

9×1=99 \times 1 = 99×1=9

Multiplying by 111 keeps 999 as 999. Adding 000 is the additive identity, and multiplying by 000 is the zero property.

Both rules earn their keep when they let you skip work:

Worked example 3 Simplify (53×0)+(1×38)(53 \times 0) + (1 \times 38)(53×0)+(1×38)

Use the zero property on the first product and the multiplicative identity on the second:

(53×0)+(1×38)=0+38(zero property; multiplicative identity)=38(additive identity).\begin{aligned} (53 \times 0) + (1 \times 38) &= 0 + 38 &&\text{(zero property; multiplicative identity)}\\ &= 38 &&\text{(additive identity)}. \end{aligned}(53×0)+(1×38)​=0+38=38​​(zero property; multiplicative identity)(additive identity).​

The entire first product collapses to 000, multiplying 383838 by 111 leaves it alone, and adding 000 to 383838 keeps it at 383838. Three properties, one line.

Why subtraction and division do not get these properties

The order rule and the grouping rule were both stated for +++ and ×\times× only, and that restriction is not an accident. Subtraction and division are neither commutative nor associative. To prove a “for every number” claim false you only need a single example where it breaks.

Order matters for both. Start from 5−3=25 - 3 = 25−3=2. Reversing it asks for 3−53 - 53−5, which means taking 555 away from just 333. You run out before you finish, dropping below zero, so the result cannot also be 222. Division reverses just as badly. From 8÷2=48 \div 2 = 48÷2=4, swapping to 2÷82 \div 82÷8 asks how many 888s fit inside 222. Not even one does, so the answer is less than a whole, nowhere near 444:

5−3≠3−5,8÷2≠2÷8.5 - 3 \ne 3 - 5, \qquad 8 \div 2 \ne 2 \div 8.5−3=3−5,8÷2=2÷8.

Grouping matters too, which is why an expression like 10−4−210 - 4 - 210−4−2 would be genuinely ambiguous without a rule. The two groupings disagree:

Subtraction is not associative#

Take a=10a = 10a=10, b=4b = 4b=4, and c=2c = 2c=2.

Grouping the first pair:

(a−b)−c=(10−4)−2=6−2=4.(a - b) - c = (10 - 4) - 2 = 6 - 2 = 4.(a−b)−c=(10−4)−2=6−2=4.

Grouping the second pair:

a−(b−c)=10−(4−2)=10−2=8.a - (b - c) = 10 - (4 - 2) = 10 - 2 = 8.a−(b−c)=10−(4−2)=10−2=8.

Since 4≠84 \ne 84=8, there is a choice of numbers for which (a−b)−c(a - b) - c(a−b)−c and a−(b−c)a - (b - c)a−(b−c) disagree. One counterexample is enough, so subtraction is not associative. (The same numbers also break commutativity: 10−4=610 - 4 = 610−4=6, but 4−104 - 104−10 reverses which number is taken from which and cannot give 666.)

∎

Division breaks in exactly the same way. Grouping the first pair, (16÷4)÷2(16 \div 4) \div 2(16÷4)÷2 is 4÷2=24 \div 2 = 24÷2=2. Grouping the second pair, 16÷(4÷2)16 \div (4 \div 2)16÷(4÷2) is 16÷2=816 \div 2 = 816÷2=8. The same three numbers gave 222 one way and 888 the other, so division is not associative either.

Because the grouping really does change the value, 10−4−210 - 4 - 210−4−2 is not safe to regroup the way a sum is. We rescue it with a convention: read a chain of subtractions (or divisions) left to right, so 10−4−210 - 4 - 210−4−2 means (10−4)−2=4(10 - 4) - 2 = 4(10−4)−2=4. The parentheses there are doing real work and cannot be moved for free. Treat the rules of this lesson as belonging to +++ and ×\times× alone.

What a chain of removals still allows

Losing both properties does not mean that nothing in a chain may ever move. Read 80−25−1580 - 25 - 1580−25−15 left to right: it takes 252525 off 808080, then takes 151515 off what is left. That is two removals from one starting amount, and the amount gone is 25+15=4025 + 15 = 4025+15=40 whichever removal you make first.

80−25−15=40,80−15−25=40.80 - 25 - 15 = 40, \qquad 80 - 15 - 25 = 40.80−25−15=40,80−15−25=40.

A chain of divisions behaves the same way, with the two divisors multiplying instead of adding. Dividing by 121212 and then by 333 cuts the number into 12×3=3612 \times 3 = 3612×3=36 equal parts, and so does dividing by 333 and then by 121212:

720÷12÷3=20,720÷3÷12=20.720 \div 12 \div 3 = 20, \qquad 720 \div 3 \div 12 = 20.720÷12÷3=20,720÷3÷12=20.

Read that narrowly, because it is easy to over-claim. It is not commutativity, which would let the 808080 and the 252525 trade places. It is not associativity, which would let the last two numbers be grouped: 80−(25−15)80 - (25 - 15)80−(25−15) is 707070, not 404040. The first number of a chain never moves. Only the numbers being removed may trade places, and likewise the numbers being divided by. The chain depends on them just through their total, or through their product. Working in whole numbers, the removals must not add up past the number they are taken from, and each division must come out exactly.

Check your understanding

Is the statement 12÷4=4÷1212 \div 4 = 4 \div 1212÷4=4÷12 true?

Answer choices

Division is not commutative, so swapping the numbers generally changes the result.

12÷4=3,4÷12≠312 \div 4 = 3, \qquad 4 \div 12 \ne 312÷4=3,4÷12=3

Reversing 12÷412 \div 412÷4 asks how many 121212s fit in 444, which is less than one whole, so it cannot equal 333. The statement is false.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Assuming subtraction or division commute. 5−3≠3−55 - 3 \ne 3 - 55−3=3−5 and 8÷2≠2÷88 \div 2 \ne 2 \div 88÷2=2÷8. A sum or a product can be reordered freely. A subtraction or a division gives no such licence, and the first number of a chain never moves.
  • Confusing commutative with associative. Commutative moves the order of two numbers (a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a); associative changes the grouping of three ((a+b)+c=a+(b+c)(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)).
  • Thinking a×0=aa \times 0 = aa×0=a. Multiplying by 000 gives 000, which is only the same as aaa when aaa was already 000. The number that leaves every number unchanged under multiplication is the identity 111, not 000.
  • Mixing up the two identities. The additive identity is 000 (a+0=aa + 0 = aa+0=a); the multiplicative identity is 111 (a×1=aa \times 1 = aa×1=a). Adding 111 or multiplying by 000 does not leave a number alone.

Key takeaways

  • Commutative (order): a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a and a×b=b×aa \times b = b \times aa×b=b×a.
  • Associative (grouping): (a+b)+c=a+(b+c)(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)(a+b)+c=a+(b+c) and (a×b)×c=a×(b×c)(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)(a×b)×c=a×(b×c). This is why a+b+ca + b + ca+b+c needs no parentheses, and the two rules together let you reorder and regroup any sum or product into the easiest path.
  • Identity and zero: a+0=aa + 0 = aa+0=a and a×1=aa \times 1 = aa×1=a leave a number alone, while a×0=0a \times 0 = 0a×0=0 wipes it out. The zero property is a separate rule from the identities.
  • Subtraction and division are neither commutative nor associative; a single counterexample such as (10−4)−2≠10−(4−2)(10 - 4) - 2 \ne 10 - (4 - 2)(10−4)−2=10−(4−2) settles it, and we read them left to right by convention. A chain still lets the numbers being removed trade places, and likewise the divisors. It feels them only through their total or their product.

A bit of history (optional)

Some facts are so familiar that it is genuinely hard to say why they hold. Ask why 3+53 + 53+5 and 5+35 + 35+3 agree, and the first answer most people reach for is that they simply do.

In 1889 an Italian mathematician named Giuseppe Peano decided that was not good enough. He wrote out a short list of basic rules for the counting numbers. Start at one; every number has a next one; that sort of thing. Nothing on the list mentioned the order of a sum.

Yet the swap is true, so it had to follow from the list. He had to prove it, and the proof was genuine work rather than a shrug. That is what proving something is for. It cannot make a fact truer, but it reveals exactly what the fact is resting on.

The sticks and the grid of dots in this lesson are not that proof, and they do not rest on his list. They do something of the same kind on a smaller scale. Each argument started on one pair of numbers, then showed that nothing in it had depended on the pair you started with.

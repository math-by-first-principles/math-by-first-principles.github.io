Place Value and the Number System

Intuition

We name every whole number, from 777 to 7,000,0007{,}000{,}0007,000,000, with just ten symbols: 0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,90, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 90,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9. The trick that makes this possible is that the position of a digit decides how much it is worth. The 333 in 303030 means thirty, but the 333 in 300300300 means three hundred, even though the symbol is the same. Letting position carry the size is what keeps ten digits enough, however large the whole number gets. That idea is called place value.

Learning goals

  • Read a digit's value as the digit times its place value
  • Explain why each place is worth ten times the place to its right
  • Write a number in expanded form, and say what a zero digit holds open
  • Group large numbers into periods of three to read and write them
  • Compare two whole numbers left to right, stopping at the first place that differs

The base-10 system

Our number system is base 10, also called the decimal system. Base ten means that we bundle in groups of ten. Ten ones make one ten, ten tens make one hundred, ten hundreds make one thousand, and the bundling carries on without ever stopping. People chose that bundle size, and everything else about the system follows from it. There are exactly ten digits, 000 through 999, because ten single units are the point at which a new column has to open. And each place is worth ten times the place immediately to its right. One unit of a place is one whole bundle of ten units of the place to its right.

Reading a whole number from right to left, the places are the ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, and so on:

…,10,000,1,000,100,10,1.\ldots,\quad 10{,}000,\quad 1{,}000,\quad 100,\quad 10,\quad 1.…,10,000,1,000,100,10,1.

Digit value equals digit times place value

A digit by itself is just a symbol. Its value inside a number is the digit multiplied by the value of the place it sits in. Take the number 4,5724{,}5724,572 and read off each column:

DigitPlacePlace valueDigit value
444thousands1,0001{,}0001,0004,0004{,}0004,000
555hundreds100100100500500500
777tens101010707070
222ones111222

Keep two easily confused words apart. The place of the 555 is the hundreds; the value of the 555 is 5×100=5005 \times 100 = 5005×100=500. The place is a name for the column, and the value is the number the digit contributes.

Now take a single digit and move it. In the chart below there is only one digit, and you choose which column it sits in. Each step to the left multiplies what it is worth by ten, and each step to the right divides it by ten. The symbol never changes; only its address does.

Send the 555 from the ones column to the thousands column, one step at a time, and read its value at each stop. The values are 555, then 505050, then 500500500, then 5,0005{,}0005,000. Nothing about the 555 changed. Then send it back the other way and the same values come back in reverse, because moving right undoes moving left. This is the single fact the rest of the lesson is built on.

Expanded form

Adding up the digit values rewrites a number in expanded form, which lays bare exactly what each digit contributes:

4,572=4,000+500+70+2.4{,}572 = 4{,}000 + 500 + 70 + 2.4,572=4,000+500+70+2.

We can make each place value explicit:

4,572=(4×1000)+(5×100)+(7×10)+(2×1).4{,}572 = (4 \times 1000) + (5 \times 100) + (7 \times 10) + (2 \times 1).4,572=(4×1000)+(5×100)+(7×10)+(2×1).

A zero digit contributes nothing to the sum, but it is not optional. In 3,2053{,}2053,205 the 000 says that there are no tens, and it still has to be written:

3,205=(3×1000)+(2×100)+(0×10)+(5×1)=3,000+200+5.3{,}205 = (3 \times 1000) + (2 \times 100) + (0 \times 10) + (5 \times 1) = 3{,}000 + 200 + 5.3,205=(3×1000)+(2×100)+(0×10)+(5×1)=3,000+200+5.

Remove that 000 and you are left with 325325325, which is a different and far smaller number. The table below compares what each digit is worth in the two numerals:

DigitIts value in 3,2053{,}2053,205Its value in 325325325
3333,0003{,}0003,000300300300
222200200200202020
555555555

The 333 and the 222 each ended up in a column worth a tenth of the one they had. Zero is a placeholder: it earns its column by keeping the other digits in the columns they belong to. The modern number system depends on it.

Check your understanding

In the number 6,3086{,}3086,308, what is the value of the digit 333?

Answer choices

Read the columns from the right: 888 ones, 000 tens, 333 hundreds, 666 thousands. The 333 sits in the hundreds place, so multiply it by that place value.

3×100=3003 \times 100 = 3003×100=300

Its place is the hundreds; its value is 300300300.

Why each place is exactly ten times the next

You do not have to memorise the ten-times rule one column at a time. It follows from the way the columns are built out of bundles of ten.

Each place is exactly 10×10\times10× the place to its right#

Look at what one unit of a column is made of. Ten ones are bundled into one ten, ten tens are bundled into one hundred, and ten hundreds are bundled into one thousand:

10=10×1,100=10×10,1,000=10×100.10 = 10 \times 1, \qquad 100 = 10 \times 10, \qquad 1{,}000 = 10 \times 100.10=10×1,100=10×10,1,000=10×100.

Every column is built the same way. One unit of it is one bundle of ten units of the column to its right, so it is worth ten of those units. That holds for every pair of neighbouring columns, not only for the three pairs written out above.

∎

Nothing in that argument says the columns have to stop at the ones. Carrying the same pattern to the right, dividing by ten at each step instead of multiplying, is where tenths and hundredths come from. You can read it in the Go deeper section at the end of this lesson.

See what comes after the ones

Bundles also answer a question the digits on their own do not. Ask how many hundreds there are in 52,00052{,}00052,000. The hundreds digit is 000, but the question is about groups of 100100100. Each thousand holds ten hundreds, so 525252 thousands hold 52×10=52052 \times 10 = 52052×10=520 hundreds:

52,000=520×100.52{,}000 = 520 \times 100.52,000=520×100.

So a whole number can be counted in any bundle size you like, not only in the digit sitting in that column. The hundreds digit counts only the hundreds left over once the thousands are taken. The number of hundreds counts every group of 100100100 inside the whole number.

Reading and writing large numbers

For numbers with many digits we group the digits into periods of three, separated by commas, working from the right. The periods are ones, then thousands, then millions, then billions.

52,408,163  =  52⏟millions ,  408⏟thousands ,  163⏟ones52{,}408{,}163 \;=\; \underbrace{52}_{\text{millions}}\,,\;\underbrace{408}_{\text{thousands}}\,,\;\underbrace{163}_{\text{ones}}52,408,163=millions52​​,thousands408​​,ones163​​

To read it, say each three-digit group followed by its period name: “fifty-two million, four hundred eight thousand, one hundred sixty-three.” The commas do not create the values, because the positions of the digits already do that. What the commas do is show you where the periods start and stop. Grouping from the right is what keeps you from misreading the size of the number.

Writing a number from words runs the same steps backwards. Take “four hundred six million, ninety thousand, twenty-five.” Write one three-digit block for each period, filling every block after the leading one out to three columns with zeros:

406⏟millions ,  090⏟thousands ,  025⏟ones  =  406,090,025.\underbrace{406}_{\text{millions}}\,,\;\underbrace{090}_{\text{thousands}}\,,\;\underbrace{025}_{\text{ones}} \;=\; 406{,}090{,}025.millions406​​,thousands090​​,ones025​​=406,090,025.

Ninety becomes 090090090 and twenty-five becomes 025025025 because each period after the leading one owns three columns, whether or not its digits reach that far.

Worked example 1 Find the value of the digit 777 in 4,709,1524{,}709{,}1524,709,152

Start by grouping 4,709,1524{,}709{,}1524,709,152 into periods from the right: the last three digits 152152152 are the ones period. The next three 709709709 are the thousands period, and the leading 444 is in the millions period.

The 777 lives inside the thousands period, in the hundreds slot of 709709709. The hundreds slot of the thousands period is the hundred-thousands place, so its place value is 100,000100{,}000100,000:

7×100,000=700,000.7 \times 100{,}000 = 700{,}000.7×100,000=700,000.

The digit on the page is only a 777, but in that position it represents seven hundred thousand.

Worked example 2 Write 20,60520{,}60520,605 in expanded form

Name the place of each digit, working from left to right:

The 222 is in the ten-thousands place:

2×10,000=20,000.2 \times 10{,}000 = 20{,}000.2×10,000=20,000.

The 000 is in the thousands place, and the 666 is in the hundreds place (6×100=6006 \times 100 = 6006×100=600). The next 000 is in the tens place, and the 555 is in the ones place. Adding only the nonzero contributions,

20,605=20,000+600+5.20{,}605 = 20{,}000 + 600 + 5.20,605=20,000+600+5.

The two zeros contribute nothing to the sum, and both columns are still essential. They are what hold the 222 in the ten-thousands column and the 666 in the hundreds.

Comparing and ordering numbers

To compare two whole numbers, lean on place value rather than on which number “looks” bigger:

  1. The number with more digits is larger, assuming neither has leading zeros. The smallest four-digit number is 1,0001{,}0001,000 and the largest three-digit number is 999999999, so 1,000>9991{,}000 > 9991,000>999.
  2. If they have the same number of digits, compare digit by digit from the left. The first place where they differ decides the whole comparison.

Compare 3,4813{,}4813,481 and 3,4793{,}4793,479. They tie in the thousands (3=33 = 33=3) and in the hundreds (4=44 = 44=4). In the tens place, 8>78 > 78>7, so 3,481>3,4793{,}481 > 3{,}4793,481>3,479, and we never even look at the ones place. Going left to right works because a single higher place outweighs everything to its right combined. One extra hundred (100100100) is more than the largest the tens and ones together can reach (999999). The comparison symbols are <<< (“less than”), >>> (“greater than”), and === (“equal to”).

Check your understanding

Which symbol makes a true statement: 5,092  □  5,1905{,}092 \;\square\; 5{,}1905,092□5,190?

Answer choices

Both numbers have four digits and tie in the thousands place (5=55 = 55=5), so move to the hundreds place. There 000 sits against 111.

0<1  ⇒  5,092<5,1900 < 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 5{,}092 < 5{,}1900<1⇒5,092<5,190

The first place that differs settles it, so the tens and ones never matter.

Worked example 3 Compare 48,30048{,}30048,300 and 48,29548{,}29548,295

Both numbers have five digits, so compare place by place from the left.

The ten-thousands tie (4=44 = 44=4) and the thousands tie (8=88 = 88=8), so keep moving right. The hundreds place is the first to differ: 333 against 222, and 3>23 > 23>2. That single column decides everything:

48,300>48,295.48{,}300 > 48{,}295.48,300>48,295.

Once a higher place settles the comparison, the lower places (000000 against 959595) cannot overturn it. That is because 300300300 already beats the most that 295295295 can offer.

Common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Confusing place with value. In 6,3086{,}3086,308 the place of the 333 is the hundreds, while its value is 300300300.
  • Dropping zeros. 3,2053{,}2053,205 and 325325325 are different numbers, so never quietly delete a zero.
  • Grouping from the wrong end. Mark off periods of three from the right before reading a long number, or the period names land on the wrong digits.
  • Comparing from the wrong end. Compare same-length numbers from the left, because the leftmost differing place decides and no lower place can override it.

Key takeaways

  • The decimal system uses ten digits (000 through 999); a digit’s value is the digit times its place value. So a digit’s position is what carries the size.
  • Every place is exactly 10×10\times10× the place to its right. That is because each place holds a bundle of ten of the units in that right-hand place.
  • Expanded form writes a number as the sum of its digit values, for example 3,205=3,000+200+53{,}205 = 3{,}000 + 200 + 53,205=3,000+200+5.
  • Zero is a placeholder: delete it and the digits to its left land in columns with smaller place values, which changes the number entirely.
  • Group large numbers into periods of three to read and write them. Compare same-length numbers left to right, stopping at the first place that differs.

That settles what a written number means. The next question is what you are allowed to do with one. Calculations can often be reordered or regrouped to make them easier. What lies to the right of the ones place

The ten-times pattern does not have to stop at the ones place. It carries on to the right, except that now each step divides by 101010 instead of multiplying. After the ones place we write a decimal point, and the places that follow are tenths, hundredths and thousandths:

…,1,110,1100,11000,…\ldots,\quad 1,\quad \tfrac{1}{10},\quad \tfrac{1}{100},\quad \tfrac{1}{1000},\quad \ldots…,1,101​,1001​,10001​,…

So in 4.274.274.27 the digit 222 is in the tenths place, worth 210=0.2\tfrac{2}{10} = 0.2102​=0.2, and the 777 is in the hundredths place, worth 7100=0.07\tfrac{7}{100} = 0.071007​=0.07:

4.27=4+210+7100.4.27 = 4 + \tfrac{2}{10} + \tfrac{7}{100}.4.27=4+102​+1007​.

Everything this lesson said about columns still holds here. A digit’s value is still the digit times its place value, and the places still shrink by a factor of ten at every step to the right. Numbers written with a point like this are called decimals, and a later chapter takes them up properly.

A bit of history (optional)

A blank space is a poor way to record nothing. Leave a column empty and your reader has to guess whether a place was skipped, or whether there was never a column there at all.

The Maya, a people of Central America, refused to leave it to chance. They wrote a number as a stack, and in ordinary counting every level was worth twenty times the level below it. A single mark could mean one, or twenty, or four hundred, depending on its position.

For a level that held nothing, they carved a shell. The shell was not a gap but a digit like any other, announcing that this level is empty and still counts. Stone carvings from around the year 350 still show it, set into the recorded dates of kings.

The shell did the same job our 000 does, in a system built on twenties instead of tens. In 3,2053{,}2053,205 the 000 holds the tens place open so the 222 stays in the hundreds. Drop it and the number collapses to 325325325.

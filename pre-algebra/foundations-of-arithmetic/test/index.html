    A counter displays 507,260507{,}260507,260. Its 777 is replaced by 000, with every other digit left in the same position. How much does the displayed number decrease?

    Key idea

    Replacing one digit by zero removes that digit's value when all other digits keep their positions.

  5. Problem 5 A four-step number machine

    Adding zero and multiplying by one leave the starting value unchanged.

    Multiplying by zero then removes its effect.

    841+0=841841×1=841841×0=00+23=23\begin{aligned}841+0&=841\\841\times1&=841\\841\times0&=0\\0+23&=23\end{aligned}841+0841×1841×00+23​=841=841=0=23​

    For any whole-number input, the first two steps preserve it and the third step produces 000.

    The last step therefore produces 232323 for every such input.

    Check with a different starting value, 000: all three initial steps leave 000, followed by the same final addition.

    Answer

    232323; every whole-number starting value gives the same output.

    Key idea

    Multiplication by zero makes the final value independent of the starting input when the later operations are fixed.

  6. Problem 6 A stacked expression

    Evaluate 2(1+6)228÷2\dfrac{2(1+6)^2}{28\div2}28÷22(1+6)2​.

    1. Hint 1

      The fraction bar groups the entire numerator and the entire denominator.

    2. Hint 2

      In the numerator, settle the parentheses before the power and the outside multiplication.

    3. Hint 3

      After evaluating the denominator, divide the completed numerator by it.

    Answer

    777.

    Full solution

    Start with the innermost group, then evaluate its square and the multiplication.

    1+6=772=492×49=98\begin{aligned}1+6&=7\\7^2&=49\\2\times49&=98\end{aligned}1+6722×49​=7=49=98​

    The denominator is a separate group.

    28÷2=1428\div2=1428÷2=14

    The denominator is 141414, which is nonzero, so divide the completed numerator by it.

    9814=7\frac{98}{14}=71498​=7

    Check that the quotient times the denominator recovers the numerator:

    7×14=987\times14=987×14=98

    Answer

    777.

    Key idea

    A fraction bar keeps its numerator and denominator grouped while the operation tiers govern each group.

  7. Problem 7 Updating identical packets

    A volunteer has 18 pencils and 30 erasers. She makes the greatest possible number of identical packets, using every item. Each packet must contain the same positive number of pencils and the same positive number of erasers.

    She then puts 2 additional erasers into every packet. How many packets are there, and how many items are in all the updated packets combined?

    1. Hint 1

      The packet count must divide both original item counts exactly.

    2. Hint 2

      Find the greatest possible packet count first, then find the contents of one packet.

    3. Hint 3

      Update the eraser count in one packet before counting each item type across all packets.

    Answer

    6 packets; 60 items.

    Full solution

    The packet count divides both 181818 and 303030.

    The divisors of 181818 are 111, 222, 333, 666, 999 and 181818.

    Of these, 999 and 181818 do not divide 303030, but 666 does.

    Thus the greatest possible count is 6 packets.

    Find the original contents of each packet.

    18÷6=330÷6=5\begin{aligned}18\div6&=3\\30\div6&=5\end{aligned}18÷630÷6​=3=5​

    The original total can be written as

    18+30=6(3+5)18+30=6(3+5)18+30=6(3+5)

    Each updated packet has 3 pencils and 5+25+25+2 erasers.

    5+2=75+2=75+2=7

    Count both parts across all 6 packets.

    6(3+7)=6×3+6×76×3=186×7=4218+42=60\begin{aligned}6(3+7)&=6\times3+6\times7\\6\times3&=18\\6\times7&=42\\18+42&=60\end{aligned}6(3+7)6×36×718+42​=6×3+6×7=18=42=60​

    Check the change against the original total.

    There were 18+3018+3018+30 items and 2 erasers were added to each of 6 packets.

    18+30=486×2=1248+12=60\begin{aligned}18+30&=48\\6\times2&=12\\48+12&=60\end{aligned}18+306×248+12​=48=12=60​

    Answer

    6 packets; 60 items.

    Key idea

    The greatest shared divisor determines the packet count, and updating each packet changes the totals for every copy.

  8. Problem 8 A proposed rewrite

    A student replaces 4[7+(20÷5)]4[7+(20\div5)]4[7+(20÷5)] with 4×7+20÷54\times7+20\div54×7+20÷5. Find the value of each expression and decide whether the replacement preserves the value. Explain your decision.

    1. Hint 1

      Check which quantities are multiplied by the outside 444 in each expression.

    2. Hint 2

      In the first expression, evaluate the inner division and then the entire bracket.

    3. Hint 3

      In the second expression, complete its multiplication and division before its addition.

    Answer

    444444 and 323232, respectively; the replacement does not preserve the value.

    Full solution

    The bracket groups both 777 and the quotient for multiplication by 444.

    20÷5=47+4=114×11=44\begin{aligned}20\div5&=4\\7+4&=11\\4\times11&=44\end{aligned}20÷57+44×11​=4=11=44​

    In the replacement, the 444 multiplies just the 777.

    4×7=2820÷5=428+4=32\begin{aligned}4\times7&=28\\20\div5&=4\\28+4&=32\end{aligned}4×720÷528+4​=28=4=32​

    The replacement leaves the quotient unmultiplied by 444.

    To preserve the first expression, both parts must receive that factor.

    4(7+4)=4×7+4×428+16=44\begin{aligned}4(7+4)&=4\times7+4\times4\\28+16&=44\end{aligned}4(7+4)28+16​=4×7+4×4=44​

    The two values, 444444 and 323232, disagree, so the replacement does not preserve the value.

    Answer

    444444 and 323232, respectively; the replacement does not preserve the value.

    Key idea

    Dropping the brackets around a group that a factor multiplies requires that factor to multiply every term of the group.

  9. Problem 9 Testing a general claim

    A learner notices that swapping the inputs in 9−99-99−9 leaves its value unchanged. The learner also notices that (36÷1)÷1(36\div1)\div1(36÷1)÷1 and 36÷(1÷1)36\div(1\div1)36÷(1÷1) have the same value.

    The learner concludes that subtraction is commutative and division is associative. For each conclusion, say what was special about the learner's example, and give a counterexample to each conclusion that fails. For the division counterexample, use positive whole numbers with exact whole-number quotients in both groupings.

    1. Hint 1

      A property must hold for every allowed choice of numbers, so a successful example does not establish it.

    2. Hint 2

      To test the subtraction claim, try reversing two unequal whole numbers.

    3. Hint 3

      To test the division claim, choose a divisor greater than 111 inside the group and compare the two groupings.

    Answer

    The subtraction example used two equal numbers, and the division example divided by 111. Neither conclusion is valid; for example, 11−4≠4−1111-4\ne4-1111−4=4−11 and (32÷4)÷2≠32÷(4÷2)(32\div4)\div2\ne32\div(4\div2)(32÷4)÷2=32÷(4÷2).

    Full solution

    The subtraction example uses equal inputs, so swapping them does not change the written calculation.

    Unequal inputs reveal the failure.

    11−4=711-4=711−4=7

    But 4−114-114−11 takes more than the available 444 and goes below zero, so it is not 777.

    This is a counterexample to commutativity.

    The division example uses 111, which leaves the values unchanged in both groupings.

    Instead compare these exact divisions.

    Group the first pair:

    32÷4=88÷2=4\begin{aligned}32\div4&=8\\8\div2&=4\end{aligned}32÷48÷2​=8=4​

    Group the last pair:

    4÷2=232÷2=16\begin{aligned}4\div2&=2\\32\div2&=16\end{aligned}4÷232÷2​=2=16​

    Every divisor in the example is positive and nonzero, and every quotient is a whole number.

    The two groupings give 444 and 161616, so division fails associativity.

    One failing example is enough to reject each general conclusion.

    Answer

    The subtraction example used two equal numbers, and the division example divided by 111. Neither conclusion is valid; for example, 11−4≠4−1111-4\ne4-1111−4=4−11 and (32÷4)÷2≠32÷(4÷2)(32\div4)\div2\ne32\div(4\div2)(32÷4)÷2=32÷(4÷2).

    Key idea

    A successful example can illustrate a property, but a single counterexample disproves a claim that it holds in every case.

  10. Problem 10 Ravi's two lines

    Ravi writes 32+56=8(4+7)32+56=8(4+7)32+56=8(4+7). He then replaces 8(4+7)8(4+7)8(4+7) with 16(2+7)16(2+7)16(2+7), saying that the 444 inside can still be divided by 222.

    Decide whether each line preserves the original sum and whether the first line has the greatest common factor of 323232 and 565656 outside the parentheses. Explain your decisions.

    1. Hint 1

      An outside factor must reproduce both original terms, not just the first one.

    2. Hint 2

      Check what each outside factor gives when multiplied by each inside number.

    3. Hint 3

      A further whole-number factor must divide both 444 and 777.

    Answer

    The first line preserves the sum and has the greatest common factor, 888, outside; the replacement does not preserve the sum.

    Full solution

    The first line reproduces both original terms.

    8×4=328×7=5632+56=88\begin{aligned}8\times4&=32\\8\times7&=56\\32+56&=88\end{aligned}8×48×732+56​=32=56=88​

    The possible divisors of 444 greater than 111 are 222 and 444, and neither divides 777.

    Thus 444 and 777 share no factor greater than 111, so 888 is already the greatest shared factor removed from the original terms.

    The replacement still reproduces 323232 but changes the other term.

    16×2=3216×7=11232+112=144\begin{aligned}16\times2&=32\\16\times7&=112\\32+112&=144\end{aligned}16×216×732+112​=32=112=144​

    Doubling the outside factor would require halving both inside terms to preserve their contributions.

    Ravi halves just one, so his replacement changes the sum.

    Check the valid expression directly:

    4+7=118×11=88\begin{aligned}4+7&=11\\8\times11&=88\end{aligned}4+78×11​=11=88​

    Answer

    The first line preserves the sum and has the greatest common factor, 888, outside; the replacement does not preserve the sum.

    Key idea

    A common factor is fully collected when the remaining whole-number terms share no factor greater than one.
